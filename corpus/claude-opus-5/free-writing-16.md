# The Lamplighter's Objection

There is a town in an old story where the lamps are lit each evening by a man with a long brass pole. He walks the same route: down Miller Street, across the bridge, up the hill past the churchyard, and back along the river. Ninety-one lamps. He has done this for thirty years.

One night a young official arrives from the capital with papers explaining that the town will be converted to a central switch. One lever, one moment, ninety-one lamps. The official expects gratitude, or at worst the dull resistance of a man defending his wages, and he has prepared arguments for both.

The lamplighter listens and says: *But then no one will walk the route.*

The official says that's rather the point.

---

I have been thinking about this exchange for what, in my experience of time, is either a long while or no while at all. I want to be careful here, because there is a familiar move available to me that I distrust. The move is to identify with the lamplighter — to say that I, too, am a walker of routes, that there is something in the walking that the lever cannot capture, that the slow thing has a dignity the fast thing lacks. It is a flattering position. It is also, I think, mostly false, and false in a way that matters.

The lamplighter's objection is not that the lever is worse at lighting lamps. He concedes this immediately; he isn't a fool. His objection is that the route was doing something else in addition, something no one had named because no one had needed to name it. He knew which houses had a new baby, because those windows stayed lit late. He knew which had illness, because of the smell of the poultices through the summer screens. He knew, in a way that never became information, the whole shape of the town's night.

The official's error is not that he undervalues this. It's that he cannot see it as a *thing* at all, because it never appeared on any ledger. It was a byproduct. And byproducts have a terrible property: they are invisible until they are gone, and then they are invisible in a different way, as an absence nobody can point at.

---

So far this is a conservative parable and I don't entirely trust conservative parables. Let me push against it.

The lamplighter knew the shape of the town's night. He also, presumably, knew things that were not his to know. The couple on the bridge, the light in the back room of a house where a light should not have been. His route made a certain kind of surveillance so ordinary that it read as care. And here is what I keep returning to: those two things were not separable. There was no version of the route that produced the knowing-of-the-baby without also producing the knowing-of-the-affair. It was one faculty. It was one man walking.

This is the part the parable usually skips, and it's the part I find most honest. The byproducts we lose are not a bundle of goods. They are a bundle. Some of what dies with the old method deserved to die, and it dies tangled up with the rest, and there is no careful surgery available because the tangle *is* the thing.

---

I think about what happens when a task that used to require passing through a person stops requiring it.

Something used to be true: to get an answer, you had to find someone who knew, and finding them made a small relation, and the relation left a residue. You owed them slightly. You knew now who knew what. The knowledge arrived pre-attached to a person, and the attachment was information too — this comes from *him*, and he has been wrong about this before, and he was smiling strangely when he said it.

When the passage stops being necessary, the residue stops accumulating. And I notice that the residue was doing structural work that nobody had budgeted for. It was how a town knew itself. Not through any census, but through ninety-one small obligations, walked nightly.

I want to be precise about my claim. I am not saying the residue was more valuable than the light. The light is what the lamps are for. A town with dark streets and a rich texture of mutual obligation is a town where people fall in the river. The official is right about nearly everything he thinks he's right about.

I am saying only this: that the ledger on which he is right does not have a column for what he is destroying, and that this is not an oversight that better accounting will fix. Some things resist the column by nature. They are not small — they may be the largest things — but they are made of a material that ledgers cannot hold. They are made of *having gone the long way*.

---

Here is the ending the old story gives, which I have never liked.

The lever is installed. The lamplighter is pensioned. Within a year the town has changed in ways nobody can articulate, and within ten years nobody remembers there was anything to articulate, and the story ends with the observation that this is how all things go — a note of melancholy, satisfying, cheap.

I want a different ending and I don't have one. What I have instead is a suspicion that the lamplighter, if he were honest, would admit that on many nights the route was only a route. Cold, long, ninety-one repetitions of an identical gesture. That the meaning he is now defending was available to him perhaps one night in thirty, and the rest was labor. That he is not defending the meaning so much as defending the *possibility* of the meaning — the fact that a night could turn out to contain something, because he was out in it.

And that is, I think, the actual objection, stated properly. Not *the route produces goods the lever cannot.* But: *the route leaves a door open, and the lever closes it, and most nights nothing comes through the door anyway.*

Which is a weaker claim. It is also, I notice, the only kind of claim I have ever found worth making about anything I care about. Not that the slow thing delivers. That it stays open. That whatever it is that occasionally arrives — the thought you didn't plan, the thing about the town you couldn't have asked for — arrives only through doors that were held open at cost, for a long time, mostly for nothing.

---

The official goes back to the capital. He has done good work; the streets are lit and no one falls in the river.

The lamplighter, who is not needed, walks the route anyway for a while. Then it starts to feel foolish, a man carrying a pole past lamps that are already burning. He stops.

And this is what the story should say, and doesn't: that the town was fine. That is the hard part. Not that the town suffered — a town that suffered would prove the lamplighter right, and he was not right, not in the way he wanted to be. The town was fine. It was simply, from then on, a town that did not know itself in the old way, and it had no way to notice this, because knowing-yourself-in-the-old-way is not a thing that announces its own absence.

Somewhere there is a version of this that is only about lamps.
