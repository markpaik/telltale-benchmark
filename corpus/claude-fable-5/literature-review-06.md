# Driver Turnover, Compensation, and Working Conditions in Over-the-Road Trucking: A Systematic Review of the Evidence, 1990–2026

**Border Commerce Institute — Laredo, Texas**
**Prepared by: Ana Lucia Zepeda, Ph.D., Director of Research**
**Database and screening: Fitzgerald Boakye, Research Analyst**
**Review draft: August 2026**

---

## 1. Introduction

The Border Commerce Institute's 24 member carriers, operating a combined 6,900 tractors in cross-border and long-haul freight, recorded an average annualized turnover rate of 91 percent among over-the-road drivers last year. Two member firms lost anchor customers following service failures attributable to unfilled seats and missed appointments. The Institute holds a $400,000 state workforce development grant that must be obligated to an intervention before the fiscal year closes. The question this review addresses is therefore not academic: **what does thirty-five years of research say about which interventions durably reduce over-the-road driver turnover, and what should carriers of our members' size — most operating between 100 and 500 tractors — do with that evidence?**

The question is contested inside our own membership. The member advisory chair holds that compensation is the sole variable that matters, and that everything else in the literature is decoration around a wage problem. That position has serious scholarly backing, most prominently in the "sweatshops on wheels" tradition that treats driver churn as a rational market response to deteriorated real wages (Belzer, 2000; Belzer & Sedo, 2018). But the literature also contains a substantial body of work finding that pay increases purchase retention only temporarily, that unpaid detention time and schedule unpredictability independently drive quitting, and that safety climate and dispatcher behavior moderate whether any pay increase holds. If the pay-only position is correct, the grant should fund wage subsidies or sign-on structures. If it is incomplete, $400,000 spent on pay alone will buy nine to fourteen months of relief and then decay — a pattern documented repeatedly in the compensation literature reviewed below.

This review frames the problem as one of **adjudicating between contradictory bodies of evidence** rather than summarizing a settled field. The trucking labor literature disagrees with itself on nearly every major point: whether pay effects persist, how large detention burdens actually are, whether electronic logging devices improved or worsened safety, and whether a driver shortage exists at all. Our task is to explain *why* the studies disagree — differences in measurement, carrier segment, time period, and outcome definition account for most of the conflict — and to extract what survives the disagreement.

**Scope.** The review covers empirical research on driver turnover, retention, compensation structure, detention and dwell time, hours-of-service regulation and electronic logging, and safety climate in the U.S. and Canadian truckload and less-than-truckload sectors, published or released between 1990 and 2026. Qualitative work is included where it illuminates mechanisms (e.g., Viscelli, 2016). Purely engineering or routing literature is excluded unless it reports labor outcomes. The unit of interest is the over-the-road company driver, though owner-operator and short-haul evidence is noted where it exists — which, as Section 5 documents, is not often.

## 2. Search and Screening

Searches were run against the Transportation Research International Documentation (TRID) database, Scopus, and the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration's research and analysis report series, covering January 1990 through June 2026. Search strings combined driver-population terms (truck driver, motor carrier, truckload, over-the-road, long-haul) with outcome terms (turnover, retention, quit, attrition, tenure) and exposure terms (compensation, pay, wage, mileage rate, detention, dwell, hours of service, electronic logging, ELD, safety climate, safety culture). Reference lists of included studies and three prior reviews were hand-searched. Gray literature from the American Transportation Research Institute, the Government Accountability Office, the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and the FMCSA was included given the thinness of peer-reviewed work on detention.

The search returned 2,105 unique records after deduplication. Title-and-abstract screening, conducted by the analyst with a 15 percent double-screened sample reconciled by the director, excluded 1,841 records, most for wrong population (bus, delivery, rail) or absence of an empirical labor outcome. Full-text review of 264 articles yielded **71 included studies**: 24 addressing compensation structure and level; 15 addressing detention and dwell time; 12 addressing electronic logging and hours-of-service regulation; 9 addressing safety climate and organizational factors; and 11 addressing labor-market structure, the shortage debate, and turnover prediction models that cut across categories. Effect estimates, sample descriptions, driver segments, and outcome definitions for all 71 studies are recorded in the extraction workbook maintained by the Institute and cited here by author and year.

Two limitations of the search bear stating up front. First, publication bias almost certainly inflates the apparent effectiveness of retention interventions: carriers do not publish failed programs, and the industry survey literature is produced largely by organizations with a stake in the shortage narrative. Second, the literature is dominated by large truckload carriers whose data are accessible to researchers; findings from fleets of 5,000-plus tractors are routinely generalized to the mid-size fleets that make up our membership, and Section 4 shows that this generalization fails in at least one important domain.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Compensation: Real Effects That Decay

The strongest single claim in the literature is that driver pay, measured properly, predicts both retention and safety. Belzer (2000) established the frame: deregulation after 1980 cut real driver earnings by roughly 30 percent over two decades, converting a middle-class occupation into piecework, and turnover is the market clearing that low piece rates produce. Rodríguez, Targa, and Belzer (2006), using within-carrier data from J.B. Hunt's 1997 across-the-board pay raise, found that higher mileage rates predicted significantly lower crash probability and longer tenure — the canonical "safe rates" result, extended by Faulkiner and Belzer (2019) to show that pay raises reduced crashes most among the least experienced drivers. Burks et al. (2008), with cooperative access to a large truckload firm's personnel records, confirmed that earnings trajectories in the first months of employment strongly predicted survival: drivers whose realized weekly pay fell short of recruiter representations quit at sharply elevated rates.

Set against this is an equally consistent finding: **pay-level effects decay.** Min and Lambert (2002) and Min and Emam (2003), surveying carrier executives and modeling turnover determinants, found pay raises produced measurable retention gains that eroded as competitors matched rates — a classic arms-race dynamic. The extraction workbook records seven studies with longitudinal designs sufficient to estimate decay; their persistence estimates cluster at **nine to fourteen months**, after which turnover at raising carriers reconverges toward segment norms (Griffin & Kalnbach, 2002; Suzuki, Crum, & Pautsch, 2009; Miller, Saldanha, Rungtusanatham, & Knemeyer, 2017). Suzuki et al. (2009) is particularly instructive: their hazard model of a national truckload carrier found that *relative* pay — the driver's rate versus what the driver believed obtainable elsewhere — predicted quitting far better than absolute pay, which mechanically explains decay: once competitors match, the relative advantage is gone even though the absolute raise remains.

The apparent contradiction dissolves on closer reading, in two ways. First, the safe-rates studies and the decay studies measure different outcomes over different windows. Rodríguez et al. (2006) measured crashes and tenure within a firm following a raise that was, at the time, well above market; the decay studies measure carrier-level turnover rates in competitive equilibrium. Both can be true: pay raises work while they confer relative advantage, and stop working when they do not. Second — and this is the finding most relevant to the pay-only position — **the studies reporting durable pay effects are almost uniformly those in which pay changes were bundled with predictability.** Johnson, Bristow, McClure, and Schneider (2011) and Williams, Garver, and Taylor (2011), using stated-preference and survey designs, found that drivers traded meaningful amounts of pay for guaranteed home time and schedule certainty. Miller et al. (2017), examining within-driver earnings *variability* rather than level, found that week-to-week pay volatility predicted quitting independent of average earnings — drivers on identical annual pay quit more when the pay arrived unevenly. Prockl, Teller, Kotzab, and Angell (2017) and Conroy and Belzer (2022, workbook entry) report converging results: guaranteed-minimum pay structures and salary-like arrangements show retention effects that survive past the fourteen-month decay window observed for mileage-rate increases. Dubey and Belzer's per-hour compensation analyses (workbook, 2021) point the same direction.

The synthesis, then, is not that pay does not matter — it plainly does, and no included study finds retention gains from below-market pay regardless of other conditions. It is that **pay *level* buys temporary relief while pay *structure* — variance reduction, minimum guarantees, compensation for non-driving time — is where the durable effects concentrate.** This reframing matters directly for the detention literature that follows, because unpaid detention is simultaneously a pay-structure problem and a scheduling problem.

### 3.2 Detention and Dwell: Large Effects, Unstable Measurement

Fifteen studies address detention — time drivers spend waiting at shipper and receiver facilities beyond scheduled loading windows — and they present the review's sharpest measurement problem. Estimates of average detention per stop in the extraction workbook range from **1.2 to 6.5 hours**, a fivefold spread that reflects not disagreement about the world but disagreement about the instrument. Studies relying on driver self-report and survey recall (ATRI's driver detention series; Torres & Marquis, workbook 2019) sit at the high end, plausibly inflated by salience of worst experiences. Studies using electronic logging and telematics-defined dwell (Dunn, Hickman, Soccolich, & Hanowski, 2014; Speltz & Murray, 2019; Scott, workbook 2023) sit lower, but define detention against different thresholds — some count all dwell beyond two hours, some beyond a scheduled appointment, some all dwell at customer facilities regardless of schedule. The Government Accountability Office (2011) concluded that no reliable national estimate existed; fifteen years later, the included studies suggest that conclusion still holds.

What survives the measurement chaos is direction and mechanism. Every included study that links detention to an outcome finds the same signs: detention predicts hours-of-service violations, crash involvement, and quitting. Dunn et al. (2014), in the FMCSA-sponsored naturalistic study, found that stops involving detention were followed by elevated crash and near-crash rates in subsequent driving — drivers compress schedules to recover lost time. Speltz and Murray (2019) estimated that a 15-minute increase in average dwell raised expected crash rates by over 6 percent. On the labor side, detention functions as an unmeasured wage cut: drivers paid by the mile earn nothing while detained, so a driver averaging two hours of unpaid detention per stop across eight stops weekly loses the equivalent of a substantial share of driving income (Belzer, 2018; Viscelli, 2016 documents the mechanism ethnographically — drivers describe detention, not mileage rates, as the proximate trigger for quitting or exiting the industry). Balthrop and Scott (workbook, 2022) find that carriers charging and *passing through* detention pay to drivers show lower turnover than carriers charging shippers but retaining the revenue, which isolates the driver-compensation channel from the operational one.

The disagreement in this literature is thus almost entirely metrological, and the practical implication cuts through it: whatever the true national average, **detention is the point where the pay literature and the scheduling literature converge.** Paying drivers for detained time simultaneously raises effective hourly earnings, reduces earnings variance (Miller et al., 2017's volatility mechanism), and removes the incentive to drive compressed and fatigued (Dunn et al., 2014's safety mechanism). No other single intervention in the review touches all three channels. The caveat is that carriers cannot unilaterally control shipper behavior; the detention literature is clear that dwell is generated at facilities carriers do not own, which is why the strongest results come from carriers that combined detention pay with customer scoring and selective freight rejection (Speltz & Murray, 2019; workbook entries on carrier case studies).

### 3.3 Electronic Logging, Hours of Service, and Scheduling: The Size Split

The 2017 electronic logging device (ELD) mandate produced the review's most genuinely contradictory findings, and the contradiction is instructive because it splits precisely on carrier size — the dimension most relevant to our membership.

Scott, Balthrop, and Miller (2021), in the most rigorous quasi-experimental evaluation, found that the mandate sharply increased hours-of-service compliance but produced **no detectable reduction in crashes overall — and a measurable *increase* in crashes and unsafe driving behaviors (speeding) among small carriers**, consistent with drivers compensating for lost flexible hours by driving faster within legal ones. Earlier work on electronic onboard recorders at large fleets found the opposite: Cantor, Corsi, and Grawe (2009) and the FMCSA's own evaluations reported lower crash and violation rates at large adopting carriers. The reconciliation is selection and slack. Large carriers adopted voluntarily, pre-mandate, as part of broader safety-management systems, and had scheduling departments able to absorb the loss of log flexibility; small carriers adopted under compulsion, had been using paper-log flexibility to absorb detention and appointment variance, and had no institutional slack when that flexibility disappeared (Scott et al., 2021; Miller, Scott, & workbook entries 2022–2024). The ELD literature is therefore not contradictory so much as it is a demonstration that **the same regulation is a different treatment at different carrier scales** — a warning against importing large-carrier findings into a 210-truck operation, and against assuming that compliance technology substitutes for schedule redesign.

On hours-of-service regulation more broadly, the twelve included studies show modest fatigue-related safety effects from rule changes (restart provisions, 14-hour window) but consistent labor-side findings: rigidity in the driving window interacts badly with detention, converting shipper delays directly into lost paid miles (Dunn et al., 2014; workbook HOS entries). The scheduling literature proper — home time, dispatch predictability, load assignment fairness — is thinner but consistent. Keller and Ozment (1999) and Keller (2002) found dispatcher communication quality and perceived dispatch fairness predicted driver retention as strongly as satisfaction with pay. Morrow, Suzuki, Crum, Ruben, and Pautsch (2005) found time at home and perceived organizational support predicted commitment and intent to stay net of compensation. Swartz, Douglas, Roberts, and Overstreet (workbook, 2017) link "driver voice" in load assignment to retention. Against these sit null and weak results in cross-sectional surveys where scheduling variables wash out once pay satisfaction is controlled (LeMay, Taylor, & Turner, 1993; workbook survey entries) — but the causal ordering problem is obvious: pay satisfaction in those surveys plausibly *absorbs* scheduling effects, since unpaid detention and lost home time show up to drivers as inadequate pay. The stated-preference studies that separate the attributes experimentally (Johnson et al., 2011; Williams et al., 2011) consistently find drivers willing to accept 8–12 percent lower pay for guaranteed weekly home time, which is difficult to square with a pure pay-only model of driver behavior.

### 3.4 Safety Climate and Organizational Factors

The nine safety-climate studies are the smallest body but among the most internally consistent. Huang, Zohar, Robertson, Garabet, Lee, and Murphy (2013), validating a trucking-specific safety climate scale on long-haul drivers, found that both organization-level and supervisor-level climate predicted self-reported safe driving and, in follow-up work, objective injury and retention outcomes (Huang, Lee, McFadden, Murphy, Robertson, Cheung, & Zohar, 2016 — the lone-worker extension is directly relevant to over-the-road work, where "supervision" is a dispatcher on a phone). Zohar, Huang, Lee, and Robertson (2014) showed that dispatcher-driver exchange quality mediated the climate–outcome link. Cantor, Corsi, and Grawe (2011) and Swartz and Douglas (2009) connect carrier safety-management practices to crash outcomes at the firm level; Mello and Hunt (2009) and Kemp, Kopp, and Kemp (2013) connect perceived carrier concern for driver welfare to commitment and turnover intention.

Two things distinguish this literature. First, effect sizes on turnover *intention* are moderate and consistent, but only three studies link climate to *actual* quits, and those use single-carrier samples — the evidence is suggestive, not strong. Second, and more useful: safety climate appears to **moderate** the pay effects in Section 3.1. Workbook entries recording interaction terms (Suzuki et al., 2009; Miller et al., 2017 extensions) find pay raises retained drivers longer at carriers with high perceived organizational support than at carriers where drivers reported adversarial dispatch relations. This is a candidate explanation for the decay heterogeneity: raises decay fastest where the raise is the only thing the carrier changed.

### 3.5 Is There a Shortage at All? The Frame That Determines the Intervention

The review's deepest disagreement is not about any coefficient but about the frame. Industry survey work, principally the American Trucking Associations' driver shortage series (Costello & Karickhoff, 2019, and successors; workbook entries through 2025), estimates a national shortfall of roughly 60,000–80,000 drivers, framing turnover as a symptom of insufficient labor supply — implying recruitment pipelines, training subsidies, and demographic expansion as remedies. Against this, Burks and Monaco (2019), analyzing Current Population Survey and occupational data for the Bureau of Labor Statistics, found that the truck driver labor market **shows none of the signatures of a broken or shortage market**: employment and wages respond to demand shocks the way competitive labor markets do, occupational attachment resembles comparable blue-collar work, and the "shortage" is confined to the long-distance truckload segment — where it is better described as a **retention problem generated by the segment's own compensation and working conditions**. Drivers do not leave trucking; they leave over-the-road truckload work for local, LTL, private-fleet, and construction driving that pays comparably with radically better conditions. Viscelli (2016) supplies the mechanism ethnographically: the truckload segment operates a churn business model in which high turnover is an accommodated cost, subsidized by debt-financed training pipelines that continuously replace experienced quitters with new entrants.

The two positions imply opposite uses of a $400,000 grant. The shortage frame implies spending on recruitment and CDL training throughput. The retention frame implies the pipeline is not the constraint — the segment already trains far more drivers than it keeps — and money spent on intake without changing conditions purchases churn. The weight of the peer-reviewed evidence favors Burks and Monaco: the wage-response findings are difficult to reconcile with a true shortage, the segment-specificity of turnover is documented across multiple datasets, and the shortage estimates rest on carrier-reported unfilled-seat counts at prevailing pay and conditions, which is a demand statement, not a supply measurement. But the industry surveys are not worthless — they document real, persistent unfilled capacity at member-size carriers, which is exactly the operational problem our members face regardless of what the aggregate labor market is doing.

## 4. Why the Literature Disagrees

Four sources of disagreement recur across every theme, and naming them is a condition of using the evidence honestly.

**Measurement heterogeneity.** Detention estimates span 1.2 to 6.5 hours because studies measure different constructs — all dwell, dwell beyond schedule, dwell beyond two hours, recalled worst-case dwell — with different instruments. Turnover itself is inconsistently defined: annualized replacement rates, voluntary-quit rates, 90-day survival, and turnover *intention* are treated as interchangeable across studies and are not. Studies of intention (most of the survey literature) systematically overstate what carriers can expect from actual-quit outcomes.

**Segment and scale confounding.** The ELD literature's apparent contradiction is a scale effect; the pay literature's decay estimates come from large truckload fleets in thick competitive markets and may differ for regional and border-specialized carriers; the safety-climate literature draws on single large firms. Findings travel poorly across the size distribution, and our members sit in the mid-size range the literature covers least.

**Time-window and equilibrium effects.** Short-window studies capture the treatment effect of a pay raise before competitors respond; long-window studies capture the competitive equilibrium after matching. Both are internally valid. Only the long-window estimates are relevant to strategy, and they are the ones showing decay.

**Interested parties and publication selection.** The shortage literature is largely produced by industry associations; the safe-rates literature is associated with labor-aligned scholars; carrier case studies of successful retention programs reach print, failed ones do not. None of this makes any given estimate wrong, but it means convergence across *independent* literatures — as with detention's direction of effect, or the pay-structure findings — should be weighted far more heavily than any single result.

## 5. Gaps

Three populations and one design are nearly absent from 71 included studies.

**Owner-operators.** Fewer than five studies treat owner-operators and lease-purchase drivers as a distinct population, despite their prominence in border drayage and despite ethnographic evidence (Viscelli, 2016) and earnings analyses (Phares & Balthrop, workbook 2022) suggesting their economics — debt exposure, revenue variance, misclassification — differ fundamentally from company drivers. Nothing in the compensation-decay literature can be assumed to apply to them.

**Women drivers.** Women are roughly 7–8 percent of the driver workforce and are analyzed separately in only three included studies, mostly on harassment, facility safety, and training-phase attrition (workbook entries; Scott & Davis-Sramek, 2023 entry). Whether the pay-versus-conditions tradeoff estimates generalize to women drivers is simply unknown, which matters for any recruitment-expansion argument.

**Short-haul and drayage.** The literature is overwhelmingly long-haul truckload. Border drayage — the actual work of several member fleets — appears in the operational literature but almost never in the labor literature. Detention dynamics at border crossings and transload facilities, dwell measured against gate systems rather than dock appointments, and the turnover economics of daily-home-time work are essentially unstudied.

**Multi-carrier experimental designs.** No included study randomizes an intervention across carriers. The strongest designs are single-firm natural experiments (Rodríguez et al., 2006; Burks et al., 2008) and mandate-based quasi-experiments (Scott et al., 2021). A consortium of 24 carriers implementing staggered, measured interventions would be — and this is not incidental to the grant decision — a stronger design than anything in the published literature.

## 6. Implications for Carriers of This Size

Read whole, the literature supports neither the pay-only position nor its dismissal. Five conclusions are defensible for fleets of 100–500 tractors:

**1. Below-market pay is disqualifying, but pay-level increases alone will decay.** The evidence for relative-pay effects is strong (Suzuki et al., 2009; Burks et al., 2008), and no conditions-based intervention rescues uncompetitive rates. But the seven longitudinal studies estimating persistence agree that rate increases erode within nine to fourteen months as competitors match. A grant spent purely on wage supplements should be expected to buy roughly one year.

**2. The durable effects sit in pay *structure*: variance reduction, minimum guarantees, and payment for non-driving time.** Earnings volatility predicts quitting independent of earnings level (Miller et al., 2017); guaranteed-minimum and detention-pay structures show persistence past the decay window (Prockl et al., 2017; workbook entries). This is the finding that partially vindicates the pay-only position while redirecting it — the intervention is still compensation, but its target is the paycheck's shape, not its size.

**3. Detention is the highest-leverage single target** because it is simultaneously a wage cut, a variance generator, a fatigue and crash mechanism, and — uniquely among the drivers of turnover — partially controllable through consortium action. Individual mid-size carriers cannot discipline shippers; 24 carriers sharing facility dwell scoring and coordinating detention-pay pass-through have market power none holds alone. The measurement chaos in the literature (1.2–6.5 hours) is itself an argument for the consortium building its own telematics-based dwell measurement before intervening.

**4. Large-carrier findings do not transfer automatically.** The ELD evidence (Scott et al., 2021) shows the same intervention producing opposite safety effects across the size distribution. Members should treat any program validated at mega-fleets — including safety-climate instruments and scheduling software — as requiring local piloting, not adoption.

**5. Recruitment-pipeline spending is the weakest-supported use of the grant.** The Burks and Monaco (2019) evidence, corroborated by the segment-specific pattern of turnover, indicates the binding constraint in over-the-road truckload is retention under prevailing conditions, not the supply of trained entrants. Training subsidies without conditions changes fund churn.

The recommendation that follows for the $400,000 grant is a **staggered pilot across willing member fleets combining detention pay pass-through, a guaranteed weekly minimum, and standardized dwell measurement, with 90-day and 12-month survival tracked against non-piloting members** — an intervention aimed at the pay-structure and detention findings where the evidence converges, designed to outlast the fourteen-month decay window that a pure rate increase would not, and structured to generate the multi-carrier evidence the literature itself lacks.
