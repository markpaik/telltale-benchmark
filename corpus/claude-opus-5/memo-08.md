# Saugatuck Hills Regional School District

## Internal Memoranda — Facilities and Program Consolidation Study, FY28 Budget Development, and Special Education Corrective Action

---

**TO:** Building Principals; Central Office Administrators; Directors and Coordinators
**FROM:** Dr. Chidinma Eze, Superintendent of Schools
**DATE:** March 3, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Opening of the Facilities and Program Consolidation Study — Scope, Timeline, and Expectations for Administrative Participation

I am writing to inform you that, effective today, the district is opening a formal Facilities and Program Consolidation Study. The Board of Education authorized this work at its meeting of February 24 by a vote of six to three, directing administration to return no later than the first regular meeting in November with a recommendation and supporting analysis. This memo describes what the study will examine, how it will be conducted, what I expect from each of you, and what I am asking you not to do.

I want to be direct about why we are here. Three conditions have converged, and none of them is going to resolve itself.

The first is enrollment. We served 4,610 students in October 2020. Our October 2025 count was 4,190. That is a decline of 420 students, roughly nine percent, over five years. Our own demographer's mid-range projection carries us to approximately 3,940 by 2031, and the low-range projection is closer to 3,800. The decline is not evenly distributed. It is concentrated in the elementary grades and in the northern and western portions of the district, which is a function of housing stock, of the aging of the neighborhoods that were built out in the 1960s and 1970s, and of a birth cohort that has been shrinking in Fairfield County for more than a decade. Our secondary enrollment will continue to look comparatively healthy for another three or four years simply because the larger cohorts are still moving through, and then it will not.

The second condition is the operating budget. Marco Sanfilippo will circulate a detailed memo within the week, but the headline is that we are projecting a $3.2 million gap for FY28 against a current-year budget of $103 million. That gap is driven by contractual salary obligations, a medical claims trend that has run well above our assumptions for two consecutive years, special education outplacement and transportation costs that have grown faster than our enrollment has shrunk, and a state grant environment that is flat in nominal terms and therefore declining in real ones. None of those drivers is a one-time event.

The third condition is the February 10 referendum. Voters rejected the $46 million renovation bond, 54 to 46, on 31 percent turnout. I have heard that result characterized several ways in the weeks since, and I want to be careful about what I claim it means. Thirty-one percent turnout on a February referendum is not a mandate in either direction. What I take from it is narrower and, I think, defensible: this community is not prepared to borrow $46 million to renovate square footage it may not need. The bond package included substantial work at Ashford Lane Elementary — roof, envelope, mechanical systems, and accessibility upgrades — in a building currently operating at 54 percent of its rated capacity with 218 students enrolled. That combination of facts was raised repeatedly at the public information sessions in January, and I do not think we answered it well. We are not going to be able to return to the voters with a substantially similar package and expect a different outcome. Any future capital request has to rest on a facilities footprint the district can defend.

So the study will proceed. Its scope is as follows.

We will evaluate each of our six buildings against capacity utilization, condition and deferred maintenance liability, program suitability, transportation implications, and the cost of continued operation. We will model the consolidation of elementary attendance areas under multiple scenarios, including a scenario in which all six buildings remain open. We will separately examine the configuration of our self-contained special education programs, which are currently distributed across four buildings in a pattern that reflects where space happened to be available in 2014 rather than any considered judgment about program design. And we will produce cost estimates for each scenario that distinguish between recurring savings, one-time transition costs, and costs that are avoided rather than eliminated.

I want to be transparent that administration is entering this study with a working hypothesis, because you will hear it discussed in the community regardless and I would rather you hear it from me. The hypothesis is that closing Ashford Lane Elementary and redistricting its students to our two remaining elementary schools would generate recurring savings in the range of $1.9 million annually and would allow us to consolidate special education programming into two hub sites. That is a hypothesis. It is not a decision, and I have not made one. The study exists to test it, and it will test it against alternatives, including partial-closure and grade-reconfiguration models that some board members have asked us to examine.

Here is the timeline.

Through the end of March, the business office and the facilities director will complete the building condition and capacity analysis. In April and May, the demographic consultant will finalize projections at the attendance-area level and we will run redistricting models. Also in April, Hannah Weissman will complete the special education program configuration analysis, which is on a compressed schedule for reasons I address below. In June, we will publish a preliminary findings document and begin community forums, with at least two forums at each elementary school and one districtwide session. Forums will continue through September. In early October, I will present a draft recommendation to the Board's Facilities Committee. The full recommendation goes to the Board on November 3. If the Board votes to close a building, the closure would take effect for the 2027–28 school year, which gives us ten months to execute.

One matter requires me to move faster than that timeline in one area. In January the Connecticut State Department of Education completed a special education monitoring review of this district. The review sampled individualized education programs across all six buildings and found that 19 percent of the sampled IEPs were out of compliance, with findings concentrated in two areas: transition planning for students aged sixteen and above, and documentation of delivered service minutes against prescribed minutes. Our corrective action plan is due to the state by June 30, and the state will conduct a follow-up verification visit in the fall.

I want to say clearly what the relationship between that finding and this study is, and what it is not.

It is not the case that our compliance problems were caused by our facilities configuration, and it would be dishonest for us to suggest otherwise. The transition planning findings reflect inconsistent practice and inadequate supervision. The service minutes findings reflect a documentation system that does not reliably capture what our staff actually deliver, which is a different problem and in some respects a more fixable one. Neither of those is a building problem.

It is the case that our current program distribution makes supervision harder than it needs to be. Hannah supervises staff across four sites for two self-contained programs. Related service providers travel. Consultation happens by phone or not at all. If we are going to correct our practice, a configuration that puts program staff in proximity to one another and to their supervisor is an asset. I want you to hold both of those things at once, and I want you to be careful in how you speak about it publicly. If we tell this community that we are closing a school to fix our special education compliance, we will deserve the response we get. The compliance work proceeds on its own schedule and its own logic, and it proceeds whether or not any building closes.

What I expect from each of you.

Provide data promptly and accurately when the business office or the facilities director requests it. Some of what we ask for will feel intrusive — room-by-room utilization, scheduling detail, storage and non-instructional space use. Provide it anyway, and provide it accurately. A study built on optimistic square-footage reporting is worse than no study.

Second, do not speculate publicly about outcomes. You will be asked. Staff will ask, parents will ask, and in some cases board members will ask. The honest answer is that the study is open, that no decision has been made, and that the recommendation will be public in October before the Board acts in November. That answer is true. Use it.

Third, if you believe the study is proceeding from a bad premise, tell me. I would rather hear it in March than read it in the newspaper in October. I am specifically interested in hearing from anyone who thinks the enrollment projections are wrong or who has information about housing development that our demographer may not have.

Fourth, be attentive to your staff. The people most affected by this study are the ones with the least ability to influence it. That is true at every building, and it is most acutely true at Ashford Lane, where Odalys Marte's faculty has been reading about the referendum in the newspaper for six weeks. I have asked Odalys to communicate directly with her staff today, before this memo circulates further, and I will be at Ashford Lane on Thursday morning.

I recognize this is difficult. I have done this work in another district and I did not enjoy it there either. What I can commit to is that the process will be transparent, that the data will be published rather than characterized, and that no one will learn about a decision affecting their employment from a public meeting.

**TO:** Dr. Chidinma Eze, Superintendent; Board of Education Finance Committee
**FROM:** Marco Sanfilippo, Business Manager
**DATE:** March 11, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Revised FY28 Budget Projection — Baseline and Consolidation Scenarios

This memo revises the preliminary FY28 projection I circulated in December and presents two scenarios: a baseline that assumes all six buildings remain in operation, and an alternative that assumes closure of Ashford Lane Elementary effective July 1, 2027. I have also updated several assumptions that have moved since December, and I want to walk through those first, because they account for more of the change than anything on the facilities side.

### Revisions to Baseline Assumptions

Our current-year budget is $103,040,000. The December projection showed an FY28 gap of $2.6 million. The revised figure is $3.2 million. The $600,000 increase comes from four adjustments.

Medical claims are the largest. We are self-insured with stop-loss coverage above $250,000 per claimant. Through February we are running 11.4 percent above the projection we built into the current budget, driven by three high-cost claimants and by a specialty pharmacy trend that our consultant tells me is regional rather than particular to us. I had assumed a 6.5 percent trend for FY28. I am now assuming 9 percent, which adds $410,000. I want to flag that this assumption is the single largest source of uncertainty in the projection. If the trend reverts to 6.5 percent, we recover most of that. If it holds at 11 percent, we lose another $300,000 beyond what is shown here.

Second, special education outplacement. We had projected 26 outplacements for FY28 at an average net cost of $88,000 after excess cost grant reimbursement. Based on IEP activity through February and on four students currently in the referral process, I am now projecting 29. That adds $264,000. I would note for the Finance Committee that the excess cost grant reimburses at a declining effective rate as statewide claims grow against a capped appropriation; we received 72 percent of eligible costs in FY25 and 68 percent in FY26, and I have assumed 66 percent for FY28. That assumption has cost us roughly $190,000 relative to a flat-rate assumption, and it is not conservative — it is the trend.

Third, transportation. Our contract renews July 1, 2027, and the escalator is tied to a fuel index plus a labor factor. My December estimate assumed 4 percent. Our contractor has signaled 6 to 7 percent, which is consistent with what neighboring districts are seeing. I have modeled 6.5 percent, adding $178,000.

Fourth, and offsetting, state grant revenue came in $250,000 better than I had projected for the current year, and I have carried a portion of that improvement forward.

Salary and wage obligations are essentially locked. The teachers' contract runs through June 2029 with a 3.1 percent general wage increase in FY28 plus step movement; the administrators' agreement runs through June 2028; the paraeducator and clerical units settle this spring. Total salary and benefit obligation for FY28 is $79.4 million against 620 FTEs, which is 77 percent of the budget. That number is the reason the facilities question is on the table. When three-quarters of your spending is people, and your people are under contract, the only levers that move meaningful money are the ones that change how many people you need.

### Baseline Scenario: Six Buildings

Under the baseline, FY28 expenditure requirement is $106,900,000. Against an assumed revenue envelope of $103,700,000 — which reflects a 0.6 percent increase in the town appropriations consistent with what the two finance boards have signaled informally — the gap is $3,200,000.

Closing that gap without a facilities change requires reductions of 3.1 percent in a budget that is 77 percent contractual salary. I have modeled what that looks like, and the Finance Committee should understand it concretely. It is approximately 28 to 32 positions, and because we cannot reduce contractually mandated services, the reductions fall on elementary specials, secondary electives with enrollments under fifteen, library media staffing, one of two middle school assistant principals, all remaining non-mandated tutoring, districtwide instructional coaching, and a further deferral of the technology replacement cycle, which is now four years behind a four-year cycle.

That package produces $2.4 million. The remaining $800,000 would come from the fund balance, which stands at $2.9 million, or 2.8 percent of budget. Our policy target is 4 percent. Our bond counsel has advised that sustained draws below 3 percent will attract rating agency attention at our next issuance. I do not recommend the fund balance draw, and I present it only to be complete about what a six-building FY28 requires.

### Consolidation Scenario: Ashford Lane Closure

Under the closure scenario, recurring annual savings are estimated at $1,910,000. The composition matters more than the total, so I will break it out and then discuss reliability.

**Staffing: $1,340,000.** Ashford Lane operates with 31 staff FTEs. Of these, redistricting 218 students across Bridgemill and Hollow Road generates a need for approximately 12.5 FTEs at the receiving schools, primarily classroom teachers and paraeducator support. The remaining 18.5 FTEs are the savings. This figure includes the building principal, the school secretary, two custodians, the library media specialist, the school counselor at 0.5, and the art, music, and physical education positions, which at 218 students are staffed above what enrollment alone would generate but below what two additional sections at each receiving school would require. Average loaded cost of $72,400 per FTE.

**Facilities operations: $412,000.** Utilities, custodial supplies, grounds, routine maintenance, contracted services, and building-level insurance. This assumes the building is mothballed rather than sold or repurposed, and it carries $38,000 annually for minimum heating, security monitoring, and periodic inspection to prevent deterioration. If the Board disposes of the property, that $38,000 goes away and there is a one-time proceeds question that I have not modeled.

**Deferred maintenance avoidance: $0 recurring, $8,600,000 avoided over ten years.** Ashford Lane carried $8.6 million of the failed $46 million bond package. I do not count avoided capital as operating savings and I will not present it that way. But the Finance Committee should understand that a decision to keep Ashford Lane open is a decision to fund $8.6 million of work over the next decade, and the roof and the univents are not optional in a building we intend to occupy.

**Transportation: negative $88,000.** Redistricting increases average ride times and route mileage. Three additional routes, partially offset by the elimination of two Ashford Lane routes. This is a cost, not a savings, and I have netted it into the total.

**Special education program consolidation: $246,000.** Hannah Weissman's analysis identifies savings from consolidating the two self-contained programs into hubs at Bridgemill and Hollow Road, principally through reduced related-service travel time, more efficient paraeducator assignment across combined classrooms, and the elimination of one 0.5 program coordinator position that exists because of geographic fragmentation. Hannah should speak to the programmatic case; I am reporting the fiscal one, and I would characterize this line as the least certain in the package.

One-time transition costs are estimated at $340,000: moving and storage, furniture and equipment reconfiguration at the receiving schools, technology infrastructure, signage and records, two receiving-school classroom conversions, and $60,000 for supplemental counseling and transition programming during the first year.

### Comparison

The closure scenario produces an FY28 gap of $1,290,000 rather than $3,200,000. That is still a gap. It still requires reductions, and I estimate 11 to 13 positions rather than 28 to 32. But it is a gap the district can close without dismantling the elective and specials programs, and it preserves the fund balance.

### Cautions

I want to register four.

The savings are recurring but they are not growing. The $1.9 million is a one-time downward reset. In FY29 our salary and medical trends resume against a smaller base. If enrollment continues to decline at the projected rate, we will be having a version of this conversation again in FY31 or FY32, and there will be one fewer elementary school to consider. I do not say that as an argument against closure. I say it because the Board should not be told that this solves a structural problem.

Second, the staffing savings assume we can actually reduce 18.5 positions. Fourteen of the 31 Ashford Lane staff are non-tenured. The remaining 17 have contractual placement rights, and the interaction between those rights and the positions generated at the receiving schools will determine how much of the modeled savings we realize. Human resources is working the seniority analysis and I would not treat the $1,340,000 as firm until that is complete. My planning assumption is that we realize 90 to 95 percent of it in year one and the full amount by year two.

Third, the transportation estimate is preliminary and based on a redistricting model that will change.

Fourth, and I want to say this plainly: the $1.9 million figure will be quoted in public without its caveats. It already has been. I would ask that when administration cites it, the citation include the one-time transition cost and the transportation offset, because if the first version of the number the public hears is the gross figure and the second version is lower, we will have created a credibility problem we do not need.

**TO:** Special Education Teachers; Related Service Providers; Paraeducators; Building Administrators
**FROM:** Hannah Weissman, Director of Special Services
**DATE:** March 24, 2026
**SUBJECT:** State Monitoring Findings, Corrective Action Plan, and Program Configuration Analysis

I have wanted to write to you since January and I have deliberately waited until I could tell you something specific rather than something reassuring. This memo covers the state monitoring findings, what our corrective action plan will require, and — separately — the program configuration work I have been asked to complete as part of the district's consolidation study. I am addressing both because you will hear them discussed together, and I want to be precise about where they connect and where they do not.

### The Findings

The State Department of Education completed its review in January. Reviewers sampled IEPs across all six buildings and interviewed staff, administrators, and families. Nineteen percent of the sampled IEPs were found out of compliance. The findings cluster in two areas.

**Transition planning.** For students aged sixteen and above, reviewers found transition plans that did not reflect age-appropriate transition assessment, postsecondary goals written in language that was not measurable, and courses of study that did not connect to the stated goals. In several cases the transition page appeared to have been carried forward from a prior year with the date changed.

**Delivered service minutes.** Reviewers found that our documentation did not reliably demonstrate that prescribed service minutes were delivered. In a substantial share of the sample, service logs were incomplete, aggregated in ways that could not be reconciled to individual IEPs, or absent for portions of the year.

I want to be careful about how I characterize the second finding, because there is a real distinction between a service that was not delivered and a service that was delivered but not documented. In the cases I have personally reviewed, the great majority appear to be documentation failures. That is not a defense. Under the regulations, an undocumented service is a service the district cannot demonstrate it provided, and a family reading that record has no way to know the difference. But I am not telling you that you failed to serve children. I am telling you that our system failed to capture what you did, and that we cannot prove what we know to be true.

The transition finding is different and I am not going to soften it. Some of those plans were not adequate. I have read them. The responsibility for that is mine before it is yours — I did not build the supervisory structure, the training, or the review process that would have caught it.

### The Corrective Action Plan

The plan is due to the state by June 30 and the verification visit will occur in the fall, most likely October or November. Six components:

**Records review and correction.** We will review every IEP for a student aged sixteen or older — 214 students — and every IEP flagged in the state sample. Where a transition plan is deficient, we will reconvene the PPT and rewrite it with the student present. This is not a paper exercise. Reviews begin April 6 and conclude by June 12. Case managers will receive released time; I am working with principals on coverage.

**Transition planning protocol.** A single district protocol specifying required assessments, timing, the format for postsecondary goals, and how the course of study connects to them. Draft by May 1, with feedback from a working group of six teachers — I need volunteers and I will ask directly if I do not get them.

**Service documentation system.** We are replacing our current approach. The new system will require entry within 48 hours, will reconcile automatically against prescribed minutes, and will flag variances at four weeks rather than at annual review. Selection by May 15, configuration over the summer, implementation with training in August. I know that additional documentation requirements land on people who are already stretched. My commitment is that the new system replaces existing logs rather than adding to them, and that if it takes longer than four minutes per student per week, I will consider it a failed implementation and say so.

**Professional development.** Two full days in August, one on transition planning and one on documentation and progress monitoring, plus four half-day sessions across the year. Attendance is required. I am pursuing state funds to compensate for August days beyond the contractual calendar.

**Supervisory structure.** Monthly case file review by building-level special education leads using a common protocol, with quarterly reporting to me. Currently no one systematically reads IEPs after the PPT approves them. That is the gap that allowed a 19 percent noncompliance rate to develop without anyone knowing.

**Family communication.** Every family whose child's IEP was found out of compliance receives written notice, an offer to reconvene, and where warranted, an offer of compensatory services. Notices go out in April.

### Program Configuration Analysis

Separately, Dr. Eze has asked me to analyze the configuration of our two self-contained programs — the STRIDE program serving students with significant intellectual and developmental disabilities, and the Bridge program serving students with intensive social-emotional and behavioral needs — as part of the consolidation study. My analysis recommends consolidating each program into a single hub site: STRIDE at Bridgemill, Bridge at Hollow Road.

Our current configuration distributes these programs across four buildings. That configuration exists because in 2014 we placed classrooms where there was space. Nothing about it reflects a considered judgment.

The programmatic case for hubs, briefly. Related service providers currently spend a documented average of 5.2 hours per week traveling among buildings — for a full-time speech-language pathologist, thirteen percent of the work year spent in a car. Consolidation recovers most of that as direct service time. Second, teachers in these programs are professionally isolated; a hub allows shared planning, coverage during crises, and the informal consultation that is how this work actually improves. Third, our current staffing produces classrooms of four and five students requiring the same paraeducator coverage as classrooms of eight, which is neither efficient nor, at four students, programmatically ideal for peer interaction. Fourth, supervision. I cannot adequately supervise eleven classrooms across four buildings. Two hubs, I can.

The costs are real and I will not minimize them. Some students will travel farther. Some will leave a building where they have established relationships. Consolidation concentrates students with significant needs in two buildings, which requires attention to inclusion opportunities so that a hub does not become a program that happens to share an address with a school. And any transition is disruptive for a population for whom transitions are hard.

I want to address directly the question I have been asked most often since January.

Some of you have asked whether this configuration work is a response to the monitoring findings — whether we are moving programs because the state told us to. The answer is no. The state's findings concern IEP content and documentation. They do not concern where classrooms are located, and the corrective action plan proceeds identically whether every building stays open or not.

But I would be dishonest if I said the two are unrelated. The supervisory gap that allowed our compliance problems is harder to close across four sites than two. When I say I cannot adequately supervise eleven classrooms in four buildings, that is not a hypothetical — it is a description of how we got a 19 percent noncompliance rate. So: the findings did not cause the configuration recommendation, and the configuration recommendation is not a remedy for the findings. But a configuration that makes supervision possible is part of how we sustain compliance once we achieve it. Both things are true and I would ask you to resist the pressure, which will come from several directions, to collapse them into one.

The configuration recommendation goes to Dr. Eze in April and becomes part of the study. No decision has been made. If it is adopted, changes would take effect in the 2027–28 school year, and every affected student's PPT would convene during the 2026–27 year to discuss placement individually. No student's placement changes without a PPT.

I will hold three open sessions — April 8 at Bridgemill, April 9 at Hollow Road, April 14 remote. Come with objections. I would rather hear them now.

**TO:** Dr. Chidinma Eze, Superintendent
**FROM:** Odalys Marte, Principal, Ashford Lane Elementary School
**DATE:** September 15, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Status Report — Community Forums and Staff Placement Concerns

You asked for a written summary before the October Facilities Committee presentation. This covers the four community forums held at Ashford Lane between June and September, the questions that have recurred, and the staff placement situation, which is where I most need your attention.

### Community Forums

Four sessions: June 11 (attendance 94), July 16 (41), August 27 (156), September 10 (203). The August and September attendance reflects the return of families from summer and, I think, a growing recognition in the community that this is real.

Attendance is not the only measure. The June session was angry in a diffuse way — people had read about the referendum and the study and arrived expecting a decision to argue with. The September session was different. People came with the enrollment data, the capacity figures, and the savings estimate, and they argued about them specifically. Several parents had read the demographer's report. One had built a spreadsheet reconciling the $1.9 million against the transportation offset and the transition costs, and his arithmetic was correct.

I want to say something about that shift, because it will not show up in the minutes. This community is not opposed to the closure because it does not understand the numbers. A meaningful portion of it understands the numbers and opposes the closure anyway, on grounds that are not primarily fiscal. I think administration should stop expecting that better data presentation will change minds. It will not, and continuing to respond to value arguments with capacity utilization figures is being received as evasion.

Recurring questions, in rough order of frequency:

**Class size at the receiving schools.** Asked at every forum, more than any other question, and the answers we have given have not satisfied. Parents want to know the projected class size at Bridgemill and Hollow Road, by grade, and they want it in writing. What we have said is that projections show averages within contractual limits. What parents hear is that we are describing averages while they are asking about their child's classroom. At the September forum a parent asked whether any grade level at either receiving school would exceed 24 students in any section, and I could not answer. I need that answer before October.

**Walkability and neighborhood.** Roughly 40 percent of Ashford Lane students walk. Under the redistricting model, nearly all would be bused, with estimated ride times of 22 to 31 minutes. Parents raise safety, after-school access, and the practical difficulty of afternoon pickup for families with multiple children in multiple buildings. One parent framed it as the district asking her family to absorb an hour a day of her child's life to save the district money. I did not have a good response.

**Special education.** Attendance at these questions has been high and the tone has been more anxious than angry. Families of students in the STRIDE classroom here want to know where their children will be, who will teach them, and whether their PPT will genuinely be a decision point or a formality. Hannah's presence at the August and September forums helped considerably. Her willingness to say that she did not yet know some answers earned more credibility than a confident answer would have.

**Building disposition.** Persistent and increasingly pointed. If the building is mothballed at $38,000 a year, why not lease it? If it is sold, who decides and where do proceeds go? Several people have observed that a mothballed building is a building that could reopen, and they want to know what enrollment threshold would trigger that. We have no answer, and the absence of one is read as evasion.

**The referendum.** Recurring. The argument, roughly: voters rejected a bond, and administration responded by proposing a closure that was not on the ballot. I have explained the connection between the two as you framed it in March. It has not landed well with the people who arrive already believing that the closure was the plan all along and the referendum was a formality. I do not think that belief is correct, but it is sincerely held and it is not going to yield to explanation.

### Staff Placement

This is the section I most need you to read.

I have 31 staff. Fourteen are non-tenured. Since the study opened in March, I have lost four staff to other districts — a third-grade teacher with six years here, a special education teacher with three, a school psychologist at 0.6, and a paraeducator with eleven years. All four told me directly that the uncertainty was the reason. The special education teacher had been part of Hannah's transition protocol working group.

I want to be blunt about what a nine-month study does to a building. Every one of my non-tenured staff is applying elsewhere. They would be foolish not to. The two strongest early-career teachers I have — one in second grade, one in fifth — have both told me they have interviews. I expect to lose at least one and possibly both by January. If the Board votes to close in November, those teachers will be gone before the closure they were waiting on takes effect. If the Board votes not to close, they will still be gone.

The district's stated position is that placement decisions cannot be made before the Board acts. I understand the legal and contractual reasoning. But the practical consequence is that we are conducting a nine-month process during which the people with the most options exercise them, and we are disproportionately losing exactly the staff a receiving school would most want. By the time we are able to make placement offers, the people we would offer positions to will have accepted positions elsewhere. That is not a hypothetical concern; it has already happened four times.

I would ask you to consider three things.

First, whether the district can make any binding statement about placement before November — not a specific assignment, but a commitment that tenured staff will be placed within the district and that non-tenured staff in positions generated at receiving schools will receive first consideration. I recognize the contractual complexity. I also recognize that saying nothing has a cost we are currently paying.

Second, whether human resources can accelerate the seniority analysis Marco referenced in March. My staff are attempting to calculate their own positions using incomplete information, and the resulting rumors are worse than the reality.

Third, whether there is any mechanism — a stipend, a retention commitment, anything — for staff who remain through June 2027. I recognize the fairness problem in paying people to stay in a building we are closing. I would ask you to weigh it against the cost of arriving at July 2027 with a receiving-school staffing plan built around people who left in February.

### A Personal Note

I have tried to run these forums honestly. I have not told my community that the building will stay open, and I have not told my staff that their jobs are safe. Several people have told me they find that useful and several have told me they find it cold.

What I have found hardest is being the person who conducts a process whose outcome I cannot influence, in front of people who reasonably believe I should be advocating for them. I have not advocated publicly against the closure, because I do not think that is my role and because, having read the budget analysis, I am not certain I would be right. But I want you to know that the position is corrosive, and I do not think I am the only administrator in this district who feels it.

I will have the class size answer if you can get it to me. That is the question that will determine how the October forum goes, and I would rather walk in with a number than with a range.

**TO:** Leticia Ramos-Byrd, Board Chair; Members of the Board of Education
**FROM:** Dr. Chidinma Eze, Superintendent of Schools
**DATE:** October 20, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Response to Written Objection of October 6 Regarding Redistricting and Class Size

This memo responds to the written objection submitted on October 6 by two members of the Board regarding the redistricting model and its class size implications. At the Chair's request, I am responding in writing so the response is part of the record before the November 3 vote. I have attached the objection in full, and I have made no attempt to summarize it in a way that softens it. Where I think it is correct, I say so.

### The Objection

The objection makes four claims. First, that the redistricting model produces class sizes at Bridgemill and Hollow Road that exceed what the district would tolerate absent fiscal pressure. Second, that administration has presented class size as district averages rather than as section-level projections, and that this is misleading. Third, that the $1.9 million savings figure has been presented publicly without its offsets. Fourth, that the process has not seriously evaluated alternatives to closure.

### Class Size: Section-Level Projections

The second claim is correct, and I accept it without qualification. We have presented averages. Averages are the wrong unit. A parent does not enroll a child in an average.

I have therefore had the business office produce section-level projections, which are attached in full and which I will summarize.

Under the redistricting model, in 2027–28, Bridgemill and Hollow Road would operate 46 sections across grades K through 5. Of those, 31 sections project between 19 and 22 students. Nine sections project between 23 and 24. Six sections project at 25 or 26, distributed as follows: two grade 4 sections at Bridgemill at 25; one grade 5 section at Bridgemill at 26; two grade 3 sections at Hollow Road at 25; one grade 4 section at Hollow Road at 25.

Our contractual limit for grades 4 and 5 is 27, and for grades K through 3 is 24. The two grade 3 sections at Hollow Road projecting at 25 exceed the contractual limit and would require either an additional section or a contractual waiver. I am recommending an additional section, which reduces the modeled staffing savings by approximately $72,400 and reduces the six sections at 25 or 26 to four.

So the answer to the question the objection asks is this: under the model, four sections would operate at 25 or 26 students, all in grades 4 and 5, all within contract, all above the district's historical average of 21.3. That is the number. I should have produced it in June.

On whether those class sizes are ones we would tolerate absent fiscal pressure: partly. We have operated sections at 25 and 26 in grades 4 and 5 in six of the last eight years, most recently at Hollow Road in 2024–25. So it is not accurate to say the model produces class sizes we have never accepted. It is accurate to say it produces more of them, more durably, and by design rather than as a consequence of enrollment we could not predict. I think that distinction is the real substance of the objection and I do not want to argue past it.

I would add one thing that cuts the other way. Under the baseline scenario — six buildings, $3.2 million gap — the reduction package eliminates 28 to 32 positions. Some of those are classroom teachers, and the class size consequence at all five remaining elementary schools would be comparable to or worse than what the closure model produces at two. The choice before the Board is not between the closure model and the status quo. It is between the closure model and the reduction package, and I would ask that the class size comparison be made on those terms.

### The Savings Figure

The third claim is substantially correct. The $1.9 million figure has been cited publicly, including by me, without consistently including the transportation offset of $88,000, the one-time transition costs of $340,000, or the caveat regarding realization of staffing savings in year one. Marco flagged this risk in his March memo and I did not manage it adequately.

For the record: the estimate is $1,910,000 recurring, already net of the transportation offset. One-time transition costs of $340,000 fall in FY28. Human resources' completed seniority analysis now indicates that year-one realization is likely 93 percent, with full realization in year two. The additional grade 3 section reduces the recurring figure to approximately $1,838,000. Net FY28 impact, accounting for transition costs and partial realization, is approximately $1,369,000. Full recurring benefit beginning FY29 is approximately $1,838,000.

I would ask the Board to use those figures. I will use them.

### Alternatives

The fourth claim I dispute, though not entirely.

The study evaluated four alternatives beyond closure and the status quo: grade reconfiguration into a K–2 and 3–5 pairing; partial closure with Ashford Lane operating as a two-grade annex; shared services with a neighboring district; and closure of a different elementary school.

Grade reconfiguration produces savings of approximately $640,000, principally through specials staffing efficiency, but increases transportation cost by $210,000 and requires every elementary student to change buildings once. Net recurring benefit is roughly $430,000 against substantially greater disruption than closure. The partial-closure annex model produces $310,000, because the fixed costs of operating a building — heat, custodial, administration, food service — do not scale down proportionally with occupancy. Shared services with our neighbor was explored for transportation and business office functions; discussions continue and may yield $150,000 to $200,000, and I recommend pursuing it regardless of the November vote, but it is not an alternative to closure at this scale. Closing a different elementary school produces lower savings, because both alternatives operate at higher utilization, and requires substantially more redistricting.

Where the objection has force is that this analysis appeared in the September Facilities Committee packet and was not presented in the June preliminary findings document or at the community forums. A member of the public attending every forum would reasonably conclude that alternatives were not seriously examined, because they did not hear them examined. That is a communications failure and it is mine.

### Two Further Matters

I want to raise two things the objection does not address.

First, staff attrition. Odalys Marte reported to me in September that Ashford Lane has lost four staff since March, with more expected. This is a real cost of an extended process and it falls on the receiving schools as much as on Ashford Lane, because the staff we are losing are the ones we would most want to place. Whatever the Board decides on November 3, I would ask that it decide, and that it not defer to a subsequent meeting for further study. The cost of continued uncertainty now exceeds the value of additional analysis.

Second, the special education corrective action plan. Our plan was submitted June 26 and accepted by the state July 31. The verification visit is scheduled for November 12 — nine days after the Board's vote. Hannah Weissman has completed 214 transition plan reviews, the documentation system went live in August, and our internal audit of a 40-IEP sample in September found a noncompliance rate of 4 percent, down from 19.

I raise this for two reasons. The first is that the Board should know that this work has progressed on its own schedule and its own merits, independent of the facilities question, which is what I said in March and which has turned out to be true. The second is that I have heard the closure defended in public on the grounds that it will fix our special education compliance. It will not, it has not, and the 4 percent figure was achieved with six buildings open. I would ask members of this Board not to make that argument, whichever way they intend to vote. It is not true, and the families whose children are in those programs will know it is not true.

### Closing

I do not expect this memo to change the votes of the two members who submitted the objection, and I am not writing it for that purpose. I am writing it because two of the four claims are correct, and correcting the record before the vote is more important than winning the argument.

I will present the final recommendation on November 3. It remains a recommendation to close Ashford Lane Elementary effective July 1, 2027, with redistricting as modeled, with the additional grade 3 section at Hollow Road, and with special education program hubs at Bridgemill and Hollow Road. I hold that recommendation with less certainty than I would like, and I would rather the Board know that than not.
