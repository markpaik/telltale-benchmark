**SUBJECT:** Owner-Operator Conversion Program and Albany Cross-Dock Opening

This memo announces two decisions the executive team finalized last week, both of which will change how every terminal in this network operates over the next ten months. I am putting them in one memo because they are connected, and I want every terminal manager to understand them the same way at the same time.

First, Meadowlands will convert all 310 owner-operators currently under contract to employee driver positions, effective January 1, 2027. There will be no exceptions, no grandfathered lease agreements, and no hybrid arrangements. Every driver pulling a Meadowlands trailer on January 2 of next year will be a W-2 employee of this company. The decision follows the New Jersey Department of Labor's audit assessment, which Camille Beauregard has briefed the leadership team on separately and which some of you have heard about secondhand. I will not rehash the legal posture here, except to say this: the regulatory environment in New Jersey and in every state we touch has moved decisively against the independent contractor model as we have run it, and the executive team and the board have concluded that the model is no longer sustainable for a carrier of our size and profile. We are not converting because we lost an argument. We are converting because the argument is over.

Second, the Albany cross-dock will open on November 15, 2026. This is not a soft target. A national retail customer has awarded us contingent volume worth approximately $31 million annually, and the award is expressly conditioned on Albany being operational by that date with a 98.5 percent on-time service commitment from day one. Tomasz Wisniewski has terminal director responsibility for the buildout and will report to me directly on it through opening day. Every terminal manager in the northern tier — Carteret, Paterson, and Newburgh in particular — should expect linehaul schedule changes, freight flow changes, and requests for loaned dock supervision as we stand Albany up. When Tomasz asks for help, the answer is yes, and if it cannot be yes, the escalation comes to me the same day.

Now, what I need from each of you between today and conversion.

On the conversion itself: Lorraine Petrucci's office will issue the complete pay, benefits, and seniority package in early April. Until that package is published, no terminal manager, dispatcher, or fleet supervisor is to make any representation to any owner-operator about what their pay, benefits, seniority, or equipment disposition will look like as an employee. I understand the questions are already coming, and I understand silence feels like a bad answer. The worse answer is a promise we cannot keep, made in a driver's room, that ends up in a grievance or a lawsuit. The approved response is: "The full package will be published in April, it will be in writing, and every affected driver will get a one-on-one meeting before they have to decide anything." Say that, and only that.

On retention: our driver attrition is running 31 percent annualized against an industry average near 24, and that number is before we ask 310 independent businesspeople to become employees. Some owner-operators will leave rather than convert. Our internal planning assumes we retain roughly 80 percent, and every point below that number is a tractor we have to seat with a new hire in the worst driver market I have seen in my career. Terminal managers are the front line of retention. I want each of you to know, by name, which of your owner-operators are flight risks, which are opinion leaders in the driver room, and which have equipment situations — leases, balloon payments, older tractors — that make conversion attractive or painful. Send that assessment to Lorraine's office by March 20. It will shape the transition offers.

On equipment: the company will acquire 218 tractors to replace owner-operator equipment that will not come into the fleet. Sanjay Bhattacharya's budget memo will cover the financing. What terminal managers need to know is that deliveries begin in August and run through December, and yard space, shop capacity, and licensing workflow at your terminals must absorb them. Maintenance directors will receive a separate delivery schedule.

On Albany: the customer's on-time commitment does not begin when Albany is comfortable. It begins November 15. Freight that misses Albany's cutoffs from your terminal counts against that 98.5 the same as a failure inside Albany's four walls. We will run network dry runs the last two weeks of October, and I expect terminal managers to treat those dry runs as live freight.

I will hold a terminal managers' call every other Thursday starting March 12 through year-end. Bring problems early. The two worst things a terminal manager can do this year are surprise me in November and promise a driver something in March.

Cheikh

**FROM:** Camille Beauregard, General Counsel

**SUBJECT:** New Jersey Department of Labor Audit Assessment — Response Deadline and Litigation Hold
**PRIVILEGED AND CONFIDENTIAL — ATTORNEY-CLIENT COMMUNICATION**

On January 20, 2026, the New Jersey Department of Labor and Workforce Development issued its final determination closing the audit of our owner-operator program covering calendar years 2022 through 2024. The Department has assessed $2,412,886, comprising back contributions to the unemployment insurance, temporary disability, and family leave insurance funds, statutory interest, and penalties. The determination concludes that the audited owner-operators were employees under New Jersey's ABC test for the full audit period. We have 45 days from the date of the determination to respond — a deadline of March 6, 2026. If we do not appeal or pay by that date, the assessment becomes final and collectible, and the Department may pursue liens and personal liability against responsible corporate officers.

I want the leadership team to understand three things about this determination, because each of them bears on decisions we are making right now.

First, the legal merits. The ABC test presumes employment and puts the burden on us to prove all three prongs: that the driver is free from our control, that the work is outside our usual course of business or performed outside our places of business, and that the driver is engaged in an independently established trade. The auditors' findings focus on prong B — that hauling freight is the usual course of business of a freight carrier — and on control indicators including our dispatch practices, forced-dispatch language in older lease agreements, decal and equipment requirements, and settlement deductions. I will not put a percentage on our odds in writing, but I will say that New Jersey appellate authority on prong B is unfavorable to motor carriers, and no LTL carrier of our profile has prevailed on comparable facts in this state since 2019. An appeal is defensible; a reversal is unlikely.

Second, the exposure is not limited to $2.4 million. The determination letter expressly states that a subsequent audit period — 2025 forward — remains open. That sentence is not boilerplate. It means that every week we continue operating the current model, we accrue additional contribution liability, interest, and penalties for a period the Department has already told us it intends to examine, under a legal standard it has already applied against us. The 2022–2024 assessment averaged roughly $800,000 per year across the audited population; a second assessment covering 2025 and 2026 should be modeled at a comparable or higher run rate, and Sanjay should reserve accordingly. Beyond the Department, a final misclassification determination is discoverable and usable: by plaintiffs' counsel in personal injury litigation seeking to establish our control over drivers, by the IRS and other state agencies under information-sharing agreements, and potentially by drivers themselves in wage-and-hour claims. Given the two nuclear verdicts in our region in the past eighteen months and our insurance renewal posture, the litigation-leverage dimension of this determination should not be underestimated.

Third, our procedural options. We can pay in full, which closes 2022–2024 but concedes the classification question and invites the second audit to proceed on settled ground. We can appeal to the Office of Administrative Law, which preserves our rights, likely takes 12 to 18 months, and requires either payment under protest or a bond. Or we can appeal and simultaneously pursue a negotiated resolution, which is my recommendation. Filing the appeal by March 6 costs us nothing in leverage and preserves every option; the Department's counsel has historically been willing to compromise penalties and interest, particularly where an employer commits to prospective compliance. The single most valuable settlement asset we can bring to that negotiation is a completed or committed conversion of the owner-operator fleet. I want to be plain about this: the appeal and the conversion are not in tension. The appeal contests what we owe for the past. The conversion stops the meter for the future and materially improves the terms on which the past gets resolved. Anyone who argues we should delay conversion "because we're appealing" has the relationship between the two exactly backwards, and I will say so in any forum where that argument is made.

Effective immediately, a litigation hold applies to all documents, emails, dispatch records, settlement statements, lease agreements, and communications concerning owner-operator classification, dispatch practices, or the audit. Retention schedules are suspended for these categories. Do not delete, and instruct your teams accordingly; my office will circulate formal hold notices this week. Additionally, no one outside this leadership team is to communicate with the Department, and all internal written discussion of classification issues should be routed through my office so that privilege is preserved. If a terminal manager or driver asks about the audit, the answer is that the company is responding through counsel.

I will file the appeal on or before March 6 unless the executive team directs otherwise. Please treat that date as fixed.

Camille

**TO:** Executive Leadership Team; All Terminal Managers; Finance Staff
**FROM:** Sanjay Bhattacharya, Chief Financial Officer
**DATE:** March 16, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Revised 2026–2027 Operating Budget — Conversion Cost Impact, Tractor Financing, and Insurance Renewal

This memo replaces the operating budget distributed in December. The December budget assumed continuation of the owner-operator model and did not contemplate the Albany award, the audit assessment, or the insurance renewal outcome. All four assumptions have changed, and the revised budget reflects them. I am going to walk through the numbers in more detail than a budget memo usually carries, because I want every manager who owns a cost line to understand why margins will compress in 2027 and what we are doing about it.

**Cost per mile.** Converting 310 owner-operators to employee drivers raises our fully loaded cost per mile by 19 cents on the affected miles. To be precise about what that figure contains: it is the net difference between owner-operator settlement economics and employee driver economics, and it includes wages and accessorial pay at the rates Lorraine's office will publish, employer payroll taxes, workers' compensation, health and welfare benefits, paid time off accrual, and the ownership costs — depreciation or lease expense, maintenance, and licensing — of the tractors the company will now provide, offset by the settlement payments, fuel surcharge pass-throughs, and chargebacks we will no longer make. The converted population runs approximately 28.6 million revenue miles annually. At 19 cents, the annualized gross impact is approximately $5.4 million. Because conversion is effective January 1, 2027, the 2026 impact is limited to transition costs of roughly $1.1 million — recruiting, transition payments, orientation pay, and early tractor deliveries — and the full run rate lands in 2027.

**Tractor acquisition and financing.** We require 218 tractors. Our planning assumes 92 converting drivers will sell their tractors to the company where equipment meets our age and condition standards; those purchases are budgeted at $6.8 million and will be handled through the transition offers in Lorraine's package. The remaining 218 units break down as follows: 130 will be purchased — a mix of 74 new units at an average of $172,000 and 56 late-model used units at an average of $118,000 — for total purchase capital of approximately $19.3 million, financed through equipment term loans over 60 months at rates currently quoted between 7.1 and 7.6 percent, producing annual debt service of approximately $4.6 million. The other 88 units will be taken on full-service leases at an average of $2,140 per month, or approximately $2.3 million annually, which keeps residual risk and major maintenance off our books during a period when I do not want additional balance sheet strain. Deliveries are scheduled August through December. I have negotiated delivery-contingent payment terms so that we are not servicing debt on tractors sitting undelivered on a dealer's lot. Combined with the tractor purchases from converting drivers, total equipment capital deployment is approximately $28.4 million, against which we have secured a $32 million equipment facility with our lead bank, closed March 11.

**Insurance.** The liability renewal bound at 24 percent above expiring, moving annual premium from $9.6 million to $11.9 million, driven by the two nuclear-verdict settlements in the region and market-wide capacity withdrawal at our attachment points. We took a higher retention — $750,000, up from $500,000 — to hold the increase at 24 percent; the quoted increase at expiring retention was 31 percent. Terminal managers should understand what the higher retention means: the first $750,000 of every liability loss is now our money. Safety performance is no longer only a compliance metric; it is a direct P&L line, and it will be treated as one in terminal scorecards beginning Q2. I will note, because it is relevant to the conversion debate, that our brokers advise that an all-employee fleet with company-controlled equipment, maintenance, and telematics is viewed more favorably at renewal than a mixed fleet, and that a final misclassification determination is precisely the kind of fact underwriters and plaintiffs' counsel both price.

**Audit reserve.** We have reserved the full $2.4 million assessment and an additional $1.7 million against the open second audit period, per Camille's guidance. These reserves run through 2026 results.

**Revenue and the bottom line.** The Albany award phases in at approximately $2.2 million monthly beginning mid-November, reaching the full $31 million annual run rate by Q2 2027, contingent on service performance. Incorporating all of the above, the 2026 operating ratio moves from the December plan of 94.1 to a revised 95.9, and the preliminary 2027 plan shows 96.6 before mitigation. That is not acceptable as a landing point. Mitigation actions in the 2027 plan include a general rate increase averaging 5.4 percent effective February 2027, targeted repricing of the 40 lowest-margin accounts, linehaul network savings of approximately $1.8 million enabled by Albany relay operations, and the retention savings Lorraine's memo will quantify — every point of attrition below 31 percent is worth roughly $190,000 in avoided recruiting, orientation, and productivity-curve cost. With mitigation, the 2027 plan closes at 94.8.

Department budget holders will receive revised line-item budgets by March 27. Variance reporting moves to monthly for all terminals through the conversion.

Sanjay

**TO:** All Owner-Operators; All Terminal Managers; Executive Leadership Team
**FROM:** Lorraine Petrucci, Vice President of Human Resources
**DATE:** April 6, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Employee Conversion Package — Pay, Benefits, Seniority, and Equipment Transition

This memo is the complete and official statement of the terms under which owner-operators will convert to employee driver positions effective January 1, 2027. It supersedes anything you may have heard in a driver's room, on a dock, or from a dispatcher. If a term is not in this memo or in the individual written offer you will receive, it is not part of the package. Every converting driver will have a one-on-one meeting with an HR representative and their terminal manager between April 20 and June 30 to review their individual offer, and no one is required to sign anything before that meeting.

**Pay.** Converting linehaul drivers will be paid 68 cents per mile on all dispatched miles, practical route, with no reduction for empty miles. City and peddle-run drivers will be paid $29.50 per hour with time-and-a-half over 40. Accessorial pay is standardized across the fleet: $22.00 per stop after the first, detention at $25.00 per hour after the first hour, and hourly pay at $29.50 for dock work, breakdowns, and required training time. During the first 90 days after conversion, every converting driver is guaranteed minimum weekly earnings of $1,450, regardless of freight levels, so that no one takes a pay cut while dispatch patterns settle. We modeled every current owner-operator's 2025 settlements against this structure. For roughly 85 percent of you, net take-home compensation as an employee — after accounting for the truck payment, fuel, maintenance, insurance, and self-employment taxes you will no longer pay — is equal to or better than what you netted last year. For those where the model shows a shortfall, your individual meeting will address it directly, and terminal-specific adjustments are authorized within limits I control.

**Benefits.** The standard 60-day benefits waiting period is waived for all converting drivers. Medical, dental, and vision coverage is effective January 1, 2027 — the day you convert. The company pays 78 percent of the medical premium. You will be eligible for the 401(k) with the company match of 50 percent on the first 6 percent immediately, with no vesting delay on your own contributions and the standard three-year vesting on the match. Company-paid life insurance at one times annual earnings and short-term disability are included. I want to say plainly to owner-operators who have been buying individual health coverage or going without: for many of you, the benefits package alone is worth $14,000 to $19,000 a year, and it belongs in any honest comparison of the two models.

**Seniority.** This is the issue I have been asked about most, by converting drivers and by current employee drivers, and the rules are as follows. For vacation accrual, benefits milestones, and service awards, converting drivers will be credited with their full continuous tenure as a Meadowlands owner-operator, measured from first dispatch date. A driver who has pulled our freight for nine years will accrue vacation as a nine-year employee, which means four weeks, not one. For run selection, bid boards, and layoff protection, converting drivers will slot at their conversion date — January 1, 2027 — behind all current employee drivers. I know this is a compromise, and I know neither group gets everything it wants. Current employee drivers who have held company seniority for years will not be leapfrogged on the bid board by drivers who converted last month; converting drivers will not be treated as new hires when it comes to the vacation and benefits their years of service have earned. Among converting drivers, bid position within the January 1 class will be ordered by first dispatch date. These rules are final and uniform across all twelve terminals.

**Equipment transition.** The company will offer to purchase tractors from converting drivers where the unit is model year 2019 or newer and passes our shop inspection, at 95 percent of current wholesale book value, with proceeds available to pay off equipment liens at closing. For drivers whose tractors do not meet the standard or who are underwater on equipment loans, HR will connect you with our transition counselors, and hardship assistance up to $4,000 is available case by case. Every driver who converts and remains actively employed through March 31, 2027 will receive a transition completion payment of $6,000, paid in two installments.

**Attrition and why this package looks the way it does.** Our driver turnover is running 31 percent against an industry average near 24. Some of that is the market; some of it is us, and this package is built to fix our share of it. Guaranteed transition pay, day-one benefits, honored tenure, and standardized accessorials are retention decisions as much as conversion decisions, and they apply where noted to the existing employee fleet as well — the accessorial standardization covers everyone effective July 1, 2026.

Deadline: converting drivers must return signed offers by September 1, 2026 so that equipment, payroll, and benefits enrollment can be completed by year-end. Drivers who decline will see their lease agreements terminated effective December 31, 2026, per the notice provisions in those agreements. My door, and every terminal HR representative's door, is open between now and then.

Lorraine

**SUBJECT:** Response to the April 28 Fleet Managers' Letter — Why the Conversion Will Not Wait for the Appeal

I received the letter signed by nine fleet managers on April 28 asking the company to delay the owner-operator conversion until the appeal of the Department of Labor assessment is decided. I want to start by saying that I respect both the letter and the way it was delivered — through channels, in writing, with reasons. That is exactly how disagreement should work inside this company, and nothing in this memo should discourage anyone from doing it again. But the answer is no, and you are owed a full explanation of why, not a one-line dismissal. So here it is.

Your letter makes three arguments: that converting before the appeal is decided concedes the legal question and wastes the appeal; that the conversion's $5.4 million annual cost is being incurred voluntarily when a favorable appeal ruling might make it unnecessary; and that forcing the issue now, during the worst driver market in years and while we are trying to open Albany, risks losing owner-operators we cannot replace. Let me take them in order.

On the first argument, I will tell you what Camille Beauregard told the leadership team in January, because it is the clearest thing anyone has said in this entire debate: the appeal and the conversion are not in tension, and the argument that we should wait has the relationship exactly backwards. The appeal contests what we owe for 2022 through 2024. It is about the past. It cannot change the law we operate under going forward — even a complete victory on appeal would mean only that we escape one assessment under facts as they existed then, in front of one administrative tribunal, while the ABC test remains the law of New Jersey and the Department has already told us in writing that a second audit period, covering 2025 to the present, is open. Every week we run the current model, that second assessment grows. We are not preserving an option by waiting; we are buying additional liability at roughly $70,000 a month for the privilege of hoping. And no carrier of our profile has won this fight in this state in seven years. Camille has also been direct that a committed conversion is our single most valuable asset in negotiating down the penalties and interest on the existing $2.4 million assessment. Waiting weakens the appeal position. It does not protect it.

On the second argument — cost — I want to push back on the framing that the 19 cents per mile is the cost of converting and zero is the cost of not converting. The cost of not converting includes the accruing second-period liability I just described. It includes what our insurance brokers told Sanjay during the renewal that came in 24 percent higher: that a final misclassification determination is exactly the kind of finding plaintiffs' counsel builds a nuclear verdict on, because it establishes company control over drivers we claimed were independent, and that underwriters price that exposure whether we like it or not. Two verdicts in our region in eighteen months should tell every fleet manager what the stakes are the next time one of our trucks is in a serious accident and a jury hears that the State of New Jersey already found we misclassified the driver. The 19 cents is real, it is painful, and Sanjay's budget shows exactly how we absorb it. But it is a known, bounded, budgeted number. The alternative is unbounded.

On the third argument — driver flight — this is the one I take most seriously, because you are the people who talk to these drivers every day, and a 31 percent attrition rate is a fact, not a projection. But consider what delay actually does to retention. If we announce that conversion is postponed pending an appeal that will take twelve to eighteen months, we are asking 310 owner-operators to live in limbo for a year and a half, knowing conversion is probably coming but unable to plan for it, while every recruiter in the Northeast tells them Meadowlands' contractor model is under state enforcement. Uncertainty is what drives people out the door. What Lorraine's package offers instead is certainty: a published rate, day-one benefits, honored tenure, guaranteed transition pay, and a real offer for their equipment. Early returns support this — as of Friday, 118 signed offers are in, five weeks after the package was published and four months before the deadline, including some of the drivers your own March risk assessments flagged as likely to leave. And Albany, which several of you cited as a reason to delay, is in fact a reason to proceed: the new terminal needs domiciled employee drivers on a service commitment we cannot subcontract, and four of its linehaul seats are being filled by converting owner-operators who asked for the transfer.

The decision stands: every driver is an employee on January 1, 2027. What I am asking from the nine of you who signed the letter is the same thing I would ask of anyone who argued a position and lost — not silence, but execution. You know these drivers better than anyone in the company. Between now and September 1, I want fleet managers in every one-on-one conversion meeting where the driver requests it, and I want your flight-risk lists updated monthly for Lorraine. If the package is failing a specific driver, escalate the specific case; the hardship and adjustment authority exists to be used. What we will not do is relitigate the direction.

My door remains open to any of you, individually or together.

Cheikh
