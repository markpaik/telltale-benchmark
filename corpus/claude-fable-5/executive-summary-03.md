# Executive Summary 1 of 4

## K–2 Structured Literacy Initiative

**Program period evaluated:** 2024–25 school year | **Grant expiration:** September 30, 2025
**Prepared by the Office of Research and Accountability | September 9, 2025**

### Finding and Recommendation

The K–2 Structured Literacy Initiative is the strongest performer in the district's grant portfolio and the only one of the four evaluated programs for which the evidence supports continuation without structural change. Across the twelve participating elementary schools, the share of K–2 students reading at or above the spring benchmark rose from 41 percent to 58 percent in a single year — a seventeen-point gain — and the first-grade cohort's improvement held across all four demographic subgroups the office tracks. The grant that funds the program expires in three weeks, on September 30, 2025. The Office of Research and Accountability recommends that the district move immediately to sustain the program through a combination of general fund absorption and a renewal application, and that the Board Finance Committee authorize a bridge appropriation at its September 9 meeting so that no coaching position lapses while renewal is pending.

### The Program and Its Funding

The initiative was funded at $2.4 million over the grant cycle and reached approximately 1,900 kindergarten, first-grade, and second-grade students across twelve of the district's elementary schools during 2024–25. The money bought three things: a common structured literacy curriculum aligned to South Carolina's Read to Succeed requirements, replacing a patchwork of school-selected materials; a school-based literacy coach at each of the twelve sites, responsible for modeling lessons, observing instruction, and running weekly data meetings with grade-level teams; and an initial and ongoing training sequence for every K–2 classroom teacher in the participating schools. Personnel — the twelve coaches and substitute coverage for teacher training days — consumed roughly two-thirds of the award. Curriculum materials, decodable text libraries, and assessment licenses account for most of the remainder. Materials purchases are largely complete and do not recur at full cost; the continuing expense of the program is, overwhelmingly, the coaching staff.

### What the Evaluation Found

The headline result is the benchmark movement: from 41 percent of participating K–2 students at or above the spring reading benchmark before implementation to 58 percent at the end of 2024–25. That gain is large by the standards of literacy interventions at this scale, and it did not come from movement in one grade or one school. Eleven of the twelve sites improved; the twelfth held flat but did not decline.

The result the office weights most heavily is the subgroup finding. In the first-grade cohort — the grade with the fullest implementation, since those students received the program in both kindergarten and first grade — gains held across all four subgroups the office tracks: students eligible for free or reduced-price meals, students with disabilities, multilingual learners, and students identified by race and ethnicity. In most district initiatives, an average gain of this size conceals a widening gap; here it does not. The distance between the highest- and lowest-performing subgroups on the spring benchmark narrowed slightly rather than growing. If the pattern holds as the current first graders move into second grade, the district will have evidence that the program is closing gaps, not merely lifting a mean.

The evaluation also examined fidelity, because literacy initiatives routinely post first-year gains that evaporate when coaching support is withdrawn. Coach logs and walkthrough data show that the weekly grade-level data meetings occurred at better than 90 percent of the scheduled rate at ten of the twelve sites, and that lesson pacing stayed within the curriculum's intended windows. The two sites with weaker fidelity are the flat-performing school and one other; the correlation between fidelity and outcomes at the school level is exactly what the research literature predicts, and it is the strongest internal evidence that the coaching layer — not the materials alone — is producing the results.

The per-pupil cost of the program in 2024–25 was approximately $1,260. Set against the district's downstream costs for reading intervention, retention, and special education referrals driven by unremediated reading difficulty, the office judges this a favorable price, and it falls further in continuation years because the materials investment is behind us.

### What the District Should Do Before September 30

Three actions are needed before the grant expires, and the sequencing matters. First, the Board Finance Committee should authorize a general fund bridge appropriation — the office estimates $1.05 million to $1.15 million for a full continuation year, essentially the coaching payroll plus assessment licenses — effective October 1, so that the twelve coaches, who are currently on grant-contingent contracts, receive continuation notices before the funding lapses. Second, the district should file the renewal application with the grantor; the office's preliminary read is that the outcome data make Piedmont Vale a strong renewal candidate, and any renewal award would offset the bridge appropriation rather than duplicate it. Third, the Superintendent should direct the Chief Academic Officer to prepare a two-year expansion analysis for the Board's November meeting, covering the cost and staffing implications of extending the model to the district's remaining elementary schools, since the schools outside the initiative are now visibly behind the schools inside it.

### What Happens If the Program Stops

If the grant lapses without district action, twelve literacy coaches lose their positions on September 30 — three weeks into the instructional year. The office wants the Committee to be clear-eyed about what follows. The coaches are the mechanism by which teachers new to structured literacy sustain the practice; the district hired several of them from outside and will not easily rehire them midyear if funding is later restored. The curriculum materials remain in buildings, so instruction would not revert overnight, but every comparable district experience the office reviewed shows fidelity decaying within a year of coach withdrawal, with benchmark gains following it down. The students most exposed are this year's kindergartners — roughly 950 children who would receive the materials without the instructional support that made the materials work — and the current second graders, whose gains are one unsupported year away from consolidating into durable reading skill. The district would, in effect, have paid $2.4 million to demonstrate that the model works and then declined to collect the return. The office recommends against that outcome in the strongest terms available to it.

## Online Credit Recovery Program

**Program period evaluated:** 2024–25 school year | **Grant expiration:** September 30, 2025
**Prepared by the Office of Research and Accountability | September 9, 2025**

### Finding and Recommendation

The online credit recovery program is not working, and the evidence is sufficient to say so without hedging. Of the 1,240 high school students enrolled during 2024–25, 46 percent completed a course. Of those completers, 31 percent passed the corresponding state end-of-course exam. The pass rate for the same courses taught in person across the district's high schools was 68 percent. A program in which fewer than half of enrollees finish, and fewer than a third of finishers can pass the external exam attached to the content, is not recovering credit in any sense the district should stand behind. The grant expires September 30, 2025. The Office of Research and Accountability recommends that the district decline to renew the program in its current vendor-delivered form, allow the grant to close, wind down the platform contract at the end of the fall semester, and redirect the function to a teacher-led hybrid model beginning in January 2026. The recommendation to wind down at semester's end rather than on the grant expiration date is deliberate: approximately 400 students are currently mid-course, and the district owes them a completion pathway before the platform goes dark.

### The Program and Its Funding

The program was funded at $890,000 and delivered through a commercial online platform under a vendor contract, with district staff providing lab supervision at each high school rather than instruction. Students who had failed a course — most commonly Algebra I, English II, Biology, and U.S. History, the four courses carrying state end-of-course exams — were enrolled to retake the content online, working self-paced through vendor modules during a scheduled lab period or after school. The vendor license and content fees consumed roughly 70 percent of the award; lab monitor stipends and a part-time district coordinator account for the balance. Enrollment reached 1,240 students across the district's high schools, concentrated among juniors and seniors for whom the recovered credit bears directly on graduation.

### What the Evaluation Found

Three numbers organize the evaluation, and each is worse than the one before it. First, completion: 46 percent of enrolled students finished a course, meaning 670 students — a majority of enrollees — spent seat time in the program during 2024–25 and exited with nothing. Interviews with lab monitors and a student survey the office fielded in April point to consistent causes: the self-paced format collapses without an adult who knows the content, the platform's remediation loops send struggling students back through the same material that did not work the first time, and lab monitors, who are supervisors rather than certified teachers in the subject, cannot answer substantive questions.

Second, the pass rate. Among the 570 completers, 31 percent passed the corresponding end-of-course exam. The in-person comparison figure of 68 percent is not a perfectly matched benchmark — credit recovery students failed the course once already and arrive with weaker preparation — but the office also compared this year's completers against the district's prior teacher-led summer credit recovery cohorts, who carry the same prior-failure profile. Those cohorts historically passed the exam at rates in the mid-50s. The gap that matters is not online versus first-time in-person instruction; it is online versus every other way the district has ever delivered credit recovery, and the online model loses each comparison by a wide margin.

Third, and most seriously, the integrity question. Students who complete the online course receive the credit whether or not they pass the exam, because course credit and exam performance are formally separate under state policy. The district is therefore issuing credits — and, for seniors, diplomas — backed by demonstrated mastery in fewer than one case in three. The evaluation cannot quantify how many 2025 graduates carry recovered credits in courses whose content they could not pass an exam in, but the number is not small, and the Committee should understand that continuing the program as-is means continuing to accumulate that liability.

The per-completer cost was approximately $1,560; the cost per completer who also passed the exam — the only outcome the office regards as genuine recovery — was approximately $5,030. The district's teacher-led summer model has historically delivered a passing completer for well under half that figure.

### What the District Should Do Before September 30

The office recommends the following sequence, and this is the one place in these summaries where a list serves better than prose:

1. **Decline renewal.** Notify the grantor by September 30 that the district will not seek continuation, and file the closeout report with the outcome data reported honestly.
2. **Extend the vendor license through December 19, 2025, only,** at prorated cost the office estimates at $85,000–$95,000 from general fund contingency, solely to allow the roughly 400 mid-course students to finish. No new enrollments after September 30.
3. **Attach certified-teacher support to the wind-down term.** Assign content-certified teachers to the labs for the fall semester so current students finish with instructional help rather than in the conditions that produced this year's results.
4. **Stand up the replacement for January.** Direct high school leadership to design a teacher-led hybrid credit recovery model — certified teachers of record, small sections, platform content used as a supplement rather than the instructor — for spring semester and summer 2026, with a budget and staffing plan to the Board in November.
5. **Audit the credit record.** Direct the registrars, with this office's support, to identify current students holding recovered credits in exam-bearing courses they have not passed, so counselors can plan supports before those students reach graduation review.

### What Happens If the Program Stops

Stopping is the recommendation, so the relevant question is what a badly managed stop would cost versus a managed one. An abrupt shutdown on September 30 strands 400 mid-course students, most of them juniors and seniors for whom the credit is graduation-critical; several dozen seniors would enter spring semester short of credits with no recovery vehicle in place, and the district's graduation rate would take a direct and avoidable hit. The managed wind-down described above protects those students at modest cost. Staff impact is limited: the lab monitor role is a stipended duty, not a position, and the part-time coordinator can be redirected to standing up the replacement model. The students who need credit recovery do not disappear when the program does — roughly 1,200 of them will need the function again this year — which is precisely why the recommendation pairs the shutdown with a January replacement rather than leaving a vacuum. The failure mode the office is guarding against is not ending the program; it is ending the program without building its successor.

## Community School Wraparound Model — Redbud, Saluda Creek, and Tanner's Mill Elementary Schools

**Program period evaluated:** 2024–25 school year | **Grant period:** through June 30, 2027
**Prepared by the Office of Research and Accountability | September 9, 2025**

### Finding and Recommendation

The community school wraparound model produced a genuine result at one of its three sites and nothing measurable at the other two, and the difference between the sites is explainable, which makes it actionable. At Redbud Elementary, chronic absenteeism fell six percentage points in 2024–25 — the largest single-year attendance improvement recorded at any district school in the past five years. At Saluda Creek and Tanner's Mill, attendance and every other tracked indicator were flat, and Tanner's Mill lost 44 percent of its program staff during the year. The grant does not expire this month; it runs through June 2027, which gives the district something it lacks with the two expiring programs: time to fix rather than a forced up-or-down decision. The Office of Research and Accountability recommends that the district continue Redbud without change, place Tanner's Mill under a corrective staffing and management plan with a December 15 checkpoint, hold Saluda Creek to a spring 2026 performance review, and commit now to the principle that grant year three will fund what works rather than what was originally mapped.

### The Program and Its Funding

The community school model was funded at $1.6 million for 2024–25 across three elementary sites selected for high poverty rates and chronic absenteeism above 25 percent. The model places a full-time community school coordinator in each building to broker services families otherwise reach unevenly or not at all: an attendance outreach team making home visits, co-located health screenings and a telehealth link, a food pantry and weekend meal program, adult education and benefits-navigation referrals for parents, and after-school programming run with two community partner organizations. Staffing — the three coordinators, six family outreach workers, and partner-agency contracts — consumes about 75 percent of the budget; the remainder covers program operations, transportation for after-school participants, and the food programs. The theory of the model is straightforward: chronic absenteeism among young children is overwhelmingly a family-circumstance problem, and a school that helps solve the circumstance recovers the attendance.

### What the Evaluation Found

Redbud is the proof of concept. Chronic absenteeism there fell from 29 percent to 23 percent, a six-point drop, against a district elementary trend that was essentially flat. The improvement was concentrated exactly where the model predicts: among the roughly 120 students the outreach team carried on its active caseload, absenteeism fell by more than eleven points, while students outside the caseload moved little. Redbud's outreach workers completed over 700 documented home visits; its coordinator has been in post since launch; its principal integrated the program team into the school's weekly attendance meetings from the first month. Teacher survey responses at Redbud on family engagement items moved from the district's bottom quartile to above the median. Nothing in the Redbud data suggests luck; everything suggests implementation.

Saluda Creek and Tanner's Mill delivered the same budget and the same nominal model with no measurable movement — chronic absenteeism at both sites shifted less than one point, within noise. The evaluation's site-level process data explain most of the gap. At Tanner's Mill, staff turnover ran 44 percent: the coordinator resigned in November, the position sat vacant for eleven weeks, and three of the site's outreach positions turned over at least once. A caseload model cannot survive that; families who had begun engaging with one worker were handed to another or to no one, and the home-visit count at Tanner's Mill was less than a third of Redbud's. Exit interviews point to a compensation problem — the outreach role is paid below what the two partner agencies pay for comparable work, and Tanner's Mill's departing staff largely moved to those agencies — and to a supervision gap after the coordinator left. At Saluda Creek, staffing held but integration did not: the program operated alongside the school rather than inside it, the principal treated the coordinator as a partner-agency guest rather than a cabinet member, and referrals from teachers to the outreach team ran at half Redbud's rate. Same money, same model on paper, different school-level ownership, different result.

The cost picture: at roughly $533,000 per site, Redbud's result prices out to a defensible figure given what chronic absenteeism costs the district in intervention, retention, and lost state funding tied to attendance. The same spend at the two flat sites bought infrastructure and activity but, so far, no outcome.

### What the District Should Do Before the Next Decision Points

The near-term deadlines here are the district's own, not the grantor's, and the office recommends the Board impose them. Before October 15: adjust the outreach worker salary band to market, which the office estimates costs $60,000–$75,000 annually across the program and is the cheapest fix in this entire report relative to the damage its absence caused. Before December 15: Tanner's Mill must have a permanent coordinator in post, its outreach positions filled, and a caseload rebuilt to at least 100 students, verified by this office; failure to meet the checkpoint should trigger consolidation of the Tanner's Mill budget into an expanded Redbud caseload or a fourth-site relocation. At Saluda Creek, the Superintendent should make the principal's ownership of the model an explicit expectation this fall — including the coordinator's seat in the school's leadership and attendance meetings — and the site should show at least a two-point absenteeism improvement by the spring 2026 review to hold its year-three funding. The office will report against both checkpoints.

### What Happens If the Program Stops

A full stop would be a serious error at Redbud and a tolerable one, on current evidence, at the other two sites — which is why the office recommends neither a full stop nor a full continuation. Ending Redbud's program would cut off active case management for about 120 families whose children's attendance recovered because of it; the research on attendance interventions is consistent that gains of this kind reverse when the support is withdrawn, and Redbud would likely give back most of its six points within two years. Nine program staff across the three sites would lose positions, and the two partner agencies — whose relationships with the district extend beyond this grant — would absorb contract losses that would complicate future partnerships. At Saluda Creek and Tanner's Mill, the honest accounting is that families have so far received services without the model's core outcome materializing; stopping there forfeits sunk investment and community goodwill but not, yet, demonstrated results. The corrective-plan approach preserves the option to concentrate the full $1.6 million where it demonstrably works, and the office regards protecting Redbud as the non-negotiable element of any path the Board chooses.

# Executive Summary 4 of 4

## One-to-One Device Refresh Program

**Program period evaluated:** Program inception through August 2025 | **Grant closeout report due:** Fall 2025
**Prepared by the Office of Research and Accountability | September 9, 2025**

### Finding and Recommendation

The one-to-one device refresh is a financial control failure first and a program problem second, and the Committee should read this summary in that order. The program was approved at $5.9 million and has spent $8.4 million — a $2.5 million overrun, 42 percent above the approved amount — with deployment finishing seven months behind schedule and a first-year device breakage rate of 19 percent, roughly two and a half times the 6-to-8-percent range that districts of comparable size report. Every student now has a device; the refresh itself is materially complete. The Office of Research and Accountability recommends that the district not unwind the deployment — clawing back devices from classrooms would compound the loss — but that the Board direct an independent review of grants management controls before the closeout report is filed, ratify or restructure the general fund exposure created by the overrun, and fund a breakage-mitigation program immediately, because at the current damage rate the district will face an unbudgeted seven-figure replacement cost within three years. The Committee should also understand the compliance dimension: expenditures charged beyond an approved grant amount are subject to grantor disallowance, and the closeout report cannot be filed until the district knows how much of the $8.4 million the grantor will actually bear.

### The Program and Its Funding

The device refresh, managed by Grants Manager Chandra Pillai, was approved at $5.9 million to replace the district's aging one-to-one fleet — student laptops and tablets across all 34 schools, along with charging infrastructure, device management licensing, and cases. Total expenditure to date is $8.4 million. The office's reconstruction of the overrun, from purchase orders and change documentation, attributes it to four compounding causes: a mid-procurement shift to a higher-specification device model that added roughly $900,000 without a corresponding budget amendment; unit price increases between the budget estimate and the executed purchase orders, worth approximately $600,000; accessory, licensing, and warranty purchases that were under-scoped in the original budget, roughly $550,000; and replacement purchases already made against year-one breakage, roughly $450,000. None of these individually is unusual in a large technology procurement. What is unusual — and what the office cannot explain from the documentation — is that the cumulative variance passed $1 million, then $2 million, without a budget amendment coming to the Board or, so far as the record shows, a written escalation to the Chief Financial Officer. The overrun was absorbed through the grants payables process and surfaced only during this evaluation's reconciliation.

### What the Evaluation Found

**On cost.** The $2.5 million gap between approved and actual spending is currently sitting against the district's accounts in a way that must be resolved before grant closeout. If the grantor holds the district to the approved $5.9 million — the standard posture — the general fund absorbs $2.5 million that was never appropriated for this purpose. CFO Lindqvist's office and this office agree on the reconciled figure; what remains open is the grantor's treatment of it, and the district should not assume flexibility it has not negotiated.

**On controls.** The documentation trail shows purchase orders approved within delegated authority individually while the aggregate ran far past the approved program total. That is a controls design problem as much as a personnel one: the district's grants management process tracks spending against individual purchase authorities but has no automated stop against the grant award ceiling. The office's recommendation for an independent review is directed at the process; determinations regarding individual accountability belong to the Superintendent and are outside this evaluation's scope. The office notes for completeness that the same controls gap exists for every other grant the district holds, including the three programs covered in the preceding summaries.

**On breakage.** Nineteen percent of deployed devices were broken, lost, or rendered unusable in year one. At fleet scale that is roughly one device in five, and at the fleet's average unit cost it implies an annual replacement exposure in the range of $1.1 to $1.3 million if nothing changes — none of it budgeted. The office examined the distribution: breakage is not uniform. It is concentrated in the schools that deployed earliest, before cases arrived (cases were among the under-scoped accessory purchases and lagged devices by up to four months at some sites), and among the youngest grade levels, where several comparable districts either use ruggedized devices or keep devices in classroom carts rather than sending them home. Roughly a third of the breakage, in other words, traces to sequencing decisions the district controlled.

**On the schedule.** Deployment finished seven months late. The instructional cost of the delay fell unevenly: schools at the end of the deployment sequence ran a full additional year on end-of-life devices, with the predictable effects on state testing logistics, curriculum platforms that the old devices ran poorly, and teacher planning built around technology that arrived mid-year. The delay also pushed a substantial share of spending into a later fiscal period than budgeted, which contributed to the overrun going unnoticed.

**On the program itself.** It bears stating plainly amid the findings above: the district's students now have current devices, teacher survey responses on device reliability and instructional use are the best they have been since one-to-one began, and the refresh's educational purpose has been substantially achieved. The failure here is in how the district bought and managed the program, not in whether students should have it.

### What the District Should Do Before Closeout

Four actions, in order. First, the Board should direct an independent review — external auditor or internal audit if the Board judges it sufficiently independent — of the grants management control environment, scoped to the device program's variance history and to the ceiling-tracking gap that affects all district grants, with findings due before the closeout report is filed. Second, CFO Lindqvist should open the closeout conversation with the grantor now, before filing, to establish what portion of the $8.4 million the grantor will recognize; the Board should then formally appropriate whatever general fund amount the district must absorb, rather than leaving a $2.5 million exposure unratified on the books. Third, the district should implement breakage mitigation this fall: complete the case deployment, adopt cart-based storage for the primary grades, institute a family device-care agreement with a modest repair-fee structure that includes a hardship waiver, and stand up a student-staffed repair program at the high schools, which comparable districts report cuts both cost and turnaround. The office estimates the mitigation package at under $200,000 against a seven-figure annual exposure. Fourth, the Board should adopt a standing requirement that any grant-funded program projecting a variance above 5 percent of its award returns to the Board for amendment — a rule that would have surfaced this problem two million dollars ago.

### What Happens If the Program Stops

"Stopping" means something different here than for the other three programs, because the money is spent and the devices are in students' hands. The office addresses the two forms a stop could take. If the district declined to fund ongoing breakage replacement and repair, the fleet would degrade at the observed rate: within two years, on current numbers, a third or more of students would lack a working device, with the shortfall concentrated — as breakage already is — in the earliest-deployed, highest-poverty schools, recreating the inequity the refresh was funded to eliminate and stranding the instructional and assessment systems the district has built on the assumption of one-to-one access. If the district instead attempted to recover cost by withdrawing devices from classrooms, it would forfeit the one thing the $8.4 million actually bought. Neither is a serious option, which is precisely why the office's recommendations focus on controls and sustainment rather than continuation-versus-termination. The staff consequence to plan for is different in kind from the other programs: the independent review may produce personnel findings, and the Superintendent should be prepared to act on them; separately, the district's technology support staffing was scoped for a lower breakage rate than the fleet is producing, and the mitigation plan above is what keeps that gap from becoming a budget request next spring.
