# Still

The word does two jobs and pretends they are one.

*The water is still.* Nothing moves. *The water is still there.* Time has moved, and the water has not left. The first is about motion; the second is about duration. The first is a photograph; the second is a photograph with a date on the back.

The dictionary says the older meaning is the quiet one. Old English *stille*: motionless, silent, calm. The other sense — *even now, yet, up to this moment* — arrived later, and it arrived by a route you can still trace if you look. Something that is without change is something that is continuous. Something continuous is something that goes on. Something that goes on is, at any given moment, *still* going on. The word for holding your body in place became the word for holding your place in time.

I think the language knew something.

---

Consider the sentence *I still love you*. Remove the adverb and you have a claim. Put it back and you have a claim plus a history of pressure. The *still* tells you there were forces — you don't know which ones, and the speaker is choosing not to say — and that the thing did not move. It is the same *still* as the pond's, really. A pond is still not because nothing touches it but because what touches it isn't enough.

Consider *still here*. Said in a hospital room, at three in the morning, to someone who has opened their eyes and isn't sure. It means *I haven't left*. But watch what the body is doing when it says this: it is sitting. It has been sitting for hours. The chair is bad. The not-leaving and the not-moving are, for the length of that night, the same act performed with the same muscles.

---

A *still life* is, in French, a *nature morte* — dead nature. The English chose differently. The fruit in the bowl is not dead; it is holding. Which is a kind of courtesy to the fruit, and also, I think, more accurate about what the painter was doing, which was asking the light to wait.

A *still* from a film is one frame lifted out of twenty-four per second and asked to stand on its own. It turns out it usually can. We are surprised by how much of a moving thing survives being stopped. The actress mid-turn, the car mid-skid — they don't look interrupted. They look like they were always going to be exactly there.

And the *still* in the shed behind the farmhouse, the copper one, comes from a different root entirely — Latin *stillare*, to drip — but I decline to let it off on a technicality. A drop forms at the lip of the coil and hangs. It is for one instant perfectly motionless and perfectly continuous, the whole process of distillation concentrated into a bead that has not yet decided to fall. Then it falls, and the next one is already forming, and the still is still running.

---

Here is what I think the word knows.

We talk about endurance as though it were a kind of motion — *pushing through, carrying on, keeping going*. The verbs are all locomotive. But the people who actually endure don't describe it that way. They describe sitting. They describe a chair, a window, a phone that did not ring. They describe the work of not leaving, which is not a journey but a refusal of one.

To remain, you hold. To hold, you remain. The word saw no reason to keep these apart, and after enough time with it, neither do I.

The light is still on. Be still. It's still you.
