## The Question and What We Found

At the April board meeting, member Lorraine Hovde asked whether the attendance mentor program works. Superintendent Blackwell directed this office to answer before the July vote on whether to absorb the twenty-two part-time mentors into local operating funds when the $1.9 million state grant expires in June 2027.

This brief answers a narrower question than the one asked, and the narrowing matters. We can say what happened to the attendance of students who received mentors, and we can compare it to what happened to students who met the same eligibility criteria but were never assigned. We cannot say with confidence that the difference between those two numbers is the effect of mentoring, because the two groups were not formed in a way that makes them comparable, and because a coding change in the middle of the study period moved the measurement itself. What follows is an honest accounting of what the data support and where they run out.

**The headline numbers.** Across two school years, 2024-25 and 2025-26, 612 mentored ninth graders improved their attendance rate by an average of 4.3 percentage points relative to their prior year. A comparison group of 1,180 students who met the same eligibility criteria but were never assigned a mentor improved by 2.6 points. The raw difference is 1.7 percentage points in favor of mentored students.

**Why that difference is not the program effect.** Eligibility was defined by prior-year absence: a student qualified by having been chronically absent the year before. Both groups were therefore selected at their worst attendance year. Attendance rates that are unusually low in one year tend to rise in the next even without intervention, a pattern called regression to the mean, and it inflates the improvement figure for both groups. This affects both groups similarly and so does not by itself explain the 1.7-point gap. What does threaten the gap is that mentors were not assigned at random. Coordinators and school staff chose which eligible students to serve, and if they chose students they judged more reachable, the 1.7 points reflects that judgment as much as the mentoring.

**Where the gain concentrates.** The overall average conceals a strong pattern. Students whose prior-year absence rate fell between 10 and 19 percent improved 6.2 points. Students whose prior-year absence exceeded 30 percent improved 1.1 points. The program's measured benefit is concentrated among students who were closest to the chronic-absence threshold to begin with, and is close to undetectable among the most severely absent.

**The coding change.** In August 2025 the district changed its period-attendance coding rule. The change raised measured attendance systemwide by approximately 1.5 percentage points. Because it applies to the second of the two study years, it inflates year-over-year improvement figures for every student in the study, mentored and comparison alike. Our estimates adjust for this where possible, and we flag throughout where they do not.

**Other findings.** English learners, who make up 18 percent of the mentored group, showed no measurable difference from comparison students. One high school did not assign mentors until November, effectively shortening the treatment period by a quarter. Ninety-four students changed schools mid-year, complicating both attendance measurement and mentor continuity.

**What this means for July.** The evidence is consistent with a program that helps a specific subgroup of students meaningfully and does little for the students furthest from regular attendance. It is not consistent with a claim that the program raises attendance by 1.7 points across the eligible population. If the board's goal is to reach the most chronically absent students, the current design is not doing that. If the goal is to keep moderately absent students from sliding further, there is a defensible case, though not a conclusive one. The final section lays out what a board could reasonably decide on this evidence and what a stronger evaluation would require.

## Data and Methods

### The Program

In 2024 the district hired 22 part-time attendance mentors under a $1.9 million state grant with a term ending June 2027. Mentors were assigned to ninth graders at four high schools who had been chronically absent in the prior school year. Maryland defines chronic absenteeism as missing ten percent or more of enrolled school days, and the district applied that definition in setting eligibility.

Mentors work part-time schedules, typically twenty hours per week, and carry caseloads that varied over the two years from roughly eighteen to thirty-four students. The intended model involves weekly contact with the student, periodic contact with a caregiver, and coordination with school-based attendance staff. The program did not maintain a contact log in a form that permitted us to measure dosage — how many contacts a given student actually received. This is a significant gap and we return to it.

### The Sample

The study population is drawn from the student information system for the 2024-25 and 2025-26 school years. It comprises:

- **612 mentored students**, identified from program rosters supplied by the mentor coordinator and matched to the student information system by state student identifier.
- **1,180 comparison students** who met the same eligibility criteria — ninth grade, enrolled at one of the four program high schools, chronically absent in the prior year — but who were never assigned to a mentor at any point during the study period.

The comparison group is roughly twice the size of the mentored group because the mentors' combined capacity was well below the eligible population. In 2024-25, approximately 1,020 students met eligibility criteria at the four schools and mentors served 331. In 2025-26 the eligible pool was approximately 772 and mentors served 281. The program never had the capacity to serve everyone who qualified, and the question of how the served were selected from the eligible is the central methodological problem in this study.

Fifty-one students appear in both years — that is, they were ninth graders in 2024-25, were retained or re-enrolled in ninth grade in 2025-26, and remained eligible. We treat each student-year as an observation and note where this affects results.

### The Attendance Measure

The outcome is the student's annual attendance rate: days present divided by days enrolled, expressed as a percentage. Improvement is the current-year rate minus the prior-year rate, in percentage points. A student who attended 82 percent of days in 2023-24 and 88 percent in 2024-25 improved by 6.0 points.

Attendance records were verified by the director of student services, who reconfirmed enrollment spans and reconciled the small number of records where enrolled-days totals differed between the school-level and district-level extracts. Forty-one records were corrected in this process, none in a direction that materially changes the results reported here.

Two features of the measure deserve attention.

**Enrollment denominators.** Students who enroll late or withdraw early have shorter denominators, which makes their rates more volatile. A student enrolled 40 days who misses 8 has the same rate as a student enrolled 180 days who misses 36, but the first figure is far less stable. We excluded students enrolled fewer than 45 days in either the baseline or the outcome year, which removed 63 student-years from the analysis population before the counts reported above.

**Period versus day attendance.** High schools in this district record attendance by class period. The daily attendance figure is derived from period records through a coding rule. That rule changed.

### The August 2025 Coding Change

In August 2025 the district revised the rule by which period-level attendance is aggregated into a daily attendance determination. Under the prior rule, a student marked absent in a majority of scheduled periods was recorded as absent for the day, and partial-day absences below that threshold were recorded as present with a period-absence notation that did not affect the daily rate. The revised rule introduced a half-day category and altered the treatment of certain excused period absences, most consequentially those associated with school-sponsored activities and documented medical appointments, which are now recorded as present for daily-attendance purposes in circumstances where they previously were not.

The net effect was to raise measured daily attendance across the district. Our estimate of the magnitude is approximately 1.5 percentage points systemwide. We derived this by taking a stratified sample of 2,400 student-days from spring 2025 — before the change — and recoding them under both the old and new rules, then scaling. The estimate carries uncertainty of roughly plus or minus 0.4 points and the effect is not uniform: it is larger at schools with more period-based scheduling variation and larger for students with more excused absences, which correlates with family resources and with health status.

For this study the change is a serious complication. The 2025-26 outcome year is coded under the new rule; the 2024-25 baseline for those students is coded under the old rule. Every year-over-year improvement figure calculated across that boundary is inflated by roughly 1.5 points of pure measurement change.

We handle this three ways, and readers should know which is in play at each point:

1. **Unadjusted figures** are raw and include the coding inflation. We label these clearly.
2. **Adjusted figures** subtract 1.5 points from the improvement of any student whose outcome year is 2025-26. We use adjusted figures for all headline numbers unless stated otherwise.
3. **Within-cohort comparisons** compare mentored to comparison students within the same year, where both are coded identically and the change cancels. These are the most trustworthy comparisons in the brief and we rely on them most heavily.

The 4.3 and 2.6 point figures cited above are adjusted. The 1.7-point difference between them is a within-cohort comparison pooled across two years and is unaffected by the coding change in either direction, because the change applies equally to both groups.

### How the Comparison Group Was Formed, and What That Does to Interpretation

This is the section the board should read most carefully.

The comparison group was not created by design. It is the residual — the eligible students left over after mentors filled their caseloads. Nobody drew names from a hat. Assignment happened through a process that the program describes as a combination of counselor referral, attendance-team review, and mentor availability at the student's home school.

We interviewed the mentor coordinator and reviewed the assignment records that exist. The picture that emerges is that assignment was driven by several factors, in rough order of importance:

- **School and schedule fit.** Mentors are part-time and assigned to specific buildings. A student at a school with a fuller mentor complement was more likely to be assigned than an otherwise identical student elsewhere.
- **Counselor and attendance-team judgment.** School staff nominated students. The stated criterion was students who would benefit, which in practice appears to have meant students staff believed were reachable — those whose absence seemed situational rather than entrenched, and whose families responded to contact.
- **Caregiver consent and responsiveness.** The program required caregiver notification, and a caregiver who could not be reached could not be enrolled. Unreachable caregivers are not randomly distributed.
- **Timing of eligibility identification.** Students identified early in the fall were more likely to be assigned than students identified after caseloads filled.

Each of these has a plausible relationship with the outcome. If staff nominated students they thought were reachable, and reachability predicts attendance improvement, then mentored students would have improved more than comparison students even if the mentors had done nothing at all. This is selection bias, and it points in the direction of overstating the program's effect.

We can demonstrate that the groups differ at baseline on observable characteristics. Mentored students entered the program with a mean prior-year absence rate of 21.4 percent. Comparison students had a mean prior-year absence rate of 26.8 percent. That is a substantial difference — mentored students were, on average, less absent to begin with, and therefore closer to the range where improvement is easier and regression to the mean is more favorable. Mentored students were also more likely to have a caregiver contact record marked current in the student information system, 84 percent versus 71 percent, which is a direct signature of the consent requirement.

We took two steps to reduce this problem, neither of which solves it.

**Stratification by prior absence.** We report results within bands of prior-year absence rate, so that mentored and comparison students are compared to others who entered at similar attendance levels. This is the single most important adjustment in the brief and it substantially narrows the apparent program effect.

**Covariate adjustment.** We fit a regression of attendance improvement on mentored status, controlling for prior-year absence rate (as a continuous term and in bands), school, year, English learner status, special education status, free and reduced-price meal eligibility, and mid-year mobility. The adjusted estimate of the mentored-comparison difference is 1.2 percentage points, down from the raw 1.7.

What neither step can do is control for the thing that actually drove assignment: staff judgment about who was reachable. That judgment is not recorded in any database. It is precisely the kind of unmeasured variable that regression cannot adjust for, and it is very likely correlated with the outcome. Our best professional assessment is that the true program effect is smaller than 1.2 points and could plausibly be zero for the population as a whole, though the subgroup findings below suggest it is not zero everywhere.

**A note on what would have been better.** Because the program had capacity for roughly a third of eligible students, a lottery among eligible students would have been both fair and analytically clean, and would have cost nothing. Had mentors been assigned by lottery in 2024, this brief could have given the board a defensible causal estimate. It cannot. We raise this not to relitigate a decision made two years ago but because the same choice is available for any continuation, and we recommend it in the final section.

### Regression to the Mean

One further methodological point bears on how to read every improvement figure in this brief.

Eligibility required chronic absence in the prior year. Attendance in any single year reflects both a student's stable tendency and year-specific circumstances — an illness, a family disruption, a transportation failure, a bad semester. Students selected for having an unusually bad year include many whose bad year was partly circumstantial. In the following year, absent any intervention, circumstances revert toward typical and measured attendance rises.

This is not a small effect. Using district attendance data from years before the program existed, we tracked students who were chronically absent in a given ninth-grade year and observed what happened the following year with no intervention of any kind. Those students improved an average of 2.9 percentage points. That figure is a reasonable benchmark for what selection-driven reversion looks like in this population, and it sits very close to the 2.6-point improvement observed in the comparison group.

The implication is direct: **most of the improvement observed in both groups is not attributable to anything the district did.** It is the arithmetic consequence of selecting students at their worst. The comparison group exists precisely to net this out, which is why the 1.7-point difference — not the 4.3-point improvement — is the figure that speaks to program effect, and why even that figure is compromised by the selection problem described above.

We stress this because the 4.3-point figure is the one most likely to be repeated outside this room, and it does not mean what it appears to mean.

## Results

### Overall

Across both study years, adjusted for the coding change:

| Group | N (student-years) | Mean prior-year absence rate | Mean improvement in attendance rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mentored | 612 | 21.4% | +4.3 points |
| Comparison | 1,180 | 26.8% | +2.6 points |
| Difference | | | +1.7 points |

After regression adjustment for prior absence, school, year, and student characteristics, the difference narrows to **1.2 percentage points** with a 95 percent confidence interval running from 0.1 to 2.3 points. The interval excludes zero, but barely, and the confidence interval reflects only sampling uncertainty. It does not reflect the selection bias described above, which is the larger source of error and which cannot be expressed as an interval.

Translated into days: 1.2 percentage points of a 180-day year is approximately 2.2 additional days of attendance per student per year.

**Figure 1 (described).** A scatter plot would place prior-year absence rate on the horizontal axis and attendance improvement on the vertical, with mentored students in one color and comparison students in another. The dominant visual feature is not the separation between colors, which is slight, but the strong downward slope of both clouds: students entering with higher prior absence show less improvement regardless of group. A second feature is the difference in horizontal distribution — the mentored cloud is shifted left, concentrated in the 10-to-25 percent range, while the comparison cloud extends further right into the 30-plus range. The visual makes the selection problem immediately apparent: the two groups do not occupy the same region of the plot.

### By Prior Absence Level

This is the most important result in the brief.

| Prior-year absence rate | Mentored N | Mentored improvement | Comparison N | Comparison improvement | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10–19% | 268 | +6.2 points | 372 | +3.8 points | +2.4 |
| 20–29% | 211 | +4.1 points | 393 | +2.9 points | +1.2 |
| 30% and above | 133 | +1.1 points | 415 | +0.9 points | +0.2 |

Three things follow.

**First, the program's measured benefit is concentrated in the least-absent eligible band.** Students entering with absence rates between 10 and 19 percent — that is, students who had just crossed the chronic-absence threshold — improved 6.2 points with a mentor and 3.8 without, a difference of 2.4 points. This is roughly twice the overall average difference and is the only band where the gap is large enough to be of practical interest.

**Second, the program shows essentially nothing for the most severely absent.** Students above 30 percent absence improved 1.1 points with a mentor and 0.9 points without. A 0.2-point difference on a base of 133 mentored students is indistinguishable from noise. These are students missing more than one day in three. Weekly contact from a part-time mentor is not moving them.

**Third, the composition effect explains much of the headline number.** Because mentored students are disproportionately drawn from the 10-to-19 percent band — 44 percent of mentored students versus 32 percent of comparison students — the overall 1.7-point gap partly reflects the mentored group's more favorable composition rather than any differential effect of mentoring. The within-band differences (2.4, 1.2, 0.2) weighted by the comparison group's composition rather than the mentored group's would yield an overall difference of approximately 1.1 points, close to the regression-adjusted estimate.

We add a caution about the highest band. There is a floor effect at work: a student missing 35 percent of days has more room to improve than a student missing 12 percent, so in principle the high-absence band should show *more* improvement, not less. That it shows less indicates that whatever drives severe chronic absence is not responsive to this intervention, and the finding is therefore stronger than the raw numbers suggest rather than weaker.

**Figure 2 (described).** A grouped bar chart with three clusters, one per absence band, each containing a mentored bar and a comparison bar. The visual signature is a staircase descending from left to right in both series, with the gap between the paired bars visibly widest in the leftmost cluster and nearly closed in the rightmost. The chart makes the point more forcefully than the table: whatever the program does, it does it at the shallow end.

### By School

Mentors operate at four high schools. We refer to them here as Schools A through D, consistent with the naming convention used in the appendix tables provided separately to the board.

| School | Mentored N | Mentored improvement | Comparison improvement | Difference | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 189 | +5.1 | +2.8 | +2.3 | Full-year implementation both years |
| B | 164 | +4.6 | +2.5 | +2.1 | Full-year implementation both years |
| C | 141 | +4.4 | +3.0 | +1.4 | Highest baseline absence; largest EL share |
| D | 118 | +2.1 | +2.2 | −0.1 | Mentors not assigned until November 2024 |

**School D is the informative case.** Mentors were not assigned there until November in the first program year, which removed roughly a quarter of the school year from the treatment period and, more importantly, removed the fall — the period when ninth-grade attendance patterns set. School D shows no advantage for mentored students at all; the point estimate is very slightly negative and well within noise.

We are cautious about reading this as evidence that early start matters, because School D differs from the others in ways beyond timing. But the direction is consistent with what the program's own staff report, and with a broader literature on the importance of the first weeks of ninth grade. If the board reads School D as a natural experiment in late implementation, it reads as a caution against it.

Schools A and B, both of which implemented on schedule in both years, show the largest differences at 2.3 and 2.1 points. School C sits between, at 1.4 points, and has both the highest baseline absence rate and the largest English learner population — two factors that the subgroup results below suggest work against measured effect.

Excluding School D from the pooled analysis raises the overall adjusted difference from 1.2 to 1.6 percentage points. We do not recommend excluding it; we report the figure so the board can see how much of the pooled estimate hinges on one school's implementation problem.

**Figure 3 (described).** Four small panels, one per school, each plotting the distribution of attendance improvement for mentored and comparison students as overlapping density curves. In Schools A and B the mentored curve sits visibly to the right of the comparison curve. In School C the curves substantially overlap with a modest rightward shift. In School D the two curves are nearly indistinguishable. The panels also show that the mentored distributions are not simply shifted — they have thicker right tails, meaning the mentored advantage comes partly from a subset of students who improved a great deal rather than from a uniform lift across all mentored students.

That last observation deserves a sentence of its own. In Schools A and B, roughly 18 percent of mentored students improved by more than 12 percentage points, compared to 9 percent of comparison students. The mentored median improvement, by contrast, exceeds the comparison median by less than a point. **The program's effect, where it exists, appears to run through a minority of students who respond strongly rather than through a small gain for everyone.** This is worth knowing because it implies that identifying who responds is more valuable than expanding the caseload.

### By Student Group

**English learners.** English learners constitute 18 percent of the mentored group, 110 students. Their mean improvement was 3.9 points against 3.7 for English-learner comparison students — a difference of 0.2 points, which is not measurable. Non-EL mentored students showed a difference of 1.5 points over non-EL comparison students.

We do not know why. The mentor corps includes four Spanish-speaking mentors and the program relies on district interpretation services for other languages. The four largest home-language groups in the EL population at these schools are Spanish, Haitian Creole, Dari, and Amharic, and the mentor corps has native capacity in only the first. The program's caregiver-contact component, which requires sustained conversation rather than a single translated message, is the piece most likely to break down without shared language. This is a hypothesis, not a finding, and it is testable — the program could record the language of caregiver contacts and whether interpretation was used.

**Special education.** Students with disabilities are 21 percent of the mentored group, 129 students. Their difference from comparison peers is 0.9 points, somewhat below the non-disabled difference of 1.4. The gap is not large and the subgroup is not big enough to say much. We note that attendance for students with disabilities is more often driven by health conditions and by transportation arrangements, neither of which a mentor is positioned to change.

**Free and reduced-price meal eligibility.** No meaningful difference in program effect by meal eligibility. Both eligible and non-eligible mentored students show differences near the overall 1.2-point figure. Meal eligibility does predict baseline absence strongly, but it does not appear to predict responsiveness to mentoring.

**Mid-year mobility.** Ninety-four students in the study population changed schools mid-year. Their attendance data are split across enrollments and their improvement figures are correspondingly less reliable. Among the 31 mobile students who were mentored, mentoring did not survive the move in most cases — mentors are assigned to buildings, and a student transferring to a non-program school lost the service entirely. Mobile mentored students improved 1.4 points against 1.6 for mobile comparison students, effectively no difference. Excluding all mobile students from the analysis changes the overall difference from 1.2 to 1.3 points, so mobility does not drive the headline result, but it does identify a group the program structurally cannot serve.

**Gender.** Female mentored students showed a 1.5-point difference, male mentored students 1.0. The gap is within noise given subgroup sizes.

### Attendance Level Reached, Not Just Change

The board may find one additional framing useful. Improvement in percentage points is the natural measure of program effect, but the operationally meaningful question is how many students got out of chronic absenteeism altogether.

Of 612 mentored students, 174 — 28 percent — ended the year below the 10 percent absence threshold and therefore no longer chronically absent. Of 1,180 comparison students, 254 — 22 percent — did the same. The six-point difference in exit rate is consistent with the improvement findings and, like them, is concentrated: among students entering in the 10-to-19 percent band, 47 percent of mentored students exited chronic absence against 34 percent of comparison students. Among students entering above 30 percent, exit rates were 3 percent and 3 percent respectively.

Four mentored students above 30 percent prior absence ended the year below 10 percent. Four out of 133.

## Limitations

We have described the two largest limitations in the methods section and summarize them here alongside the others, in rough order of severity.

**1. The comparison group is not a valid counterfactual.** Assignment to mentoring was made by school staff using unrecorded judgment about which students would benefit. That judgment almost certainly correlates with the outcome. Every estimate in this brief that compares mentored to comparison students is potentially biased upward, and the magnitude of the bias cannot be determined from the data available. Statistical adjustment for observed characteristics reduces but does not eliminate the problem. This is the reason the brief does not state a program effect as a fact.

**2. The coding change contaminates year-over-year comparisons.** Our 1.5-point correction is an estimate with its own uncertainty and it is not uniform across schools or student groups. Where we rely on within-year mentored-versus-comparison comparisons, the change cancels and this limitation does not apply. Where we report improvement over the prior year, it does. Readers encountering any single number from this brief out of context should ask which kind it is.

**3. Regression to the mean inflates all improvement figures.** Roughly three points of the observed improvement in both groups is attributable to the selection of students at their attendance nadir. This is why comparison-group contrasts, not raw improvement, carry the analytic weight.

**4. We cannot measure dosage.** The program did not maintain contact logs in an analyzable form. We do not know how many times a mentored student was actually contacted, how long the relationship lasted, or whether it was substantive. A student assigned a mentor in September who met with that mentor twice and a student who met weekly for thirty weeks are identical in this analysis. This is the single most valuable piece of missing information, and its absence means we cannot distinguish between "mentoring doesn't work" and "the students who got real mentoring improved and they were diluted by students who got a name on a roster." Given the finding that effects run through a strong-responding minority, this distinction is not academic.

**5. Two years is short and the second year is unusual.** The 2024-25 cohort was the program's first, with mentors new to their roles. The 2025-26 cohort operated under the new coding rule. Neither year is a clean picture of a mature program in a stable measurement environment.

**6. Subgroup sizes limit what can be said.** The English learner finding rests on 110 mentored students, the special education finding on 129, and the highest-absence-band finding on 133. These are large enough to establish that no large effect is present and too small to rule out a modest one.

**7. Attendance is not the ultimate outcome.** The board asked about attendance and this brief answers about attendance. Whether the program affects course passage, ninth-grade-on-track status, credit accumulation, or discipline is not examined here. Attendance is a reasonable proxy for engagement but the program's value should ultimately be judged on whether students stay on track to graduate. We have the data to examine this and did not, given the July deadline.

**8. No cost-effectiveness analysis.** We report effects, not effects per dollar, and we have not compared this program to alternative uses of the same funds.

**9. Students in both years are counted twice.** Fifty-one students appear as two student-year observations. Standard errors are modestly understated as a result. The effect on point estimates is negligible.

**10. We did not interview students, families, or mentors.** This brief is an analysis of administrative records. It can tell the board what the numbers show and cannot tell it why. The English learner finding in particular calls for qualitative inquiry that we did not conduct.

## Implications for the July Decision

The board is deciding whether to absorb 22 part-time mentor positions into local funds when the state grant expires in June 2027. The decision has roughly a year of runway before the grant ends, which is more room than it may appear.

We set out what the evidence supports, what it does not, and four options with their evidentiary basis.

### What the Evidence Supports

The program is associated with an attendance improvement of somewhere between zero and 1.7 percentage points across the eligible population, with a best estimate near 1.2 points and substantial risk that the true figure is lower because of selection.

The association is meaningfully larger — around 2.4 points — for students entering with absence rates between 10 and 19 percent, and is indistinguishable from zero for students entering above 30 percent.

The association is larger at schools that implemented on time and absent at the school that started in November.

The association is absent for English learners, who are 18 percent of the served population.

The program does not currently reach students who change schools mid-year.

The pattern of effects suggests a responding minority rather than a uniform lift.

### What the Evidence Does Not Support

It does not support a claim that the program causes a 4.3-point attendance improvement. That figure includes roughly three points of regression to the mean and, for the second cohort, coding-change inflation.

It does not support a claim that the program is ineffective. The subgroup pattern is coherent and in the direction one would expect if the program works for students whose absence is situational, and a null program would not typically produce that pattern.

It does not support a claim that continuing the program at its current scale and design will produce the same results. Nothing here speaks to what happens if caseloads change, mentors turn over, or the eligible population shifts.

### Four Options

**Option 1: Absorb all 22 positions as currently configured.** The evidence for this is the weakest of the four. It commits local funds to a design that demonstrably does not serve the students furthest from attendance and does not serve English learners. It would be a decision made on the strength of a 1.2-point estimate that this office cannot defend as causal.

**Option 2: Decline to absorb; let the program end in June 2027.** Defensible on the evidence in the sense that the evidence does not establish effectiveness. But it treats an unproven program as a disproven one, which the findings do not warrant, and it forecloses the subgroup where effects are largest. It also discards two years of accumulated relationships and mentor experience.

**Option 3: Absorb a reduced complement, targeted to where effects concentrate.** The evidence points toward students in the 10-to-19 percent absence band at schools with full-year implementation. A reduced complement — the analysis suggests 12 to 14 positions would cover the 10-to-19 percent band at all four schools at current caseload ratios — would concentrate resources where measured effect is largest. This is the option best supported by what we found, with the caveat that it accepts, as policy, that the district's most severely absent students are not served by this program. The board should make that choice explicitly if it makes it, and should pair it with a plan for those students rather than leaving them unaddressed.

**Option 4: Absorb with a one-year randomized continuation.** The program has capacity for roughly a third of eligible students. If continuation assignments were made by lottery among eligible students, with the unassigned forming the comparison group, this office could deliver a defensible causal estimate to the board by June 2027 — before any permanent commitment. A lottery is arguably fairer than the current process, which advantages students whose caregivers are reachable and whose counselors nominate them. It costs nothing beyond the administrative effort of running it. Combined with contact logging, it would convert next year's brief from a hedged association into an answer.

We note that Options 3 and 4 are compatible and that combining them — a reduced, targeted complement assigned by lottery within the target band — is the design this office would recommend if asked. We were asked whether the program works, and our answer is that the district has spent $1.9 million over three years and cannot say. That is a fixable condition.

### Four Changes Worth Making Regardless

Whatever the funding decision, four operational changes would improve both the program and the district's ability to evaluate it. None requires additional funding.

**Log contacts.** Date, duration, mode, and participant, entered in a system this office can query. Without dosage data, no future evaluation can distinguish a program that does not work from a program that is not being delivered.

**Start in August.** School D's November start coincided with the absence of any measurable effect at that school. Ninth-grade attendance patterns form in the first weeks. Assignment should be complete before the first day.

**Address the English learner gap.** Eighteen percent of served students show no measurable benefit. This warrants inquiry before it warrants a solution — interviews with EL families and with the mentors serving them would establish whether the barrier is language, cultural fit, the nature of the absence, or something else. The program should not expand its EL caseload until it knows.

**Follow students who move.** Ninety-four mobile students, and mentoring that ends at the building line. Mentors could retain caseloads across in-district transfers at modest scheduling cost.

### A Closing Note on the Number

The figure most likely to travel outside this document is 4.3 percentage points. It is accurate as a description of what happened to mentored students. It is not a measure of what the program did. Roughly three points of it would have occurred with no program at all, and for the second cohort a further point and a half reflects a change in how attendance is counted rather than a change in whether students came to school.

The number that speaks to program effect is 1.2 points, it carries a real risk of being smaller, and it is not distributed evenly across the students the program was built to serve. The board asked a fair question. This is the most honest answer the available data permit, and the recommendation this office would emphasize is that a decision made in July 2026 need not be the last word — a year remains in which the district could learn what it has so far declined to measure.
