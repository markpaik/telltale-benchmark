## Performance Audit: In-House Street Resurfacing Program, Department of Public Works

**Audit period:** July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025 (Fiscal Year 2025)
**Report issued:** October 6, 2025

### Finding

The in-house street resurfacing program, returned to city operation in 2023 under Public Works Director Guadalupe Renteria, exceeded its production target, delivered work at a substantially lower unit cost than the prior contracted arrangement, and coincided with a marked decline in pavement-related complaints. The program completed 128 lane-miles against a 90 lane-mile annual plan — 142 percent of target — at an average cost of $61,000 per lane-mile, compared with $84,000 per lane-mile under the terminated contract, a unit-cost reduction of 27 percent. Pavement complaints logged through the city's service request system fell 44 percent over the audit period. This office finds that the decision to bring resurfacing in house has, through two full construction seasons, produced measurable gains in output, cost efficiency, and resident-reported street condition.

### Recommendation

The City Auditor recommends that the Budget and Finance Committee treat the current program structure as the baseline for fiscal 2026 budgeting and consider a measured expansion of the annual lane-mile plan, funded in part by the demonstrated unit-cost savings, rather than a return to contracted delivery. The committee should direct Public Works to submit, before budget adoption, a capital equipment replacement schedule and a workforce succession plan, because the audit identified equipment age and crew staffing depth as the two principal risks to sustaining current performance. The committee should also require that Public Works formalize its unit-cost accounting methodology in written procedure so that the year-over-year comparisons this office has relied upon remain auditable as the program grows.

### Evidence

The evidentiary basis for this finding rests on three sources: the department's project-level cost records, independent verification of completed lane-miles, and complaint data drawn from the citywide service request system.

On cost, this office reconstructed the fully loaded cost per lane-mile for the fiscal 2025 season, including labor, materials, equipment depreciation and fuel, and an allocated share of departmental overhead. The resulting figure of $61,000 per lane-mile is a conservative, all-in number; it is not a marginal cost that excludes fixed expenses. The comparison figure of $84,000 per lane-mile under the prior contract is drawn from invoiced amounts in the final two contract years, adjusted to current dollars. Auditors tested a sample of twenty-two resurfacing projects, tracing material invoices, crew timesheets, and equipment logs to the department's cost ledger, and found the ledger accurate in all sampled cases. The savings are real, and they are not the product of deferred maintenance on equipment or of scope reductions in the work itself: core thicknesses and material specifications on sampled projects matched or exceeded those in the prior contract's technical requirements, a point auditors confirmed through field measurement on a subsample of eight completed segments.

On output, the department reported 128 lane-miles completed against a plan of 90. Auditors verified the reported total by comparing the department's completion log against work orders, GIS pavement records, and physical inspection of a random sample of segments. No overstatement was found. The overrun against plan was achieved primarily through longer effective crew seasons — the in-house crews began work roughly five weeks earlier in the spring than the contractor historically mobilized — and through the department's ability to redirect crews to nearby segments when a planned project was delayed, flexibility the contract structure did not afford.

On outcomes, pavement complaints fell from 2,340 in fiscal 2024 to 1,310 in fiscal 2025, a 44 percent decline. Auditors caution, as we do in the full report, that complaint volume is an imperfect proxy for pavement condition, and that some of the decline may reflect weather or reporting behavior. Nonetheless, the decline was concentrated in corridors where resurfacing occurred, which supports attributing a substantial share of it to the program.

The audit also identified risks. The paving train's principal equipment is aging, and a failure of the primary paver mid-season would idle crews and erase much of the cost advantage. Three of the program's senior crew members are eligible for retirement within two years, and the department has no documented plan for transferring their expertise. Finally, the department's cost accounting, while accurate, exists largely in spreadsheets maintained by one analyst; this is a single point of failure for the very data that makes the program's performance demonstrable.

### Management Response

Director Renteria concurred with the finding and with all three recommendations. Management stated that a capital replacement schedule for the paving fleet will be submitted with the department's fiscal 2026 budget request, that a crew apprenticeship and cross-training plan will be in place by January 31, 2026, and that the cost accounting methodology will be documented as a standing departmental procedure by December 31, 2025. Management noted, and this office acknowledges, that any expansion of the lane-mile plan will require lead time for equipment procurement and hiring, and cautioned the committee against assuming that the current unit cost will hold precisely at a larger scale. The full management response is appended to the complete report.

# Executive Summary No. 2 of 4

## Performance Audit: Downtown Homeless Outreach Contract

**Audit period:** January 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025
**Report issued:** October 8, 2025

### Finding

The city's downtown outreach program, delivered by a contracted vendor under a $2.8 million agreement, has fallen far short of its central contractual outcome and has ceased complying with its own reporting obligations. Over the audit period the vendor logged approximately 1,900 street contacts but placed 47 individuals into housing against a contractual target of 300 — roughly 16 percent of the committed outcome. Compounding the performance shortfall, the vendor has not filed the quarterly outcome reports required by its contract since December 2024, meaning the city has operated for three consecutive quarters without the data the contract designates as the basis for monitoring performance and authorizing payment. This office finds that the program is not delivering the outcomes the city purchased, that the contract's monitoring provisions have not been enforced, and that the city cannot currently demonstrate what its $2.8 million has bought beyond a count of street contacts whose quality and disposition it cannot verify.

### Recommendation

The City Auditor recommends that the committee condition any fiscal 2026 appropriation for this program on four actions, and this is one of the few places in this cycle where an enumerated list is warranted, because each item is a distinct precondition:

1. **Immediate enforcement of the reporting requirement.** The city should invoke the contract's remedies for non-reporting, including withholding of payment as the contract permits, until the vendor files all delinquent quarterly reports and they are verified.
2. **A cure period with a defined performance floor.** If the city elects to continue the contract, it should issue a formal notice of deficiency establishing a cure period of no more than 120 days with a specific, verifiable housing placement threshold; failure to meet it should trigger termination.
3. **Restructuring of any successor agreement around verified outcomes.** Future payment should be tied substantially to independently confirmable housing placements and retention at six months, not to activity counts such as street contacts.
4. **Assignment of contract monitoring to a named position with documented responsibility.** The audit found that no single city employee held clear, documented responsibility for enforcing this contract's terms, a gap the administering department must close regardless of the vendor's fate.

### Evidence

The gap between activity and outcome is the core of this finding. The vendor's 1,900 logged street contacts represent, on their face, a substantial field presence. But a contact is an input, not a result, and the audit found the contact data itself difficult to verify: the vendor's logs do not consistently distinguish unique individuals from repeat encounters, so this office cannot determine how many distinct people the program actually reached. Auditors' best estimate, based on a sample of log entries containing sufficient identifying detail, is that repeat contacts constitute a large share of the total, though the records do not permit a precise figure — itself a finding about the state of the program's data.

The housing placement figure of 47 is better documented, because placements generate corroborating records with housing providers, and auditors were able to independently confirm 43 of the 47 claimed placements through provider records; the remaining four could not be confirmed or disproven. Even taking all 47 at face value, the program achieved 16 percent of its contracted target at, by the end of the audit period, essentially full expenditure of the contract amount. Expressed as a unit cost, the city has spent roughly $59,600 per confirmed housing placement, a figure the committee may compare against regional benchmarks in the full report, which generally fall well below half that amount for comparable outreach-to-housing models.

The reporting failure is independently serious. The contract requires quarterly outcome reports as a condition of payment. The last report was filed in December 2024, covering the quarter ending that month. Payments nonetheless continued through the audit period. Auditors reviewed the payment file and found no documented waiver, no deficiency notice, and no correspondence demanding the missing reports until this audit began, at which point the administering department made an initial inquiry to the vendor. The failure is therefore twofold: the vendor breached, and the city did not notice or did not act. Interviews established that responsibility for monitoring the contract was informally shared between two divisions after a staff departure in early 2025, and that neither believed it held the enforcement role.

This office does not find that the outreach workers in the field are idle or that the underlying need has diminished; field observation during the audit confirmed active street presence. The finding is narrower and more consequential: the city structured this contract around a housing outcome, the outcome has not materialized at anything near the contracted level, and the mechanisms designed to detect and correct that shortfall were allowed to lapse.

### Management Response

The administering department partially concurred. Management agreed that the reporting lapse is unacceptable, stated that a formal deficiency notice was issued to the vendor on September 29, 2025, and committed to designating a named contract monitor by November 15, 2025. Management disputed the characterization of the placement target, arguing that the 300-placement figure was set before regional housing supply tightened and is no longer attainable by any vendor, and asked the committee to weigh the value of street contacts and service referrals not captured in placement counts. This office's rejoinder, set out in full in the report, is that if the target was unattainable, the contractually proper course was a documented amendment, not silent underperformance — and that the absence of reporting deprived the city of exactly the information needed to have that conversation. The vendor was offered the opportunity to respond and submitted a letter, appended to the full report, attributing the reporting gap to staff turnover and committing to file all delinquent reports by November 30, 2025.

## Performance Audit: Curbside Composting Pilot Program

**Audit period:** July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025 (Fiscal Year 2025)
**Report issued:** October 10, 2025

### Finding

The curbside composting pilot has demonstrated genuine resident demand and meaningful diversion — 3,400 participating households and 610 tons of organic material diverted from landfill during the audit period — but two operational weaknesses cloud the case for citywide expansion. Participation has plateaued at 22 percent of eligible households in the pilot zones, well below the levels that would make a citywide rollout cost-effective on current assumptions, and the contamination rate of 18 percent is roughly triple the threshold accepted by the receiving processor, a problem that has twice during the audit period triggered load rejection fees when entire truckloads were refused at the gate. This office finds that the pilot has proven the concept but not the operating model: the program as currently run diverts material at a cost per ton that is materially inflated by contamination penalties and by route economics spread across a modest participation base, and expansion before these problems are corrected would scale the weaknesses along with the strengths.

### Recommendation

The City Auditor recommends that the committee fund the pilot for one additional year at its current footprint rather than approving the expansion the department has requested, and that it condition future expansion on two measurable improvements: reduction of the contamination rate to at or below the processor's 6 percent contract threshold for two consecutive quarters, and demonstrated movement in participation, whether through growth in the existing zones or through evidence from a targeted enrollment effort that higher participation is achievable. The committee should further direct the department to develop and cost a contamination reduction plan — combining cart tagging, targeted resident education, and if necessary suspension of service to repeatedly non-compliant households — and to present a revised citywide cost model that reflects actual pilot economics, including rejection fees, rather than the pre-pilot assumptions on which the original expansion projections rest.

### Evidence

The pilot's accomplishments are real and verified. The figure of 3,400 participating households was confirmed against cart deployment records and route data; the 610 tons of diverted material was confirmed against processor weight tickets. At the city's current landfill tipping fee, that diversion represents avoided disposal cost, and the environmental case — methane avoidance and soil product generation — is set out in the full report. Resident satisfaction among participants is high: a survey conducted during the audit found 87 percent of participating households rating the service favorably, and churn among enrolled households is low.

The difficulty lies in the economics beyond the enrolled base. Participation of 22 percent means collection trucks traverse full routes to serve roughly one household in five, and route costs are therefore spread thinly over the diverted tonnage. Auditors calculated the pilot's all-in cost per diverted ton, inclusive of collection, carts, processing fees, and program administration, and found it substantially above the landfill alternative on a pure cost basis — a gap that would narrow considerably at participation rates in the 40 to 50 percent range that peer cities with mature programs report. Participation grew briskly in the pilot's first two quarters, then flattened; the department has not conducted a structured effort to understand why eligible non-participants have declined to enroll, and this office recommends that such an effort precede any expansion decision.

Contamination is the more acute problem. The processor's contract sets a 6 percent contamination threshold; the pilot's measured rate over the audit period averaged 18 percent, with plastic bags and glass the dominant contaminants. Twice during the year — in October 2024 and again in March 2025 — inbound loads exceeded the processor's rejection limit and entire truckloads were refused, incurring rejection fees and requiring the material to be landfilled anyway, the worst of both outcomes: the city paid composting collection costs and landfill disposal costs on the same tonnage. The fees themselves are modest in absolute terms, but auditors regard them as a warning rather than a cost item, because the processor has advised the department in writing that persistent contamination at current levels jeopardizes the contract renewal due in fiscal 2027. Losing the processing outlet would strand the program entirely; there is no alternative organics processor within economical hauling distance.

The audit traced contamination to an identifiable and correctable source: the pilot's resident education consisted of a single mailer and a cart-hanger at enrollment, with no follow-up, no cart inspection, and no feedback to households setting out contaminated material. Peer programs that have driven contamination below 5 percent uniformly use routine cart tagging — an "oops" notice on contaminated carts, with collection suspended after repeated violations. The department has the staffing latitude to implement tagging within the current budget, and this office found no reason the practice could not begin within the current fiscal year.

### Management Response

The department concurred with the finding on contamination and partially concurred on expansion. Management committed to launching a cart-tagging and education program by January 2026, with quarterly contamination reporting to the committee, and accepted the two-quarter, 6 percent benchmark as the trigger for revisiting expansion. On participation, management argued that plateau at 22 percent partly reflects the demographics of the initial pilot zones and that expansion into different neighborhoods would itself test whether higher participation is achievable; this office regards that as a reasonable hypothesis but an insufficient basis for citywide commitment, and the recommended targeted enrollment effort is designed to test it at pilot scale first. Management agreed to submit the revised cost model, incorporating actual pilot economics, with its fiscal 2026 budget materials.

# Executive Summary No. 4 of 4

## Performance Audit: Permitting System Replacement Project, Office of the Chief Information Officer

**Audit period:** Project inception through June 30, 2025
**Report issued:** October 14, 2025

### Finding

The permitting system replacement project, sponsored by Chief Information Officer Kwame Boateng, was approved by Council at $4.6 million and has now consumed $7.9 million across two vendors — a 72 percent cost overrun — following termination of the original implementation vendor in August 2024. The project has no re-baselined budget or schedule approved by Council, and its central service outcome has moved in the wrong direction: average residential permit turnaround, which the business case promised to improve, has worsened from 21 days before the project began to 34 days during the audit period, as staff divide time between the legacy system, the partially deployed replacement, and manual workarounds bridging the two. This office finds that the project is over budget without authorization, behind any defensible schedule, and currently degrading the service it was funded to improve; that the vendor transition was executed without a full accounting of recoverable versus stranded costs; and that governance failures — chiefly the absence of independent project oversight and of any requirement to return to Council when costs breached the approved amount — allowed a $3.3 million overrun to accumulate without a single formal decision point at which the city's governing body could weigh continuing against alternatives.

### Recommendation

The City Auditor recommends that the committee decline to appropriate further funds for this project until the CIO presents a formal re-baseline package for Council approval, and that the committee treat that package as a genuine decision point among three options — complete the current implementation, re-scope it to a reduced deployment, or terminate and pursue an alternative — rather than as a ratification of continuation. The re-baseline package should include an independent third-party assessment of the second vendor's work to date and the realism of its remaining plan; a complete accounting of the $7.9 million spent, distinguishing costs that produced usable assets from stranded costs of the terminated first engagement; a firm completion cost and date to which the CIO commits; and an interim service plan to bring residential permit turnaround back toward the 21-day pre-project baseline while implementation continues, because residents should not continue to bear the project's dysfunction at the permit counter. The committee should further establish, for this project and as standing policy for major technology projects, a requirement that any projected cost variance above 10 percent of the Council-approved amount be reported to the committee within 30 days and that expenditure beyond the approved amount requires prior Council action.

### Evidence

The financial facts are not in dispute. Council approved $4.6 million. Expenditures through June 30, 2025 total $7.9 million: approximately $3.1 million paid to the first vendor before its termination in August 2024, $3.6 million paid to the second vendor since, and $1.2 million in city-side costs including project staff, licensing, and data migration work. Auditors traced these figures to invoices and payroll allocations and found the totals reliable. What the audit could not find was any Council action authorizing expenditure beyond $4.6 million. The overrun was absorbed through a sequence of internal transfers within the IT budget and through charging project staff time to operating rather than capital accounts — practices that, whatever their technical permissibility line by line, had the cumulative effect of funding a 72 percent overrun on a Council-approved project without Council's knowledge or consent. This office regards that as the audit's most serious governance finding, distinct from and larger than the project's technical troubles.

The vendor transition compounded the cost problem. When the first vendor was terminated in August 2024 — for cause, on grounds of missed milestones and defect rates the full report details — the city held roughly $3.1 million of that vendor's work product. Auditors asked what portion of that work the second vendor was able to reuse. Neither the CIO's office nor the second vendor could provide a documented answer; the second vendor's own project records indicate it rebuilt substantial portions of the configuration from scratch. Absent documentation, this office conservatively estimates that between $1.8 million and $2.4 million of first-vendor spending produced no asset the project now uses. The city has not pursued recovery from the first vendor, and the contract's termination provisions, which auditors reviewed, provide at least a colorable basis for doing so; we recommend the City Attorney evaluate that question, and the full report refers it.

The service impact falls on residents and builders. Residential permit turnaround averaged 21 days in the twelve months before the project's disruption began and averaged 34 days during the audit period — a 62 percent deterioration. The mechanism is documented in the full report: permit technicians now enter data into the legacy system, re-enter subsets into partially deployed replacement modules, and maintain manual tracking spreadsheets to reconcile the two, and interviews with counter staff consistently described the dual-system burden as the dominant cause of delay. The deterioration is not a temporary cutover dip; it has persisted for more than a year. Permit fee revenue timing has slowed correspondingly, and the audit received unsolicited correspondence from three builders describing project financing costs attributable to permit delay — anecdotal, but consistent with the measured data.

On governance, the audit found the project has no steering committee with authority independent of the sponsor, no independent quality assurance contractor of the kind standard for implementations of this size, and no written escalation thresholds. Every consequential decision — including vendor termination and the selection of the successor — was made within the sponsoring office and reported afterward, if at all. The pattern is a familiar one in failed public-sector technology projects, and the standing-policy recommendation above is intended to prevent its recurrence beyond this project.

### Management Response

The Chief Information Officer partially concurred. Management concurred that the project exceeded its approved budget and committed to presenting a re-baseline package to Council by December 31, 2025, and accepted the recommendation for an independent third-party assessment, which management states will be procured in November 2025. Management concurred with the interim service recommendation and stated that two temporary permit technicians have been assigned to reduce the backlog. Management did not concur with the characterization of the funding transfers as circumventing Council authority, asserting that each transfer complied with the city's budget administration rules; this office's position, stated in the full report, is that rule-by-rule compliance does not answer the governance question when the aggregate effect is a 72 percent unauthorized overrun, and we stand by the finding. Management deferred to the City Attorney on cost recovery from the terminated vendor. Mayor Toussaint's office submitted a separate response supporting the recommended standing policy on cost-variance reporting for major technology projects, and this office urges the committee to adopt it during the current budget cycle.

# Supplementary Materials for the October 21, 2025 Hearing

## Auditor's Cross-Cutting Observations

Although each of the four audits stands on its own findings, the committee may find it useful to consider three patterns that emerged across the fiscal 2025 cycle, because they bear on budget decisions beyond any single program.

The first pattern concerns the difference between measuring activity and measuring outcomes. The street resurfacing program succeeded in part because its measures — lane-miles completed, cost per lane-mile, complaint volume — are outcomes the city can verify independently and that map directly to what residents experience. The downtown outreach contract failed in part because its dominant recorded measure, street contacts, is an activity count that neither the vendor's records nor the city's monitoring could translate into results, and the one true outcome measure in the contract went unenforced. The composting pilot sits between the two: its diversion tonnage is a verified outcome, but its expansion case rests on participation and contamination assumptions the pilot has not yet validated. As the committee shapes fiscal 2026 performance expectations, this office recommends it insist, program by program, on at least one measure that is independently verifiable and that a resident would recognize as the point of the program.

The second pattern concerns what happens when Council-approved terms are breached without a return to Council. In two of the four audits — the outreach contract and the permitting project — the original authorization was exceeded or violated in a material way, and in neither case did the breach generate a formal decision point. The outreach vendor stopped reporting and continued to be paid; the permitting project spent $3.3 million beyond its approved amount through internal transfers. These are different failures with different causes, but they share a structural root: the city has no standing rule that converts a material variance into an obligation to come back to the governing body. The standing-policy recommendation in the permitting summary — reporting of projected variances above 10 percent within 30 days, and prior Council action for expenditure beyond approved amounts — would address the capital-project half of this gap. This office recommends the committee consider a parallel rule for outcome-based contracts: a missed reporting deadline or a projected outcome shortfall beyond a defined threshold should trigger mandatory notification to the committee by the administering department.

The third pattern concerns the fragility of institutional knowledge. The resurfacing program's cost accounting lives with one analyst; its crew expertise is concentrated in three retirement-eligible employees. The outreach contract's monitoring lapsed when one staff member departed and no one was formally reassigned the duty. The permitting project's decisions were made within a single office without independent review. In each case a program's health depended on individuals rather than on documented process, and in two of the three cases the dependence failed. The recommendations across the four reports — documented procedures, named monitors, succession plans, independent oversight — are variations on one corrective principle, and this office encourages the committee to view them as such rather than as four unrelated administrative requests.

## Objectives, Scope, and Methodology

These audits were conducted under the authority of City Charter Section 7.04, which directs the City Auditor to examine the economy, efficiency, and effectiveness of city programs and to report findings to the Mayor and Council. All four audits were performed in accordance with generally accepted government auditing standards. Those standards require that the audit be planned and performed to obtain sufficient, appropriate evidence to provide a reasonable basis for the findings and conclusions based on the audit objectives. This office believes the evidence obtained provides that basis for each of the four reports.

The objective common to all four audits was to determine whether the program achieved its intended outcomes at the cost authorized, and whether management's controls were adequate to detect and correct deviations. Audit-specific objectives, scope limitations, and detailed methodology appear in each full report. In general terms, the work included reconciliation of program expenditures to the city's financial system; independent verification of reported outputs through sampling, field inspection, and third-party records; structured interviews with program management, line staff, vendors, and where relevant the receiving processor and housing providers; comparison against contractual terms, Council authorizations, and peer-city benchmarks; and review of each program's monitoring and governance records.

Two scope limitations warrant the committee's attention. In the downtown outreach audit, the vendor's contact logs did not reliably distinguish unique individuals, which prevented this office from determining the true reach of the program; the audit's conclusions rest on the verified placement data and on the documented reporting breach, neither of which is affected by the limitation. In the permitting audit, the absence of documentation on reuse of the first vendor's work product required this office to estimate stranded costs as a range rather than a figure; the estimate is conservative, and the independent assessment recommended in that report should resolve it.

Each department was provided a draft of its report and given fourteen days to respond. All responses are reproduced in full, unedited, in the appendices to the respective reports. The outreach vendor, as an affected external party, was likewise offered the opportunity to respond and did so.

## Follow-Up and Reporting

Consistent with this office's standing practice, each recommendation in the consolidated schedule will be tracked until implemented or until the committee formally accepts a management decision not to implement it. This office will report implementation status to the committee twice: an interim status memorandum in February 2026, timed to precede final budget deliberations, and a full follow-up report in October 2026 alongside the fiscal 2026 audit cycle.

Three items carry dates that fall before the February memorandum and are consequential enough that this office will notify the committee chair directly if they are missed: the outreach vendor's filing of delinquent reports by November 30, 2025; the designation of a named outreach contract monitor by November 15, 2025; and the CIO's submission of the permitting re-baseline package by December 31, 2025. The committee should treat a missed date on any of these three as information relevant to the corresponding fiscal 2026 appropriation.
