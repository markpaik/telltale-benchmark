## I. Executive Summary

Tuscarora County closes the first half of fiscal 2026 in a materially different position than the adopted budget contemplated. Against a $196 million general fund, the county is tracking toward a combined mid-year variance of roughly $5.7 million in unfavorable general-fund exposure, driven principally by sheriff's office overtime, a widening operating loss at Tuscarora Manor, and a revenue shortfall at the Register of Deeds tied to the regional mortgage slowdown. Roads and Bridges shows a spending pattern that looks favorable on paper — 61 percent of its paving allocation spent against 38 percent of planned centerline miles completed — but that gap reflects a cost problem, not savings, and an unbudgeted $3.8 million bridge emergency on Route 4012 has not yet been formally added to any appropriation. Human Services, the county's largest pass-through function at $71 million, carries $1.9 million in unreconciled grant receivables that the June external audit letter identified as a control weakness, and that finding is addressed separately in Section IV. The 911 Center is operating below the state-mandated ninety-second answer standard on nearly one in twelve calls, a direct function of a 20 percent dispatcher vacancy rate.

None of these conditions individually threatens the county's fund balance, but taken together they point to a second half in which the Board will need to make discrete decisions on overtime authorization, capital reprioritization, nursing home subsidy policy, and grant accounting controls, several of which carry budget amendment or transfer implications requiring Board action under the county's home rule charter. This report presents the county-wide position first, then works through each department, then the audit finding, then closes with the specific matters staff recommend the Board take up before or at the August 6 meeting.

## II. County-Wide Fiscal Position at Mid-Year

Total general fund appropriations for fiscal 2026 are $196,000,000, supporting 1,240 budgeted full-time positions across the county's 148,000 residents. Through June 30, county-wide general fund expenditures stand at approximately 49.4 percent of the annual appropriation, close to the straight-line pace one would expect at the midpoint, but that aggregate figure obscures significant divergence at the department level, summarized below.

The county entered fiscal 2026 with an unassigned general fund balance of approximately $23.9 million, or roughly 12.2 percent of the general fund, consistent with the Board's reserve policy floor of 10 percent. If the second-half trend lines identified in this report hold without corrective action, staff project year-end fund balance would fall to approximately $18.2 million, or about 9.3 percent of the general fund — below the policy floor for the first time since fiscal 2021. This is a projection, not a certainty; several of the drivers below are addressable through mid-year budget actions the Board can take in August and September, and staff have flagged specific options in Section V.

Revenue performance is mixed. Property tax collections, the largest single general fund revenue source, are running at 96 percent of budgeted mid-year collections, within normal seasonal variance. The Register of Deeds shortfall described in Section III is the most significant negative revenue variance identified to date. State and federal reimbursement revenue tied to Human Services is running on schedule in the aggregate but is the subject of the reconciliation concern discussed in Section IV, which affects the county's ability to certify amounts owed versus amounts received at any given point in time, not the underlying cash position.

## III. Departmental Reports

### A. Sheriff's Office — Marvin Delgado, Sheriff

| Metric | Budget/Target | Actual | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total Department Budget | $34,000,000 | — | — |
| Overtime Spending | Within budget | $2,100,000 over | Unfavorable |
| Authorized Deputy Positions | 214 | 183 filled | 31 vacant (14.5%) |
| Jail Rated Capacity | 340 | 388 avg. daily population | +48 (14.1% over capacity) |

The sheriff's office is $2.1 million over its overtime line against a $34 million total budget at the midpoint of the year, and the cause is straightforward: 31 of 214 authorized deputy positions are vacant, a 14.5 percent vacancy rate, and the minimum staffing requirements for road patrol and jail operations do not flex downward when positions go unfilled. Every open patrol slot and every open corrections slot is being covered by overtime for the deputies and corrections officers who remain, and the jail's population problem compounds the staffing problem directly.

Average daily population at the county jail stands at 388 against a rated capacity of 340, a 14 percent overage that has held for most of the first half. Sheriff Delgado's submission attributes the elevated population to a combination of case processing delays at the Court of Common Pleas and an increase in pretrial detentions tied to a bail policy change adopted by the president judge in late 2025. Overcrowding at this level requires additional corrections officer coverage per shift under the county's accreditation standards, and with corrections positions among the vacant 31, that coverage is being purchased at time-and-a-half.

The office reports that its recruiting pipeline has improved modestly — six conditional offers are pending academy slots for the fall class — but the department does not project closing the vacancy gap before the fourth quarter at the earliest, meaning the overtime pressure will likely continue at a reduced but still elevated rate through Q3. Absent Board action, the department's overtime line is projected to finish the year approximately $4.0–$4.4 million over budget on an annualized basis. The sheriff's office has requested a mid-year transfer from salary savings associated with the vacant positions themselves — a standard mechanism, since unfilled positions generate base salary savings that partially offset overtime cost — but Whitcomb's office estimates that mechanism covers only about 55 percent of the overage, leaving a net general fund gap of roughly $1.8–$2.0 million for the year that has no identified offsetting source.

### B. Roads and Bridges — Public Works

| Metric | Plan | Actual | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paving Allocation | $27,000,000 | $16,470,000 spent (61%) | — |
| Planned Centerline Miles | 100% | 38% completed | -62 pts |
| Asphalt Price Change | — | +14% | Unfavorable |
| Route 4012 Bridge Repair | Unbudgeted | $3,800,000 (est.) | New obligation |

Roads and Bridges presents the clearest case in this report of a spending pace that misleads if read without the underlying unit cost. The department has spent 61 percent of its $27 million paving allocation but completed only 38 percent of planned centerline miles, a mismatch entirely explained by a 14 percent rise in asphalt prices since the budget was adopted last fall. In effect, the department is buying less roadway for more money: the same dollar that was budgeted to resurface a given stretch now covers roughly 85–87 percent of that distance. If asphalt prices hold at current levels for the remainder of the paving season, the department projects it will exhaust the full $27 million allocation having completed approximately 62–65 percent of the originally planned centerline miles for the year, deferring the balance into fiscal 2027.

Layered on top of the price pressure is the Route 4012 bridge closure. The bridge closed in April after an inspection found deterioration requiring emergency repair, and the current repair estimate is $3.8 million — a cost that does not exist anywhere in the adopted fiscal 2026 budget. Public Works has been managing the closure through detour signage and has not yet drawn down county funds against the $3.8 million estimate because it is pursuing PennDOT emergency bridge funding, for which the county has applied but not yet received a determination. If state funding does not come through, or comes through only in part, the Board will need to identify a funding source — most plausibly a transfer from the capital reserve fund or a supplemental bond authorization — before repair work can proceed on the county's own dime. Staff flag this as one of the more consequential open items for the second half, both for its dollar size and because a bridge closure of this duration has secondary effects on emergency response times and school bus routing in the affected township.

### C. Human Services — Grant-Funded Programs

| Metric | Amount |
|---|---|
| Total State/Federal Pass-Through | $71,000,000 |
| Unreconciled Grant Receivables | $1,900,000 |
| Share of Pass-Through Unreconciled | 2.7% |

Human Services administers $71 million in state and federal pass-through funding across roughly a dozen grant programs, including child welfare, mental health and developmental disabilities services, and aging services. At mid-year, the department carries $1.9 million in grant receivables — amounts the county has expended on behalf of grant programs and is owed reimbursement for — that have not been reconciled against the corresponding grant ledgers. This is the item the county's external auditors flagged in their June management letter, and it is treated in full in Section IV of this report. For purposes of the department's own operating picture: the $1.9 million figure represents a cash-flow and control issue rather than a budget overage. The county has, in all likelihood, already spent the money correctly and is simply behind in documenting and drawing down the corresponding reimbursement, but until the reconciliation is complete the department cannot state with confidence that the full $1.9 million is in fact collectible, and a portion could ultimately prove unallowable under grant terms if documentation gaps aren't closed before applicable grant closeout deadlines, some of which fall in Q4 of this calendar year.

Program delivery within Human Services is otherwise reported as on track against caseload and service targets for the first half, and the department has not requested any supplemental appropriation. The immediate need is administrative capacity to complete the reconciliation, addressed in Section IV.

### D. Tuscarora Manor — Sheila Rourke, Administrator

| Metric | Budget | Actual | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Licensed Beds | 180 | 180 | — |
| Average Census | — | 142 (78.9%) | — |
| Budgeted Operating Loss (Annual) | $3,100,000 | — | — |

Tuscarora Manor, the county-operated nursing home, is running well ahead of its budgeted loss for the year: $4.6 million against a budgeted loss of $3.1 million, a $1.5 million unfavorable variance that traces to two connected causes. First, census sits at 142 of 180 licensed beds, or 78.9 percent occupancy — below the roughly 85 percent occupancy the budget assumed as break-even-adjacent for the facility's fixed cost base. Fewer residents means less Medicaid per-diem and private-pay revenue against a cost structure — building, utilities, base staffing — that does not scale down proportionally.

Second, and driving much of the cost side of the gap, 27 percent of nursing hours at the facility are being covered by agency (temporary contract) nursing staff rather than county employees, and agency nursing typically costs the county 60–80 percent more per hour than a filled staff position once agency markup is included. Administrator Rourke's submission attributes the reliance on agency staff to the same regional healthcare labor shortage affecting long-term care facilities generally, and reports that the facility has active recruitment underway for eleven RN and LPN positions, with two offers extended in June. Until vacancy rates among nursing staff improve, agency reliance — and its cost premium — will likely persist.

The Manor's loss is subsidized from the general fund, so the $1.5 million overage flows directly into the county-wide exposure summarized in Section II. Staff note that this is not a new or one-time problem: the Manor has run an operating loss every year since at least fiscal 2019, consistent with the pattern at most publicly operated nursing facilities in Pennsylvania, but the magnitude of this year's overage is larger than in recent years and warrants Board attention to whether the facility's rate structure, staffing model, or scope of operation should be revisited, a question staff raise again in Section V.

### E. 911 Center — Emergency Communications

| Metric | Standard/Budget | Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Calls Answered (Mid-Year) | — | 214,000 |
| Authorized Dispatcher Seats | 46 | 37 filled (9 vacant, 19.6%) |
| Ninety-Second Answer Standard | 100% compliance target | Missed on 8% of calls |
| Overtime (est.) | Budgeted | +$780,000 (est.) |

The 911 Center answered 214,000 calls in the first half of the year with 9 of 46 authorized dispatcher seats vacant, a vacancy rate of nearly 20 percent that is now producing a measurable service-level problem: the center missed the state's ninety-second answer standard on 8 percent of calls, versus a target of full compliance. Pennsylvania's 911 oversight statute treats the ninety-second standard as a performance benchmark tied in part to continued state 911 fund disbursement eligibility, so this is not solely an operational concern — it carries a funding dimension the Board should be aware of heading into the second half, and staff recommend the county emergency management coordinator brief the Board directly on compliance risk before the next state reporting cycle.

As with the sheriff's office, the vacancy-driven overtime cost is real: covering the same call volume with 80 percent of authorized staffing means the dispatchers on the roster are working substantial mandatory overtime, which staff estimate at approximately $780,000 above budget on a mid-year pace, though this figure should be treated as an estimate pending the center's formal mid-year close. The center's director reports that dispatcher recruitment is difficult county-wide for the same regional labor-market reasons affecting corrections and nursing, compounded by a state-mandated certification and training pipeline that takes new hires 12–16 weeks to reach solo dispatch status, meaning even successful recruiting does not translate to relief on the same timeline as, for example, a clerical hire.

The Register of Deeds recorded 11,400 documents in the first half of the year, down 22 percent from the same period last year, a decline the office attributes to the sharp slowdown in mortgage refinancing and home sale activity regionally as interest rates have remained elevated. Because recording fees are a per-document charge, the volume decline translates directly into an estimated $940,000 shortfall in fee revenue against budget for the year. The office notes this trend is consistent with what neighboring counties are reporting and is macroeconomic in origin rather than tied to any operational issue in the office itself; staff have no reason to project improvement in the second half absent a change in mortgage rates, and recommend the county's revenue forecast for fiscal 2027 planning purposes assume continued softness in recording volume.

## IV. Audit Finding: Grant Reconciliation

The county's external auditors, in their management letter accompanying the fiscal 2025 audit and delivered in June, identified weak grant reconciliation practices as a significant deficiency, concentrated in — though the auditors noted not exclusive to — Human Services. The finding, in substance, is that the county's grant accounting function has not consistently reconciled amounts recorded as receivable from state and federal grantors against the underlying grant ledgers and drawdown requests on a timely basis, and that the $1.9 million in unreconciled Human Services receivables identified in Section III is the most visible current manifestation of that gap.

The auditors' letter identifies three contributing conditions: first, that grant reconciliation has historically been performed by a single grants accountant position within Human Services without a documented backup or cross-training arrangement, creating a single point of failure; second, that the county's financial system does not automatically flag receivables aging past a defined threshold (the auditors recommend 90 days) for supervisory review, so aging receivables can persist without triggering any procedural response; and third, that the reconciliation process is manual and spreadsheet-based rather than integrated with the general ledger, increasing both the labor required and the risk of error.

None of the three conditions is unique to Tuscarora County — the auditors noted similar findings are common among counties of comparable size administering large pass-through grant portfolios — but the Board should treat the finding as a control weakness requiring a documented corrective action plan, both because it is now a matter of audit record and because unreconciled receivables that are ultimately found unallowable or uncollectible would represent a direct hit to the general fund, since the county would have expended funds it cannot recover.

Staff, in coordination with the Controller's office and Human Services, propose the following corrective action plan for Board consideration:

1. **Immediate:** Assign a second staff member, cross-trained, to grant reconciliation duties within 30 days, funded from existing Human Services administrative allocation.
2. **By September 30:** Complete reconciliation of the $1.9 million in currently flagged receivables, with a written determination on collectibility for each grant line.
3. **By December 31:** Implement an aging report with automatic 90-day flagging within the county's financial system, developed with the IT department and the system vendor.
4. **Ongoing:** Quarterly reconciliation status reporting to the Board as part of the standard quarterly financial update, beginning with the Q3 report.

The Controller has indicated informal concurrence with this approach and staff recommend the Board formally adopt the corrective action plan at the August 6 meeting so that the county can represent to the auditors, ahead of the fiscal 2026 audit cycle, that the finding has been addressed rather than repeated.

## V. Second-Half Outlook: Matters Before the Board

Several of the conditions described above will resolve themselves through the normal course of the year; several will not, and require Board decisions in the second half. Staff group these into three categories.

**Decisions requiring near-term Board action (August–September):**

- **Sheriff's office overtime.** With a projected $1.8–$2.0 million net general fund gap after applying vacancy salary savings, the Board will need to either authorize a supplemental appropriation, direct the sheriff's office to further restrict overtime authorization (with corresponding operational trade-offs the sheriff has stated he cannot make safely below current levels given jail overcrowding), or both.
- **Route 4012 bridge.** The Board should not wait on the PennDOT emergency funding determination to identify a fallback funding source for the $3.8 million repair estimate, given the bridge has now been closed since April and the detour is affecting emergency response and school transportation. Staff recommend the Board authorize a capital reserve transfer as a contingent measure, reversible if state funding comes through.
- **Grant reconciliation corrective action plan.** As detailed in Section IV, formal Board adoption is recommended at the August 6 meeting.

**Decisions likely required before year-end:**

- **Tuscarora Manor's structural loss.** A $1.5 million overage against an already-budgeted $3.1 million loss, on top of a multi-year pattern of losses, warrants a Board-level policy conversation about the Manor's staffing model, rate structure, and long-term operating posture — not necessarily resolvable by August 6, but staff recommend the Board direct Administrator Rourke and the Human Services director to return with options (status quo, staffing model revision, or a broader operational review) before the fiscal 2027 budget process begins in earnest this fall.
- **911 Center staffing and state compliance.** Given the funding implications of missing the ninety-second answer standard, staff recommend a direct briefing from the emergency management coordinator, with a staffing and recruitment update, before the county's next state 911 compliance reporting deadline.

**Items to monitor, no action currently recommended:**

- **Register of Deeds revenue.** Macroeconomic in origin; staff recommend building continued softness into fiscal 2027 revenue projections rather than taking action against the current-year budget.
- **Roads and Bridges paving pace.** The 38 percent centerline-mile completion against 61 percent of budget spent will likely continue at a similar ratio for the remainder of the paving season; absent Board direction to add funds, staff recommend the department simply complete as much of the planned program as the existing $27 million allows and carry the remainder into the fiscal 2027 planning process, which is the department's own recommendation.

Taken together, the county's mid-year position is manageable but tightening. The projected draw on fund balance — from roughly 12.2 percent to an estimated 9.3 percent of the general fund absent corrective action — would put the county below its own reserve policy floor for the first time in five years. None of the individual drivers is alarming in isolation; several are shared with neighboring counties (regional labor shortages affecting corrections, nursing, and dispatch staffing alike; mortgage-market softness affecting recording revenue; construction cost inflation affecting paving) and reflect conditions largely outside the county's direct control. But the audit finding on grant reconciliation is a control issue the county can and should fix directly, and the sheriff's office overtime and Route 4012 bridge items require Board decisions with real dollar consequences in the near term.

Staff further recommend the Board direct Human Services and Tuscarora Manor to prepare options for the Manor's structural operating loss ahead of the fiscal 2027 budget cycle, and direct the 911 Center to brief the Board on dispatcher staffing and state compliance status before the next reporting deadline.

## Appendix C: Glossary of Terms Used in This Report

**Fund balance** — the accumulated difference between the general fund's revenues and expenditures over time; the county's reserve policy sets a floor of 10 percent of the general fund appropriation.

**Pass-through funding** — state or federal grant dollars that flow through the county's books to support a program but that the county did not raise through its own taxing authority; Human Services' $71 million figure is composed almost entirely of pass-through revenue.

**Receivable (grant context)** — an amount the county has already spent on a grant-eligible activity and is owed reimbursement for from the granting state or federal agency; until reconciled and drawn down, it remains a paper asset rather than cash in hand.

**Rated capacity (jail context)** — the maximum inmate population the county jail is designed and licensed to house under state corrections standards; population above rated capacity generally triggers additional staffing and, in some circumstances, state monitoring requirements.

**Ninety-second answer standard** — the Pennsylvania 911 oversight benchmark requiring emergency call centers to answer incoming calls within ninety seconds; sustained noncompliance can affect a county's eligibility for continued disbursement from the state 911 fund.

**Agency staffing** — temporary clinical or corrections staff supplied by a third-party staffing agency at an hourly rate that typically exceeds the fully loaded cost of a filled county position, used to cover vacant shifts on a short-term basis.
