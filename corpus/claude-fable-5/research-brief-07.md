# Does the Attendance Mentor Program Work? Evidence from the First Full Cohort

**Monocacy Valley Public Schools — Office of Research and Evaluation**

**Research Brief 2026-03**

Prepared by Imani Fitzgerald, Research Analyst

Program rosters supplied by Rosa Iglesias, Mentor Coordinator

Prepared at the direction of Superintendent Terrence Blackwell for the Board of Education

## The Question and What We Found

At the April 2026 meeting of the Board of Education, board member Lorraine Hovde asked a direct question: does the attendance mentor program work? The program, launched in 2024 under a $1.9 million state grant that expires in June 2027, placed 22 part-time attendance mentors in the district's four high schools and assigned them to ninth graders who had been chronically absent the year before. Superintendent Terrence Blackwell directed this office to answer the question before the board's July vote on whether to absorb the mentors into local funds when the grant ends. This brief is that answer.

The short version is this: the program appears to help, but less than the raw numbers suggest, and not for everyone.

Mentored students improved their attendance by 4.3 percentage points on average between 2024-25 and 2025-26. Students who met the same eligibility criteria but were never assigned a mentor improved by 2.6 points. The difference between those two numbers — about 1.7 percentage points, or roughly three additional days of school in a 180-day year — is our best estimate of what the program itself contributed. Three cautions surround that estimate. First, both groups were selected because they had a bad attendance year, so some of the improvement in both groups is simply students drifting back toward their own normal — a pattern called regression to the mean — rather than anything the district did. The comparison group exists precisely to net that out, and it is why we emphasize the 1.7-point difference rather than the 4.3-point raw gain. Second, the district changed its period-attendance coding rule in August 2025, which raised measured attendance systemwide by roughly 1.5 points; that inflates the raw gains for both groups but largely washes out of the between-group comparison. Third, mentored students were not randomly assigned, so the comparison is informative but not airtight.

The pattern beneath the average matters as much as the average. The program's benefit is concentrated among students whose prior-year absence rate fell between 10 and 19 percent — the moderately chronically absent — who improved 6.2 points, well ahead of comparable unmentored students. Students who had missed more than 30 percent of the prior year improved only 1.1 points, essentially indistinguishable from similar students without mentors. English learners, who make up 18 percent of the mentored group, showed no measurable difference from comparable unmentored English learners. One high school did not assign its mentors until November and saw roughly half the gains of the other three, and 94 students moved between schools mid-year, complicating the picture at the margins but not changing the overall conclusion.

For the July decision, the evidence supports continuing the program in some form, but it does not support continuing it exactly as designed. A program refocused on students in the 10-to-19 percent prior-absence band, paired with a different intervention for the most severely absent students and a deliberate strategy for English learners, would likely deliver more attendance per dollar than a flat extension of the current model. The remainder of this brief explains how we reached these conclusions, in enough detail that the board can judge the strength of the evidence for itself.

## 1. Background: The Program and the Decision in Front of the Board

Monocacy Valley Public Schools serves 11,400 students across 17 schools in and around Frederick, Maryland. Like districts across the state, Monocacy Valley saw chronic absenteeism climb after 2020 and settle at a level well above its pre-pandemic norm, with the ninth-grade year emerging as a particular point of vulnerability: students who disengage in the transition to high school are substantially more likely to fail courses, fall off track for graduation, and eventually drop out.

In 2024 the district competed successfully for a $1.9 million state grant to address this problem and used it to hire 22 part-time attendance mentors. The mentors are not truant officers and not counselors. Their job is relational: each mentor carries a caseload of roughly 25 to 30 ninth graders, greets them in the morning, checks in when they are absent, calls home, helps troubleshoot the ordinary obstacles that keep teenagers out of school — transportation, work schedules, family caregiving, anxiety about a particular class — and connects families to district services when the obstacles are larger than a conversation can solve. Mentors were assigned to ninth graders at the district's four high schools — Monocacy Ridge, Carrollton Manor, Sugarloaf Valley, and Braddock Heights — who had been chronically absent the year before, meaning they had missed 10 percent or more of their enrolled days as eighth graders.

The grant hired mentors in the fall of 2024 and supported a partial start-up cohort during the remainder of that school year, but 2025-26 was the first year the program operated at full scale from (nearly) the start of the year, with complete rosters, trained mentors, and a stable assignment process. This evaluation therefore focuses on the 2025-26 cohort, using 2024-25 as the baseline year. It is the fairest test of the program as it would actually run if the district continued it.

The grant expires in June 2027. The state funds one more full year of operation, 2026-27, but the board must decide this July whether to commit local funds beyond that point, because the decision affects staffing commitments, the FY2028 budget framework, and whether the district retains or releases the mentors it has trained. Absorbing the program into local funds would cost approximately $640,000 per year at current staffing levels — the annualized equivalent of the grant. Board member Hovde's question in April — does it work? — is therefore not academic. It is the threshold question for a recurring commitment of local money.

Superintendent Blackwell directed the Office of Research and Evaluation to answer the question using the district's own records. Imani Fitzgerald, research analyst, designed and ran the study. Andre Beauchamp, director of student services, verified the attendance records against source systems at each high school. Rosa Iglesias, mentor coordinator, supplied the program rosters that identify which students were assigned to which mentors and when.

## 2. Data and Methods

### 2.1 Data sources

The analysis draws on three sources. The first is the district's student information system, which records daily and period-level attendance for every enrolled student. We extracted attendance records for the 2024-25 and 2025-26 school years for all students who were district eighth graders in 2024-25 and ninth graders in 2025-26, along with enrollment dates, school of enrollment, and demographic and program flags (English learner status, special education status, and eligibility for free or reduced-price meals). Attendance data for 2025-26 run through the end of the third marking period (mid-April 2026), the most recent verified cut at the time of analysis; both years are measured over the same portion of the calendar so that the two years are comparable.

The second source is the mentor program roster maintained by the mentor coordinator's office, which records every student assigned to a mentor, the assignment date, the mentor's name, and any changes in assignment during the year. The third is the attendance policy documentation maintained by student services, which we used to characterize the August 2025 coding change described below.

Director Beauchamp's office verified the attendance extract in two ways: by reconciling aggregate daily attendance counts against the counts each high school certified to the state, and by hand-checking a random sample of 120 individual student records against the source period-attendance logs. Discrepancies affected fewer than 1 percent of student-days and were corrected before analysis.

### 2.2 The sample and how the comparison group was formed

The mentored group consists of the 612 ninth graders who appeared on program rosters in 2025-26 and remained enrolled in the district long enough to have attendance measured in both years. They are distributed across the four high schools as follows: 168 at Monocacy Ridge, 155 at Carrollton Manor, 147 at Braddock Heights, and 142 at Sugarloaf Valley.

The comparison group consists of 1,180 ninth graders who met the same eligibility criterion — chronic absence in 2024-25, meaning a prior-year absence rate of 10 percent or higher — but were never assigned a mentor. Why were eligible students left unassigned? Capacity, mostly. Twenty-two part-time mentors carrying caseloads of 25 to 30 students can serve about 600 students, and roughly 1,800 rising ninth graders met the eligibility bar. Schools filled caseloads through a mix of counselor referral, order of registration, and administrator judgment about which students seemed most likely to engage. This is the single most important methodological fact in this brief, and it cuts in two directions.

On one hand, the comparison group is genuinely comparable in the way that matters most: every student in it cleared the same absence threshold in the same year, measured the same way. At baseline, the two groups look similar on observable characteristics. The mentored group's average 2024-25 attendance rate was 82.4 percent; the comparison group's was 82.9 percent. The groups are within a few percentage points of each other on the share of English learners (18 percent mentored, 16 percent comparison), students with disabilities (19 versus 17 percent), and students eligible for free or reduced-price meals (58 versus 55 percent). The distribution across prior-absence severity bands is also similar, as Section 3.2 shows.

On the other hand, assignment was not random, and the ways schools filled caseloads could bias the estimate in either direction. If counselors referred the students they worried about most, the mentored group would be negatively selected — harder cases — and our estimate would understate the program's effect. If administrators favored students "most likely to engage," the mentored group would be positively selected and the estimate would overstate it. We cannot fully rule out either possibility. What we can do is compare like with like within severity bands, schools, and student groups, which we do throughout Section 3, and note that the baseline similarity of the two groups limits how large this bias can plausibly be.

There is a second, subtler consequence of how eligibility was defined, and it is the reason this brief does not lead with the 4.3-point raw gain. Because both groups were selected on having a bad attendance year, both were, in a statistical sense, caught at their worst. Some students in both groups had 2024-25 absences driven by one-time events — an illness, a family disruption, a housing move — and would have attended better in 2025-26 no matter what the district did. This regression to the mean shows up as improvement in both groups and is the main reason the comparison group improved 2.6 points with no intervention at all. The design of this study is built around subtracting that shared drift out: the program's estimated effect is the mentored group's gain minus the comparison group's gain, not the mentored group's gain alone. Any presentation of this program's results that cites the 4.3-point figure without the comparison group alongside it is overstating what the program did.

The 94 students who transferred between district schools mid-year are handled on an intent-to-treat basis: they are analyzed with the group they started the year in, whether or not their mentoring continued after the move. Fifty-nine of the movers were in the mentored group and 35 in the comparison group. Section 3.5 tests whether this choice matters; it does not change the conclusions.

### 2.3 The August 2025 coding change

In August 2025, the district changed its rule for converting period-level attendance into daily attendance. Under the old rule, a high school student marked absent for three or more periods was recorded as absent for the full day. Under the new rule, adopted to align with updated state reporting guidance, period absences are converted proportionally, so a student who misses three of seven periods is recorded as 0.43 of a day absent rather than a full day.

The mechanical effect of this change is to raise measured attendance for any student who misses parts of days — which describes most chronically absent high school students — without any change in actual behavior. Student services estimates, and our own reprocessing of a sample of 2024-25 records under both rules confirms, that the change raised measured attendance systemwide by roughly 1.5 percentage points.

The change matters for interpreting this study in one way and not in another. It matters for the raw gains: because 2024-25 was measured under the old rule and 2025-26 under the new one, roughly 1.5 points of every student's measured year-over-year improvement is a bookkeeping artifact. Adjusted for the coding change, the mentored group's real gain is closer to 2.8 points and the comparison group's closer to 1.1. It largely does not matter for the between-group difference: the rule change applied identically to both groups in the same year, so it adds the same artificial lift to each and subtracts out of the difference. Our reprocessing check found the inflation nearly identical across the two groups — 1.5 points for the mentored group, 1.4 for the comparison group — so the net program estimate of 1.7 points is essentially untouched. Throughout Section 3 we report raw figures, because they match what schools see in their dashboards, and we flag adjusted figures where the distinction matters.

### 2.4 Measures and analytic approach

The outcome is each student's attendance rate — days attended divided by days enrolled — in 2024-25 and 2025-26, with the change between years as the primary measure. We also report the share of each group that remained chronically absent (below 90 percent attendance) in 2025-26 as a plainer-language secondary outcome.

The analytic approach is a difference-in-differences comparison: the mentored group's change minus the comparison group's change, computed overall and within subgroups defined by prior-year absence severity, school, English learner status, special education status, and meal eligibility. We also estimated the same contrasts in a regression framework that adjusts for baseline attendance, school, and demographic characteristics; the adjusted estimates track the simple differences within 0.2 points throughout, so the tables in this brief present the simple differences for transparency. Differences of roughly 0.8 points or larger between the two full groups are unlikely to arise from chance given the sample sizes; subgroup contrasts require larger differences to clear the same bar, and we note where subgroup samples are too small to support firm conclusions.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Overall

Mentored students raised their attendance from 82.4 percent in 2024-25 to 86.7 percent in 2025-26, a gain of 4.3 percentage points. Comparison students rose from 82.9 to 85.5 percent, a gain of 2.6 points. The difference — 1.7 percentage points — is our headline estimate of the program's effect. On a 180-day calendar, 1.7 points is about three days of school per mentored student per year: the raw 4.3-point gain is worth nearly eight days, but roughly five of those days would likely have been recovered anyway, as the comparison group's trajectory shows, and about a quarter of both groups' measured gains reflects the coding change rather than behavior.

The secondary outcome tells the same story in plainer terms. Every student in both groups was chronically absent in 2024-25 by construction. In 2025-26, 59 percent of mentored students were still chronically absent, against 67 percent of comparison students. The program did not solve chronic absence for most of its students — a majority remained below the 90 percent line — but it moved a meaningfully larger share of them across it than would have crossed on their own.

**Figure 1 (described).** A figure accompanying this brief would show two lines, one for each group, tracking average attendance across the two years. Both lines start at nearly the same point in 2024-25, around 82 to 83 percent, and both rise in 2025-26 — the visual signature of regression to the mean plus the coding change lifting everyone. The mentored line ends visibly above the comparison line. The gap between the two endpoints, not the slope of either line, is the program's effect.

### 3.2 By prior absence level: where the program works and where it does not

The average conceals the program's most important feature. We divided both groups into three bands by 2024-25 absence rate: students who missed 10 to 19 percent of the year (moderately chronically absent), 20 to 29 percent (severely absent), and 30 percent or more (the most disconnected students, who missed roughly two months of school or more).

**Table 1. Change in attendance rate, 2024-25 to 2025-26, by prior-year absence band (percentage points)**

| Prior-year absence | Mentored (n) | Mentored gain | Comparison (n) | Comparison gain | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10–19% | 310 | +6.2 | 610 | +3.4 | **+2.8** |
| 20–29% | 200 | +2.9 | 380 | +2.0 | **+0.9** |
| 30% or more | 102 | +1.1 | 190 | +1.2 | **–0.1** |
| All students | 612 | +4.3 | 1,180 | +2.6 | **+1.7** |

The gradient is steep and consistent. For students in the 10-to-19 percent band — about half of both groups — mentoring is associated with a 2.8-point advantage over similar unmentored students, well beyond what chance would produce. For the 20-to-29 percent band, the advantage shrinks to 0.9 points, at the edge of statistical reliability. For students who had missed 30 percent or more of the prior year, mentored students gained 1.1 points and comparison students gained 1.2: there is no evidence the program helped this group at all.

This pattern is not surprising given what mentors actually do. A morning greeting, a phone call home, and help with a bus problem are well matched to a student who misses a day here and two days there. They are not matched to a student who has missed two months of school, whose absence typically reflects deeper circumstances — untreated health conditions, housing instability, full disengagement from schooling — that a part-time mentor with a 28-student caseload cannot address. Mentors interviewed informally by the coordinator's office during the year said as much: several described their highest-absence students as needing "a case manager, not a check-in."

Note also that the raw gain of 6.2 points in the top band should be read with the same discipline as the overall figure: comparison students in that band gained 3.4 points on their own, the largest regression-to-the-mean drift of any band, which is exactly what one expects — students with moderate one-year absence spikes are the most likely to bounce back naturally. The program's contribution is the 2.8-point difference, not the 6.2.

**Figure 2 (described).** A bar chart accompanying this section would show three pairs of bars, one pair per band, mentored beside comparison. In the left pair (10–19%), the mentored bar stands clearly taller. In the middle pair, the mentored bar is modestly taller. In the right pair, the bars are the same height. The chart's message is that the program's effect is not a uniform lift but a targeted one, and the targeting currently in place — everyone above the 10 percent line — does not match where the effect lives.

### 3.3 By school: the cost of a late start

**Table 2. Change in attendance rate by high school (percentage points)**

| School | Mentored (n) | Mentored gain | Comparison gain | Difference | Mentoring began |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monocacy Ridge | 168 | +5.1 | +2.9 | **+2.2** | September 2025 |
| Carrollton Manor | 155 | +4.8 | +2.6 | **+2.2** | September 2025 |
| Sugarloaf Valley | 142 | +4.6 | +2.5 | **+2.1** | September 2025 |
| Braddock Heights | 147 | +2.5 | +2.4 | **+0.1** | November 2025 |

Three of the four high schools show closely clustered results: a net advantage of 2.1 to 2.2 points for mentored students. The fourth, Braddock Heights, shows essentially none. Braddock Heights did not assign its mentors until November 2025, a delay caused by a staffing gap in the assistant principal position that owned the assignment process. Its mentored students received roughly seven months of the program instead of nine and a half, and — perhaps more importantly — missed the first weeks of ninth grade, when attendance habits for the year are largely set and when the other schools' mentors were making their initial home contacts.

Two readings of the Braddock Heights result are possible. The first is a dose-response reading: less mentoring, less effect, which if anything strengthens the causal case for the program at the other three schools — the one school where the treatment was weak is the one school where the effect is absent. The second is a school-effects reading: something else about Braddock Heights in 2025-26 suppressed results there. We cannot fully separate these with one school and one year, but the fact that Braddock Heights' comparison students gained 2.4 points — right in line with comparison students elsewhere — suggests the school's overall attendance environment was normal and the shortfall is specific to the mentored group's late start. Either way, the operational lesson is the same: if the board continues the program, assignments must be complete before the first day of school, and the assignment process should not depend on a single administrator's position being filled.

### 3.4 By student group: the English learner gap

**Table 3. Change in attendance rate by student group (percentage points)**

| Group | Mentored (n) | Mentored gain | Comparison gain | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| English learners | 110 | +2.7 | +2.6 | **+0.1** |
| Non-English learners | 502 | +4.7 | +2.6 | **+2.1** |
| Students with disabilities | 116 | +3.6 | +2.5 | **+1.1** |
| Free/reduced-price meals | 355 | +4.1 | +2.5 | **+1.6** |
| All students | 612 | +4.3 | +2.6 | **+1.7** |

The most striking row is the first. English learners make up 18 percent of the mentored group — 110 students — and their gain of 2.7 points is statistically indistinguishable from the 2.6-point gain of comparable unmentored English learners. The program's entire measured effect is carried by non-English learners, whose 2.1-point net advantage exceeds the overall average.

The data cannot tell us why, but the program's design suggests candidates. Of the 22 mentors, three speak Spanish and none speaks the other home languages represented in the caseloads. Mentor contact logs, sampled by the coordinator's office, show that home phone contact — the program's core activity — occurred about 40 percent less often for English learner students' families than for others, and that when it occurred it more often ran through the district's telephone interpretation line, which mentors described as workable for scheduled calls but poorly suited to the quick, informal check-ins that make up most of their family contact. A mentoring model built on relationships and rapid communication will underperform where language sits between the mentor and the family. This is fixable — through bilingual hiring, dedicated interpretation support, or partnership with the district's family liaison staff — but it did not fix itself in year one.

Results for students with disabilities (a 1.1-point net advantage) and students eligible for free or reduced-price meals (1.6 points) sit near the overall average, with subgroup samples large enough to say the program is not obviously failing either group but not large enough to distinguish their results sharply from the mean. Results by gender and by race/ethnicity (not tabled) likewise cluster within a point of the overall estimate, with no subgroup showing the kind of sharp deviation that English learner status shows.

### 3.5 Movers and sensitivity checks

Ninety-four students changed schools within the district mid-year — 59 mentored, 35 comparison. Mentored students who moved generally lost their mentor, since caseloads are school-based; only 14 of the 59 were picked up by a mentor at the receiving school. As noted in Section 2.2, all movers are analyzed with their original group. Re-estimating the overall effect with movers excluded raises the net estimate slightly, from 1.7 to 1.9 points, consistent with movers diluting the treatment. Re-estimating with only continuously enrolled, continuously mentored students yields 2.0 points. We report the intent-to-treat figure of 1.7 as the primary estimate because it reflects the program as it actually operates, mobility included, which is the realistic condition under which the board would fund it.

Two further checks: restricting the analysis to the three on-time schools yields a net estimate of 2.2 points, and the regression-adjusted estimate controlling for baseline attendance, school, and demographics is 1.6 points. The conclusion is stable across every specification we ran: the program's effect is real, modest, and in the range of 1.5 to 2.2 percentage points depending on how strictly one defines exposure.

## 4. Limitations

The board should weigh five limitations, in roughly descending order of importance.

**Assignment was not random.** This is the fundamental constraint on how confidently we can call the 1.7-point difference a causal effect. Schools chose which eligible students received mentors, and those choices could embed differences between the groups that our data do not capture — motivation, family engagement, the seriousness of the circumstances behind the prior year's absences. The baseline similarity of the groups, the stability of the estimate across specifications, and the dose-response pattern at Braddock Heights all support a causal reading, but a randomized assignment — which the district could implement in 2026-27 simply by lotterying caseload slots among eligible students, since the program is oversubscribed anyway — would settle the question far more decisively. We recommend it regardless of the funding decision.

**Selection on a bad year guarantees improvement in both groups.** Because eligibility required chronic absence in 2024-25, both groups were sampled at a low point, and much of their measured improvement is reversion toward their own baselines. The comparison design handles this for the net estimate, but it means the raw figures that will circulate — "mentored students improved 4.3 points" — overstate the program's contribution by roughly a factor of two and a half, before even accounting for the coding change. Communications about the program should lead with the net figure.

**The coding change contaminates year-over-year levels.** Roughly 1.5 points of every student's measured gain is an artifact of the August 2025 period-attendance rule change. This inflates both groups equally and leaves the net estimate essentially intact (our reprocessing found the inflation within 0.1 points across groups), but it means neither this year's attendance dashboards nor any single-group before-and-after comparison can be taken at face value, for this program or any other district initiative measured across the rule change.

**One school's late start limits generalizability, and the year is one year.** Braddock Heights contributes a quarter of the mentored sample under conditions the program does not intend to repeat, which pulls the overall estimate down relative to the program as designed. Conversely, everything in this brief rests on a single cohort in a single year; attendance is volatile, and a second cohort could look different. The 2026-27 grant year offers a free replication.

**Mid-year mobility and the outcome window.** Ninety-four movers blur the boundary between treated and untreated students; sensitivity checks show this matters little, but it adds noise. And attendance for 2025-26 is measured through mid-April rather than the full year. Spring attendance typically sags for chronically absent students; if mentored students hold up better in the final quarter, the estimates here are conservative, and we will update the figures with full-year data before the July vote if the board wishes.

One limitation of scope rather than method deserves mention: this study measures attendance, not learning. Whether the recovered days translate into course credits and on-track status is the question that ultimately justifies the spending, and it can be examined for this cohort once 2025-26 grades are final.

---

## 5. Implications for the July Decision

The board's question was whether the program works. The answer the evidence supports is: yes, modestly, for about half the students it serves — and the decision in July should be shaped as much by the program's pattern of effects as by its average.

**On the core question of continuation.** A net effect of 1.7 percentage points — about three days per student per year, and 2.1 to 2.2 points at the schools that started on time — is a real effect, comparable to what published evaluations of similar mentor and check-in models typically find. At an annualized local cost of roughly $640,000 for about 600 students, the district would be paying a little over $1,000 per student per year, or on the order of $350 per additional day of attendance recovered. Whether that is a good price is a judgment for the board, but it is not an unreasonable one by the standards of attendance interventions, and it buys something the district's other absence responses — letters, conferences, legal referral — have not demonstrably delivered.

**On targeting.** The strongest finding in this brief is that the effect lives almost entirely in the 10-to-19 percent prior-absence band, where the net gain is 2.8 points, and disappears above 30 percent, where it is zero. A continued program that concentrated caseloads on the moderate band would serve the students the model actually helps and would likely raise the return on every local dollar. The most severely absent students should not simply be dropped; they need a different and more intensive response — case management, health and housing referral, credit recovery pathways — that the board could fund partly from what tighter mentor targeting frees up. Continuing to spread mentors across the full eligibility range means knowingly assigning a sixth of the caseload to students the data say the model does not reach.

**On English learners.** The program as delivered produced no measurable benefit for the 110 English learners on its rosters. If the board continues funding, it should condition continuation on a concrete plan — bilingual mentor hiring as positions turn over, structured interpretation support for family contact, or formal pairing with family liaison staff — with English learner results reported separately next year.

**On execution.** The Braddock Heights experience suggests that the difference between a program that works and one that does not can be as simple as whether caseloads are assigned before the first day of school. Full-year effect at three schools; no effect at the school that started in November. If the program continues, on-time assignment should be an owned, monitored obligation at every school, not a byproduct of one administrator's calendar.

**On learning more before the money is fully committed.** The grant funds one more year regardless of the July vote. That year is an opportunity the district should not waste: randomize assignment among eligible students in the moderate band, fix the English learner supports and the assignment timeline, and measure the 2026-27 cohort with the coding rule now stable across both years. A July decision to fund continuation contingent on the 2026-27 results replicating would let the board commit in principle while reserving final judgment for evidence considerably stronger than one non-randomized cohort can provide.

The Office of Research and Evaluation will present these findings at the board's July meeting and can supply full-year attendance updates, school-level detail, or additional subgroup analyses on request.

## Technical Notes

*Sample construction.* The analytic sample includes all students enrolled as district eighth graders in 2024-25 and ninth graders in 2025-26 with at least 60 enrolled days in each year and a 2024-25 absence rate of 10 percent or higher. Students who left the district mid-year in 2025-26 are excluded from both groups; exit rates were similar (7.1 percent mentored, 7.8 percent comparison), limiting the scope for attrition bias.

*Measurement.* Attendance rates are days attended divided by days enrolled, measured over the period from the first day of school through the end of the third marking period in each year. The 2024-25 rates reflect the pre-August-2025 coding rule; 2025-26 rates reflect the current rule. Adjusted figures cited in the text reprocess 2024-25 period records under the current rule for a 25 percent sample of both groups.

*Inference.* Standard errors for group differences are clustered at the school level. The overall net estimate of 1.7 points has a 95 percent confidence interval of approximately 0.9 to 2.5 points. Band-level estimates: 10–19 percent, 1.8 to 3.8; 20–29 percent, –0.1 to 1.9; 30 percent and above, –1.4 to 1.2. The English learner net estimate's interval spans –1.2 to 1.4 points.

*Records and replication.* The analysis files, extract specifications, and verification logs are archived with the Office of Research and Evaluation and available to board members on request through the superintendent's office.
