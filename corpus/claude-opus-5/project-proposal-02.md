## 1. Executive Summary

We ask the Board to authorize a $62.3 million capital program executed over thirty months, beginning July 1 of this year. The program has three linked parts:

1. **Format conversion.** Eleven stores are rebuilt to a smaller-footprint format anchored by prepared foods, expanded fresh perimeter, and a reduced center-store assortment. Capital: $38.6 million.
2. **Distribution consolidation.** The Porterville and Lindsay distribution points are closed and their volume moves into a single 240,000-square-foot leased facility in Goshen. Capital: $19.4 million, plus assumption of a fifteen-year lease.
3. **Systems and working capital.** Replenishment, labor scheduling, and production planning systems required to run a prepared-foods business at scale. Capital: $4.3 million.

Financing is $40.0 million drawn against an expanded revolving credit facility and $22.3 million from a sale-leaseback covering four owned store sites in Hanford, Delano, Reedley, and Clovis. No new equity is contemplated and no shareholder capital call is requested.

The business case rests on a straightforward reading of our position. Six consecutive quarters of same-store sales decline, most recently negative 3.1 percent, have occurred while eleven discount stores opened inside our trade areas. We are losing the price-sensitive center-store basket and we are not going to win it back. The format conversion moves gross profit dollars toward categories where discount formats do not compete effectively and where our operators, our supplier relationships, and our customer base give us a defensible position. Distribution consolidation removes $4.9 million of annual operating cost from a three-node network that was designed for a store count and a case-mix we no longer have.

Timing is not fully within our control. The Goshen lease option expires August 1. It cannot be extended on current terms; the landlord has a competing letter of intent and has said so in writing. Losing that building does not end the consolidation case, but it delays it by an estimated eighteen to twenty-four months and raises the capital requirement by approximately $9 to $12 million, because no comparable facility exists in the corridor and the alternative is ground-up construction or a substantially inferior split-node arrangement.

We are aware this proposal asks the Board to approve a prepared-foods investment two years after a prepared-foods pilot failed. Section 4 addresses that history directly and in detail. We are also aware that the Chair and Mr. Rubén Alvarado have opposed the Porterville closure. Section 8 sets out what we can and cannot do to address that opposition, and Section 11 offers the Board a structured alternative if it wishes to approve the format conversion while deferring the distribution decision.

**The decision requested on May 21** is set out in full in Section 12. In summary, we ask for: authorization of the capital program; authorization for the CFO to execute the revolver expansion and the sale-leaseback; authorization for the Supply Chain VP to exercise the Goshen lease option before August 1; and adoption of the reporting and gate structure described in Section 10.

## 2. The State of the Business

### 2.1 What the numbers say

Sales last year were $538.0 million across 34 stores, against $551.7 million the prior year and $563.2 million the year before that. Same-store sales by quarter over the last six quarters: negative 0.8, negative 1.4, negative 2.2, negative 2.0, negative 2.7, negative 3.1. The trend is not flattening. It is steepening, and the steepening began roughly two quarters after the first cluster of discount openings.

Transaction count is down 5.4 percent year over year. Average basket is up 2.4 percent, which is almost entirely price. Units per basket are down 3.9 percent. In plain terms: fewer people are coming in, and the ones who come are buying less. The basket is holding its dollar value only because inflation has done the work.

The decline is not evenly distributed. Broken out by category:

| Category | Share of sales | Comp trend, trailing 4 quarters | Gross margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Center store dry grocery | 31% | −7.8% | 22.1% |
| Frozen | 9% | −5.1% | 24.6% |
| Dairy | 11% | −3.4% | 27.0% |
| Meat and seafood | 14% | −1.1% | 29.8% |
| Produce | 13% | +0.6% | 33.2% |
| Bakery and deli | 8% | +2.9% | 41.5% |
| Prepared and food service | 3% | +8.2% | 47.9% |
| Non-food, HBC, general merch | 11% | −9.6% | 26.3% |

Two things stand out. First, the categories bleeding fastest are precisely the categories the discount chains built their formats around: packaged dry grocery, frozen, and non-food. Second, the categories that are growing — produce, bakery, deli, prepared — are our highest-margin categories and represent only 24 percent of sales.

### 2.2 What the competitive picture says

Eleven discount stores opened inside our trade areas over the past twenty-six months. Nine of the eleven sit within 2.4 miles of one of our stores. Their assortment runs roughly 2,200 to 3,400 SKUs against our 24,000 to 31,000. Their price index on the overlapping items runs 14 to 19 percent below ours. We have run the pricing comparison quarterly since the second opening; the gap has not narrowed and we do not have a cost structure that lets us narrow it.

This is worth stating without hedging: **we cannot win a price fight on packaged center store.** Their supply chain is built for it, their labor model is built for it, their private-label penetration is above 80 percent against our 19 percent, and their store payroll per square foot is roughly a third of ours. Every dollar we have spent on promotional depth in dry grocery over the past six quarters has bought traffic that did not persist past the promotion. We have measured this. Promotional lift in center store averaged 31 percent during event weeks and post-event baseline recovered to 99.2 percent of pre-event — meaning we bought volume at negative incremental margin and retained essentially none of it.

### 2.3 What our stores actually are

Our 34 stores average 41,300 square feet. Nine were built before 1988. The median store dedicates 58 percent of selling area to center store and non-food — the two weakest category groups — and 11 percent to perimeter fresh preparation. Our sales per square foot is $384 against a regional independent benchmark of $471 and a conventional chain benchmark of $522.

We are carrying square footage that generates declining sales at declining margin, and we are heating, lighting, staffing, and stocking it.

### 2.4 What we are good at

The picture is not uniformly bad and the strategy depends on the parts that are not.

Produce comps are positive in a declining business. Our meat departments hold share against the discounters at a 14-to-19-point price disadvantage, which tells us customers are making a quality judgment and paying for it. Deli and bakery are up 2.9 percent. Prepared foods — the small, uneven, underinvested three percent of our business — grew 8.2 percent last year at a 47.9 percent margin, and it grew in stores where nobody was trying particularly hard.

We have 4,100 employees, many of them long-tenured. We have supplier relationships in the Valley that a national discounter does not have and cannot replicate quickly. We have name recognition and forty-one years of standing in these communities.

The question is not whether we have assets. It is whether we deploy them against the parts of the basket where they matter.

## 3. The Proposal: The Fresh Market Format

### 3.1 What changes

Eleven stores convert to a format we are calling Fresh Market. The physical changes:

**Footprint.** Selling area reduces from an average 41,300 square feet to approximately 28,500. The recovered space is either subleased (six sites where the configuration permits demising) or returned to the landlord (three sites where lease terms allow reduction at renewal) or converted to expanded production and cold storage (two sites).

**Assortment.** SKU count reduces from an average 27,400 to approximately 12,000. The reduction is concentrated in center store: duplicate sizes, slow-moving brands in categories with three or more national options, and the non-food and general merchandise sets which reduce by roughly 70 percent. Fresh and prepared SKUs increase.

**Prepared foods.** Each converted store gets a full production kitchen — hot line, cold prep, rotisserie, scratch bakery finishing, and a self-service and full-service hot and cold bar. Target prepared-foods penetration at maturity is 14 to 17 percent of store sales against the current 3 percent chainwide.

**Perimeter.** Produce expands roughly 40 percent in linear feet. Meat and seafood convert from primarily case-ready to a service counter with in-store cutting at nine of the eleven. Bakery converts from thaw-and-sell to par-bake and scratch finishing.

**Checkout and front end.** Reduced lane count, expanded self-checkout, and a dedicated grab-and-go transaction path for prepared purchases, which behave differently from a full grocery shop and should not queue behind one.

### 3.2 Why this answers the problem

The format is a deliberate concession on center store and a deliberate concentration on categories where we can win.

Prepared foods competes against restaurants, not against discount grocery. The relevant comparison for a customer buying a hot rotisserie chicken and two sides at $11.99 is a $34 restaurant check, not a $6.40 raw chicken. Discount formats have not entered this category meaningfully in our markets and their operating model — minimal labor, minimal perishable handling, no production — makes entry costly for them.

The margin arithmetic is the core of the case. A converted store trades roughly $2.9 million of annual center-store sales at 22 percent margin for roughly $2.1 million of prepared and fresh sales at 44 percent margin. That is a sales decline of $800,000 and a gross profit increase of approximately $286,000 per store. Add the occupancy and operating cost recovered from 12,800 fewer square feet — approximately $195,000 annually per store — and the contribution improvement before incremental labor is roughly $481,000.

Incremental labor per converted store runs approximately $310,000 annually — one kitchen manager and roughly twenty-two hourly food service positions, net of reductions in center-store stocking and front-end labor. Net contribution improvement per store: approximately $171,000 annually at maturity, which we define as month 19 post-conversion.

Across eleven stores at maturity: **approximately $1.88 million of annual contribution improvement from the format conversion alone.** The distribution consolidation contributes a further $4.9 million. Total run-rate improvement at full maturity, projected in month 42: approximately $6.8 million against a $62.3 million investment.

We want the Board to see that number clearly, because it is not a spectacular return on its own. The format conversion is not primarily a growth investment. It is a repositioning that converts a declining business into a defensible one. The alternative — Section 7 — is a business that continues to decline at 2 to 3 percent annually against competitors who are still opening stores.

## 4. What the 2024 Pilot Got Wrong

The Board pulled a three-store prepared-foods pilot in 2024 after fourteen months and $2.4 million of losses. Any request to invest in prepared foods again has to account for that. We have gone through the pilot in detail — store-level P&Ls, production records, waste logs, labor schedules, and interviews with eleven people who worked on it. Here is what we found.

### 4.1 The pilot was a bolt-on, not a conversion

The three pilot stores kept their full 27,000-SKU assortment and their full footprint. Prepared foods was added into approximately 900 square feet carved out of the deli and a corner of the frozen aisle. The stores carried the cost of the new program on top of the entire existing cost structure with no offsetting reduction.

This is the single largest error and it accounts for roughly half the loss. The economic case for prepared foods depends on trading out low-margin space and low-margin sales. The pilot did not trade anything out. It added $780,000 of annual cost per store against incremental sales that, even at a 46 percent margin, could not cover it. A conversion works because of what it removes. The pilot removed nothing.

### 4.2 Production was scaled to the wrong demand curve

Pilot stores produced to a flat daily plan built from a chain-average forecast. Actual prepared-foods demand is sharply peaked: 41 percent of daily volume in our current stores occurs between 4:30 and 7:00 p.m., with a secondary lunch peak. The pilot produced roughly even quantities across the day, which meant shortage at peak and waste after.

Recorded shrink in the pilot averaged 22.4 percent of prepared-food cost of goods. Industry benchmark for a well-run program is 6 to 9 percent. On pilot volumes, that gap alone was approximately $610,000 across the fourteen months.

### 4.3 There was no production planning system

Production quantities were set by handwritten sheets, adjusted by whoever was on shift. There was no forecasting tool, no ingredient-level requirement planning, and no linkage between production plan and replenishment order. Stores over-ordered ingredients to avoid running out, which drove both ingredient waste and working capital.

The $4.3 million systems component of this proposal exists because of this finding. Roughly $1.6 million of it is production planning and forecasting software with ingredient-level bill-of-materials and demand modeling. This is not optional infrastructure. A prepared-foods business run without it is a business run on guesses, and we ran it on guesses.

### 4.4 Staffing was wrong in kind, not just in number

The pilot staffed its kitchens by promoting deli clerks. There was no kitchen manager role, no culinary hiring, and no training program beyond a two-day vendor session. Deli work and production cooking are different jobs. Yield management, batch cooking, HACCP documentation, and labor scheduling against a production plan are skills our deli associates were never asked to have and were not taught.

Turnover in pilot kitchen positions ran 94 percent annualized. The three stores collectively went through eleven different people functioning as de facto kitchen leads in fourteen months.

This proposal budgets nineteen kitchen managers as dedicated, externally recruited or internally developed roles at a compensation level that will attract people who have run production kitchens. It budgets $840,000 for training, including a six-week program before any converted store opens and an ongoing certification structure.

### 4.5 The menu was too large and changed too often

The pilot ran 118 prepared SKUs across the three stores with a rotating weekly feature program. At the volumes those stores did, most items sold in quantities too small to produce efficiently. Fourteen items accounted for 61 percent of prepared sales; the remaining 104 accounted for 39 percent and generated the overwhelming majority of the waste.

The conversion format launches with 44 prepared SKUs, of which 30 are fixed and 14 rotate seasonally rather than weekly. Expansion beyond 44 requires demonstrated volume and is gated at the chain level, not left to store discretion.

### 4.6 It was measured against the wrong benchmark

The pilot was evaluated on prepared-food department P&L in isolation. That measurement made it look worse than it was in one respect and hid a real problem in another. It ignored basket effects — prepared purchasers in the pilot stores had baskets 23 percent larger than non-prepared purchasers, and roughly 40 percent of that difference was incremental. It also ignored that the pilot stores' center-store performance continued to deteriorate at the chain rate, meaning the program never had a chance to change store-level economics because store-level economics were never in scope.

Conversion performance will be measured at the store P&L level with defined metrics set out in Section 10.

### 4.7 What the pilot got right

Two findings from the pilot are load-bearing for this proposal and should not be lost.

First, **demand was real.** Prepared-food sales in the pilot stores grew every month for the first eleven months and reached 9.1 percent of store sales at peak, from a standing start, with a badly executed program, poor availability at peak hours, and no marketing. The customer wanted this.

Second, **the quality judgment held.** Customer research conducted in month nine scored the pilot stores' prepared offering at 4.1 out of 5 on quality and 3.2 on availability. People liked the food. They could not reliably get it.

The pilot failed on execution and on economic structure. It did not fail on demand.

## 5. Distribution Consolidation

### 5.1 The current network

We operate three distribution points:

| Facility | Sq ft | Function | Annual throughput | Operating cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Porterville | 118,000 | Dry, non-food, general merch | 2.9M cases | $8.2M |
| Lindsay | 64,000 | Produce, refrigerated cross-dock | 1.8M cases | $5.1M |
| Visalia (HQ adjacent) | 92,000 | Frozen, meat, backhaul staging | 1.4M cases | $4.4M |

Total network operating cost: $17.7 million annually against 6.1 million cases, or $2.90 per case. Regional benchmark for a single-node operation at our volume is $2.05 to $2.30.

The network was built when we had 41 stores and a different case mix. Since then we have closed seven stores, reduced center-store volume by 19 percent over three years, and grown perishable volume, which the current network handles across three buildings and requires stores to receive from three separate delivery streams on different schedules.

The specific inefficiencies:

**Duplicated fixed cost.** Three management structures, three receiving operations, three maintenance contracts, three sets of dock equipment, three yard operations. Approximately $2.4 million of annual cost is duplication.

**Cube utilization.** Porterville runs at 61 percent of rack capacity, Lindsay at 74 percent, Visalia at 68 percent. We are paying for and heating space we do not use.

**Transportation.** Stores receive 4.6 deliveries per week on average across the three streams. A consolidated operation delivers 3.1 times per week on mixed pallets. Estimated transportation savings: $1.9 million annually, primarily from reduced trip count and improved trailer cube.

**Store labor.** Each receiving event costs store labor. Reducing from 4.6 to 3.1 weekly receipts saves approximately $412,000 annually in store receiving labor across 34 stores.

**Perishable handling.** The Lindsay-to-Visalia refrigerated transfers required for meat and produce staging add a handling step and a temperature-exposure event. Consolidation eliminates both.

### 5.2 The Goshen facility

The building is 240,000 square feet on 18.4 acres, built in 2019, with 36-foot clear height, 42 dock doors, and existing refrigerated and frozen chambers totaling 58,000 square feet. It sits four miles from the Highway 99 and Highway 198 interchange, which puts it within 55 minutes of 31 of our 34 stores.

We hold an option to lease at $0.58 per square foot per month, triple net, on a fifteen-year term with two five-year renewals. Current market for comparable Central Valley distribution space is $0.71 to $0.79. The option expires **August 1** and cannot be extended. The landlord has a competing letter of intent from a third-party logistics provider and has confirmed in writing that the option will not be renewed.

Buildout capital of $19.4 million covers racking, refrigeration expansion (we need 84,000 square feet of temperature-controlled space, not 58,000), conveyor and sortation, dock equipment, WMS implementation, fleet reconfiguration, and transition costs including dual-running during cutover.

### 5.3 If the option lapses

We want to be direct about this, because it is the reason this proposal comes to the May meeting rather than the August one.

We surveyed available Central Valley distribution space in the 180,000-to-280,000 square foot range within a 40-minute radius of our store centroid. There are four candidates. Two are older buildings with 24-foot clear that would require substantially more capital to reach usable configuration and would still deliver less cube. One is committed through 2029. One is a build-to-suit opportunity requiring an estimated 22 months to occupancy at an estimated $31 to $34 million in capital, against $19.4 million for Goshen.

If the option lapses, our realistic paths are: consolidate into two nodes rather than one, capturing roughly 45 percent of the projected savings at roughly 70 percent of the capital; or wait for a build-to-suit, delaying benefit by roughly two years while continuing to absorb $17.7 million of annual network cost. Neither is fatal. Both are meaningfully worse.

## 7. Options Considered

### 7.1 Stand pat

Continue the current format and the three-node network. Invest maintenance capital only, roughly $6 to $8 million annually.

Modeling the current trend forward with the eleven known discount stores maturing and an assumed three additional openings over five years, we project same-store sales declining 2.4 to 3.2 percent annually, with sales reaching approximately $462 million in year five. Gross margin compresses roughly 90 basis points as mix shifts further toward the categories under attack. EBITDA falls from $24.1 million to approximately $12.8 million.

At $12.8 million of EBITDA against $18.4 million of existing revolver debt and normal maintenance capital, we remain solvent. We do not remain a business capable of investing in anything. And a business declining at 3 percent with no investment capacity is a business that eventually sells at a distressed multiple.

We rejected this option. We note for the record that it is the lowest-risk option in the next eighteen months and the highest-risk option over five years.

### 7.2 Compete on price

Reduce center-store retails to within 6 to 8 percent of discount pricing on a 1,200-item key value item list, funded by vendor negotiation and margin investment.

Modeled cost: $9.4 million of annual margin investment for a projected traffic recovery of 1.8 to 2.6 percent. Even at the top of that range, the gross profit dollars recovered do not cover the margin given away. This is the strategy our promotional testing already disproved at small scale.

Rejected.

### 7.3 Close underperforming stores

Close the six weakest stores, harvesting working capital and eliminating losses.

The six weakest stores generate $71 million in sales and $1.9 million of positive four-wall contribution. They are weak, not unprofitable. Closing them removes $1.9 million of contribution and strands roughly $3.1 million of overhead currently absorbed. It also removes volume from a distribution network whose problem is that it has too little volume for its cost.

Rejected as a standalone strategy. We note that if conversion economics fail to materialize, store closure becomes the fallback, and Section 10's gate structure preserves that option.

### 7.4 Third-party fulfillment

Exit self-distribution entirely and source through a wholesale distributor. We solicited indicative terms from two.

Both would service our stores at a delivered cost of goods approximately 4.9 to 6.2 percent above our current landed cost, offset by elimination of $17.7 million in network operating cost and release of roughly $14 million in distribution working capital and asset value.

The arithmetic is closer than we expected. At a 5.5 percent cost-of-goods increase on approximately $340 million of cost of goods flowing through distribution, we would pay roughly $18.7 million more for product against $17.7 million of network cost eliminated — a net penalty of about $1.0 million annually, plus the one-time benefit of asset release.

We rejected it for three reasons. First, the penalty grows if our volume declines further, because wholesale pricing tiers step down with volume and we are on the wrong side of that trend. Second, it eliminates our ability to source directly from Valley growers and producers, which is a meaningful part of what differentiates our produce and meat departments and is central to the Fresh Market format. Third, it makes us structurally dependent on a supplier who also serves competitors, at the moment we are trying to differentiate.

We would revisit this if the consolidation fails to deliver.

### 7.5 Convert stores without consolidating distribution

Approve the $38.6 million format conversion, keep the three-node network, defer the Goshen decision.

This is the option most responsive to the concerns the Chair has raised, and it is a genuinely available path. Its cost: the $4.9 million of annual distribution savings does not materialize, which removes roughly 72 percent of the program's projected benefit; the Goshen option lapses and any future consolidation costs $9 to $12 million more and arrives two years later; and the three-node network is poorly suited to the perishable and prepared-ingredient flow the converted format requires, which will add store-level cost and complexity we have not modeled.

It is not irrational. It is expensive. Section 11 sets out how it could be structured if the Board prefers it.

### 7.6 Recommended: full program

The recommended option is the combined program described in Sections 3, 5, and 6. It is recommended because the two halves reinforce each other: the format conversion needs a distribution network built for perishable and prepared-ingredient flow, and the distribution consolidation needs the volume density and simplified assortment that conversion produces.

### 8.3 Labor relations

The Porterville and Lindsay warehouses are covered under our UFCW Local 8 agreement, which runs through March 2027 and reopens then. The current agreement contains no restriction on facility closure but does contain successorship, notice, and severance provisions that we have reviewed with outside counsel. Our obligations: 90 days' written notice to the union, an obligation to bargain over the effects of the closure (not the decision itself), and severance at the contractual formula, which our estimate above reflects at 1.4x the contractual minimum.

We propose to exceed the contractual minimum deliberately. The reopener in 2027 will be a difficult negotiation regardless. Handling this closure at the minimum would make it worse, and the incremental cost of the enhanced package — approximately $410,000 — is small against what an adversarial 2027 negotiation costs.

We recommend notifying the union within ten business days of Board approval and beginning effects bargaining immediately rather than at the 90-day mark.

### 8.4 The Porterville question

The Chair and Mr. Rubén Alvarado have opposed the Porterville closure. We understand the objection to rest on the founder's origins in Porterville, the company's fifty-year presence there, and the community consequence of removing 61 jobs from a city where we are among the larger private employers.

We are not going to argue that those considerations are illegitimate. They are part of what the company is.

What we can say is this. The Porterville *store* is not closing and is not proposed to close. It remains open, staffed, and serving that community. What closes is a warehouse whose function moves 27 miles. Of the 61 positions, we project 34 to 41 will transfer to Goshen if the affected employees accept, based on our commuting analysis and prior experience with the 2019 Woodlake consolidation, where 71 percent of eligible employees transferred. The net employment reduction attributable to Porterville is therefore likely to be in the range of 20 to 27 positions, not 61.

We can also commit to the following, if the Board directs: first hiring preference at the Porterville store for any warehouse employee who declines transfer and wishes to remain in Porterville; a guaranteed offer of employment at Goshen at no less than current wage rate for every affected employee, held open twelve months; and a contribution to a Porterville workforce transition fund administered with the local community college.

What we cannot do is keep Porterville open and capture the consolidation benefit. The network's cost problem is structurally a three-building problem. Running two buildings instead of three captures roughly 45 percent of the benefit at 70 percent of the capital, as noted in Section 7.5, and Porterville is the larger and more expensive of the two closure candidates. Retaining Porterville and closing only Lindsay captures approximately $1.6 million of the $4.9 million.

We present that as a genuine option in Section 11, priced honestly, so the Board can weigh it against the considerations it finds important. We do not recommend it, but we would rather the Board choose it knowingly than have us pretend it does not exist.

## 10. Risks, Mitigations, and Gates

**Conversion economics underperform.** *Mitigation:* Wave 1 gate. Two stores convert first at $7.1 million of capital. After four months of post-conversion operation, the Board receives a report against defined thresholds: prepared-foods penetration of at least 9 percent of store sales, prepared shrink below 12 percent, store-level four-wall contribution at or above pre-conversion, and customer transaction count at or above 96 percent of pre-conversion. Failure on two or more thresholds halts Waves 2 through 4 pending Board reauthorization. Maximum exposure before the gate: $7.1 million of store capital.

**Distribution transition disrupts stores.** *Mitigation:* Sixty days of dual-running at each transfer, with Porterville and Lindsay retained and operating until Goshen demonstrates sustained fill rate above 97.5 percent and on-time delivery above 96 percent. Transition costs of $1.6 million assume this dual-run period.

**Goshen construction delay.** *Mitigation:* Fixed-price contract with liquidated damages; refrigeration is the long-lead item and is ordered in month 2 with a deposit before general construction begins.

**Covenant pressure.** *Mitigation:* The Wave 1 gate is timed to precede peak draw. Monthly covenant projections to the Audit Committee beginning month 1. If projected fixed charge coverage falls below 1.35x on a forward twelve-month basis — a 0.10 turn cushion above the covenant — capital spending suspends automatically pending Board review.

**Base business declines faster than modeled.** Every projection here assumes non-converted stores decline at 2.4 percent. *Mitigation:* We cannot mitigate this so much as monitor it. If chainwide comps at non-converted stores fall below negative 4.0 percent for two consecutive quarters, we will return to the Board with a revised plan that will likely include store closures.

**Kitchen manager recruitment fails.** *Mitigation:* Recruiting begins in month 4, thirteen months before the first Wave 2 store opens. Wave 1 requires only four managers. If we cannot recruit and certify the Wave 2 cohort of seven by month 13, Wave 2 delays rather than opening understaffed. This is a firm rule and it is the direct lesson of the pilot.

**Labor action.** *Mitigation:* Early notification, effects bargaining beginning immediately, enhanced severance, and transfer guarantees as described in Section 8.

**Sale-leaseback pricing deteriorates.** *Mitigation:* Two competing bidders; authorization sought at a floor of $21.0 million, below which the CFO returns to the Board. If the SLB cannot be executed at or above the floor, the program is resized rather than funded entirely on the revolver.

**Discount competitors respond.** *Mitigation:* Limited. The format is chosen partly because response is costly for them. We accept this risk.

## 11. Alternative Structures if the Board Prefers

**Option A — Format conversion only.** Approve $38.6 million plus $4.3 million systems; defer distribution. Goshen option lapses. Financing requirement drops to $42.9 million; sale-leaseback could reduce to two sites. Foregoes $4.9 million annual benefit; future consolidation costs $9–12 million more.

**Option B — Retain Porterville; close Lindsay only.** Approve full format conversion plus a reduced distribution program consolidating Lindsay into Goshen while Porterville continues. Capital reduces to approximately $14.8 million for distribution. Annual benefit reduces from $4.9 million to approximately $1.6 million. Twenty-two positions eliminated rather than 83. Note that this leaves Goshen substantially underutilized while paying full rent on 240,000 square feet, which we would need to address through subleasing.

**Option C — Full program with reduced Wave 1 risk.** Approve the full distribution consolidation and the systems investment, but authorize only Wave 1 store conversions ($7.1 million) with Waves 2–4 requiring separate Board authorization at the month-14 meeting. Total initial authorization: $30.8 million. This is the most conservative structure that preserves the Goshen option.

Management recommends the full program. If the Board is not prepared to authorize it, **Option C is our recommended fallback**, because it preserves the time-sensitive distribution decision while limiting store-conversion exposure to two stores.
