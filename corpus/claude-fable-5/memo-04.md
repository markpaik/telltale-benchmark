# CITY OF ASHMONT FALLS
## Lead Service Line Replacement Program — Internal Memorandum Packet

---

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** All Department Heads; Honorable Members of the City Council
**FROM:** Yolanda Priestley, City Manager
**DATE:** November 17, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Initiation of the Lead Service Line Replacement Program; Organizational Assignments and Master Schedule

This memorandum formally establishes the City of Ashmont Falls Lead Service Line Replacement Program and sets out the schedule, organizational responsibilities, and immediate obligations that will govern the City's compliance with the federal Lead and Copper Rule Improvements through the 2037 replacement deadline. I ask each department head to read this memorandum in full and to treat the assignments in it as standing direction until superseded.

On October 15, 2026, the Water Division filed the City's service line inventory with the New York State Department of Health. The inventory identified 6,140 service lines that are either confirmed lead, lead-connected galvanized, or of unknown material and therefore presumed lead for regulatory purposes. This figure is more than double the planning estimate of 2,800 lines on which our preliminary capital program, our state revolving fund application, and our early rate modeling were built. I want to be direct with the council about what that means: the scope of this program has changed fundamentally, and the financial plan that accompanied our loan application is no longer adequate to the task. Finance Director Petrosyan will bring forward a revised financing plan once we have real bid prices in hand, but no one should assume the numbers presented to council last spring still describe the program we are now required to deliver.

The federal rule requires full replacement of all lead and presumed-lead lines by the end of 2037. Against an inventory of 6,140 lines, that is an average replacement pace of roughly 614 lines per year sustained for a decade, in a system with 14,800 total accounts. Put differently, over the next ten years we will excavate and replace the service connection at better than four of every ten accounts in the system. This is the largest sustained construction program this utility has ever undertaken, and it will touch streets, sidewalks, private yards, and household plumbing in every neighborhood of the city.

**Program Organization.** Public Works Director Faisal Chaudhry will serve as program director with overall responsibility for design, procurement, construction management, and the property access and consent process. Water Superintendent Grace Muriuki will direct field operations, inventory verification, water quality sampling, and the customer notification obligations the rule imposes, and will report construction and compliance status to Mr. Chaudhry and to me on a monthly cycle. Finance Director Nadia Petrosyan owns the financing plan, the state revolving fund loan administration, the rate proposal, and the billing mechanics for the private-side charge discussed below. The Law Department will support the consent, refusal, and lien questions that the private-side work will generate; the Health Department will coordinate prioritization data with the Water Division. I will chair a program steering meeting on the second Tuesday of each month, and I expect the principals named above to attend personally.

**Schedule.** The first construction package, covering approximately 620 lines in the Millbrook and South Ashmont pressure zones, is in final design now and will be advertised for bids in January 2027, with bid opening in February and a notice to proceed targeted for late spring. Subsequent packages of roughly 600 to 650 lines each will be advertised annually, with design of each package beginning eighteen months before its construction season. Superintendent Muriuki will complete physical verification of the unknown-material lines on a rolling basis ahead of each package, because every unknown that verifies as copper or plastic is a line we do not have to replace and money we do not have to spend. I have asked her to treat verification as a first-order priority, not a byproduct of construction.

**Prioritization.** The Health Department has identified 411 households in the affected inventory with children under six years of age. Consistent with the department's request and with sound public health practice, those addresses will be prioritized within each construction package to the extent geography and contracting logistics allow, and any of the 411 addresses that fall outside the first two packages will be evaluated for accelerated standalone replacement. Superintendent Muriuki will ensure that every prioritized household receives filter kits and flushing guidance in advance of and following replacement.

**The Private-Side Question.** Roughly half of each service connection lies on private property between the curb stop and the meter. The rule requires full replacement; partial replacements that leave lead in the ground on the private side are both non-compliant in most circumstances and demonstrably harmful, because disturbance of a partially replaced line can elevate lead levels at the tap. The financing plan as currently drafted proposes to bill property owners for the private-side portion of the work, which our engineers estimate will average $3,100 per property. I am aware that the council's public utilities committee has serious reservations about this charge, and I expect that debate to continue. My direction to staff in the interim is twofold: first, design the program so that no replacement is delayed or made partial because of a billing dispute — public health comes first and cost recovery follows; second, develop the hardship, deferral, and rental-property provisions that any defensible version of this charge will require, so that the council is choosing among fully developed options rather than reacting to a bare invoice amount. Mr. Chaudhry will bring forward a consent and refusal policy in the spring; Ms. Petrosyan will address cost recovery in her revised financing memorandum.

**Communications.** Every owner and occupant of an affected address will receive the notifications the rule requires, and beyond the regulatory minimum I want this program communicated early, plainly, and repeatedly. Twenty-nine percent of affected addresses are rentals, which means the person who receives the water bill is frequently not the person drinking the water. Notification materials must reach tenants directly, not only owners of record, and must be available in Spanish as well as English.

I will report program status to the council quarterly, beginning in February 2027 with the results of the first bid opening. Questions on assignments should come to my office this week.

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Yolanda Priestley, City Manager
**FROM:** Nadia Petrosyan, Finance Director
**CC:** Faisal Chaudhry, Public Works Director; Grace Muriuki, Water Superintendent
**DATE:** March 6, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Revised Financing and Rate Plan for the Lead Service Line Replacement Program Following the February 24 Bid Opening

Bids for Construction Package 1 were opened on February 24, 2027. Three bids were received. The low bid, from Katonah Underground Utilities, Inc., prices the work at an average of $14,900 per line — 38 percent above the engineer's estimate of $10,800 per line. The second and third bids came in at $15,700 and $16,450 per line respectively, which tells me the low bid is not an outlier or an error; it is the market. This memorandum sets out what that price does to the program's finances and what I recommend we do about it.

**What the bid means at program scale.** At $14,900 per line applied across the full inventory of 6,140 lines, the total program cost is approximately $91.5 million, before escalation, before restoration overruns, and before the soft costs of design, inspection, and administration, which historically run 12 to 15 percent on work of this kind. Even assuming a meaningful share of the unknown-material lines verify as non-lead — Superintendent Muriuki's early verification data suggests perhaps a fifth may — we should plan around a program in the range of $75 to $85 million over eleven years. The financing plan council reviewed last spring assumed a program of roughly $30 million built on the old inventory count and the old unit price. That plan is dead, and I do not propose to resuscitate it with marginal adjustments.

**Current resources.** The City holds an $18 million state revolving fund loan commitment, of which $6.2 million is principal forgiveness. The repayable balance of $11.8 million carries a subsidized rate of 2.1 percent over thirty years, producing annual debt service of approximately $530,000 beginning in fiscal 2028. The forgiveness component is the cheapest money this program will ever see, and my first recommendation is that we not leave any of it stranded: the $18 million should be fully drawn against Packages 1 and 2, and we should submit a new intended-use-plan listing this spring for a second SRF round covering Packages 3 through 5. Our demographics place several of the affected census tracts within the state's disadvantaged-community criteria, which is the strongest available argument for additional principal forgiveness, and I will make it aggressively. I am also preparing applications under the federal WIIN lead reduction grant program and will pursue the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law lead set-aside funds that flow through the state; those are competitive, and I am not budgeting them until awarded.

**Revised rate recommendation.** The plan presented to council proposed an 11 percent increase phased over two years — 6 percent in fiscal 2028 and 5 percent in fiscal 2029. That structure was sized to the old program. I now recommend increases of 8 percent in fiscal 2028 and 8 percent in fiscal 2029, a cumulative increase of approximately 16.6 percent, with a rate study in fiscal 2030 to set the following biennium. For the median residential account, the fiscal 2028 increase is approximately $3.40 per month. I recognize that recommending a larger increase than previously advertised is unwelcome; the alternative is to under-fund a mandated program in its first two years and confront a far steeper correction later, after the cheap money is committed elsewhere and the contractor market has hardened further. The revised rates, combined with the SRF draws, fund Packages 1 and 2 in full and carry the debt service, design costs, and program staffing through fiscal 2029.

**The bid price itself.** I do not recommend rejecting the low bid. Rebidding costs us a construction season against a fixed federal deadline, and nothing in the bid tabulation suggests the market will return a better price in six months — the spread among the three bidders was narrow, and every utility in the region is bidding the same work against the same deadline with the same handful of qualified contractors. I do recommend, and Mr. Chaudhry concurs, that Package 2 be restructured to reduce unit cost: larger contiguous work areas to cut mobilization, owner consent secured before advertisement rather than during construction so bidders are not pricing access risk, and an alternate bid item for trenchless installation where soil conditions allow. The engineers believe these changes could recover 8 to 12 percent of the unit price. We will find out.

**The private-side charge.** The financing plan continues to assume recovery of the private-side cost, averaging $3,100 per property, from property owners. Across the program this represents approximately $19 million. I want the fiscal stakes of the committee's objection stated plainly: if the council eliminates the charge entirely, that $19 million moves onto the rate base, which is equivalent to roughly two additional percentage points of rate increase sustained across the life of the program, borne by all 14,800 accounts including the majority whose lines are not lead and who receive no direct benefit from the private-side work. That is a policy choice the council is entitled to make, but it should be made with the arithmetic in view. My office is developing, at your direction, a hardship framework — income-qualified waivers, interest-free installment billing over up to ten years, and deferral with a lien payable at transfer of the property — so that the council can consider a modified charge rather than only the binary of full charge or none. I will have that framework costed by the time Mr. Chaudhry's consent policy comes forward.

I recommend award of Package 1 to Katonah Underground at the March 24 council meeting, adoption of the revised rate ordinance on the same agenda, and authorization to submit the second SRF listing. I am available to brief the public utilities committee in advance of both.

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Yolanda Priestley, City Manager
**FROM:** Faisal Chaudhry, Public Works Director
**CC:** Grace Muriuki, Water Superintendent; Nadia Petrosyan, Finance Director; City Attorney
**DATE:** April 14, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Program Policy — Property Owner Consent, Access, and Handling of Refusals for Private-Side Service Line Replacement

With construction on Package 1 beginning in June, we need a settled, uniform policy for obtaining property owner consent to replace the private-side portion of each service line and for handling owners who refuse or cannot be reached. This memorandum sets out that policy. It has been reviewed by the City Attorney and, on the sampling and notification requirements, by Superintendent Muriuki. Unless you direct otherwise, it takes effect immediately and will govern all packages.

The starting principle is the one you set in November: no replacement will be delayed, and no partial replacement will be performed, because of a billing dispute. The federal rule effectively requires full replacement of the line from main to meter, and a partial replacement that leaves lead pipe in the ground on the private side can make water quality at that tap worse in the near term, not better. Consent and cost recovery are therefore separated in this policy. The access agreement an owner signs authorizes the City's contractor to enter the property and replace the line; it is not conditioned on agreement about the charge, and the charge is not collected at signing. An owner who signs the access agreement while disputing the charge gets the replacement, and the dispute goes to Finance afterward. We will not put a field crew in the position of negotiating money on a front porch.

**Obtaining consent.** Each affected address will receive a consent packet by mail no later than 120 days before its scheduled construction window, containing the access agreement, a plain-language description of the work, the restoration commitment described below, the estimated private-side charge and the payment options Finance is developing, and the health information the rule requires. Where the property is a rental — 29 percent of the inventory — the packet goes to the owner of record at the tax billing address, and a separate occupant notice goes to the property address, because the tenant needs the health information and the construction schedule regardless of who signs the agreement. Non-responders get a second mailing at 60 days, a door hanger and in-person visit at 30 days, and a final certified letter. That sequence — four documented attempts through at least two methods — matches what the rule and state guidance expect us to demonstrate before an address can be classified as a refusal, and every attempt will be logged in the program database by date, method, and outcome. Addresses on the Health Department's under-six priority list get an additional in-person visit from Water Division staff rather than the contractor's outreach subconsultant, and I have asked the Health Department to make its community health workers available for those visits where the household is already known to them.

**Restoration.** Half the refusals in comparable programs are really disputes about lawns, driveways, and landscaping. The access agreement commits the City to like-for-like restoration of disturbed surfaces — pavement, concrete, sod, and plantings of reasonable value — with photographic documentation of pre-construction conditions at every address. The contractor's restoration performance is a pay-item with retainage, and a restoration complaint line will be staffed in my office, not the contractor's. Owners will be told all of this in the first mailing, in those words.

**Handling refusals.** An owner who affirmatively refuses after the full contact sequence, or who cannot be reached after it, will be documented as a refusal in the inventory as the rule provides. The public-side portion will not be replaced in isolation; the address will be deferred, not partially replaced. Every refused address receives a certified letter stating that a lead line remains in service, enclosing filter information and health guidance, and stating that the City will return in future packages — refusal is not permanent removal from the program, and each refused address will be re-solicited at least once every construction season. Where the refused address is a rental, the occupant receives the same health notice directly, and the Law Department is examining whether the City's rental registration ordinance can require disclosure of a known lead service line to prospective tenants; I recommend we pursue that. Where the refused address houses a child under six, the Health Department will be notified for direct follow-up.

Two categories require escalation beyond this policy. First, absentee or unresponsive rental owners: where an occupied rental has a confirmed lead line and the owner cannot be brought to consent after two full solicitation cycles, I will bring the Law Department a recommendation on compulsory access, whether through the existing plumbing code's authority over connections to the public system or through an ordinance amendment. I would rather have that authority and rarely use it than watch tenants drink through lead pipe because a landlord in another state will not return a certified letter. Second, vacant and abandoned properties, of which the inventory contains an estimated 140: these will be handled through the existing nuisance abatement process, with replacement cost added to the abatement lien.

I want to flag one operational change that supports Ms. Petrosyan's cost concerns: beginning with Package 2, consent will be secured before advertisement, and the bid documents will list only consented addresses, with a unit-price mechanism to add late consents by change order. Bidders priced access uncertainty into Package 1; we should not pay for that uncertainty twice.

Mr. Sub-inspectors and outreach staff will be trained on this policy the week of May 12. I will report consent rates to the monthly steering meeting; my working target for Package 1 is 90 percent consent by notice to proceed, and I will tell you promptly if we are not going to hit it.

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Faisal Chaudhry, Public Works Director; Yolanda Priestley, City Manager
**FROM:** Grace Muriuki, Water Superintendent
**CC:** Nadia Petrosyan, Finance Director
**DATE:** October 30, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Program Status After the First 400 Replacements — Production, Field Findings, and Water Quality Sampling Results

Construction on Package 1 began June 8. As of October 24, the contractor has completed 400 full service line replacements, and this memorandum reports on production rates, what we are learning underground, and the post-replacement water quality sampling program. On the whole the news is better than the bid price led us to fear, with two exceptions I address at the end.

**Production.** Katonah Underground is running three crews. In June, the crews averaged 1.4 completed lines per crew per day, well below the 2.0 assumed in the schedule. The causes were the ordinary ones of a program's first weeks — utility locates arriving late, meter pit conditions differing from records, and crews learning the paperwork. By September the average had reached 2.3 lines per crew per day, and the best crew is consistently completing three when the addresses are contiguous. At current rates the contractor will complete the consented Package 1 addresses by the end of November, roughly three weeks behind the baseline schedule but recoverable within the season. The single biggest driver of productivity is contiguity: when the crew moves door to door along a block, mobilization time collapses. This validates the Package 2 restructuring, and I recommend we go further and give the contractor authority to sequence within each work area rather than dictating order from the office.

**Field findings on the inventory.** Of the 400 completed addresses, 92 were classified as unknown material going in. On excavation, 21 of the 92 — just under 23 percent — proved to be copper for the full length and required no replacement beyond the exploratory pit. Separately, my staff has verified 310 additional unknowns ahead of construction by potholing and interior inspection, with a similar non-lead rate. If 20 to 23 percent holds across the roughly 2,900 unknowns in the inventory, the true replacement obligation is closer to 5,500 lines than 6,140, which is worth several million dollars and should be reflected in Finance's next model run. On the other side of the ledger, 34 addresses classified as lead-only turned out to have galvanized interior plumbing stubs or galvanized sections downstream of the lead that the rule requires us to replace as well; these added an average of $1,700 per affected address in change orders. Net, the field findings are favorable, but the inventory is a forecast, not a fact, and every package will contain surprises in both directions.

**Priority households.** Of the 411 households with children under six, 96 fell within Package 1's geography, and 91 of those are complete; the remaining five include two scheduling conflicts and three consent refusals, all of which the Health Department is following up in person. Every priority household received a certified pitcher filter and six months of replacement cartridges before construction, and my staff, not the contractor, made those deliveries.

**Water quality sampling.** Replacement disturbs the line, and lead levels at the tap typically spike in the days immediately following the work before declining well below pre-replacement levels. Our protocol, which meets and exceeds the rule's requirements, is as follows: every household receives a high-velocity flushing instruction sheet and an in-person walkthrough at completion; every household receives a six-month supply of filter cartridges with instructions to filter all water for cooking and drinking during that period; and we collect a tap sample at each address between three and seven days after replacement and a follow-up sample at approximately three months. Results to date: of the 400 completed addresses, 361 first-round samples have been collected and analyzed. 303 of those — 84 percent — came back below 5 parts per billion, and 41 more fell between 5 and 10 ppb. Seventeen addresses exceeded 10 ppb on the first sample, which is the disturbance spike the literature predicts; all seventeen received immediate re-notification, confirmation of filter use, and an accelerated resample, and of the eleven resampled so far, ten have dropped below 5 ppb. One address remains elevated at the resample and is under investigation; the likely culprit is interior lead solder, which is outside the program's scope but not outside our health obligation, and the Health Department has been engaged. Three-month sampling begins in earnest in November and I will report those results in the winter status memo. Nothing in the data so far suggests a systemic problem; it suggests the protocol is working.

**Two problems.** First, restoration is running about four weeks behind pipe work. The contractor's restoration subcontractor is under-crewed, and we have 60-odd addresses with temporary asphalt patch or unseeded lawn heading into winter. Mr. Chaudhry's office has issued a cure notice and is holding restoration retainage; I flag it because restoration complaints are the face of this program on every block, and the consent rates for Package 2 will be earned or lost on how Package 1 yards look next spring. Second, consent: Package 1 closed at 87 percent consent against the 90 percent target. The 41 non-consenting addresses skew heavily toward absentee rental owners, exactly as Mr. Chaudhry's April policy anticipated, and I support moving forward with the compulsory-access recommendation for occupied rentals. Every one of those 41 addresses received the certified refusal letter and filter information, and each will be re-solicited with Package 2.

I am available to walk the steering committee through the sampling data in detail at the November meeting.

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Councilman Warren DuBois, Chair, Public Utilities Committee
**FROM:** Yolanda Priestley, City Manager
**CC:** Honorable Members of the City Council; Nadia Petrosyan, Finance Director; Faisal Chaudhry, Public Works Director
**DATE:** November 20, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Response to Your November 6 Written Objection to the Private-Side Replacement Charge

Thank you for your November 6 letter setting out the committee's objection to billing property owners for the private-side portion of service line replacement. You have raised the objection with care and in writing, and it deserves an answer in kind. I will take your arguments in order, tell you where I think you are right, where I think the arithmetic pushes back, and what I am prepared to recommend to the full council as a result.

Your first argument is that the charge is regressive — that $3,100 lands hardest on the households least able to pay it, and that lead lines are concentrated in the city's older and lower-income neighborhoods, so the burden falls precisely where the capacity is thinnest. On the facts, you are right, and nothing in this memorandum disputes it. The inventory maps closely onto pre-1950 housing stock, and pre-1950 housing stock maps closely onto the census tracts with the city's lowest median incomes. Any version of this charge that ignores that reality is indefensible, and I have never proposed one that does. The question is whether the answer to regressivity is elimination of the charge or a hardship structure that removes the burden from those who cannot bear it while retaining it for those who can. I come out for the latter, for reasons of arithmetic I will get to.

Your second argument concerns the 29 percent of affected addresses that are rentals: the owner receives the invoice, but the tenant drinks the water, and the owner will pass the cost through in rent, so the charge is ultimately paid by tenants who had no choice in the matter and captured by owners as an improvement to their asset. This is your strongest point, and I will not pretend the pass-through concern is speculative — a $3,100 capital charge on a small rental property will find its way into rent where the market allows. But note that eliminating the charge does not eliminate the transfer; it changes who funds it. Under your proposal, the rate base — all 14,800 accounts, including tenants citywide who pay water charges directly or through rent — funds a permanent improvement to the landlord's property. The tenant equity problem exists under both structures. It is a reason to be angry at neither structure but at the underlying situation, and to shape the policy so that owner-occupants of modest means and tenants are protected while owners of income-producing property, for whom this is a deductible capital expense on an appreciating asset, pay for the improvement to it.

Your third argument is fiscal precedent: that some cities — you cite Newark and Denver — replaced private sides at no direct charge to owners. True, and worth studying, but those programs were financed by resources we do not have: Newark through a county-guaranteed bond issue of a size our balance sheet cannot support, Denver through a rate base and a special regulatory arrangement an order of magnitude beyond ours. Here is our arithmetic, which Director Petrosyan has verified. The private-side cost across the program is approximately $19 million. Absorbing it into rates adds roughly two percentage points to the rate increase, sustained across the life of the program, on top of the 8-and-8 the council adopted in March. That falls on every account in the system, including the roughly 8,700 accounts that have no lead line, will get no construction on their property, and in many cases already paid privately to replace their own lines over the years. There is a genuine fairness argument on their side of the ledger too, and as manager I am obliged to put it in front of you.

Here is what I am prepared to recommend to the full council, and I would rather bring it jointly with your committee than over its objection. First, a full waiver of the private-side charge for owner-occupied households at or below 80 percent of area median income, funded first from SRF principal forgiveness attributable to disadvantaged-community tracts and any WIIN grant award, which exists for exactly this purpose. Second, for all other owner-occupants, interest-free installment billing over up to ten years — roughly $26 a month — with a no-questions deferral option that records the balance as a lien payable only on sale or transfer, so that no one is ever forced to pay before the property itself yields the money. Third, retention of the full charge, on standard installment terms, for non-owner-occupied and corporate-owned rental properties, coupled with the rental disclosure requirement Mr. Chaudhry's office has recommended and the compulsory-access ordinance for unresponsive landlords. Fourth, an annual report to your committee on waiver uptake, deferral balances, and collection experience, so the policy can be corrected with data rather than defended with pride.

Under this structure, Director Petrosyan estimates net cost recovery of $11 to $13 million rather than $19 million — real money surrendered to equity, deliberately — while sparing the rate base the full two-point increase and sparing low-income owner-occupants the charge entirely. I do not claim it resolves every tension in your letter. I claim it is better than either pole of the current debate, and that after 400 completed replacements and 84 percent of first-round samples below 5 parts per billion, this program has earned a financing structure the whole council can defend in public. I ask that the committee take it up at its December 9 meeting, and I will attend personally, with Directors Petrosyan and Chaudhry, to answer whatever you and your colleagues put to us.
