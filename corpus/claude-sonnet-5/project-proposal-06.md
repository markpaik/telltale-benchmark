---

## 1. Purpose of This Document

This proposal asks the Board of Directors to approve a $23.7 million capital plan that closes the obstetric unit at Bison Ridge Critical Access Hospital, consolidates all system obstetric delivery volume onto the Enid campus, and converts the vacated Bison Ridge space into a 14-bed inpatient behavioral health unit. It is brought forward because the current configuration is financially unsustainable, clinically fragile, and no longer matches where the system's most acute unmet need — behavioral health boarding in the Enid emergency department — actually sits. The document lays out the volumes and financial position driving this recommendation, the March 2026 CMS survey finding on medical-surgical staffing at Bison Ridge, the design and staffing of the proposed consolidation and new unit, the capital structure and funding sources, a phased 26-month schedule, the alternatives management considered and rejected, the access and community risks this plan creates along with mitigations, and the specific approval being requested of the Board on October 2.

Management is not bringing this recommendation lightly. It closes a service line in a community that has had one for decades, and it does so against organized local opposition. The Board should approve it because the alternative — continuing to operate an obstetric unit that delivered 96 babies against a break-even volume of 240, while 1,400 psychiatric boarding hours accumulate annually in the Enid emergency department with no inpatient behavioral health capacity anywhere in the system — is not a neutral choice to preserve the status quo. It is a choice to keep absorbing $3.1 million in annual losses at Bison Ridge while declining to build capacity for a documented and worsening community health crisis 52 miles away.

## 2. Why Now: Volumes, Finances, and the Survey Finding

### 2.1 System Financial Position

Cimarron Valley Health System's operating margin stands at negative 2.8 percent system-wide. The system cannot absorb indefinite losses in a single service line while other clinical needs go unaddressed. Every month this decision is deferred, the system operates further behind where it needs to be to remain viable as an independent system serving north-central Oklahoma.

### 2.2 Bison Ridge Obstetric Volume

Bison Ridge Critical Access Hospital delivered 96 babies in the last twelve months. Break-even volume for a four-obstetrician unit at that facility is 240 deliveries annually — the unit is operating at 40 percent of the volume required to sustain itself. Left unchanged, the unit is projected to lose $3.1 million next year, a loss trajectory that has been worsening for three consecutive years as regional birth volumes have declined and patients increasingly bypass Bison Ridge for larger facilities.

This volume erosion is not simply a market preference problem management can fix with marketing or scheduling changes. It reflects a structural reality of low-volume obstetric units nationally: units delivering fewer than 200 babies annually face compounding difficulty maintaining nursing competency, physician call coverage, and anesthesia availability, which in turn depresses volume further as patients and referring providers lose confidence in the unit. Bison Ridge is caught in that cycle.

### 2.3 The Physician Coverage Cliff

The volume problem is about to become a coverage problem. Two of Bison Ridge's four obstetricians have contracts that end in June 2027. Recruiting obstetricians to a 52-bed critical access hospital delivering fewer than 100 babies a year, in a rural Oklahoma market, is difficult under the best of circumstances; recruiting into a program board members and the community know is under financial strain is materially harder. Management has no credible pathway to replace these two physicians and sustain safe call coverage with the remaining two. Absent this plan, the system faces a real possibility of an unplanned obstetric closure at Bison Ridge in mid-2027 — with none of the transition planning, staff redeployment, or patient communication that a deliberate, board-approved consolidation allows.

### 2.4 The March 2026 CMS Validation Survey

In March 2026, a CMS validation survey cited Bison Ridge for nurse staffing deficiencies on the medical-surgical floor. The citation reflects the same underlying dynamic driving the obstetric volume decline: a small facility struggling to maintain adequate depth of nursing staff across multiple service lines simultaneously. Obstetric nurses at Bison Ridge currently cross-cover medical-surgical shifts to fill gaps, which strains both units and was a contributing factor identified in the survey. A facility management cannot adequately staff today, with 31 nurses split across obstetric and medical-surgical coverage, is not a facility that can safely absorb declining physician coverage in 2027. The survey finding is corrective-action-plan status now; management does not want the Board's first exposure to a serious safety event at Bison Ridge to come after this plan was tabled.

### 2.5 Psychiatric Boarding at Enid

While Bison Ridge's obstetric unit has been shrinking, a different crisis has been growing 52 miles away. The Enid emergency department logged 1,400 psychiatric boarding hours last year — patients in mental health crisis held in ED beds, averaging 31 hours per patient, because the system has no inpatient behavioral health unit and the nearest inpatient psychiatric program is in Oklahoma City, over two hours away. Thirty-one hours is not a wait; it is a multi-day stay in a space clinically and physically unsuited to psychiatric care, delivered by ED staff who are not trained as behavioral health specialists, at a direct cost to ED throughput, staff burnout, and patient dignity. This is the most acute unmet clinical need in the system today, and it exists in the same community, and arguably the same square footage economics, as the underused obstetric capacity 52 miles away.

## 3. What Is Proposed

### 3.1 Consolidation of Obstetric Services to Enid

All inpatient obstetric delivery volume currently served at Bison Ridge moves to the Enid campus. The Enid obstetric unit has existing capacity to absorb Bison Ridge's 96 annual deliveries without new construction; current Enid delivery volume leaves sufficient bed and staffing headroom, and the unit already operates with full-time anesthesia and neonatal coverage that Bison Ridge cannot independently sustain. Bison Ridge's obstetric unit closes to inpatient deliveries. Bison Ridge retains outpatient prenatal and postpartum visit capacity, and the emergency department at Bison Ridge remains staffed and equipped to stabilize and transfer an obstetric emergency, consistent with EMTALA obligations and existing regional transfer protocols with Enid.

### 3.2 New 14-Bed Inpatient Behavioral Health Unit

The vacated Bison Ridge obstetric space is renovated into a 14-bed inpatient behavioral health unit — a locked, licensed psychiatric unit built to current ligature-resistant and life-safety code standards for behavioral health construction. Fourteen beds is sized against documented system-wide boarding demand (1,400 boarding hours annually translates to a recurring daily census this unit is designed to absorb, with room to grow as demand from surrounding counties is captured) and against what Bison Ridge's physical plant and staffing base can realistically support in its first years of operation.

Locating the unit at Bison Ridge rather than at Enid uses space that is otherwise becoming a stranded asset, preserves Bison Ridge as a full-service critical access hospital with a licensed inpatient unit rather than converting it to an emergency-department-only facility, and gives the community a concrete answer to "what replaces obstetrics" rather than an abstract promise. It also physically separates acute psychiatric care from the Enid ED, reducing the risk that inpatient psychiatric beds simply become a new source of ED boarding for medical-surgical overflow.

### 3.3 Licensure and Regulatory Steps

The behavioral health unit requires a new facility license from the Oklahoma State Department of Health as a psychiatric inpatient unit, a Certificate of Need review (Oklahoma does not require CON for this bed type, but a market and need justification filing is required as part of licensure), Joint Commission behavioral health accreditation survey, and CMS Conditions of Participation certification specific to psychiatric units, including a distinct psychiatric medical record and treatment planning framework. Management estimates licensure and certification processes run in parallel with construction and require substantial completion of the physical unit before survey can occur — reflected in the schedule in Section 5.

### 3.4 Staffing Plan

CNO Danielle Kirkpatrick's staffing plan has two components:

**Redeployment.** The 31 nursing FTEs currently at Bison Ridge (obstetric and cross-covering medical-surgical staff) are offered redeployment. Some will move into behavioral health roles at the new unit following required psychiatric nursing orientation and competency training; others will fill open medical-surgical and obstetric positions at Enid created by consolidated volume; a subset with critical access hospital-specific skill sets will remain at Bison Ridge supporting its continuing ED, outpatient, and swing-bed services. No Bison Ridge nurse loses employment as a direct result of this plan; redeployment is offered to all 31 before any external behavioral health recruitment proceeds.

**New recruitment.** The behavioral health unit requires 22 new staff beyond redeployed nursing capacity: a psychiatrist medical director, psychiatric nurse practitioner or additional psychiatrist coverage for after-hours, licensed clinical social workers, psychiatric technicians, and unit support staff. This is the single highest-risk element of the staffing plan. The nearest existing inpatient psychiatric program is in Oklahoma City, meaning the system is recruiting into a market with no local pipeline of experienced psychiatric inpatient staff. CMO Rajiv Mehrotra and CNO Kirkpatrick have begun preliminary conversations with a locum psychiatry staffing firm and with two rural-psychiatry fellowship programs as bridge strategies, detailed further in Section 7.

## 6. Alternatives Considered

Management evaluated three alternatives to the recommended plan before bringing this proposal forward.

### 6.1 Freestanding Birth Center at Bison Ridge

A midwife-led freestanding birth center, licensed separately from the hospital, was evaluated as a way to preserve local delivery services without hospital-level obstetric staffing burdens. This was rejected for three reasons: Oklahoma licensure for freestanding birth centers requires transfer agreements and proximity standards that would still require rapid transport to Enid for any complicated delivery, providing limited practical improvement over the current 52-mile transfer distance for high-risk cases; birth centers are restricted to low-risk deliveries only, and Bison Ridge's declining volume base is not sufficient to support the certified nurse-midwife staffing (typically requiring 2-3 FTE midwives plus backup coverage) a freestanding center needs to be safe and financially viable; and it does not resolve the underlying nurse staffing deficiency cited in the March CMS survey, since medical-surgical coverage needs at Bison Ridge remain unchanged. This option was judged clinically thinner and financially no more sustainable than the current unit, without solving the behavioral health capacity gap.

### 6.2 Telehealth-Supported Obstetrics

A model retaining a reduced Bison Ridge OB presence supported by telehealth consultation with Enid-based maternal-fetal medicine was evaluated. Telehealth can strengthen prenatal risk assessment and support decision-making around transfer timing, and management recommends retaining telehealth prenatal consultation as a complementary service regardless of the Board's decision on this proposal. However, telehealth does not deliver a baby. It does not resolve the physician coverage cliff created by the June 2027 contract expirations, and it does not address the nurse staffing citation. This alternative was judged useful as a supplement, not as a substitute for consolidation.

### 6.3 Status Quo / Defer Decision

Continuing to operate the Bison Ridge obstetric unit unchanged while deferring a decision on behavioral health capacity was evaluated and rejected as the highest-risk path available to the Board. It guarantees the projected $3.1 million loss next year, leaves the system exposed to an unplanned closure when physician contracts lapse in June 2027 with none of the transition planning this proposal provides, does nothing to remediate the staffing pattern cited by CMS, and leaves 1,400 hours of annual psychiatric boarding — and the safety, throughput, and staff burnout costs that come with it — entirely unaddressed. Deferral is not neutral; it is a decision to accept known and worsening costs on both ends of this problem while forgoing the option to plan the transition on the system's own terms.

### 6.4 Partial Consolidation Without Behavioral Health Conversion

Management also considered closing Bison Ridge obstetrics and simply mothballing or leasing the vacated space, without committing capital to a behavioral health unit. This was rejected because it captures only the cost-avoidance half of the plan's rationale while abandoning the highest-value use of the resulting capacity and staff. It would also leave the system's most severe unaddressed clinical gap — psychiatric boarding — unaddressed indefinitely, with no comparably ready alternative site or community rationale for locating a future behavioral health unit anywhere else in the system.

## 7. Access and Community Risk, With Mitigations

The Board should not approve this plan without a clear-eyed accounting of what it costs the community west and northwest of Bison Ridge, and how management intends to manage those costs. This is the section that deserves the Board's closest attention.

### 7.1 Obstetric Access Risk

CMO Rajiv Mehrotra's analysis indicates that 41 percent of the system's service area would sit more than 45 minutes from a delivery room following this consolidation. In an obstetric emergency — placental abruption, cord prolapse, postpartum hemorrhage — 45 minutes is a clinically meaningful distance, and this is the plan's most serious and least fully mitigable risk. Management is not able to eliminate this risk; it can only reduce and manage it.

**Mitigations:**
- **Bison Ridge ED remains open and staffed** to stabilize obstetric emergencies and manage rapid transfer, with dedicated obstetric transfer protocols developed jointly by Enid and Bison Ridge clinical leadership, including standing orders for common emergencies and a dedicated transfer line to Enid Labor & Delivery.
- **Enhanced EMS coordination**, including advance notification protocols so that a patient in labor identified as high-risk is routed directly to Enid rather than to Bison Ridge, and exploration of a dedicated obstetric transport unit or mutual aid agreement with regional EMS providers serving the northwest county.
- **Expanded prenatal risk stratification**, using telehealth maternal-fetal medicine consultation (Section 6.2) to identify high-risk pregnancies early and proactively plan delivery at Enid rather than relying on emergency transfer.
- **Community childbirth education and pre-registration outreach** so that patients in the affected service area have delivery plans, transportation plans, and in some cases lodging arrangements near Enid established well before their due dates, particularly for patients living furthest from either facility.
- **Monitoring**: management commits to tracking obstetric transfer times, adverse outcomes, and EMS response data in the affected service area on a quarterly basis for the first three years post-closure, reporting results to the Board's quality committee, with a defined escalation path if data indicate the mitigations are insufficient.

None of these measures fully closes a 45-minute gap. The Board should understand that this plan accepts a real increase in obstetric transport risk for a defined population in exchange for financial sustainability and a new behavioral health service. That tradeoff is the central judgment this proposal asks the Board to make.

### 7.2 Community and Political Risk

Board Chair Loretta Pahsetopah has received petitions from the county commission and a citizens group opposing the closure. This opposition is organized, legitimate, and should be expected to continue and likely intensify between now and the October 2 vote, and through the 18-month gap between OB closure and behavioral health opening described in Section 5.

**Mitigations:**
- **Direct, early community engagement**, including town halls in the Bison Ridge service area before and after any Board vote, led jointly by CEO Trevino, CMO Mehrotra, and Board Chair Pahsetopah, with the access mitigation plan (7.1) presented in full rather than summarized.
- **Transparent communication of the behavioral health unit's community benefit**, since psychiatric boarding is a system-wide problem that also affects Bison Ridge-area residents who currently have no closer alternative than Oklahoma City for a family member in psychiatric crisis; the community should understand this plan is not simply a closure, but a service reallocation toward a documented, growing local need.
- **County and state government engagement**, including briefing the county commission directly on the financial and clinical case in Sections 2 and 3, and exploring whether county or state rural health transportation grant funding could offset EMS/transport mitigation costs in Section 7.1.
- **Retention of the Bison Ridge critical access hospital designation and continued investment**, ensuring the community understands this plan does not represent system disinvestment from Bison Ridge broadly — the facility gains a new licensed inpatient unit and retains its ED, outpatient, and swing-bed services.

### 7.3 Staffing Recruitment Risk

Recruiting a psychiatrist and 22 behavioral health staff into a market with no existing local psychiatric inpatient pipeline is a genuine execution risk to the Phase 4 timeline.

**Mitigations:**
- Locum tenens psychiatric coverage as a bridge during initial recruitment and ramp-up.
- Partnership discussions with rural psychiatry fellowship and residency programs (including loan repayment or stipend incentives funded partly through the capital budget's staffing transition line) to build an ongoing pipeline rather than a one-time recruitment push.
- Competitive compensation benchmarking against Oklahoma City and Tulsa inpatient psychiatric market rates, recognizing the system will need to pay a premium to recruit into a rural setting.
- Cross-training a subset of redeployed Bison Ridge nurses into psychiatric nursing roles, reducing total external recruitment need and building organizational familiarity with the new unit ahead of opening.

### 7.4 Funding Risk

The foundation campaign's $2.1 million pledged against a $6.2 million goal is the weakest link in the capital stack.

**Mitigations:**
- The Phase 3 checkpoint (Section 5) requires the campaign to reach 75 percent of goal by month 12 of construction or the funding plan returns to the finance committee for reassessment before further capital commitment.
- CFO Blomqvist will present bond structuring options that preserve flexibility if the foundation campaign underperforms, including phased bond drawdown tied to campaign milestones rather than full issuance at closing.
- Management recommends the Board formally task the Foundation board with an accelerated major-gifts strategy specifically for this campaign, given the community-benefit narrative in 7.2 provides a stronger fundraising case than a generic capital appeal.

## 9. The Decision Requested on October 2

Management requests that the Board of Directors approve, at its October 2, 2026 meeting, the following specific actions:

1. **Approve closure of inpatient obstetric services at Bison Ridge Critical Access Hospital**, effective with the conclusion of current obstetric physician contracts in June 2027, and approve consolidation of all system obstetric delivery volume onto the Enid campus.

2. **Approve capital authorization of $23.7 million** for the combined obstetric consolidation and behavioral health unit development, per the budget in Section 4.

3. **Authorize CFO Blomqvist to proceed with structuring and issuing up to $14 million in tax-exempt bonds**, subject to finance committee review of final terms and covenant compliance projections, with phased drawdown tied to the Phase 3 foundation campaign checkpoint described in Section 5.

4. **Direct the Foundation to launch the public phase of a $6.2 million capital campaign** in support of the behavioral health unit, with quarterly progress reporting to the Board and the Phase 3 checkpoint (75% of goal by month 12 of construction) as a formal go/no-go review point.

5. **Approve development of a 14-bed licensed inpatient behavioral health unit** in the vacated Bison Ridge obstetric space, per the design, staffing, and licensure plan in Section 3, targeted to open in December 2028.

6. **Direct management to execute the access and community risk mitigation plan in Section 7** in full, including quarterly reporting to the Board's quality committee on obstetric transfer outcomes in the affected service area for at least three years following closure.

7. **Authorize CEO Trevino and Board Chair Pahsetopah to lead direct community and county government engagement** on the transition, beginning immediately following Board approval and continuing through the full 26-month implementation period.

Management recommends approval. This plan asks the Board to make a difficult tradeoff — reduced obstetric access for a defined rural population in exchange for financial sustainability and a new behavioral health service addressing the system's most acute unmet clinical need — with eyes open to the risk that tradeoff carries, and with concrete mitigations and monitoring commitments attached to it. We believe it is the most responsible path available to a system that cannot continue absorbing a $3.1 million annual loss at 40 percent of break-even volume, cannot safely staff a unit CMS has already cited once this year, and cannot continue leaving psychiatric patients boarding for an average of 31 hours in an emergency department not built to care for them.

We look forward to the finance committee's review on September 18 and to the Board's discussion and vote on October 2.
