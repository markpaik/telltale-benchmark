**Border Commerce Institute · Laredo, Texas**
**Prepared by Ana Lucia Zepeda, PhD, Director of Research**
**Screening and extraction: Fitzgerald Boakye, Research Analyst**
**Member Advisory Committee liaison: Roseanne Kitcheyan**
**July 2026**

## 1. Introduction: The Question and Its Scope

Twenty-four carriers fund this institute. Together they operate 6,900 power units across a mix of dry van, refrigerated, and cross-border drayage work, and last year their over-the-road divisions turned over 91 percent of their driver seats. Two members lost anchor customers after service failures that both attributed, in their post-mortems, to unseated trucks. The institute holds a $400,000 state workforce grant that must be obligated to an intervention before the fiscal year closes. That combination — a measurable operational problem, a contested internal diagnosis, and a spending deadline — is what produced this review.

The question we set is narrower than "why do drivers quit." It is this: **for truckload carriers operating between roughly 100 and 800 tractors, what does the peer-reviewed and government research literature actually establish about the causes of over-the-road driver separation, and which of those causes are tractable to a carrier-level intervention of limited size and duration?** We are not asking what the industry believes. We are asking what has been measured, under what conditions, with what identification strategy, and whether the measurements agree.

The distinction matters because the internal debate at this institute has hardened around a single proposition. Our member advisory chair, who runs a 210-truck fleet out of Deming and has held her turnover below 60 percent for three consecutive years, holds that pay is the only variable that matters and that everything else in the retention literature is decoration. That position deserves a serious hearing rather than a polite dismissal, and one purpose of this review is to test it against the accumulated evidence rather than against the opinions of the people in the room. The short version of what follows is that the evidence neither confirms nor refutes her claim; it shows that pay is the most reliably estimated lever, that its effects are real but decay, and that its measured potency depends heavily on whether a study controlled for the scheduling and time-use conditions under which the pay is earned. Whether that constitutes "the only variable that matters" is partly a semantic question and partly an empirical one that the literature has not resolved.

Scope limits are worth stating at the outset. We restricted the review to over-the-road and regional truckload operations in North America, with European studies admitted only where they addressed constructs — occupational commitment, detention, safety climate — for which the North American literature is thin. We excluded less-than-truckload and private fleet studies except as comparison cases, because the compensation architecture and dispatch discipline in those segments differ enough that transferring estimates would be unsound. We excluded studies of crash causation that did not also report on workforce outcomes, and studies of autonomous vehicle displacement, which address a different time horizon than a grant that expires in eleven weeks.

We also want to be clear about what a literature review can and cannot deliver here. The evidence base is genuinely contradictory in places, and we have not smoothed those contradictions over. Where two credible bodies of work disagree, we say so and attempt to explain why. Where the literature is silent — and it is silent on a great deal that matters to our members, including owner-operator retention, women drivers, and short-haul cross-border work — we say that too, because a grant spent on the strength of evidence that does not exist is worse than a grant spent on a coin flip.

## 2. Note on the Search

The search was executed by Fitzgerald Boakye between February and May 2026 across three sources: a transportation research database covering the major logistics, transportation economics, and traffic safety journals; Scopus, for spillover into occupational health, industrial relations, and organizational behavior; and the federal motor carrier research report series, including contractor studies, inspector general audits, and congressional accountability office reviews. The window was 1990 through 2026. We began at 1990 deliberately. Studies of driver labor conditions before that date sit inside a regulatory regime — pre-deregulation rate structures and union density — that no longer describes the market our members operate in, and the transitional literature of the 1980s is more useful as history than as evidence.

Search terms combined workforce constructs (turnover, retention, separation, quit, job satisfaction, commitment, intent to leave) with occupational and industry terms (truck driver, motor carrier, truckload, commercial driver, over-the-road) and with mechanism terms (pay, compensation, mileage rate, detention, dwell, wait time, hours of service, electronic logging, safety climate, dispatch). Reference lists of included studies were hand-searched, and we ran forward citation checks on the eight most-cited items in the initial yield.

The search returned 2,105 unique records after de-duplication. Title and abstract screening removed 1,841, the large majority of them either non-U.S. freight studies without transferable constructs, engineering studies of vehicle systems, or trade press items that did not report original data. That left 264 records read in full. Of those, 193 were excluded at full text: 74 for reporting no outcome relevant to workforce separation or its proximate antecedents, 51 for describing populations outside the truckload segment, 38 for lacking any quantitative or systematically coded qualitative evidence, 19 as duplicate reporting of the same dataset already captured by an earlier or more complete publication, and 11 for methodological deficiencies severe enough that the estimates could not be interpreted, chiefly response rates below fifteen percent with no non-response analysis.

Seventy-one studies were included. Their primary thematic assignments are 24 on compensation structure, 15 on detention and dwell time, twelve on electronic logging and hours-of-service regulation, and nine on safety climate and supervisory practice. The remaining eleven address labor market structure, turnover measurement, and organizational governance, and are cross-cutting by nature. Assignments are to primary theme only; a substantial number of the compensation studies also report scheduling variables, and several of the detention studies were designed as safety studies with workforce outcomes attached secondarily. Effect estimates, sample characteristics, driver segment, identification strategy, and our internal quality rating are recorded in the extraction workbook maintained by the analyst, keyed by author and year to the reference list at the end of this document.

Two limits on the search should be recorded. First, we did not include unpublished carrier-proprietary studies, of which our members hold several, because they cannot be independently assessed. Second, publication bias is a live concern in this literature and we could not correct for it. Retention interventions that failed are unlikely to have been written up. Where our synthesis reports that a lever "works," the honest reading is that it worked in studies that were published.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Compensation: The Best-Measured Lever and Its Decay

The compensation literature is the largest and methodologically strongest body of work in this review, and it is also the literature most often misread. Twenty-four included studies address pay, and they divide into three distinct traditions that answer three different questions and are frequently quoted as though they answered the same one.

The first tradition is the wage-safety literature, which asks whether higher pay reduces crashes. Rodriguez, Rocha, Khattak, and Belzer (2003) and Rodriguez, Targa, and Belzer (2006) examined a single large carrier's pay change and reported that higher mileage rates were associated with meaningful crash reductions, an effect they attribute to selection — better drivers sort into better-paying firms and stay — rather than to any direct behavioral channel. Faulkiner and Belzer (2019) extended this line and found that the return to compensation persists after controlling for firm characteristics, while Kudo and Belzer (2020) reported that the association weakens once working hours are entered into the model, which is a significant qualification: it suggests that part of what the pay coefficient captures is the schedule that comes with the pay.

The second tradition is the turnover-prediction literature, and it is here that the decay finding originates. Suzuki, Crum, and Pautsch (2009) modeled turnover across carriers and found pay a strong predictor, but with the important caveat that the relationship is non-linear and that carriers at the top of the local pay distribution capture diminishing returns. Suzuki (2007) had earlier argued the point that our advisory chair would find least congenial: that there exists a turnover rate below which further retention spending destroys value, and that the optimum for many truckload operations is well above zero. Shaw, Delery, Jenkins, and Gupta (1998), working with organization-level data, found that pay and benefits predicted voluntary turnover but that human resource practices around staffing and supervision predicted it independently, and that the two sets of variables did not substitute for one another. LeMay, Taylor, and Turner (1993) and Richard, LeMay, Taylor, and Turner (1994) reported the same structure two decades earlier: extrinsic factors dominate, intrinsic factors persist after extrinsic factors are controlled.

The decay estimate itself — retention gains dissipating within nine to fourteen months — comes from a cluster of studies with a common design weakness. Stephenson and Fox (1996) documented pay-driven retention improvements that reverted, and attributed the reversion to competitive matching: a carrier that raises rates enjoys an advantage only until neighbors respond. Min and Lambert (2002) reported the same pattern and framed it as a prisoner's dilemma across the segment. Phares and Balthrop (2022) provided the most rigorous treatment, using market structure variation to show that wage effects on driver flows depend on local labor market concentration; in thin markets a rate increase buys durable advantage, in dense ones it buys eleven months. This is not a finding that pay does not work. It is a finding that pay works relative to alternatives, and that a carrier's alternatives change.

Set against this, a minority of studies report durable effects, and the discriminating variable in nearly all of them is predictability of home time. Williams, Garver, and Taylor (2011) segmented drivers by need structure and found a substantial cluster for whom scheduling reliability dominated compensation level in stated preference; a pay increase delivered to that segment without a scheduling change should be expected to decay, and does. Prockl, Teller, Kotzab, and Angell (2017) found that satisfaction with pay predicted retention proclivity only in interaction with satisfaction with time at home. Belzer and Sedo (2018) offered the sharpest version of the mechanism: drivers respond to low mileage rates by working longer hours, so a rate increase that is not accompanied by a schedule change is partly absorbed as reduced working time rather than expressed as increased earnings, and the retention signal is correspondingly muted. Kemp, Kopp, and Kemp (2013) and de Croon, Sluiter, Blonk, Broersen, and Frings-Dresen (2004) approach the same territory from occupational health, finding that emotional exhaustion and job strain predict turnover intention through channels that pay does not close.

Where does this leave the proposition that pay is the only variable that matters? It leaves it half-right in a way that is operationally important. Pay is the variable with the largest and most reliably estimated main effect, and no carrier can retain drivers while paying below market. But the literature does not support the stronger claim that pay is sufficient, and the specific pattern of decay that our members are experiencing — increases in 2024 that produced visible improvement through mid-2025 and then eroded — is precisely the pattern that Phares and Balthrop (2022) predict for carriers competing in dense lanes against neighbors who match. The Deming fleet's sustained low turnover is consistent with the thin-market condition in that model. It may be less a demonstration that pay is sufficient than a demonstration that pay is sufficient in a labor market with few competing employers, which is not the market most of our members occupy.

Two further compensation findings deserve mention because they cut against intuition. Hoffman and Burks (2020) found that drivers systematically overpredict their own mileage productivity, which means recruiting messages built on top-quartile earnings figures produce a predictable disappointment that surfaces as separation in months two through five. And Belman and Monaco (2001), examining the long arc of the industry, documented that real compensation for over-the-road work declined substantially after deregulation and that the retention problem is downstream of a structural wage shift no individual carrier can reverse. The practical corollary is uncomfortable: a single fleet's pay action is a positional move within a declining distribution.

### 3.2 Detention: The Most Cited Problem With the Weakest Measurement

Fifteen studies address detention and dwell. Their substantive claims are consistent; their numbers are not, and the gap between the two is the most instructive methodological fact in this review.

On substance, the literature converges. Short and Murray (2014) reported that detention is associated with both lost revenue and elevated crash involvement. The U.S. DOT Office of Inspector General (2018) concluded that a fifteen-minute increase in average dwell raises expected crash rates, while explicitly stating that available data were insufficient for more precise estimation. The Government Accountability Office (2011) had reached a comparable conclusion seven years earlier and had made the same complaint about data. Kudo and Belzer (2019) linked long working hours, of which detention is a component, to hypertension and other morbidity, and Chen, Sieber, Lincoln and colleagues (2015) and Sieber, Robinson, Birdsey and colleagues (2014) documented the health profile of the long-haul population in which those hours are worked. Apostolopoulos, Sönmez, Shattell, and Belzer (2010) and Shattell, Apostolopoulos, Sönmez, and Griffin (2010) supply the qualitative texture: waiting is experienced not merely as lost time but as a status insult, unpaid and uncontrollable, and it is described by drivers in language of disrespect rather than of economics.

On measurement, the literature fragments. Across the fifteen studies, the reported average detention per stop ranges from 1.2 to 6.5 hours. That spread is not primarily a finding about facilities. It is a finding about definitions. Studies vary on at least five dimensions:

| Design choice | Variants observed | Direction of effect on estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Clock start | Appointment time; arrival at gate; check-in at guard shack; door assignment | Later start reduces estimate |
| Free time excluded | 0, 1, or 2 hours excluded before counting | Larger exclusion reduces estimate |
| Data source | Driver self-report; dispatcher notes; telematics geofence; billing records | Self-report inflates; billing deflates |
| Denominator | All stops; only stops exceeding a threshold; only detention-billed stops | Threshold and billing filters inflate |
| Segment | Refrigerated and grocery; dry van retail DC; drayage | Refrigerated and grocery inflate |

ATRI (2019) and ATRI (2023) use telematics-derived geofence dwell across a broad carrier sample and report figures at the lower end; driver survey instruments report figures at the upper end. Neither is wrong. They are measuring different constructs, and the practice of quoting a single industry detention figure — which our members do routinely in customer negotiations — is unsupportable. Williams, Garver, and Taylor (2011) found detention exposure a strong discriminator between driver segments, which suggests a further complication: the average is doing less work than the distribution, and a fleet whose worst decile of stops runs eight hours has a retention problem that a mean of 2.1 hours will conceal entirely.

The detention literature also contains an unresolved causal question that bears directly on intervention design. Detention is correlated with quitting, but detention is also correlated with the customer mix a carrier serves, the lanes it runs, and the dispatch discipline it maintains. Cantor, Celebi, Corsi, and Grimm (2013) and Nickerson and Silverman (2003) both show that carrier governance choices sort drivers and freight simultaneously, which means that a fleet with high detention is likely to differ from a low-detention fleet in ways the detention variable does not capture. No included study randomized detention exposure or exploited a plausible natural experiment. The claim that reducing detention would reduce turnover is therefore a claim of considerable face validity and essentially no causal identification, and members should be told this plainly before the institute recommends spending grant funds on a detention-reduction pilot.

### 3.3 Electronic Logging, Hours of Service, and the Carrier-Size Contradiction

Twelve studies address logging technology and hours-of-service regulation, and they contain the most direct contradiction in the review: logging device adoption is associated with worsened crash rates at small carriers and improved outcomes at large ones.

Scott, Balthrop, and Miller (2021) documented the small-carrier deterioration and offered an explanation that has become the standard account: monitoring removes the ability to falsify logs but does not remove the economic pressure that produced the falsification, so drivers at carriers without the scheduling infrastructure to absorb the constraint respond by driving faster within compliant hours. Balthrop and Scott (2021) extended this to show that compliance heterogeneity itself varies with fleet size and prior technology adoption. Miller, Bolumole, and Schwieterman (2020) established the baseline: small and medium carriers had adopted logging devices at far lower rates before the mandate, meaning the mandate imposed a much larger behavioral shock on them.

Against this, the earlier literature reported benefits. Cantor, Corsi, and Grimm (2009) found electronic logbook adoption associated with improved safety performance, but their sample was dominated by voluntary adopters, who are systematically larger and better capitalized. Hubbard (2000) and Baker and Hubbard (2004) had shown a decade earlier that on-board monitoring adoption is itself endogenous to firm governance and freight characteristics, which makes the voluntary-adoption estimates difficult to transfer to a mandated environment. This is a case where the apparent contradiction largely dissolves under scrutiny: studies of voluntary adopters and studies of mandated adopters are estimating different parameters, and the mandate literature is the relevant one for our members.

The hours-of-service literature proper is more settled on physiology and less settled on operations. Hanowski, Hickman, Fumero, Olson, and Dingus (2007), Blanco and colleagues (2011), Jovanis, Wu, and Chen (2011), and Chen and Xie (2014) collectively establish that crash risk rises with consecutive driving hours and falls with rest breaks, and the federal restart field study (FMCSA, 2014) supports the value of consolidated nighttime sleep. What none of this literature resolves is the operational question our members actually face, which is how to schedule freight so that compliant hours are also productive hours. Crum and Morrow (2002) is the closest thing to a direct answer, finding that carrier scheduling practices — specifically, dispatch's willingness to commit to appointment times it cannot support — predict driver fatigue independently of the regulatory regime. Morrow and Crum (2004) found the same relationship for close calls.

The connection to retention is real but underdeveloped. Belzer and Sedo (2018) supply the mechanism linking hours to compensation to quitting. Beyond that, the logging literature has been written almost entirely as a safety literature, and workforce outcomes appear as secondary measures when they appear at all. We could not locate a single study that estimated the effect of a logging or scheduling change on separation rates with adequate controls.

### 3.4 Safety Climate and the Supervisory Channel

Nine studies address safety climate, and this is the smallest and most internally coherent literature in the review, which may reflect its relative youth as much as its quality.

Zohar (2010), reviewing three decades of general safety climate research, establishes the theoretical structure that the trucking applications adopt: climate is a perception of what an organization actually rewards, as distinct from what it announces, and it is formed primarily through supervisory behavior rather than through policy documents. Zohar and Luria (2005) demonstrated the multilevel structure — organization-level climate and group-level climate are distinct and the group level, meaning the dispatcher relationship in a truckload context, carries more predictive weight.

The trucking applications bear this out. Huang, Zohar, Robertson, Garabet, Lee, and Murphy (2013) developed and validated climate scales for lone workers using drivers as the exemplar case, addressing the obvious problem that standard climate instruments assume co-located work. Huang, Lee, McFadden and colleagues (2016) then found that safety climate predicted job satisfaction, engagement, and turnover through a social exchange mechanism — drivers read safety investment as evidence of employer regard and reciprocate with commitment. Swartz, Douglas, Roberts, and Overstreet (2017) reported the same relationship with intent to leave as the outcome. Douglas and Swartz (2016) established the dimensional structure. Arboleda, Morrow, Crum, and Shelley (2003) had shown much earlier that management practices, not driver attributes, are the principal antecedent of safety culture in this industry, and Mejza and Corsi (1999) and Corsi and Barnard (2003) documented the specific carrier practices that separate high- and low-performing fleets.

Set against the compensation literature, the climate work makes an interesting claim. Huang and colleagues (2016) and Swartz and colleagues (2017) both report climate effects on turnover intention that survive controls for pay satisfaction. If those estimates transfer, they describe a lever that operates independently of the compensation arms race and is therefore not subject to competitive matching in the same way. That is an attractive property for a carrier facing the decay problem. The caution is that both studies measure intent to leave rather than actual separation, that both are cross-sectional, and that common-method variance in self-report climate and self-report intention is a well-known inflation risk. Keller and Ozment (1999) is the one included study that examined the dispatcher relationship with an outcome closer to behavior, and it found dispatcher characteristics predictive of retention, but the design cannot separate dispatcher quality from the fleet characteristics that attract good dispatchers.

### 3.5 The Shortage Debate as a Cross-Cutting Dispute

Underneath the thematic literatures sits a disagreement about whether the phenomenon exists at all in the form the industry describes.

Costello and Suarez (2015) and Costello and Karickhoff (2019) present the industry survey position: a structural gap approaching 60,000 drivers, driven by demographics, licensing barriers, and lifestyle aversion among younger workers. Burks and Monaco (2019), writing for the federal statistical agency, argue directly against this — that the long-haul segment displays the characteristics of a functioning spot market with high turnover as an equilibrium feature rather than a symptom of shortage, and that a genuine shortage would have produced sustained real wage growth that the data do not show. Boyer and Prince (2020) formalize the measurement problem: "shortage" in the industry usage denotes unfilled seats at prevailing wages, which in economics is not a shortage but a price. Viscelli (2016), from an ethnographic position, argues that the churn is manufactured by a recruiting and training model that treats new entrants as a renewable input, and Belman, Monaco, and Brooks (2005) documented the working conditions that make the churn rational from the driver's side.

This dispute is not academic for our purposes, because the two positions imply opposite interventions. If the industry position is right, the correct use of a workforce grant is recruitment and pipeline development — training partnerships, licensing subsidies, entry programs. If the labor economists are right, recruitment spending funds a treadmill and the correct use is retention of incumbents. Burks, Belzer, Kwan, Pratt, and Shackelford (2010) is the most balanced treatment we found and its conclusion is that both descriptions are partially true of different segments: entry-level long-haul truckload behaves as the economists describe, while specialized and regional work shows genuine skill scarcity. Our members' 91 percent figure is concentrated in the segment where the economists' account fits best.

## 4. Why the Literature Disagrees

Four sources of disagreement recur, and distinguishing among them is necessary before any of these findings is applied.

**Construct instability.** Detention is the worst case, with the 1.2-to-6.5-hour range traceable almost entirely to definitional variance rather than to real differences in facility performance. But turnover suffers from the same problem in a less visible form. Suzuki (2007) noted that carriers compute turnover on different denominators — average seats, average drivers, year-end headcount — and that a single fleet can report figures differing by twenty points depending on the convention. Our own 91 percent is not comparable to published benchmarks without knowing their convention, and we do not.

**Endogeneity of the treatment.** Almost every relationship in this literature involves a variable the carrier chooses. Pay levels, detention exposure, logging adoption, and safety investment are all outputs of firm strategy, and firms that choose one tend to choose the others. Nickerson and Silverman (2003) and Baker and Hubbard (2004) make this point about governance and technology; it applies with equal force to compensation. Studies that report a pay-retention coefficient without an instrument are reporting a correlation between good firms and their good outcomes.

**Population heterogeneity masked by aggregation.** The logging contradiction is the clearest instance — a positive average effect and a negative small-carrier effect are both true, and the average is useless to a 210-truck fleet. Phares and Balthrop (2022) demonstrate the same structure for wages across labor markets. Whenever this literature reports a single coefficient for "the industry," the relevant question is which segment dominates the sample.

**Outcome substitution.** A large share of the climate, satisfaction, and commitment literature measures turnover intention rather than turnover. Large, Breitling, and Kramer (2014) and Johnson, Bristow, McClure, and Schneider (2011) both work with intention. The intention-behavior gap in voluntary turnover is substantial and varies with labor market conditions, which means intention-based estimates are inflated in loose markets and possibly deflated in tight ones. Griffin, Rodriguez, and Lantz (1993) and Beilock and Capelle (1990) are among the few older studies that tracked occupational rather than organizational attachment, and they suggest a further distinction our members should attend to: a driver who leaves a fleet is a different problem from a driver who leaves the occupation, and interventions that address one may not address the other.

## 5. Gaps

The literature is silent or nearly silent on four matters central to this membership.

**Owner-operators.** Cantor, Celebi, Corsi, and Grimm (2013) and Nickerson and Silverman (2003) address the ownership question from a safety and governance angle respectively; Viscelli (2016) treats it ethnographically. There is no quantitative literature on owner-operator retention as such — on why a leased operator terminates a settlement agreement, on what carrier practices predict fleet-level contractor attrition, or on whether the compensation decay finding transfers to a population paid on percentage rather than mileage. Roughly a fifth of our members' capacity is contracted. None of the estimates in this review were derived from that population.

**Women drivers.** Monaco and Habermalz (2011) is the only included study addressing gender directly, and it concerns wage determination rather than retention. We found nothing on facility design, training cohort composition, dispatch interaction, or harassment reporting as retention factors, despite these being the recurring themes in every industry discussion of the subject. This is a gap of a particular kind: not an unmeasured variable but an unstudied population.

**Short-haul and cross-border work.** The entire literature is oriented to over-the-road operations. Drayage and short-haul cross-border work — which is what a substantial portion of our Laredo-based membership actually does — involves daily home time, high stop density, and border wait exposure that has no analogue in the long-haul detention studies. Whether the pay-decay finding, the climate findings, or the detention findings transfer to a driver who crosses the bridge four times a day is unknown.

**Intervention evaluation.** This is the largest gap. Of 71 included studies, none reports a carrier-level retention intervention evaluated against a credible counterfactual. The literature is observational nearly throughout. There are no randomized pay experiments, no stepped-wedge rollouts of scheduling changes, no matched-control evaluations of detention pay programs. A field this large without a single controlled intervention study is a field that has described a problem thoroughly and tested a solution never.

## 6. Implications for Carriers of This Size

**Do not treat the 91 percent as a single phenomenon.** The turnover literature's most consistent operational finding is that separations cluster in the first ninety days and reflect expectation mismatch — Hoffman and Burks (2020) on productivity overconfidence, Williams, Thomas, and Liao-Troth (2017) on psychological contract breach. Early separations and late separations have different causes and different remedies, and a fleet that reports one number cannot tell which it has.

**Expect pay actions to decay unless paired.** The evidence supports a rate increase as a retention lever and does not support expecting it to hold. The studies reporting durable effects are those where pay moved together with home-time predictability (Prockl et al., 2017; Williams et al., 2011). Our advisory chair's position is defensible as a floor condition and not as a strategy; her fleet's results are consistent with a market-structure explanation that most members cannot replicate.

**Treat detention as a measurement project before an intervention project.** Given the 1.2-to-6.5-hour range and the absence of causal identification, a member fleet should first instrument its own dwell using a single defensible convention and examine the upper decile rather than the mean. This is cheap and it is the precondition for any customer negotiation or intervention that follows.

**Regard the logging findings as a warning specific to this size class.** Scott, Balthrop, and Miller (2021) and Balthrop and Scott (2021) describe deterioration concentrated at carriers in our members' size range. The implication is not to resist compliance technology but to recognize that monitoring without scheduling slack transfers pressure rather than relieving it.

**Consider the supervisory channel the most plausible target for a bounded grant.** Dispatcher behavior is the variable that the climate literature identifies as proximate (Zohar & Luria, 2005; Huang et al., 2016; Swartz et al., 2017), that Keller and Ozment (1999) link to retention, and that a $400,000 intervention could actually reach across 6,900 trucks. It is also the lever least subject to competitive matching. The evidence for it is weaker than the evidence for pay — cross-sectional, intention-based, method-variance-prone — and members should be told that the recommendation rests on tractability as much as on evidentiary strength.

**Build the evaluation into the grant.** Given that the literature contains no controlled intervention studies, whatever we fund should be structured to produce one: staggered rollout across member fleets, a pre-specified separation outcome with a stated turnover convention, and a commitment to report the result whether or not it favors the intervention. A field this empty rewards a single honest evaluation more than a well-executed program that generates no evidence.
