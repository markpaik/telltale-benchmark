# Merchant Onboarding Service — Portfolio Review, January 2026

**Finding:** The merchant onboarding rebuild is the strongest program in this year's portfolio and has already paid back its build cost in retained revenue. **Recommendation:** the Board should approve the program's graduation from a funded initiative to a permanent product line with a maintenance and enhancement budget, and direct Simone Adeyinka to bring a proposal for extending the same onboarding architecture to the two merchant segments — nonprofit and multi-location retail — that were deliberately excluded from the 2025 scope.

Kestrel funded this program in early 2025 to solve a specific and well-documented problem: merchants who applied for a Kestrel account were waiting a median of nine days to complete underwriting, document verification, and terminal provisioning, and a meaningful share abandoned the process before finishing it. The build was scoped at $3.1 million and was expected to compress the median onboarding window to under 48 hours while holding net revenue retention above the company's 110 percent target for newly onboarded cohorts. Gustavo Peralta's engineering team delivered a rules-based document verification pipeline, an automated underwriting decision engine for the lowest-risk merchant tiers, and a self-scheduling terminal-provisioning workflow that removed the manual queue that had been the single largest source of delay.

Against that mandate, the program delivered ahead of every target the Board set. Median onboarding time fell to 31 hours, well inside the sub-48-hour goal and a 92 percent reduction from the prior state. The team onboarded 1,914 net new merchants over the calendar year, a volume that finance has already tied to $9.4 million in incremental annualized revenue. Net revenue retention on the new cohort came in at 118 percent, eight points above target, which tells the Board that merchants who onboard through the new flow are not just arriving faster — they are transacting more and churning less than merchants onboarded under the old process. The $3.1 million build came in on budget, and Bao Tran-Nguyen's team calculates a payback period of just under five months once the retained-revenue effect is included, which makes this the fastest-paying program in the current portfolio by a wide margin.

The evidence behind the retention number matters as much as the headline figure, because a faster onboarding process that produced worse-quality merchants would not be a win. Deborah Yankelovich's compliance review found no increase in fraud-flagged accounts or chargeback rates among the automated-underwriting cohort relative to the prior manual process, which removes the most obvious objection to accelerating approval decisions. The automation was applied only to the lowest-risk tier of applicants — roughly 70 percent of total volume — with higher-risk applications still routed to manual underwriting, and that segmentation appears to be holding.

What the Board is being asked to decide is not whether the program worked; the data is not close enough to argue about. The decision is what to do with a program that has already met its objective. Bao Tran-Nguyen's recommendation is twofold. First, convert the program from a funded initiative with a defined end date into a standing product capability with its own run-rate budget, so that the underwriting rules engine and provisioning workflow receive ordinary maintenance and are not left to decay as the team that built them rotates onto other work. Second, direct a scoping proposal for extending automated underwriting to nonprofit merchants and multi-location retail chains, both of which were excluded from 2025 scope because their document requirements are more complex. Preliminary sizing from Simone Adeyinka's team suggests these two segments represent roughly 3,400 pending or stalled applications currently sitting in the legacy manual queue, which is a meaningful pool of revenue currently being onboarded at nine-day speed while the rest of the company enjoys 31-hour speed.

The exposure of waiting another quarter to make this decision is not that the current program stops working — it will continue to perform whether or not the Board acts. The exposure is opportunity cost and organizational drift. The engineering team that built the onboarding pipeline is a scarce, high-performing unit, and without a Board-approved mandate to extend the work, Gustavo Peralta will reasonably reassign those engineers to the terminal firmware and ledger migration programs, both of which are behind schedule and competing hard for the same senior engineering talent. Once that team disperses, reconstituting it to build the nonprofit and multi-location extension will cost more in ramp time than it would cost to keep the team intact through a Q2 scoping and build cycle. Every quarter the extension is delayed, an estimated 300 to 400 additional merchants in the excluded segments continue onboarding through the slow legacy path, which both depresses their first-year revenue contribution and leaves them more exposed to the abandonment risk the original program was funded to eliminate. None of this is an emergency. But it is a clear case where the cost of delay is measured in foregone revenue and a dispersing team, not in a specific compliance or safety deadline, and the Board should treat this as a straightforward capital-allocation decision rather than a risk decision.

# Terminal Firmware Modernization — Portfolio Review, January 2026

**Finding:** The firmware modernization program is materially behind schedule, and the October 31, 2026 payment-card attestation deadline is now at genuine risk of being missed because the lead firmware vendor has lost half its engineering staff. **Recommendation:** the Board should authorize immediate emergency funding to bring in a second firmware vendor to run in parallel with the incumbent, and should direct Deborah Yankelovich to open a formal dialogue with the card networks now, before a deadline miss becomes unavoidable, rather than after.

This program was funded to modernize the firmware running on Kestrel's fleet of 41,000 installed payment terminals ahead of a mandatory card-network attestation deadline of October 31, 2026, after which terminals running unsupported firmware builds may be blocked from processing transactions on major card networks. The program was structured around five milestones covering firmware architecture redesign, a pilot deployment to 2,000 terminals, a phased rollout schedule, a rollback and recovery capability, and full-fleet completion, with the lead engineering work contracted to an outside firmware vendor working alongside Gustavo Peralta's internal team.

Against that plan, the program has missed three of its five milestones. The architecture redesign and pilot deployment were completed close to schedule, but the phased rollout milestone slipped by four months, the rollback-and-recovery milestone has not been delivered at all, and the full-fleet completion milestone — originally targeted for December 2025 — has no credible date attached to it in the current plan. As of this review, 22 percent of the installed base, roughly 9,020 terminals, is still running firmware builds that will not meet the October 2026 attestation standard. The proximate cause reported by Gustavo Peralta is that the lead firmware vendor lost approximately half of its engineering team over the second half of 2025, including the two senior engineers who had architected the rollback capability, and the vendor has been unable to backfill that capacity at the pace needed to hold the original schedule.

The Board should understand what is actually at stake if this deadline is missed. Terminals that fail attestation are not simply out of compliance in an abstract sense — the card networks have the contractual right to decline transaction authorization from noncompliant hardware, which means a missed deadline converts directly into merchants who cannot process payments through Kestrel terminals starting November 1, 2026. At the current pace of remediation, roughly 9,000 terminals would remain exposed on that date, representing merchants whose transaction volume Bao Tran-Nguyen's team estimates at $14 million in monthly processed volume. This is not a program-level risk; it is a company-level revenue and reputational risk, and it is the reason Deborah Yankelovich has flagged this as the single item in this year's portfolio review that warrants standing compliance attention between now and October.

The decision in front of the Board is narrow and time-sensitive. The recommendation is to authorize emergency funding — Bao Tran-Nguyen's team estimates $1.8 to $2.4 million — to engage a second firmware vendor to work the remaining 9,020 terminals in parallel with the incumbent vendor's continued work on rollback capability and process documentation. Running two vendors simultaneously is not the preferred long-term structure, and it introduces coordination overhead the program does not currently have the management bandwidth to absorb without additional oversight, but the alternative — waiting for the incumbent vendor to rebuild its team organically — has no credible path to closing the gap before October. Alongside the vendor decision, the Board should direct Deborah Yankelovich to begin a structured conversation with the relevant card networks now. Networks have historically shown some willingness to grant short compliance extensions to processors who disclose a remediation plan proactively and in advance of a deadline; they have shown very little willingness to do so for processors who disclose only after the deadline has already passed.

The exposure of waiting another quarter on this decision is the most severe of anything in this year's review. Every month between now and a Board decision is a month the vendor capacity gap does not close on its own, and the program's own trend data shows the unsupported-terminal count declining by only about 1.5 percentage points a month at current pace — arithmetic that does not reach zero by October without a change in resourcing. Waiting until the April review to revisit this puts the decision roughly six months from the deadline, which industry benchmarks on vendor onboarding and terminal deployment logistics suggest is close to the minimum viable runway for a second vendor to be sourced, contracted, and deployed at scale. A further quarter of delay beyond that would very likely make the October date unreachable regardless of how much money the Board is willing to spend, at which point the conversation with card networks shifts from "here is our remediation plan" to "here is our failure," a materially worse negotiating position with materially worse consequences for merchants and for Kestrel's standing with the networks.

# Self-Serve Developer Tier — Portfolio Review, January 2026

**Finding:** The self-serve developer tier has failed to convert at anywhere near the rate it was funded to achieve, and its ongoing support burden is being paid for by the paid-tier customer base rather than by the tier itself. **Recommendation:** the Board should not fund a fourth quarter of the program in its current form; it should direct Simone Adeyinka to bring back, within 60 days, a choice between a substantially redesigned conversion path with a hard funding cap, or a wind-down of the self-serve tier into a smaller, invitation-only developer sandbox.

This program was funded at $2.1 million to build a low-friction, self-service signup path for independent developers and small integrators to build on Kestrel's payments API without going through the sales-assisted onboarding used for the company's core merchant base. The plan approved by this Board a year ago called for 1,500 of these self-serve signups to convert to paid, revenue-generating accounts over the course of 2025, on the theory that a large top-of-funnel volume of free signups was an acceptable and even necessary cost of acquiring a smaller number of paying developer accounts who would otherwise be expensive to reach through direct sales.

The program generated substantial top-of-funnel interest — 12,400 signups over the year, more than eight times the paid-conversion target — but converted only 380 of those signups to paid accounts. That is 25 percent of the 1,500-account plan, against a signup volume that was 827 percent of what would have been needed to hit plan at even a modest conversion rate. The gap between signup volume and paid conversion is the central fact of this review: the funnel is not failing to attract developers, it is failing almost completely to turn attracted developers into revenue. The $2.1 million budget was spent in full. On a pure cost-per-paid-account basis, the program cost roughly $5,530 for each of the 380 accounts it converted, a figure that compares unfavorably to the cost of acquiring an equivalent paying account through Kestrel's existing sales-assisted channel.

The support data makes the case for a decision now rather than later. The free-tier signup base is generating approximately 900 support tickets a month, a volume driven by developers testing integrations, encountering documentation gaps, and requesting help with API credentials — activity that is a normal and expected cost of a developer program, but one that this program's revenue was supposed to cover and does not. Finance has confirmed that the cost of servicing this ticket volume is currently being absorbed within the same support organization that services Kestrel's paid enterprise and mid-market accounts, which means paid customers are, in effect, subsidizing the support cost of a free tier that converts at 3 percent. This is the detail Bao Tran-Nguyen's team flags as the most important for the Board: the program is not merely underperforming its growth target, it is running a negative-margin structure that draws resources away from the customer base that funds the company.

The Board's decision here is whether to continue funding the program as designed, in some modified form, or to end it. A straight continuation of the current model is not recommended; nothing in the 2025 data suggests that another year at the same signup volume and support cost would produce a materially different conversion outcome absent a structural change to either the product or the funnel. Two paths are worth the Board's consideration. The first is a redesigned conversion path — Simone Adeyinka's team has an early hypothesis that the free tier's usage limits are set high enough that developers have little incentive to upgrade, and that a tighter free tier paired with a clearer mid-funnel nudge toward paid features could improve conversion without reducing signup volume materially — funded at a hard cap well below $2.1 million with a defined checkpoint at six months. The second is a wind-down of the broad self-serve tier into a smaller, curated sandbox available by invitation to developers who arrive through existing merchant relationships, which would shrink the support burden substantially at the cost of the top-of-funnel volume the program was originally built to generate.

The exposure of leaving this decision until the next quarterly cycle is primarily financial and organizational rather than a hard deadline like the firmware program. Every additional quarter run at 2025's pace adds on the order of $500,000 to $550,000 in program spend and roughly 2,700 additional support tickets, with no evidence in the current data that conversion improves simply by running the same funnel longer. There is also a quieter cost: the 900-ticket-a-month load is drawn from the same support staff who service paid accounts, and continued strain on that team risks measurable service-quality degradation for the customers who are actually generating Kestrel's revenue. This is not a program where a quarter's delay creates a cliff-edge risk, but it is a program where delay has a clear, calculable, and avoidable cost, and the Board has enough evidence now to make a decision rather than requesting another quarter of data that is unlikely to change the underlying picture.

# Core Ledger Migration — Portfolio Review, January 2026

**Finding:** The core ledger migration has spent 51 percent more than its approved budget, has migrated only 61 percent of accounts, and is now producing reconciliation failures serious enough that two of them required manual settlement of customer funds totaling $1.4 million. **Recommendation:** the Board should not authorize the migration's continuation on its current trajectory without first commissioning an independent technical and financial audit of the program, to be completed before any further funding is released, and should direct Deborah Yankelovich to assess whether the reconciliation breaks trigger any regulatory disclosure obligations.

The migration was funded at $7.5 million to move Kestrel's transaction and balance records off a legacy ledger platform and onto a new core ledger system intended to support real-time settlement, better auditability, and the transaction volume growth the company expects over the next several years. The program has run a dual-write architecture throughout the migration, meaning that transactions are written simultaneously to the old and new ledgers so that account-by-account cutovers can happen gradually with a fallback available if a given account's migration encounters problems.

Against that $7.5 million approved budget, the program has spent $11.3 million, a 51 percent overrun with 39 percent of accounts still unmigrated. Gustavo Peralta's team attributes the overrun primarily to underestimated complexity in reconciling historical transaction records that predate the legacy system's current schema, a problem that surfaced roughly five months into the build and was not incorporated into a revised budget request to this Board at the time it was identified — a process gap Bao Tran-Nguyen's review flags independently of the migration's technical performance. At the current run rate of accounts migrated per dollar spent, completing the remaining 39 percent of accounts under the existing approach would require an estimated $6.8 to $7.5 million in additional spend, which would bring total program cost to roughly $18 million against a $7.5 million approval — more than double.

The reconciliation break data is the more serious finding. The dual-write architecture is designed so that the two ledgers should always agree; a reconciliation break means they did not. Eleven such breaks occurred in the fourth quarter alone, and while most were caught and resolved through automated reconciliation tooling without customer impact, two were significant enough that they could not be resolved automatically and required manual settlement action, moving a combined $1.4 million to correct customer account balances that had diverged between the old and new systems. Deborah Yankelovich's review notes that a dual-write break resulting in incorrect customer balances is, at minimum, an operational risk event that warrants documentation, and that the frequency and severity of these breaks — accelerating rather than declining as more accounts move onto the new system — is the pattern that most concerns her from a compliance standpoint. An architecture that is supposed to get safer as testing accumulates is instead producing larger and more frequent failures as volume on the new ledger increases, which is the opposite of what a healthy migration should look like at 61 percent completion.

The Board is being asked to make a decision that is uncomfortable but necessary: whether to keep funding a program that is significantly over budget and showing a worsening risk trend, on the strength of the team's assurance that the underlying architecture is sound and the breaks are a solvable engineering problem, or to pause new funding and commission an independent audit before committing further capital. Bao Tran-Nguyen's recommendation is the latter. An independent audit — technical review of the reconciliation architecture, and financial review of how the original $7.5 million estimate was constructed and why the overrun was not flagged to the Board sooner — would give the Board a basis for deciding between three paths: fund completion at the revised cost with tightened milestone and reconciliation-break reporting, restructure the remaining migration into a smaller number of larger account batches with more conservative dual-write testing between batches, or, if the audit surfaces architecture-level problems rather than execution-level ones, consider whether the remaining 39 percent of accounts should migrate at all versus running a permanently supported legacy path for the accounts most exposed to reconciliation risk.

The exposure of waiting a further quarter without acting is twofold. Financially, every quarter of continued migration at the current pace and cost structure adds roughly $1.6 to $1.9 million in spend against a budget that has already been exceeded by nearly $3.8 million, with no revised budget currently before the Board to authorize that continued spend — meaning the program is, as of this review, operating without a valid appropriation for its ongoing burn. Operationally, the reconciliation break trend is the more urgent concern: if the frequency of breaks continues to scale with the number of migrated accounts, the program is on a path toward a break serious enough to affect a materially larger dollar amount or a materially larger number of customers than the $1.4 million incident already on record, and Deborah Yankelovich's view is that a break of that scale would very likely cross into territory requiring formal disclosure to regulators or to the card networks. Waiting a quarter to commission the audit means a quarter of continued migration activity generating exactly the risk pattern the audit is meant to evaluate, which is the wrong sequence — the Board should authorize the audit now, on a fast track, rather than allow another full quarter of migrations to proceed against an architecture whose safety is currently in question.

The four programs above draw from the same portfolio and the same finite pool of engineering, compliance, and finance capacity, and the Board should weigh the four recommendations together rather than in isolation before the January meeting closes.

## Consolidated Decisions Requested, January 2026

- **Merchant Onboarding Service:** Approve conversion to a standing product line with run-rate maintenance funding, and direct Simone Adeyinka to return with a scoping proposal for extending automated underwriting to nonprofit and multi-location retail merchants.
- **Terminal Firmware Modernization:** Authorize $1.8–$2.4 million in emergency funding for a second firmware vendor, and direct Deborah Yankelovich to open proactive dialogue with the card networks ahead of the October 31, 2026 attestation deadline.
- **Self-Serve Developer Tier:** Decline a fourth quarter of funding in the current form, and direct Simone Adeyinka to return within 60 days with either a capped, redesigned conversion path or a wind-down plan.
- **Core Ledger Migration:** Pause new funding pending an independent technical and financial audit, and direct Deborah Yankelovich to assess disclosure obligations arising from the fourth-quarter reconciliation breaks.

Two of these four items — firmware and the ledger migration — compete directly for the same senior engineering talent inside Gustavo Peralta's organization, and both are being asked to scale up at the same time the onboarding extension is asking to hold its team together. Bao Tran-Nguyen's office does not have visibility into headcount commitments deep enough to resolve that contention on the Board's behalf, and flags it here so the resourcing conflict is decided deliberately in January rather than by default in March, when whichever program has the more urgent near-term deadline will simply absorb the capacity the others were counting on.

The compliance exposure sitting inside two of the four programs — the firmware attestation deadline and the ledger reconciliation breaks — is the more consequential thread running through this review. Both are, in Deborah Yankelovich's assessment, matters that regulators and card networks will judge less on outcome than on whether Kestrel could show it identified the risk early and acted on it deliberately. Both programs currently have exactly that record: risks identified in this cycle, with clear findings and clear recommendations in front of the Board before either has crossed into a reportable event. That is the reason this review pairs an audit-and-pause recommendation for the ledger program with an emergency-funding recommendation for firmware rather than treating either as a simple continue-or-cancel choice — the Board's exposure is lowest when it acts on problems it has already found, and highest when it revisits problems it already knew about and chose to leave for the next cycle.

Bao Tran-Nguyen's office will return to the Board in April with updated figures on all four programs, including the outcome of the firmware vendor engagement, the results of the ledger migration audit, and conversion data from whichever path Simone Adeyinka proposes for the developer tier. Absent a Board decision on the items above, the April review will necessarily report the same four findings with three additional months of unmanaged cost, unmanaged risk, or both.

## Note on Sources and Methodology

The figures cited across all four program summaries were drawn from three primary sources reconciled against one another before inclusion in this review. Financial figures — approved budgets, cumulative spend, and program-level cost-per-outcome calculations — were pulled from Kestrel's finance system and confirmed against the general ledger close for December 2025; where a program's spend crossed a fiscal quarter boundary during the year, Bao Tran-Nguyen's office used the trailing twelve-month figure rather than calendar-year spend to avoid understating programs that began mid-year, which affected the terminal firmware and core ledger figures in particular. Operational metrics — onboarding time, signup and conversion counts, terminal firmware coverage, and support ticket volume — were pulled directly from each program's own reporting systems rather than from a central dashboard, on the judgment that program-owned data, while less standardized across the four programs, was less likely to have been smoothed or lagged in translation to a shared format. Where program teams and finance produced different figures for the same metric, as occurred twice during this review, the more conservative figure was used and the discrepancy was flagged to the relevant sponsor for resolution before the figure was finalized in this document.

The reconciliation-break figures in the core ledger migration summary and the terminal-coverage figures in the firmware summary were both independently confirmed with Deborah Yankelovich's compliance team before inclusion, given the regulatory sensitivity of both figures. No other figures in this review carry a compliance sign-off, and the Board should treat the financial and volume figures in the onboarding and developer-tier summaries as management-reported and not independently audited, consistent with the standard this Board has applied to portfolio reviews in prior years.

Two limitations are worth noting for the record. First, the net revenue retention figure cited for the merchant onboarding program reflects performance through the eleventh month of the cohort's life for merchants onboarded early in the year and through as little as one month for merchants onboarded in December; the blended 118 percent figure will move as the full cohort matures, and Bao Tran-Nguyen's office will report a stabilized figure in the April review once all 2025 cohorts have at least six months of data behind them. Second, the cost-per-paid-account figure cited for the self-serve developer tier allocates the full $2.1 million program spend against the 380 converted accounts and does not net out the portion of that spend attributable to platform infrastructure that will continue to be used regardless of whether the self-serve tier continues in its current form; a wind-down decision would not recover that portion of the spend, and Simone Adeyinka's forthcoming proposal should isolate that figure explicitly so the Board is comparing true incremental cost rather than fully loaded program cost when it decides between the redesign and wind-down paths.
