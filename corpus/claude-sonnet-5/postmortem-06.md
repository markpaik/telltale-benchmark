# Kettle Point Foods — Incident Postmortem
## Line 3 Undeclared Dairy Allergen Cross-Contact and Class I Recall

**Facility:** Fall River, Massachusetts
**Incident Period:** April 4 – May 2, 2026
**Document Status:** Final
**Prepared:** June 12, 2026
**Distribution:** Executive Leadership Team, Food Safety & Quality, Operations, Regulatory Affairs, Supply Chain, Board Risk Committee

---

## 1. Summary

Between April 6 and April 24, 2026, Kettle Point Foods' Line 3 — a certified dairy-free production line manufacturing a nationally distributed plant-based frozen entrée — produced and shipped 218,000 cases contaminated with undeclared milk protein (casein). The contamination originated from a maintenance change made during a scheduled shutdown the weekend of April 4, 2026, in which a diverter valve was reconfigured such that Line 3's dry ingredient transfer drew intermittently through a pneumatic header dedicated to Line 2's cheese powder system. The associated work order was closed the same day without the verification step required by the change control procedure, and the change was never routed to allergen review, despite the fact that it altered a shared material-handling path between an allergen-containing line and an allergen-free line.

The first consumer complaint, received April 17, was coded as a flavor complaint and did not trigger any food safety escalation. Two additional complaints arrived the week of April 20, including a hospitalization in Rhode Island. An internal investigation opened April 24 attributed the issue to a newly qualified dry blend ingredient supplier, quarantined that supplier's material, and cleared Line 3 to resume production without confirming the hypothesis against the actual contamination pathway. An additional 26,000 cases shipped before testing on April 29 identified casein in a lot that did not contain the suspect supplier's material, disproving the working hypothesis. Line 3 was shut down that day. The valve misconfiguration was traced the following day, and a market withdrawal effort recovered a portion of affected product ahead of a Class I recall filed with FDA on May 2, 2026 — 15 days after the first complaint and 28 days after the contaminating change was made.

This postmortem examines the detection lag between the process change and its discovery, the misdiagnosis that led to a second contaminated shipment, the change control failure that allowed an allergen-relevant modification to bypass review, the complaint handling process that failed to recognize an allergen signal, and a 2025 staffing-driven reduction in the environmental/allergen swab program that removed a layer of defense that might have caught the cross-contact earlier. The analysis focuses on the systems and decision structures that produced these outcomes rather than on the individuals who operated within them.

## 4. Root Cause

**Primary root cause:** The facility's change control process contained no mandatory gate requiring allergen risk review for maintenance or engineering changes that alter shared or adjacent material-handling infrastructure between an allergen-free line and an allergen-containing line. The diverter valve reconfiguration was treated as a routine mechanical work order rather than a change with allergen-control implications, so it was never evaluated against the criteria that would have flagged the risk — namely, that it created a physical pathway between a dedicated dairy-line air header and a certified dairy-free line's ingredient transfer system.

This is distinct from, and more fundamental than, the missed verification step on the same work order. Even a completed verification step, as scoped under the existing procedure, would likely have confirmed that the valve moved correctly and sealed properly — a mechanical check — without necessarily confirming that the reconfigured flow path was allergen-safe, because allergen safety was not a defined criterion for this class of change. The absence of an allergen-review trigger in change control is therefore the condition that allowed a mechanically "successful" change to become a food safety failure: the work order could have closed exactly as designed and still produced this outcome, because the design did not ask the allergen question.

## 5. Contributing Factors

The root cause created the conditions for contamination; the following factors independently extended its duration, increased its severity, or delayed its correction. Each is presented as a systems condition rather than an individual lapse.

**5.1 — Verification step omission on the April 4 work order.** Even within the existing change control scope, the required post-work verification was not completed before closure. This removed a secondary, mechanically-focused check that — while not designed to catch allergen risk — might have prompted closer scrutiny of the shared header path had it been performed thoroughly, since verifying flow integrity on a reconfigured diverter naturally invites questions about what else is connected to the line being altered.

**5.2 — Allergen swab program reduction (20 to 6 sites, 2025).** A 2025 headcount reduction cut the environmental/allergen swab program from 20 sample sites to 6, a 70% reduction in surveillance coverage. This reduction was made as a cost/staffing decision without a documented allergen risk reassessment of which sites could be safely dropped versus which represented critical control points for lines running allergen-free claims adjacent to allergen-containing lines. The reduced program did not include (or no longer included) a site capable of detecting residue introduced via the reconfigured pneumatic header, removing an independent detection layer that operated on a different timescale than consumer complaints and could plausibly have caught the cross-contact within the first production days rather than after 13 days and a hospitalization.

**5.3 — Complaint coding without an allergen escalation trigger.** The complaint intake taxonomy allowed a complaint consistent with an allergic or adverse reaction to be coded as a generic "flavor" complaint, with no keyword, symptom, or pattern-based trigger to route it to Food Safety & Quality for allergen-specific evaluation. This meant the first complaint — arriving 13 days into the exposure window — did not shorten the detection lag at all; only the volume and severity of the following week's complaints (including a hospitalization) forced escalation. A system that treats "off flavor" and "allergic reaction" as coding-adjacent categories with no differential trigger will systematically under-respond to early, mild signals of allergen issues, which frequently present initially as taste or texture complaints before more severe reports arrive.

**5.4 — Restart decision made without confirmatory testing against the working hypothesis.** When the investigation opened on April 24, the supplier hypothesis was treated as sufficient basis to clear the line for restart. No finished product or in-process testing was performed to confirm that milk protein was in fact associated with the suspect supplier's material specifically, or absent once that material was held. This allowed a plausible but incorrect hypothesis to drive an operational decision (restart) with direct food safety consequences, resulting in a second contaminated shipment of 26,000 cases over five additional production days.

**5.5 — No structural requirement linking "hold suspect material" to "confirm hypothesis before resuming."** Related to 5.4, the facility's investigation and restart procedures did not require that a corrective action (material hold) be validated against test data before the associated line was returned to production status. This gap meant the investigation's speed became a liability rather than an asset: acting quickly on an unconfirmed hypothesis produced a worse outcome than a slower, evidence-confirmed restart would have.

**5.6 — Revised food safety plan pending since March 2026.** An updated food safety plan had been in review since March 2026 and was not yet implemented at the time of the incident. The record does not establish with certainty that the pending revision would have specifically addressed shared-header change control or swab site allocation, but its pending status meant that whatever gaps it was drafted to close — potentially including allergen review triggers for engineering changes — remained open through the incident period.

**5.7 — No documented allergen risk reassessment following the 2025 swab program reduction.** The swab program cut was made as a standalone staffing decision. There is no record of a cross-functional review at the time of that reduction assessing which lines' allergen-free claims depended most heavily on environmental monitoring, or of a compensating control being added elsewhere (e.g., increased finished product testing frequency) to offset reduced surveillance.

## 6. Response Assessment

### 6.1 What Worked

- **Rapid shutdown once contamination was confirmed.** Once April 29 testing produced an unambiguous positive result inconsistent with the supplier hypothesis, the plant manager shut Line 3 down the same day. The decision-to-action time at this stage was effectively immediate.
- **Fast root cause tracing once the line was down.** The maintenance team traced the contamination to the April 4 valve reconfiguration within one day of the shutdown (April 30), demonstrating that the underlying diagnostic capability existed and could move quickly when the investigation was pointed at the correct system rather than an ingredient supplier.
- **Cross-functional retrieval execution.** Supply Chain's retrieval effort, run in parallel with recall preparation rather than sequentially after it, recovered approximately 71% of shipped cases across 14 states — a strong recovery rate for a Class I recall of this scope, reflecting effective distribution-level traceability and coordination once the correct scope was known.
- **Recall filing speed once root cause was confirmed.** The Class I recall was filed with FDA two days after the correct root cause was identified (April 30 → May 2), indicating that once the facility had accurate information, its regulatory response process functioned efficiently.
- **Complaint pattern eventually surfaced.** Despite the coding gap on the first complaint, the volume and severity of the following week's complaints did reach Food Safety & Quality and did trigger a formal investigation, showing the complaint system was not entirely blind to the signal — it was slow and required severity escalation to function.

### 6.2 What Did Not Work

- **Change control did not treat the valve reconfiguration as allergen-relevant.** The change was closed same-day as a routine mechanical work order with no allergen review, despite altering a physical connection between an allergen-containing line's dedicated header and a certified allergen-free line.
- **Required verification was skipped.** The work order closed without the verification step the procedure itself required, removing a scheduled opportunity to catch the issue even within the (already insufficient) scope of the existing change control process.
- **The reduced swab program did not detect the cross-contact over nine production days.** Whether due to site placement, frequency, or the specific reduction from 20 to 6 sites, environmental monitoring did not surface the contamination during the two-plus weeks it was active before consumer complaints did.
- **Complaint coding did not recognize an allergen signal in the first complaint.** A complaint arriving 13 days into the exposure window and consistent with a reaction was coded as a flavor issue and generated no escalation, costing potentially a week or more of detection time.
- **The investigation acted on an unconfirmed hypothesis with production consequences.** The supplier misdiagnosis was not itself unreasonable as an initial hypothesis, but the decision to clear the line for restart based on that hypothesis alone — without confirmatory testing — is a distinct failure. It directly produced the second contaminated shipment of 26,000 cases.
- **No compensating control was added when surveillance was reduced.** The 2025 swab program cut left no documented offset (increased finished product testing, revised risk-based site selection, or similar) to preserve overall allergen detection capability.
- **A relevant plan revision sat pending for over a month without interim risk mitigation.** Whatever gaps the pending March 2026 food safety plan revision was intended to close remained open through the incident, with no indication that its pending status prompted any interim compensating measures.

## 8. Closing Note

The individual decisions made during this incident — the diagnosis of a new supplier as the source, the same-day closure of a maintenance work order, the coding of an early complaint as a flavor issue — were each made by people operating within processes that did not require them to do otherwise. A change control system without an allergen trigger will not stop an allergen-relevant change. A complaint taxonomy without an escalation flag will not escalate an early signal. A restart procedure without a confirmatory testing requirement will not require confirmatory testing. The purpose of this postmortem is to change those processes so that the next set of reasonable, good-faith decisions made under similar conditions produces a different outcome.

### Appendix B — Recall Scope and Recovery Detail

The recall covered all Line 3 production dated April 6 through April 29, 2026, spanning nine affected production days across two production windows (April 6–24 and April 24–29). Affected product was distributed to retail and food service accounts in 14 states: Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut, New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, New Hampshire, Vermont, Maine, Maryland, Virginia, Ohio, Illinois, and Michigan.

Retrieval activity proceeded in three tracks running concurrently rather than sequentially:

1. **Distribution center and warehouse retrieval** — product still within Kettle Point-controlled or contracted third-party logistics inventory was pulled first, representing the fastest and most complete recovery segment.
2. **Retail-level retrieval** — product already shelved at retail accounts was removed through direct retailer notification and account-level pull instructions, coordinated by account management teams under Supply Chain direction.
3. **Consumer-facing notification** — public recall notice, issued in conjunction with the May 2 FDA filing, addressed product already in consumer possession, which represents the majority of the unrecovered 29%.

The 71% recovery rate reflects strong performance in tracks one and two, consistent with the facility's lot-level traceability system functioning as designed once the correct lot range was identified. The unrecovered volume is concentrated in product that had already reached end consumers before the recall was public, a function of the 28-day gap between the contaminating event and the recall filing rather than a failure of the retrieval mechanism itself.

### Appendix E — Glossary

**Change control** — The documented process by which modifications to equipment, processes, or systems are proposed, reviewed, approved, executed, and verified before being considered complete and safe for use.

**Cross-contact** — The unintentional introduction of an allergen into a product or onto food-contact surfaces via shared equipment, airflow, personnel, or other pathways, distinct from a labeling error.

**Class I recall** — The FDA's most serious recall classification, indicating a reasonable probability that use of or exposure to a violative product will cause serious adverse health consequences or death.

**Environmental/allergen swab program** — A surveillance program in which food-contact and near-food-contact surfaces are periodically sampled and tested for allergen residue to detect cross-contact before it reaches finished product.

**Root cause vs. contributing factor** — In this document, root cause refers to the single systemic condition without which the incident would not have occurred as it did; contributing factors are conditions that independently worsened the incident's severity, duration, or detection time but would not alone have caused it.

### Appendix G — Applicability to Other Facility Lines and Sites

While this postmortem's scope is limited to the Line 3 incident at the Fall River facility, several of the systemic conditions identified are not specific to Line 3 and warrant evaluation across the facility's other lines and, where Kettle Point Foods operates additional sites, across the broader network.

**Shared infrastructure risk.** Line 3's exposure arose from a shared pneumatic header connecting it to Line 2. A facility-wide infrastructure map (Action Item 2) will determine whether similar shared or adjacent connections exist between Line 1, Line 4, and any allergen-containing systems. Given that Line 3 is the facility's only certified dairy-free line, it carries the highest single-line consequence for this class of failure, but the underlying change control gap — absence of an allergen trigger for infrastructure modifications — applies to any change affecting connections between lines with different allergen profiles, regardless of which specific lines are involved.

**Swab program reductions elsewhere.** The 2025 headcount reduction that cut Line 3-relevant swab sites from 20 to 6 was part of a broader staffing action. Action Item 11 (review of all 2025 staffing-driven reductions to food safety monitoring) is scoped to determine whether comparable reductions occurred in monitoring programs supporting other lines or claims (e.g., gluten-free, nut-free designations elsewhere in the facility's portfolio) and whether those reductions carry similar uncompensated risk.

**Complaint coding taxonomy.** The complaint intake and coding system is centralized across the facility rather than line-specific. The taxonomy revision in Action Item 6 will therefore apply facility-wide upon implementation, improving allergen signal detection for all product lines, not only Line 3.

**Restart validation procedure.** The requirement for confirmatory testing prior to restart following an allergen-related shutdown (Action Item 8) is being written as a general procedure applicable to any line, not a Line 3-specific control, since the failure mode — clearing a line based on an unconfirmed hypothesis — is a procedural gap rather than a Line 3-specific condition.

Facilities leadership should treat this postmortem's findings as a prompt for a broader food safety systems review rather than a Line 3-only remediation, given that four of the five core findings (change control, complaint coding, restart validation, and monitoring program governance) are facility-level systems that happen to have failed first and most visibly on Line 3.

### Appendix L — Insurance and Claims Coordination

Recall-related costs captured in Appendix D were coordinated with the facility's product recall insurance policy and general liability carrier as follows.

**Recall expense claim.** A claim was filed against the facility's product recall insurance policy covering eligible costs associated with product retrieval, destruction, and retailer reimbursement. Claim documentation was compiled jointly by Finance and Regulatory Affairs, drawing on the retrieval execution log (Appendix J) and cost accounting summary (Appendix D). Policy deductible and coverage limits determined the portion of the $4.7 million total cost borne directly by the facility versus reimbursed through insurance; this reconciliation is tracked under separate Finance department records outside the scope of this postmortem.

**Third-party liability claims.** The three reported allergic reactions, including the Rhode Island hospitalization, have been referred to the facility's general liability carrier for claims handling. Legal and Regulatory Affairs are coordinating response to any claims arising from these incidents. Status of individual claims is tracked confidentially outside this document and is not reported here due to the sensitivity of personal health information and active claims handling.

**Retailer and distributor chargebacks.** Chargebacks from retail and distribution partners for recalled product, restocking costs, and associated administrative fees were processed through standard trade accounting channels and are reflected in the reimbursement component of Appendix D. Supply Chain maintained direct communication with the facility's largest retail accounts throughout the retrieval process to manage the chargeback process and preserve account relationships where possible.

**Coverage adequacy review.** Given the scale of this event relative to the facility's historical claims experience, Finance has initiated a review of current recall insurance coverage limits and deductible structure to confirm adequacy against a comparable future event, informed by the $4.7 million total cost figure established in this postmortem.

### Appendix M — Internal and External Communications Summary

Effective communication during the incident response spanned multiple audiences with distinct information needs. This appendix summarizes the communications approach for reference in future incident response planning.

**Internal communications.** Following the April 29 shutdown, Plant Management and Food Safety & Quality briefed facility leadership on the confirmed contamination and shutdown decision. Following the April 30 root cause identification, a broader internal briefing was held for department leads (Maintenance, Quality, Operations, Supply Chain, Regulatory Affairs) to align on the retrieval and recall execution plan ahead of the May 2 filing. Plant floor communication to production staff regarding the shutdown, its cause, and the path to restart was managed by Plant Management in coordination with Human Resources, given employee concerns about a facility employing 620 people undergoing a significant line shutdown.

**External communications — regulatory.** Regulatory Affairs maintained direct contact with FDA from the point of root cause confirmation (April 30) through the recall filing (May 2) and through the subsequent for-cause inspection, serving as the facility's single point of contact to avoid inconsistent messaging to the agency.

**External communications — retail and distribution partners.** Supply Chain led direct outreach to retail and distribution accounts in the 14 affected states, providing lot-level detail to support efficient retrieval ahead of and alongside the public recall notice, consistent with the retrieval approach described in Appendix B.

**External communications — consumers and media.** Public recall notice was issued in conjunction with the May 2 FDA filing, including product identification, lot codes, affected date ranges, and guidance for consumers regarding symptoms and return/disposal instructions. Given the severity classification (Class I) and the hospitalization among reported reactions, the notice emphasized the allergic reaction risk prominently rather than treating the recall as a routine labeling matter.

A structured after-action review of communications effectiveness — including message timing, channel selection, and consumer response volume following public notice — was conducted separately from this postmortem and is available through Regulatory Affairs upon request.
