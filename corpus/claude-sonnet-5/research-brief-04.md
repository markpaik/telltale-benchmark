# Self-Checkout, Shrink, and the 20-Store Expansion: A Data Review for the June Capital Decision

**Prepared by Nadia Farrukhzad, Manager, Retail Analytics**
**Wexford Market Group — Cranberry Township, PA**
**For: Desmond Achebe, Chief Operating Officer; Rosalind Tan, Chief Financial Officer**
**June 2026**

---

## The Question and What We Found

Chief Operating Officer Desmond Achebe asked Retail Analytics to answer a specific question before the June capital review: does converting checkout lanes to self-checkout raise shrink by enough, and in a way clear enough, to change the case for spending $11.5 million to convert 20 more stores beginning in the third quarter. Loss Prevention Director Walt Kowalczyk's objection is the proximate cause of this review — he has flagged that shrink at the 24 stores converted since 2023 is running well above shrink at the 37 stores that have not converted, and he has asked whether the rollout budgeted by Chief Financial Officer Rosalind Tan should proceed on schedule.

The short answer is that Kowalczyk is right that shrink is higher at converted stores, and right that it is worth stopping to look at before writing another check. He is not right, on the evidence available, that self-checkout itself is the primary driver, or that the pattern points toward halting the rollout. Three things came out of this work that matter for the June decision.

First, the raw comparison overstates the effect of conversion because the 24 converted stores were not a random sample. They were chosen for high sales volume, and high-volume stores already ran shrink about a fifth of a point above the rest of the chain before any self-checkout equipment arrived. Comparing converted stores to unconverted stores today mixes a selection effect that predates the equipment with whatever effect the equipment itself produced. The more defensible number is the change within the 24 stores from their own pre-conversion baseline, and that change is smaller than the headline gap — a rise of about 0.32 percentage points, not the 0.53-point gap that shows up when converted stores are compared to unconverted stores as they stand today.

Second, that within-store increase is not spread evenly. It concentrates heavily in two departments, health and beauty and fresh meat, and it concentrates even more heavily in the eleven of the 24 converted stores that removed staffed express lanes at the same time they installed self-checkout. The thirteen stores that kept at least one staffed lane running alongside the self-checkout banks show an increase close to the range that could plausibly be normal period-to-period variation. This pattern points toward lane staffing, and toward two specific departments, as the more likely levers than the self-checkout technology as a category.

Third, on the numbers available now, the labor savings the conversions have produced — roughly $6.1 million a year from an 8 percent reduction in labor hours per $1,000 of sales — exceed even the less favorable, unadjusted estimate of the added shrink cost. The financial case for continuing does not depend on resolving every open question about mechanism.

None of this proves self-checkout is shrink-neutral, and the brief says plainly where the data run out. But the evidence supports proceeding with the 20-store expansion on a modified basis — retaining staffed-lane presence and adding targeted controls in the two exposed departments — rather than pausing the program or proceeding unchanged.

## Data and Methods

### The inventory data: what a twice-yearly count can and cannot show

Wexford's only direct measure of shrink is the physical inventory count, taken twice a year at every store. Each count produces one number per store per period: the dollar value of inventory that should be on the shelf according to purchasing and sales records, against the dollar value actually counted, expressed as a percentage of sales for that period. Since the first self-checkout installations in 2023, each of the 61 stores has produced four such counts, which is where the 244 store-half-year observations in this analysis come from (61 stores times four cycles). One of those 244 was voided — a converted store's May 2025 count was thrown out after the outside counting vendor was found to have double-counted several backroom pallets, and rather than estimate around the error we excluded the observation entirely. That leaves 243 usable store-half-years: 95 for the 24 converted stores and 148 for the 37 unconverted stores.

The physical count is a good measure of the thing it measures — it is audited, it is consistent across stores, and it has been collected the same way since before the self-checkout program began, which is what makes a before-and-after comparison possible at all. Its limits matter just as much for this question. It is a snapshot every six months, not a continuous measure, so it cannot say when in a six-month window shrink occurred or distinguish a slow drift from a single bad month. It has no category detail on its own — the number that comes out of a physical count is store-wide. And it cannot, by itself, separate the effect of any one operational change from every other change happening in a store over the same six months: a remodel, a change in staffing, a shift in the local competitive environment, a bad run of weather that changed which categories moved. The physical count tells us what happened to the total. It does not tell us why.

### The scan data: what it adds, and where it starts

To get below the store-wide total, we used item-level point-of-sale scan data, which lets us reconcile expected inventory draw-down against actual recorded sales at the department level between physical counts. This is how we can say anything about which categories are driving the change rather than only the store-wide figure. The scan feed we can use for this purpose exists only from March 2025 forward. Wexford's point-of-sale systems generated transaction data before that, but it was not retained at the item level in a form usable for this kind of reconciliation until the current data warehouse went live in March 2025.

That start date has a real consequence for what this analysis can claim. The earliest self-checkout conversions happened in 2023, nearly two years before item-level scan data begins. There is no scan-level view of what category composition of shrink looked like at converted stores before conversion, or even in the first year after conversion. We can describe what category composition looks like now, in the scan-data period, and we can compare converted stores to unconverted stores within that same window — which is a fair comparison, because both store types are observed on the same calendar and with the same instrument — but we cannot show a before-and-after shift in category composition at the item level. The category findings in this brief are a cross-sectional comparison within the scan-data era, not a longitudinal one. That distinction matters and is repeated in the limitations section below.

Within the scan-data era, the physical inventory calendar gave us two cycles: May 2025 and November 2025. The May 2025 cycle is where the voided store falls, so scan-period coverage for converted stores is 23 stores in May and 24 in November, 47 store-cycles in all; unconverted stores contribute 37 store-cycles in each of the two periods, 74 in all. The category analysis below rests on 121 store-cycles of scan-reconciled data, a much thinner base than the 243 store-half-years underlying the topline shrink comparison, and the numbers should be read with that difference in weight in mind.

### Method

For the store-level comparison, we used two approaches side by side rather than picking one. The first is the comparison Kowalczyk raised: converted stores today against unconverted stores today. The second is a within-store comparison: each of the 24 converted stores against its own last pre-conversion reading. The second approach is the closer thing to a controlled comparison available in this data, because it holds the store itself constant and only lets time and the conversion vary; it is still not a designed experiment, and the limitations section addresses what that leaves open. For the category and lane-configuration breakdowns, we split the converted-store population into the subgroups described above (health and beauty and fresh meat versus all other departments; the 11 stores that removed staffed express lanes versus the 13 that kept at least one) and compared shrink patterns across those splits using the same before-and-after and cross-sectional logic. No statistical model beyond group averages and differences was used; with subgroups as small as 11 and 13 stores, a more elaborate model would create more precision than the data support, and it seemed more honest to show the plain numbers and flag the small samples than to dress them up.

## Results

### The store-level comparison, and the selection problem underneath it

The number Kowalczyk raised is accurate: converted stores are currently running shrink of 1.94 percent of sales, against 1.41 percent at unconverted stores, a gap of 0.53 percentage points. Read on its own, that comparison invites the conclusion that self-checkout adds roughly half a point of shrink. It shouldn't be read on its own, because the two groups of stores were never comparable to begin with.

The 24 stores selected for conversion since 2023 were not a cross-section of the chain. They were chosen for high sales volume — the stores management wanted to see labor-hour relief in first, because that is where the labor-hour savings would be largest. High-volume stores carry more transaction throughput, more SKU velocity in categories that are naturally harder to control (fresh departments, small high-value items), and, before any equipment changed, they already ran hotter on shrink than the rest of the chain. The last physical inventory reading before conversion equipment arrived, averaged across the 24 stores, was 1.62 percent — already about a fifth of a point above where unconverted stores sit today.

| | Shrink (% of sales) |
|---|---|
| Converted stores, last reading before conversion | 1.62% |
| Converted stores, current | 1.94% |
| Unconverted stores, current | 1.41% |
| **Within-store change, converted stores** | **+0.32 pts** |
| **Naive gap, converted vs. unconverted, current** | **0.53 pts** |

The gap that matters for judging the equipment is the smaller number: the 0.32-point rise within the 24 stores from their own baseline, which works out to roughly a 20 percent relative increase in shrink at those stores. That is still a real increase and still worth taking seriously — a 20 percent rise in shrink at a store is not noise. But it is meaningfully smaller than the 0.53-point figure that comparing converted stores to unconverted stores produces, and roughly 0.21 points of the naive gap — about 40 percent of it — is attributable to the fact that converted stores already ran hotter than the rest of the chain before the equipment showed up, not to anything that happened after.

One further caution belongs here. We do not have a pre-2023 baseline for the 37 unconverted stores, because the twice-yearly count records did not become available in a comparable, warehoused format until the self-checkout program's own tracking was set up. It is possible that shrink chainwide has drifted upward over the past three years for reasons that have nothing to do with self-checkout — a broader pattern in organized retail crime that most grocery operators in the region have reported — in which case even the 0.32-point within-store figure would overstate the equipment's own contribution, because some of that rise would have happened at those same 24 stores regardless of what checkout technology they ran. We do not have the data to size that effect, and it is flagged in the limitations section rather than backed out of the number, because backing out an effect we cannot measure would trade one kind of overstatement for a false precision.

### Where the increase concentrates: two departments, not the whole store

The within-store increase at converted stores is not spread evenly across the store, and the scan data — limited as it is to the two cycles since March 2025 — is specific enough to show where it lands. Health and beauty and fresh meat together account for about 5.8 percent and 8.6 percent of sales at converted stores respectively, roughly 14 percent of sales combined. In the same two cycles, those two departments account for 43 percent of shrink dollars at converted stores. At unconverted stores, over the identical calendar window, the same two departments make up a similar share of sales but only about 22 percent of shrink dollars.

| | Share of sales | Share of shrink dollars |
|---|---|---|
| Health & beauty + fresh meat, converted stores | ~14% | 43% |
| Health & beauty + fresh meat, unconverted stores | ~14% | 22% |

This is a cross-sectional comparison within the scan-data period, not a before-and-after view of the same converted stores — the limitation described above applies here directly, since we cannot show whether these two departments were already disproportionately represented in converted-store shrink before conversion, or whether the concentration is new. What the comparison does show is that, holding the calendar constant, converted stores carry a materially heavier share of their shrink in these two departments than unconverted stores do at the same point in time. That is consistent with a mechanism specific to self-checkout: health and beauty items are small, high per-unit-value, and easy to scan under a lower-priced look-alike code or conceal without scanning at all; fresh meat is weighed and price-looked-up rather than barcoded on a fixed label, which creates room for entering a wrong PLU code or a wrong weight at an unattended station in a way that is much harder to do past a trained cashier. Both departments are also departments where staffed checkout has historically relied on a cashier's product knowledge — recognizing that a package priced as chicken thighs is actually a ribeye — which a self-checkout station and an intermittently attending associate are less likely to catch.

We treat this as a plausible mechanism worth acting on, not as a proven causal finding. The scan-data base underneath it is 121 store-cycles, and the category split itself was not tested for statistical significance given the modest sample; it is a pattern management should treat as a strong lead for loss-prevention design, not as a settled fact.

### Lane configuration: the sharper signal

The more decisive split in the data is not by department but by what else changed in the store alongside the self-checkout installation. Of the 24 converted stores, 11 removed their staffed express lane at the same time they installed self-checkout, folding that transaction volume entirely into the unattended stations. The other 13 kept at least one staffed express lane running alongside the self-checkout bank.

The within-store shrink change is starkly different between these two groups. The 11 stores that removed staffed lanes went from a baseline of about 1.60 percent to a current reading of about 2.11 percent, a rise of 0.51 percentage points. The 13 stores that kept a staffed lane went from about 1.64 percent to about 1.79 percent, a rise of 0.15 percentage points — small enough that it sits inside the range of normal period-to-period movement in this data.

| | Baseline shrink | Current shrink | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| 11 stores, staffed lane removed | 1.60% | 2.11% | +0.51 pts |
| 13 stores, staffed lane retained | 1.64% | 1.79% | +0.15 pts |
| **All 24 converted stores** | **1.62%** | **1.94%** | **+0.32 pts** |

Weighted across the two groups, these figures reproduce the chain-wide 0.32-point converted-store increase, and they show that the increase is not evenly distributed across the 24 stores — it is disproportionately carried by the 11 stores where staffed presence at checkout dropped out entirely. Put differently, roughly three-quarters of the total rise in shrink dollars across the 24 converted stores traces to fewer than half of them, and what those stores have in common is not the self-checkout equipment, which all 24 share, but the absence of a staffed lane, which only 11 share.

This is the single most useful finding for the capital decision, and it is also the one most in need of a caveat about sample size. Eleven and thirteen stores are small groups, and a handful of stores with unusually high or low shrink in either group could move these averages meaningfully. We do not have enough stores to run this split by department as well without the subgroups becoming too small to interpret — a joint breakdown, for instance, of health-and-beauty shrink specifically among stores that removed staffed lanes, would rest on a handful of stores and was not attempted here. The finding should be read as directional, not as a precisely estimated effect.

### The labor side of the ledger

The self-checkout program was built on a labor-savings case, and that case has held up in the data reviewed for this brief. Labor hours per $1,000 of sales fell 8 percent at converted stores relative to their own pre-conversion levels, a reduction management estimates is worth roughly $6.1 million a year across the 24 converted stores. We did not independently re-derive this figure for this brief — it comes from the operations team's own payroll and scheduling data — but we note that it deserves the same scrutiny applied to the shrink numbers above rather than being accepted as a clean baseline against which shrink is the only variable in question. In particular, if high-volume stores were also better positioned to absorb labor-hour reductions than a representative store would be — more consistent traffic, more stable scheduling — the same selection effect that inflates the naive shrink comparison could also inflate the naive labor-savings comparison. We flag this rather than resolve it; a full audit of the labor-savings estimate was outside the scope of what Achebe asked for, but it should not be assumed clean simply because it currently favors the case for proceeding.

Set against the labor savings, the added shrink cost can be estimated two ways, using converted stores' total annual sales, which we put at roughly $720 million (24 stores averaging about $30 million a year, consistent with their selection as the chain's higher-volume locations, against a chain-wide average of roughly $23 million per store on $1.4 billion in total sales across 61 stores). Applying the naive 0.53-point gap to that sales base implies an added shrink cost of about $3.8 million a year. Applying the more defensible 0.32-point within-store change implies about $2.3 million a year.

| | Estimated annual cost/benefit |
|---|---|
| Labor savings, converted stores | $6.1 million |
| Added shrink cost (naive gap, 0.53 pts) | $3.8 million |
| Added shrink cost (within-store change, 0.32 pts) | $2.3 million |
| **Net benefit, conservative (naive gap) estimate** | **$2.3 million** |
| **Net benefit, within-store estimate** | **$3.8 million** |

Under either way of estimating the shrink cost — the less favorable naive comparison or the more defensible within-store change — the labor savings exceed it, and the program shows a net financial benefit somewhere between $2.3 million and $3.8 million a year across the 24 stores already converted. This does not settle the question of whether the program should expand unchanged; it does mean the financial case for the program overall does not collapse under either reading of the shrink data, including the reading closest to the one Kowalczyk has raised.

## Limitations

This analysis rests on real data collected consistently over time, but it is not a designed experiment, and several limitations bound what it can support.

**The 24 converted stores were not randomly chosen, and no adjustment fully corrects for that.** They were selected for high volume, and the within-store before-and-after comparison used throughout this brief addresses the most obvious consequence of that — the fact that high-volume stores already ran hotter on shrink — but it does not address every consequence. High-volume stores may also differ from lower-volume stores in customer mix, local crime patterns, store age, or management tenure in ways that affect how a store's shrink evolves over time regardless of what checkout technology it runs. Without a comparison group that was converted on some basis unrelated to volume, this risk cannot be fully separated from the equipment's effect.

**There is no pre-2023 shrink trend for unconverted stores.** We can compare converted stores' current shrink to unconverted stores' current shrink, and we can compare converted stores to their own past, but we cannot compare unconverted stores to their own past over the same period, because comparable warehoused inventory records were not established until the self-checkout program's tracking went live. If shrink has risen chain-wide for reasons unrelated to self-checkout — a regional trend in organized retail theft, for instance — some portion of the 0.32-point within-store rise at converted stores would reflect that broader trend rather than the equipment, and this analysis has no way to size that portion.

**The category findings are cross-sectional, not longitudinal.** Item-level scan data begins in March 2025, nearly two years after the earliest conversions. We can show that health and beauty and fresh meat carry a disproportionate share of shrink dollars at converted stores relative to unconverted stores within the same scan-data window, but we cannot show that this concentration is new, or that it grew after conversion, because there is no scan-level view of these categories before conversion at any of the 24 stores. The category story in this brief is a strong hypothesis, built on a real and current disparity, not a demonstrated before-and-after shift.

**Sample sizes for the subgroup splits are small.** Eleven stores removed staffed lanes; thirteen did not. Twenty-three or 24 stores contribute to the category analysis in any given scan cycle. These are the entire relevant populations at Wexford, not samples drawn from a larger population, so there is no sampling-error problem in the usual sense — but it does mean a small number of unusual stores, in either direction, can move a group average noticeably, and the lane-configuration and category findings should be read as directional patterns rather than precisely estimated effects.

**The excluded store may not be random either.** The May 2025 count voided for vendor error belonged to a converted store, which slightly reduces the converted-store sample in that cycle and in the scan-data base built from it. We have no reason to think this particular store was unusual, but we also have not been able to confirm it was not, since its data for that cycle does not exist to check.

**Physical inventory is a snapshot, not a continuous measure.** It cannot attribute a change in shrink to a specific week, a specific promotional period, or a specific operational change with precision; it can only compare the state of the store at two points six months apart. Several things happen at any given store over six months. The lane-configuration finding in this brief benefits from being a comparison of two clearly defined groups of stores rather than an attempt to time-stamp a cause, but the same caution about coincident changes applies to the eleven-versus-thirteen split as to the store-wide numbers: something else could differ between the 11 stores that removed staffed lanes and the 13 that kept them, beyond the staffing decision itself.

**Labor savings were not independently re-derived for this brief** and deserve the same kind of scrutiny given to the shrink figures, particularly around whether the same volume-based selection that affects the shrink comparison also affects the labor comparison.

None of these limitations argue for discarding the analysis. They argue for treating its conclusions as reasonably well-supported directional findings appropriate for a capital-allocation decision made under normal business uncertainty, not as a controlled study that has isolated a single causal effect.

## Implications for the June Decision

Achebe asked for an answer to a specific question, and the honest answer is neither "shrink is not a problem" nor "the rollout should stop." It is that the shrink increase is real, smaller than the headline comparison suggests once store selection is accounted for, concentrated enough in cause and location to be addressable, and, on the numbers available now, still outweighed by the labor savings the program has produced. That combination points toward proceeding with the $11.5 million, 20-store expansion Tan has budgeted for the third quarter, with three modifications drawn directly from the results above.

**Keep a staffed lane at every converted store.** The clearest and most actionable finding in this review is the gap between the 11 stores that removed staffed express lanes and the 13 that kept one running. The 11 carry roughly three-quarters of the chain-wide increase in converted-store shrink; the 13 show an increase close to what could be ordinary variation. Retaining at least one staffed lane at each of the 20 stores in the next wave, rather than converting checkout entirely to self-service, would forgo some of the labor savings the program is built on, but it directly targets the pattern in the data most associated with the added shrink, and the labor-savings margin over the estimated shrink cost — $2.3 million to $3.8 million a year at 24 stores — leaves room to absorb a partial staffing retention and still come out ahead.

**Add targeted controls in health and beauty and fresh meat before or alongside the next wave of conversions.** The concentration of shrink dollars in these two departments at converted stores, relative to unconverted stores over the same period, is not proof of a causal mechanism, but it is specific enough to act on with low-cost, targeted measures: locked or attendant-verified fixtures for the highest-shrink health and beauty categories, and weight-verification or attendant sign-off requirements for self-checkout transactions involving fresh meat. These are the kind of measures loss prevention already knows how to design; the case here is for pointing them at the two departments the data actually implicate rather than applying a general response across the whole store.

**Build a better comparison into the next wave.** The core weakness in this review is that the 24 already-converted stores were chosen for volume, which makes it hard to cleanly separate the equipment's effect from the effect of having already been a higher-shrink store. If the next 20 stores are also selected purely on volume, the same problem will recur and the next review a year from now will face the same ambiguity this one does. Choosing at least a subset of the next wave from stores that are not selected on volume alone — matching some converted stores to similar unconverted stores on size, shrink history, and location type — would let a future review make a cleaner comparison, and would cost nothing beyond how the store list for the rollout is assembled. We would also recommend extending the item-level scan reconciliation to cover a longer pre-conversion window at each newly converted store, so that by the time of the next review, the category-level, before-and-after comparison that this analysis could not make for the original 24 stores will be available for the next 20.

On the question actually before the June review — whether the shrink pattern at converted stores is reason to change the $11.5 million commitment — the data support proceeding, with staffed-lane retention and department-level controls built into the rollout plan rather than treated as separate initiatives. Kowalczyk's underlying concern is validated by this analysis: shrink at converted stores has risen by a real amount, and it is not evenly spread across departments or across stores. What the analysis adds is that the increase traces most clearly to a specific and changeable operational choice — removing staffed presence entirely rather than pairing it with self-checkout — and to two departments where targeted, inexpensive controls are available. That is a case for adjusting how the next 20 stores are converted, not for declining to convert them.

## Monitoring Plan for the Next Wave

If the June review adopts this recommendation, Retail Analytics proposes a specific monitoring cadence rather than waiting for the next scheduled physical inventory to find out whether the modifications are working. The physical count remains the only audited measure of shrink, and it will continue to run twice a year regardless, but the scan-data reconciliation used for the category analysis in this brief can be run monthly at low incremental cost, since the underlying feed already exists. We propose a rolling monthly reconciliation for all 20 newly converted stores from the month of installation forward, benchmarked against the pre-conversion baseline for each store rather than against the chain average, so that a store beginning to drift can be flagged well before the next physical count confirms it six months later.

Three thresholds are worth setting in advance rather than deciding on later, when a specific store's numbers might make the decision harder to make cleanly. First, any newly converted store whose monthly reconciled shrink estimate exceeds its own pre-conversion baseline by more than 0.4 percentage points — above the 0.32-point average this brief found across the original 24 — should trigger a store-level review within 30 days rather than waiting for the semiannual count. Second, if health and beauty or fresh meat shrink at any newly converted store exceeds 30 percent of that store's total shrink dollars, loss prevention should be notified directly rather than waiting for the department-level pattern to show up in a chain-wide rollup. Third, any store where a staffed lane is later removed after initial conversion — whether for cost reasons or attrition — should be tracked as a distinct group from the outset, given how much weight the lane-configuration split carried in this analysis; an informal or gradual reduction in staffed-lane hours could reproduce the pattern seen in the original 11 stores without ever showing up as a discrete "removal" decision in any log Retail Analytics currently has visibility into.

We would also recommend that the next formal review of this program, timed to the December capital cycle, use six full months of scan-reconciled data from the new wave rather than the two-cycle window available for this brief. That would roughly double the store-cycle base underlying the category analysis and, more importantly, would allow — for the first time — a genuine before-and-after comparison at the item level for at least the earliest stores in the new wave, closing the specific gap this brief could not close for the original 24.

## Appendix: Distribution of Store-Level Results

The averages presented in the body of this brief describe the center of each group, but the spread around those centers is informative in its own right and is described here rather than plotted, consistent with how this brief presents its evidence throughout.

Among the 24 converted stores, the within-store change in shrink from baseline to current reading is not tightly clustered around the 0.32-point average. A small number of stores account for a disproportionate share of the total movement. Roughly six of the 24 stores show little to no change from baseline — several are within 0.05 points of their pre-conversion reading, essentially flat — while four or five stores show increases above 0.6 points, well above the group average. This unevenness is consistent with the lane-configuration finding, since the highest-increase stores are drawn almost entirely from the 11 that removed staffed lanes, but it is not fully explained by that split alone: two of the 13 stores that retained a staffed lane also show increases above 0.4 points, and one of the 11 stores that removed staffed lanes shows almost no change at all. These exceptions are exactly the kind of case a store-level review, rather than a chain-wide policy, is suited to explain, and they are part of why this brief frames the lane-configuration finding as directional rather than deterministic.

Among the 37 unconverted stores, the spread is narrower. Current shrink readings cluster more tightly around the 1.41 percent average, with most stores falling between 1.2 and 1.6 percent and few outliers in either direction. This is worth noting because it is itself a data point: the unconverted population, not chosen on any basis related to shrink, behaves more uniformly than the converted population, which was chosen on a basis — volume — correlated with shrink to begin with. The relative uniformity of the unconverted group is one more piece of evidence, alongside the pre-conversion baseline figure, that the converted stores were a distinct population before the equipment ever arrived, not a comparable set of stores that happened to receive different treatment.

On the category side, the concentration of shrink dollars in health and beauty and fresh meat at converted stores is fairly consistent across the 23 and 24 stores contributing to the two scan-data cycles rather than driven by one or two extreme stores; roughly two-thirds of converted stores individually show these two departments carrying a share of shrink dollars above their share of sales, which is part of why we treat the category pattern as a reasonably reliable signal despite the short data window, even though we cannot yet say when it began.
