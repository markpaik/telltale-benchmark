# Executive Summary 1 of 4

## Small-Format Urban Store Pilot

**Period reviewed:** Store openings mid-2023 through fiscal year end, February 1, 2025

### Finding and Recommendation

The small-format urban pilot is the strongest performer in the fiscal 2025 pilot portfolio and the only one that beat its underwriting on every material line. The three stores opened in 2023 generated $912 in sales per square foot against a chain average of $640 — a 42 percent premium — and each reached store-level breakeven within 14 months of opening, ahead of the 18-month assumption in the original approval. The format works: the trade areas, the assortment logic, and the labor model all held up through a full fiscal year of operation, and the stores are now contributing positive cash at the four-wall level.

The Strategy and Performance Office recommends that the March capital meeting approve a second tranche of five small-format locations for fiscal 2026, with sites selected against the trade-area screen validated by the pilot, and that the format be treated as a standing growth vehicle rather than a pilot from this point forward. We further recommend that the second tranche carry the same stage-gate discipline the pilot did — no site proceeds to lease signature without independent trade-area verification — because the pilot's success is at least as attributable to site selection rigor as to the format itself, and we should not let a good result loosen the process that produced it.

### The Evidence

The pilot was approved to answer a specific question: can Palmetto Fresh Markets operate profitably at roughly one-third the footprint of a conventional store, in dense urban trade areas where our standard 35,000-to-45,000-square-foot box cannot be sited economically? The three pilot stores were opened in 2023 in urban infill locations across the three-state footprint, each in the 11,000-to-14,000-square-foot range, each carrying a curated assortment of roughly 7,000 SKUs against the 28,000-plus carried in a conventional store.

The revenue result is unambiguous. At $912 per square foot, the pilot stores are producing 42 percent more sales per foot than the chain average of $640. That premium is not a function of a single outlier location; all three stores exceeded the chain average, and the spread among the three is narrow enough that we regard the result as a property of the format and the site screen rather than a lucky draw. Basket composition skews toward fresh, prepared foods, and private label, which is favorable to margin, and the stores capture a higher share of weekday trips than conventional locations, consistent with the fill-in and meal-solution shopping occasions the format was designed to serve.

The breakeven timeline is the second pillar. The original approval assumed store-level breakeven at 18 months, with an acknowledged risk that urban labor costs and shrink could push that to 24. All three stores crossed into positive four-wall contribution by month 14. Two factors drove the outperformance. First, the compact footprint carries a leaner labor model than we assumed — the stores run efficiently with smaller crews than the pro forma provided for, because the reduced assortment simplifies stocking, ordering, and resets. Second, shrink, which we flagged as the principal urban risk at approval, came in below the conventional-store average rather than above it, which we attribute to the smaller footprint's inherent visibility and to the higher velocity of the curated assortment leaving less product aging on the shelf.

The honest caveats belong in this summary as well. Three stores is a small sample, and all three sites were selected with unusual care precisely because they were pilots; a five-store expansion tranche will test whether the site screen scales or whether the pilot benefited from cherry-picked locations. The format's SKU discipline is also fragile — there will be continuous pressure from merchandising to add items, and the economics of the format depend on resisting most of that pressure. The $912-per-foot figure is earned by velocity on a tight assortment; a small-format store that drifts toward 10,000 or 12,000 SKUs will lose the operating simplicity that produced the 14-month breakeven. Finally, urban leases in the markets we are screening have moved since 2023, and the second tranche's occupancy costs will run higher than the pilot's; the pro formas submitted with the tranche request reflect current market rents, not pilot-era rents.

None of these caveats changes the conclusion. The pilot was designed to test a hypothesis, the hypothesis was confirmed on both the revenue line and the timeline, and the appropriate response is disciplined expansion.

### Fiscal 2026 Commitments Already in Place

The chain has already committed to the following for fiscal 2026, ahead of the March meeting: two of the five proposed second-tranche sites have signed letters of intent, with lease execution contingent on capital approval at the March meeting; the fiscal 2026 capital plan as drafted carries a $9.5 million placeholder for the tranche; and the small-format assortment team — two merchandising analysts seconded from Bettina Halloran's organization — has been made permanent in the fiscal 2026 operating budget. The remaining three sites are in the screening pipeline with no binding commitments. If the March meeting declines the tranche, the two letters of intent lapse without penalty, but the site pipeline and the seconded team represent roughly $600,000 of fiscal 2026 operating commitment that would be stranded.

# Executive Summary 2 of 4

## Third-Party Delivery Partnership

**Period reviewed:** Full fiscal 2025, February 2024 through February 1, 2025

### Finding and Recommendation

The third-party delivery partnership lost money on every meaningful measure in fiscal 2025 and, on its current terms, has no credible path to contribution-positive economics. The program handled 380,000 orders during the year and carried a negative contribution margin of $1.9 million — roughly $5 of loss per order after partner fees, picking labor, and promotional funding. The losses are structural, not scale-dependent: delivery baskets ran 22 percent smaller than in-store baskets, which means each order carries less gross margin dollar content to absorb a fee structure that is largely fixed per order. Growing volume on these terms grows the loss.

The customer experience compounds the economic problem. The 31 percent substitution rate — nearly one item in three swapped or shorted — generated 1,100 refund claims during the year and is, by a wide margin, the leading driver of delivery-related customer complaints. We are paying $1.9 million a year to deliver an experience that measurably damages the brand with the customers who use it most.

The Strategy and Performance Office recommends that the chain not renew the partnership on current terms when the agreement reaches its renewal window in fiscal 2026, and that the March meeting authorize a restructuring negotiation with a defined walk-away position. Specifically, we recommend the negotiating mandate require three conditions for renewal, any one of which failing triggers exit:

1. A fee structure — reduced per-order fees, a volume-tiered schedule, or a revenue-share conversion — that closes at least $1.2 million of the $1.9 million annual contribution gap at current volumes.
2. Integration of the partner's picking workflow with our real-time inventory feed, which our analysis indicates would cut the substitution rate roughly in half.
3. Shared financial responsibility for substitution-driven refunds, which we currently absorb entirely.

We do not recommend abandoning delivery as a capability. Roughly 60 percent of delivery customers also shop our stores, and delivery order frequency correlates with total household spend. The recommendation is to stop funding a structurally unprofitable version of the capability, not to concede the channel.

### The Evidence

The economics first. The 380,000 orders delivered in fiscal 2025 generated meaningful top-line revenue, but the contribution waterfall is unforgiving. The partner's per-order fee, the picking labor we fund in-store, and the promotional co-funding required under the agreement together exceed the gross margin dollars on the average delivery basket. The core problem is basket size: at 22 percent below the in-store average, the delivery basket simply does not carry enough margin content. The fee structure was negotiated in 2023 against a pro forma that assumed delivery baskets would approximate in-store baskets. They never have, in any month of the program's operation, and there is no trend toward convergence — the gap was as wide in the fourth quarter as in the first. This is why we characterize the loss as structural. The per-order economics do not improve with volume; they replicate with volume.

The substitution problem is the second finding, and it is both a customer problem and a hidden cost. A 31 percent substitution rate means the partner's shoppers could not fulfill the ordered item as ordered on nearly one in three lines. Root-cause work by our team traced the majority of substitutions to a single mechanism: the partner's shopper application works from a stale inventory snapshot rather than our live perpetual inventory, so shoppers routinely pursue items that sold through hours earlier. The downstream cost is concrete — 1,100 refund claims in the year, all funded by us under the current agreement, plus customer-service handling time that is not captured in the $1.9 million figure. Survey data from the customer insights team shows delivery users report satisfaction scores 14 points below in-store shoppers, with substitutions cited as the leading complaint. These are our customers experiencing our brand through the partner's weakest process, and we bear the full financial and reputational cost of the failure.

The strategic value of the channel is real but should be sized honestly. Delivery represents a low-single-digit share of chain revenue. The customer-retention argument for delivery — that households who use it consolidate more of their total spend with us — is supported by the data, but it justifies operating the channel at modest cost, not at $1.9 million of annual loss with a degraded experience attached. Our modeling indicates that a restructured agreement meeting the three conditions above would bring the program to approximately breakeven at current volumes, at which point the retention benefit carries the strategic case. If the partner will not move, the exit alternatives — a smaller regional fulfillment partner, or a limited first-party model piloted from the small-format urban stores, where trade-area density suits it — are both viable and both preferable to renewal on current terms.

### Fiscal 2026 Commitments Already in Place

The partnership agreement runs into fiscal 2026, with a renewal decision required during the year; until that window, the chain is contractually committed to continuing the program on current terms, which implies roughly $950,000 of further contribution loss in the first half of fiscal 2026 at current run rates. The fiscal 2026 operating plan already carries this cost. Two commitments beyond the contract exist: the e-commerce integration budget includes $400,000 for API work connecting our perpetual inventory to partner systems — spending that is useful under either a restructured agreement or a successor partner and which we recommend proceeding — and the marketing plan includes delivery promotional co-funding through the renewal window, which we recommend suspending immediately as it is discretionary and currently subsidizes loss-making volume. No fiscal 2026 commitments exist that would impede exit at the renewal window.

# Executive Summary 3 of 4

## Private Label Expansion Program

**Period reviewed:** Full fiscal 2025, February 2024 through February 1, 2025; program led by Jamal Whitfield, SVP of Operations

### Finding and Recommendation

The private label expansion delivered its two headline objectives and exposed a real execution weakness beneath them. Penetration rose from 18 percent to 24 percent of sales during fiscal 2025 — a six-point gain in a single year, against a plan of four — and chain gross margin improved by 140 basis points, worth roughly $19–20 million of annualized gross margin dollars on the current revenue base. Those results are genuine and they are the largest margin contribution any initiative delivered in fiscal 2025.

The weakness sits in category execution. Unit velocity declined in dairy and frozen following the private label conversions in those departments, and nine items launched during the year were discontinued within twelve months of introduction. The pattern in both cases is the same: the program's pace outran its quality gates. Items reached shelves without adequate benchmarking against the national-brand equivalents they were meant to displace, and in dairy and frozen — categories where brand loyalty is strongest and quality perception is least forgiving — customers responded by buying less of the category rather than switching to our label.

The Strategy and Performance Office recommends the program continue into fiscal 2026 at a deliberately slower pace, with three specific modifications: first, a penetration target of 26–27 percent for fiscal 2026 rather than the 30 percent in the original multi-year plan, prioritizing item quality over item count; second, a pause on new private label introductions in dairy and frozen until the velocity decline in those departments is diagnosed and reversed, with existing items in those categories subjected to blind quality benchmarking against national-brand equivalents; and third, a mandatory pre-launch stage gate for all new items — blind consumer testing against the national-brand benchmark, with a defined pass threshold — so that the nine-discontinuation pattern is not repeated. The nine failed items cost an estimated $700,000 in development, packaging, markdown, and write-off costs, and the reputational cost in the affected categories is harder to measure but visible in the velocity data.

We want to be explicit that this is a recommendation to reinforce a winning program, not to rein in a failing one. The 140-basis-point margin gain is the single most valuable operating result in this review, and Jamal Whitfield's team delivered penetration growth at a pace few regional chains have matched. The recommendation exists because the same pace that produced the gain also produced the failures, and the program's next phase moves into categories where execution errors are costlier.

### The Evidence

The margin arithmetic is straightforward and favorable. Private label items carry gross margins typically 8 to 12 points above national-brand equivalents. Moving six points of sales mix into private label, concentrated in center store, produced the 140-basis-point improvement in blended gross margin. At the chain's $1.4 billion revenue base, that improvement is worth approximately $19.6 million in annualized gross margin dollars. Even after netting the program's operating costs — the expanded private label team, packaging and development spend, and the launch-failure costs noted above — the program returned its investment several times over within the fiscal year. The penetration gain also has strategic value beyond the margin line: at 24 percent, our penetration now approaches the regional-chain median, reducing a competitive disadvantage against larger competitors whose private label programs run above 30 percent.

The category-level evidence tells a more textured story. The penetration gains were broad but not uniform. Center-store categories — canned goods, dry grocery, paper, household — converted smoothly, with private label items achieving unit velocities at or near the national brands they sat beside, and category unit sales holding steady. This is the pattern a healthy private label program produces: customers switch labels without leaving the category.

Dairy and frozen broke that pattern. In both departments, category unit velocity declined following the private label conversions, meaning some customers responded to the changed assortment by purchasing less of the category — or purchasing it elsewhere — rather than accepting the private label alternative. Store-level feedback gathered by the operations team points to quality perception as the mechanism: in categories where the product is consumed directly and quality differences are immediately tasted, an item that is merely adequate does not convert loyal national-brand buyers, and a delisted favorite sends them to a competitor. The nine discontinued items reinforce the diagnosis. Post-mortems on the nine show a common thread of compressed development timelines: items moved from concept to shelf in months, without the blind consumer testing that our larger competitors treat as a mandatory gate. Several of the nine were in dairy and frozen; none of the nine had been benchmark-tested against the national brand they were positioned to displace.

The lesson we draw is about sequencing, not about ceiling. Private label penetration of 30 percent remains an appropriate multi-year ambition, but the remaining road runs through harder categories than the road already traveled. Center store converts on price; fresh, dairy, and frozen convert on quality, and quality takes time the fiscal 2025 pace did not allow. A year at 26–27 percent penetration with strong items will do more for the program's durability than a year at 30 percent with another cohort of discontinuations.

### Fiscal 2026 Commitments Already in Place

The chain has already committed to the following for fiscal 2026: supplier agreements are signed for 34 new private label items scheduled to launch during the year, of which 6 are in dairy or frozen — we recommend the 6 be held at the pre-launch gate pending the category diagnosis, which the supplier agreements permit at modest rescheduling cost; the private label team expansion of four roles approved in the fiscal 2026 operating budget is filled or in hiring; packaging redesign for the core label, budgeted at $1.1 million, is contracted and in production; and the fiscal 2026 merchandising plan as drafted assumes the original 30 percent penetration trajectory, which will need restatement to 26–27 percent if the March meeting adopts this recommendation, with a corresponding gross margin plan adjustment of roughly 40–50 basis points versus the draft plan. The blind-testing stage gate would require approximately $250,000 of incremental fiscal 2026 spend for consumer research capacity, which is not currently in the budget and is requested alongside this recommendation.

# Executive Summary 4 of 4

## Store Remodel Program

**Period reviewed:** Program approval through February 1, 2025, covering the 11 approved remodels; 4 remodels paused since January 2025

### Finding and Recommendation

The remodel program has missed its underwriting on both sides of the return equation, and the pause on the remaining four stores should hold until the program is re-scoped and re-underwritten. Eleven stores were approved at $2.3 million each; completed remodels have averaged $3.4 million, a 48 percent overrun. The remodels were underwritten to deliver an 11 percent comparable sales lift; the completed stores have delivered 6.1 percent. Cost overran by roughly half while the benefit came in at roughly half — the combination moves the program's returns from the mid-teens IRR in the original approval to the low single digits on actuals, below the chain's cost of capital. Completing the remaining four stores on the current scope and the current underwriting would deploy roughly $13.6 million of capital into a program that the completed stores demonstrate does not clear the hurdle rate.

The Strategy and Performance Office recommends the March meeting take three actions. First, formally convert January's operational pause on the four remaining remodels into a program hold, so that no further capital is committed under the existing scope. Second, commission a re-scoped remodel package — a lighter design concentrated on the elements the completed-store data shows actually drive sales lift, targeted at $1.8–2.2 million per store — with the four held stores re-underwritten individually against realistic lift assumptions of 5 to 7 percent. Third, adopt the estimating and change-order controls detailed below for all future capital construction, because the overrun pattern in this program reflects process weaknesses that will recur in any construction program the chain runs, including the small-format expansion recommended in Summary 1.

We do not recommend cancelling the four remaining remodels outright. The four stores were selected for remodel because they are aging assets in defensible trade areas, and doing nothing carries its own cost in competitive erosion. The recommendation is that they receive the remodel the data supports — a lighter package underwritten at the lift the completed stores actually achieved — rather than the remodel the original program specified.

### The Evidence

The cost overrun decomposes into three roughly equal parts. About a third of the $1.1 million average overrun traces to estimating error at approval: the $2.3 million budget was built from a 2022-era cost baseline and did not adequately reflect construction cost inflation in our markets by the time work commenced. About a third traces to scope growth during execution: refrigeration, electrical, and code-compliance work discovered after demolition in these older stores was absorbed into the remodel budgets rather than being either anticipated in contingency or separately approved. And about a third traces to discretionary scope additions — finish upgrades, expanded prepared-foods installations, additional décor — approved at the project level without program-level review. The first cause is an estimating discipline problem, the second a due-diligence and contingency problem, the third a change-control problem. All three are correctable, and none was corrected during the program because no completed-store results existed to trigger scrutiny until the program was well underway. That sequencing lesson — complete and measure one or two stores before releasing the full tranche — applies to every multi-site capital program the chain runs.

The lift shortfall is the more consequential finding, because it goes to the underwriting rather than the execution. The 11 percent comparable sales lift assumption was drawn from industry remodel benchmarks and from the results of a small number of Palmetto remodels in the late 2010s. The completed stores in this program delivered 6.1 percent — a real and persistent lift, sustained past the initial reopening period, but barely half the assumption. Element-level analysis across the completed stores shows the lift is not evenly earned by the remodel scope. The departments that drove the gains were fresh — produce, meat, and prepared foods — where the remodel visibly changed the shopping experience. The most expensive scope elements, including full décor packages and center-store refixturing, show no measurable independent sales effect. In plain terms: we spent $3.4 million per store on a package in which perhaps $2 million of scope produced substantially all of the 6.1 percent lift. This is the analytical basis for the re-scoped package recommended above — concentrate spend on fresh departments and front-of-store, underwrite at 5 to 7 percent, and the returns clear the hurdle at a $1.8–2.2 million cost.

At those re-scoped figures, an illustrative store generating $22 million in annual revenue and earning a 6 percent lift returns its capital on an acceptable timeline; at the actual $3.4 million cost and the same lift, it does not. The problem with this program is not that remodels do not work — it is that this remodel package, at this cost, against this lift, does not.

### Fiscal 2026 Commitments Already in Place

The fiscal 2026 position on the four paused stores is as follows. Design and engineering work for all four is complete and paid, representing roughly $900,000 of sunk cost, the majority of which — site surveys, structural and refrigeration engineering — remains usable under a re-scoped package. General contractor agreements for two of the four stores were executed before the January pause; both contracts permit scope revision, and one carries a cancellation provision of approximately $150,000 if terminated outright. Long-lead refrigeration equipment for one store, roughly $600,000, is ordered and non-cancellable, but is compatible with the lighter package and would be installed under either scope. The fiscal 2026 capital plan as drafted carries $13.6 million for the four completions at original scope; adoption of this recommendation would reduce that line to approximately $8–9 million, releasing $4.5–5.5 million of planned fiscal 2026 capital — capacity we note is sufficient, if the committee chooses, to fund a substantial portion of the small-format tranche recommended in Summary 1. No commitments exist that force completion at the original scope, and the cost of holding the pause through a second-quarter re-underwriting is limited to the carrying inefficiency of the four aging stores operating as-is.

# Appendix A

## Cross-Program Observations for the Committee

The four summaries above stand on their own, but the review surfaced three patterns that cut across programs and that bear on how the chain approves and governs capital going forward. The Strategy and Performance Office offers them here rather than embedding them in any single summary, because each is a property of our process rather than of any one program.

**First, the pilots that were staged outperformed the programs that were released all at once.** The small-format pilot opened three stores, measured them through a full fiscal year, and only now seeks expansion capital; its results beat underwriting. The remodel program released all eleven stores against a single approval before any completed store had produced comparable sales data; by the time the 6.1 percent lift and the $3.4 million average cost were visible, seven stores were done or committed. The lesson is not that remodels are riskier than new formats — the opposite is ordinarily true — but that sequencing is the cheapest risk control available to us. We recommend the committee adopt a standing rule for multi-site capital programs: no more than a defined minority of sites, we suggest 25 percent, may be committed before at least one site has reported a full measurement period against underwriting. Had this rule governed the remodel program, the estimating error and the lift shortfall would have been caught roughly $8 million earlier.

**Second, every fiscal 2025 shortfall traces to an assumption imported from outside rather than tested inside.** The delivery partnership's fee structure was negotiated against an assumed basket size that our own e-commerce data, even in 2023, did not support. The remodel program's 11 percent lift assumption came from industry benchmarks and decade-old internal results rather than from a test store. The private label program's dairy and frozen conversions assumed the center-store switching pattern would carry into perishables without category-specific testing. In each case, internal data existed or could have been generated cheaply, and in each case the external assumption was the one that failed. The small-format pilot, by contrast, was built almost entirely on assumptions it was designed to test, and it is the program that beat plan. The practical implication for the March meeting is that underwriting review should distinguish explicitly between assumptions we have tested and assumptions we have imported, and should apply contingency accordingly.

**Third, the chain's fiscal 2026 commitments were made ahead of the evidence in three of the four programs.** Supplier agreements for 34 private label items, contractor agreements for two remodels, and delivery promotional co-funding were all committed under fiscal 2026 plans drafted before this review closed. None of these commitments is ruinous, and the summaries above show that most are recoverable or repurposable. But the pattern means the March meeting is partly ratifying decisions rather than making them. We recommend that in future planning cycles, fiscal-year commitments tied to programs under active review carry explicit contingency language, so that the capital committee retains the optionality this review assumes it has.
