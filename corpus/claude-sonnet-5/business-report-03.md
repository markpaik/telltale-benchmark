## 1. Executive Summary

Foxglove closed the second quarter of fiscal 2026 with a consolidated store comp that was essentially flat — down roughly a tenth of a point on a blended basis — but that headline figure conceals a widening split between regions that are executing well and regions that are struggling under a combination of self-inflicted and market-driven pressure. The Northern region, lifted by a completed remodel program in three stores, posted the strongest comp in the company at 5.6 percent. The Bluegrass region held serve at 2.1 percent despite the continuing drag from two stores that lost anchor traffic when an adjacent department store closed. Ohio Valley and Appalachian, by contrast, comped down 3.4 percent and 6.2 percent respectively, and both regions carry structural questions — store manager turnover in Ohio Valley and a looming lease renewal in Appalachian — that will not resolve themselves without deliberate action in the back half.

E-commerce grew 18 percent to $44 million on a trailing-twelve-month basis, which on its face is the best growth number in the business. It is also the most expensive revenue we generate. A carrier fuel and residential-delivery surcharge introduced by our primary parcel carrier in the back half of calendar 2025, combined with a return rate of 21 percent on furniture and décor categories that were never designed for a bracket-and-box supply chain, drove the channel to a negative 2.1 percent contribution margin for the quarter. Growth at this margin profile is not free growth; it is a decision to spend money to acquire revenue, and the executive team needs to decide deliberately whether that trade continues at the current rate into the holiday season.

The distribution center relocation to Georgetown, approved by this same leadership team fourteen months ago as a long-term cost and capacity fix, delivered a short-term operational shock exactly as risk-registered but somewhat worse in duration than modeled. Replenishment broke for approximately six weeks across May and June, and company-wide in-stock fell to 87 percent against our 96 percent operating standard. That gap shows up directly in the regional comp results above — Ohio Valley and Appalachian, the two regions with the weakest comps, were also the two regions most exposed to the SKUs most affected by the transition, principally case goods and upholstery replenishment lines that route exclusively through the new facility rather than through vendor direct-ship.

Layered on top of the DC disruption, the $9.4 million spring outdoor furniture buy — placed in the fall of 2025 against a forecast that assumed a normal spring selling season — sold through at only 54 percent by the end of the quarter, and the merchant team has taken $2.8 million of markdown to clear the balance ahead of the fall reset. This was a buying and forecasting miss compounded by weather and by the in-stock problems described above; outdoor categories depend on a tight in-season replenishment cadence that the DC transition made impossible to execute in May and June, the two most important weeks of the outdoor selling season in this footprint.

The net effect on the P&L is a consolidated gross margin that came in below both plan and prior year, driven by three factors: the e-commerce contribution loss, the outdoor markdown, and incremental promotional activity in Ohio Valley and Appalachian used to defend traffic. Inventory is elevated versus the prior year on a dollar basis, concentrated in outdoor and in categories where the DC transition forced us to hold safety stock at both the old and new facilities simultaneously for several weeks.

None of this is fatal, and several of the underlying trends — the Northern remodel results in particular — are genuinely encouraging. But the back half of the year requires four things in sequence: stabilize the Georgetown facility and rebuild in-stock to standard before the September home-refresh selling season, resolve the Appalachian lease question before the September 30 exercise deadline referenced by our real estate counsel, address the Ohio Valley management vacancy problem directly rather than letting it resolve through attrition, and make an explicit, board-level decision about the e-commerce growth-versus-margin trade before Q4 marketing spend is committed. Each is discussed in detail below.

## 3. Regional Performance

### 3.1 Bluegrass Region — 12 Stores

| Metric | Q2 FY2026 | Prior Year | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comp sales | +2.1% | +3.0% | Decelerating but positive |
| Gross margin | 34.2% | 34.9% | Best-performing region on margin |
| Stores impacted by anchor closure | 2 | — | Anthem Pointe center |
| Comp, anchor-affected stores | (9.0)% | +1.5% | See discussion |
| Comp, remaining 10 stores | +4.4% | +3.3% | Underlying trend healthy |

The Bluegrass region remains the most stable performer in the chain on both traffic and margin, and the headline 2.1 percent comp materially understates the health of the underlying business once the two stores adjacent to the closed department store anchor at Anthem Pointe are set aside. Those two stores are down 9 percent for the quarter, a continuation of the pattern that began when the anchor tenant announced its closure in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2025 and vacated the space in February. The remaining ten stores in the region comped up 4.4 percent, which is the strongest sub-regional result in the company and consistent with the Lexington-area housing turnover data the merchant team has been tracking.

The Anthem Pointe situation is a center-level problem rather than a Foxglove-specific problem, and it will not resolve on its own timeline. Curtis Yamamoto's team has begun conversations with the center's ownership group about anchor backfill, but leasing timelines for a box that size typically run 12 to 18 months from vacancy to reopening, which means these two stores should be modeled at depressed traffic through at least the first half of fiscal 2027. The region has offset most of the drag through cost discipline — Bluegrass carries the lowest SG&A-to-sales ratio in the chain — and margin at 34.2 percent, while down 70 basis points year over year, remains well above the other three regions. No structural action is recommended here beyond continued monitoring of the anchor backfill process and a modest marketing reallocation toward the two affected stores to blunt the traffic decline while the center works through its leasing process.

### 3.2 Ohio Valley Region — 10 Stores

| Metric | Q2 FY2026 | Prior Year | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comp sales | (3.4)% | +1.1% | Weakest large-store-count region |
| Gross margin | 33.1% | 34.4% | Promotional intensity up |
| Store managers replaced (of 10) | 4 | 1 | 40% turnover in one quarter |
| Average store manager tenure, region | 14 months | 31 months | |

Ohio Valley is the region that should concern the executive team most in the near term, not because the comp decline is the steepest in the chain — Appalachian's is worse — but because the driver is largely internal and correctable, and it has not yet been corrected. Four of the region's ten store managers turned over during the quarter, three of them departures rather than promotions, and the region's average store manager tenure has fallen to 14 months from 31 months a year ago. Store-level execution metrics that correlate closely with manager tenure and stability — labor scheduling accuracy, planogram compliance, associate turnover — all moved in the wrong direction in the affected stores during the quarter, and the four stores with new managers comped down 6.1 percent versus down 1.8 percent in the six stores with stable leadership.

The region's gross margin also softened 130 basis points to 33.1 percent, driven by discretionary promotional markdown that district leadership authorized to defend traffic in the stores going through management transition. This is a reasonable short-term tactical response to a leadership vacuum, but it is not a substitute for filling the vacuum. Curtis Yamamoto's team has attributed the elevated turnover partly to a compensation structure that has not been refreshed since the 2023 acquisition and partly to two specific district manager departures that left store managers without consistent coaching support for several months. HR has begun exit interviews with the three departed managers; results were not final as of this writing but early themes point to compensation and to insufficient support during the DC transition specifically, since Ohio Valley stores carry a higher mix of the case-goods and upholstery categories most affected by the Georgetown replenishment gap described in Section 6.

We recommend the region receive priority attention in the store leadership stabilization plan discussed in Section 9, including an accelerated district manager backfill and a compensation benchmarking exercise for store manager roles chainwide, given that Ohio Valley's turnover may be a leading indicator rather than an isolated event.

### 3.3 Northern Region — 9 Stores

| Metric | Q2 FY2026 | Prior Year | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comp sales | +5.6% | +0.8% | Strongest comp in the company |
| Gross margin | 35.8% | 34.1% | Remodel halo effect |
| Remodel investment (3 stores) | $6.8M | — | Completed February 2026 |
| Comp, remodeled stores | +11.2% | — | |
| Comp, non-remodeled stores (6) | +2.7% | +1.0% | Still above prior year |

The Northern region is the clearest good-news story in the quarter and the strongest evidence yet that the capital allocation strategy approved by the sponsor in fiscal 2025 is working as underwritten. The $6.8 million invested across three Indiana locations — new flooring, lighting, and merchandise fixtures, plus a reconfigured showroom flow in two of the three stores — was completed in February, and the remodeled stores have comped up 11.2 percent since reopening, roughly double the return the original capital request modeled. The other six stores in the region, which received no capital investment, still comped up 2.7 percent, suggesting the region is also benefiting from a general improvement in the Indiana housing and consumer environment that is independent of the remodel program, though the remodeled stores are clearly outperforming that baseline by a wide margin.

Gross margin in the region expanded 170 basis points to 35.8 percent, the best margin result in the chain this quarter, driven by a richer mix of full-price sales in the remodeled locations and less reliance on promotional pricing to drive traffic. Lena Ostrowski's team has noted that the remodeled stores are also seeing a favorable shift toward higher-margin upholstery and case-goods categories, consistent with the merchandising thesis behind the remodel — that a more contemporary showroom environment supports higher-consideration, higher-margin purchases rather than just moving more units of lower-margin accent and décor product.

Given this result, we recommend the executive team treat the Northern remodel as a proof of concept for the two additional remodel candidates currently under evaluation for fiscal 2027 capital planning, and that the finance team formalize a post-investment return template based on the actual Northern results to sharpen future capital requests to the sponsor.

### 3.4 Appalachian Region — 7 Stores

| Metric | Q2 FY2026 | Prior Year | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comp sales | (6.2)% | (1.3)% | Weakest region in the chain |
| Gross margin | 31.9% | 33.2% | Promotional pressure |
| Charleston lease expiration | March 2027 | — | 31% renewal increase proposed |
| Charleston store, % of regional revenue | ~19% | — | Largest store in region |

Appalachian is both the weakest-performing region operationally and the region carrying the single largest near-term real estate decision on the company's calendar. The 6.2 percent comp decline is a continuation and acceleration of a trend that has been negative for five consecutive quarters, reflecting a combination of population and household-formation headwinds specific to the West Virginia and eastern Kentucky footprint, a competitive entry by a regional discount furniture chain in two of the seven markets over the past year, and the DC transition effects described in Section 6, which hit this region's replenishment-dependent categories especially hard given the region's above-average reliance on in-stock inventory rather than special order.

The more urgent item is the Charleston store lease, which expires in March 2027 and for which the landlord has proposed a 31 percent rent increase on renewal. Charleston is the largest single store in the region by revenue, representing approximately 19 percent of regional sales, and its four-wall contribution has been declining for three consecutive years even before the proposed rent increase is applied. Real estate and finance have modeled three scenarios: renew at the proposed rate, which would move the store from a modestly profitable four-wall contributor to roughly breakeven; attempt to renegotiate to a smaller increase, which the landlord's broker has signaled some flexibility on given the store's anchor position in the center but has not quantified; or decline renewal and consolidate Charleston volume into the region's next-nearest store, approximately 34 miles away, which the real estate team estimates would retain 40 to 55 percent of Charleston's current sales.

This decision needs to move through the real estate committee well before the September 30 window our outside counsel has flagged as the practical deadline for exercising renewal negotiation leverage, and it is addressed as a specific back-half action item in Section 9. Given the broader regional softness, we do not recommend renewal at the proposed 31 percent increase without a renegotiated rate materially below that figure, and the finance team is prepared to model the consolidation scenario in more detail if the executive team wants to pursue it as the primary path.

## 4. E-commerce Channel

| Metric | Q2 FY2026 | Prior Year | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Net sales (TTM) | $44.0M | $37.3M | +18.0% |
| Contribution margin | (2.1)% | +3.4% | See discussion |
| Return rate | 21% | 16% | Furniture/décor mix shift |
| Carrier surcharge impact | ~$1.6M (quarter) | — | New residential-delivery fee |
| Share of total company revenue | ~10.7% | ~9.4% | |

E-commerce delivered the fastest growth rate in the company for the fourth consecutive quarter, and on a pure top-line basis this is exactly the diversification the sponsor's original investment thesis called for — a channel that is not dependent on the four-state store footprint and that can grow independent of regional demographic headwinds like those affecting Appalachian. The problem is not the growth; it is the unit economics underneath it.

Two structural cost items moved against the channel this quarter. First, our primary parcel carrier implemented a residential large-item delivery surcharge in late calendar 2025 that was not fully reflected in our pricing or freight-to-customer charges; the cumulative quarterly impact is approximately $1.6 million, and it falls disproportionately on the furniture and larger décor categories that have driven most of the channel's unit growth. Second, the return rate on furniture and décor purchased online has climbed to 21 percent from 16 percent a year ago, driven by a mix shift toward upholstery and case goods purchased sight-unseen — categories where customers historically relied on in-store fit and fabric evaluation before buying. Reverse logistics on large furniture items is materially more expensive per unit than on the small-parcel décor and accessory items that made up a larger share of the channel's mix two years ago, and the combination of the surcharge and the return rate pushed contribution margin to negative 2.1 percent for the quarter, down from positive 3.4 percent a year ago.

Lena Ostrowski's merchandising team and the e-commerce operations team have three initiatives in progress to address this, none of which will be fully effective before Q3: a revised return policy for large upholstery items that shifts from free returns to a modest restocking fee outside of defect claims, expanded use of in-home visualization tools intended to reduce the "wrong fit" category of returns, and a renegotiation of the carrier surcharge pass-through into customer-facing delivery pricing, which is currently absorbing the increase rather than passing any of it through. None of these is a complete fix on its own, and the team's own estimate is that the combination gets contribution margin back to roughly breakeven by Q4, not back to the prior year's positive 3.4 percent.

The strategic question for the executive team, and likely for the sponsor board, is whether the current growth rate is worth continuing to fund at a negative contribution margin while these fixes take effect, or whether marketing spend behind the channel should be throttled back until unit economics improve. We do not have a house view to offer yet — the answer depends partly on how the executive team weighs customer acquisition and long-term channel value against near-term margin — but we do not think the current trajectory (18 percent growth, negative contribution margin, no clear date certain for a return to profitability) should continue unexamined into the holiday planning cycle, when e-commerce marketing spend typically increases significantly.

## 5. Distribution Center Transition

| Milestone | Date | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Georgetown facility lease commencement | January 2026 | Complete |
| Legacy facility wind-down begins | April 2026 | Complete |
| Peak replenishment disruption | May 5 – June 16, 2026 | Complete (6 weeks) |
| Company-wide in-stock, trough | 87% | Week of May 19 |
| Standard in-stock target | 96% | Ongoing |
| In-stock recovery to 93%+ | Late June 2026 | Achieved |
| Full recovery to 96% standard | Targeted August 2026 | In progress |

The Georgetown distribution center consolidation was approved by this leadership team and the sponsor board in the fiscal 2025 capital plan as a multi-year cost and capacity improvement, replacing two smaller, older facilities with a single, larger, more automated facility expected to reduce per-unit fulfillment cost by approximately 14 percent once fully ramped. That long-term thesis is intact and nothing in this quarter's results changes our view of the facility's economics over a three-to-five-year horizon. The transition itself, however, took longer and hurt more than the original project plan assumed.

The core issue was a six-week window, roughly May 5 through June 16, during which inbound receiving at the new facility and outbound shipping capability were not simultaneously at full capacity — the new facility's automated putaway system required more calibration time than the vendor's implementation timeline projected, and the legacy facility's outbound capability was intentionally wound down on the original schedule regardless. The result was a period where neither facility could fully support store and e-commerce replenishment at the same time, and company-wide in-stock fell to a trough of 87 percent against our 96 percent operating standard, concentrated in case goods, upholstery, and — critically for the reasons discussed in Section 6 — outdoor furniture, all categories that route through the DC rather than direct-ship from vendor.

The operational team, led jointly by supply chain and store operations, has since restored in-stock to the low 93 percent range as of late June and expects a full return to the 96 percent standard by the end of August, which would put the company back to normal replenishment cadence roughly six weeks ahead of the September home-refresh selling season that typically represents the second-largest volume period of the fiscal year after the holiday quarter. This timeline is a genuine priority for the back half and is discussed further in Section 9.

Two lessons from this transition are worth capturing for future capital projects of this kind. First, the project plan should have built in a longer buffer between legacy facility outbound wind-down and new facility outbound ramp, rather than running them on parallel rather than sequential critical paths; a two-to-three-week sequential buffer would likely have cut the disruption window materially, at the cost of some additional short-term dual-facility carrying cost. Second, the regions and categories most exposed to DC-routed replenishment — Ohio Valley and Appalachian, and outdoor and upholstery categories specifically — should have received advance safety stock builds ahead of the transition rather than running through it at standard inventory levels; this was flagged as an option in the original project plan but was not funded, a decision the finance team is revisiting for any future facility transition.

## 6. Outdoor Furniture Markdown

| Metric | Amount |
|---|---|
| Original buy, at retail | $9.4M |
| Sell-through as of quarter-end | 54% |
| Retail value sold | ~$5.1M |
| Retail value remaining, pre-markdown | ~$4.3M |
| Markdown taken to clear | $2.8M |
| Approximate markdown depth on remaining units | ~65% |

The spring outdoor furniture assortment was bought in the fall of 2025 at $9.4 million at retail, a program the merchant team had increased approximately 22 percent over the prior year's spring outdoor buy based on strong prior-year sell-through and favorable early consumer confidence readings for the region. Two things happened between that buying decision and the spring selling season that were not anticipated at the time of purchase. First, an unusually wet and late spring across the Ohio Valley and Kentucky footprint compressed the outdoor selling window by an estimated three to four weeks versus a typical year, based on comparable weather-normalized sales patterns the merchant team has tracked in prior seasons. Second, and more damaging, the DC transition disruption described in Section 5 landed directly on top of the compressed selling window: outdoor furniture depends on tight in-season replenishment, since customers who find a display set out of stock in their preferred configuration or fabric will generally not wait for a restock and will either walk or substitute to a competitor, and the six-week replenishment gap fell almost exactly on the peak of the (already shortened) outdoor selling season in May and June.

The combined effect was a sell-through of only 54 percent by the end of the quarter, against a plan assumption of 78 percent by this point in the season, leaving roughly $4.3 million of retail value that the merchant team has now marked down $2.8 million, an average markdown depth of roughly 65 percent on the remaining units, in order to clear the balance ahead of the fall reset and avoid carrying outdoor inventory into a second season, which both ties up floor space needed for fall categories and typically clears at even worse economics a year later given fashion and finish risk in outdoor fabric lines.

This was, in the team's own assessment, a compounding failure rather than a single-cause miss: the buy itself was defensible against the information available at purchase time, but it was not resized or hedged as the DC transition timeline firmed up over the winter, and it should have been. Lena Ostrowski's team has committed to two changes for next year's outdoor buy: building an explicit contingency reduction into any seasonal buy that falls during a known operational transition window, and adding a mid-season reforecast checkpoint — roughly 60 days before the season's peak — at which the buy can be trimmed via vendor cancellation options that the original outdoor purchase order did include but that were not exercised in time this cycle. We recommend the executive team treat this as a process fix rather than a personnel issue; the underlying buying discipline is sound, and the miss is specific to failing to connect the merchandising calendar to the supply chain project calendar during planning.

## 7. Margin and Inventory Position

Consolidated gross margin of 30.0 percent for the quarter, down 160 basis points from 31.6 percent a year ago, breaks down into three discrete, quantifiable drags plus a smaller residual freight and occupancy deleverage effect.

| Driver | Estimated Margin Impact | Section Reference |
|---|---|---|
| E-commerce negative contribution margin | ~(40) bps | Section 4 |
| Outdoor furniture markdown | ~(60) bps | Section 6 |
| Ohio Valley / Appalachian promotional intensity | ~(50) bps | Section 3.2, 3.4 |
| Freight and occupancy deleverage (residual) | ~(10) bps | — |
| **Total consolidated margin decline** | **~(160) bps** | |

Each of these is addressable on a different timeline. The e-commerce drag has a partial fix in motion now, discussed in Section 4, and should improve through Q3 and Q4 as the return policy and pricing changes take effect, though we do not expect a full return to prior-year contribution margin within fiscal 2026. The outdoor markdown is a one-time clearance event that will not repeat once the current inventory is sold through, expected substantially complete by the end of Q3. The promotional intensity in Ohio Valley and Appalachian is the most concerning of the three because it is a response to underlying traffic and leadership problems rather than a discrete, closeable event, and it will persist as long as those underlying problems persist — which is the core argument for prioritizing the Ohio Valley leadership stabilization and Appalachian lease decision discussed in Section 9.

Ending inventory at cost was $86.4 million, up 9.2 percent from $79.1 million a year ago, against a revenue base that was essentially flat. On a per-store basis this is the highest inventory position in at least three years, and it is not evenly distributed: outdoor furniture accounts for approximately $3.9 million of the year-over-year increase even after the current-quarter markdown, and case goods and upholstery categories affected by the DC transition's in-stock shortfall account for a further estimated $2.5 million of increase, reflecting both the safety stock the supply chain team built once the disruption became apparent and some double-ordering by store teams attempting to manage around out-of-stocks locally during the worst weeks of the transition. The remainder of the increase is spread across normal seasonal build for the fall reset, which is proceeding on the normal calendar and is not itself a concern.

The inventory position bears monitoring closely through Q3. If in-stock recovers to the 96 percent standard on the timeline described in Section 5, and if the outdoor clearance completes as planned, the majority of the current inventory overhang should resolve naturally without further markdown action. If the DC recovery timeline slips, or if fall sell-through underperforms plan, the executive team should expect a recommendation for additional promotional clearance activity in Q4 to prevent the overhang from compounding into the holiday season, when floor space and cash flow discipline both matter most.

## 8. Back Half Requirements

The following four priorities represent, in the finance team's assessment, the minimum set of actions required to convert this quarter's operational disruptions into a stable platform for the holiday selling season and to close out fiscal 2026 in line with the plan presented to the sponsor board in January.

| Priority | Owner | Target Completion | Success Metric |
|---|---|---|---|
| Restore company-wide in-stock to 96% standard | Supply Chain / Store Ops | End of August 2026 | In-stock rate at or above 96% for two consecutive weeks |
| Resolve Charleston (Appalachian) lease decision | Real Estate / Finance | Before September 30, 2026 | Signed renewal at acceptable rate, or approved consolidation plan |
| Stabilize Ohio Valley store leadership | HR / Curtis Yamamoto | Ongoing through Q3 | Manager tenure trending up; comp gap to stable stores narrows |
| Decide e-commerce growth-vs-margin posture for holiday | CEO / Lena Ostrowski | Before Q4 marketing commitment (October) | Explicit board-reviewed decision on Q4 spend level |

**Distribution center stabilization.** Supply chain leadership has committed to full recovery of the 96 percent in-stock standard by the end of August, roughly six weeks ahead of the September home-refresh season. This is the single highest-priority operational item on the back-half calendar, because it is the common root cause underneath both the outdoor markdown and a meaningful share of the regional comp weakness in Ohio Valley and Appalachian. We recommend the executive team request a biweekly written update from supply chain through the end of August rather than waiting for the normal monthly operating review cadence, given how directly this recovery affects the September selling season.

**Charleston lease decision.** Real estate and finance need executive direction on which of the three scenarios described in Section 3.4 to pursue — renewal at a renegotiated rate, renewal at the proposed 31 percent increase, or consolidation into the nearby store — well before the September 30 practical deadline for renewal leverage. We recommend the executive team review the full three-scenario financial model, which finance can turn around within two weeks of a request, before the next scheduled operating review.

**Ohio Valley leadership stabilization.** The region's store manager turnover is the most directly correctable driver of underperformance in this quarter's results, and it is also the item most likely to compound if left unaddressed, given the correlation observed this quarter between manager tenure and store-level comp performance. We recommend Curtis Yamamoto's team present a specific stabilization plan — covering district manager backfill, the compensation benchmarking review, and any near-term support resources for the four newly staffed stores — at the next executive operating review, rather than allowing the situation to resolve through normal attrition and hiring cadence.

**E-commerce spend decision.** The executive team, and likely the sponsor board through Devon Marsh, should make an explicit decision on e-commerce marketing spend levels for the holiday quarter before that spend is committed in October, given the current negative contribution margin. This is as much a strategic question as an operational one, and we recommend it be placed on the agenda for the next full board meeting rather than resolved solely at the operating level, given the magnitude of the channel's contribution to consolidated revenue growth and the corresponding magnitude of its current margin drag.

Beyond these four specific items, we would flag two lower-urgency but still noteworthy items for the executive team's awareness: the Bluegrass Anthem Pointe anchor backfill process, which requires no near-term decision from Foxglove but should be monitored given the ongoing traffic drag on two stores, and the Northern region remodel results, which we recommend be formally incorporated into the fiscal 2027 capital planning process given how meaningfully they outperformed the original underwriting.

## 9. Closing

Taken together, the second quarter tells a story of a chain with real underlying strength — visible clearly in the Northern region's remodel results and in Bluegrass's underlying comp once the anchor-closure drag is set aside — that took a self-inflicted operational hit from a distribution center transition that ran longer than planned, and that is now carrying the combined cost of that hit into a set of pre-existing regional and channel challenges that predate the DC project entirely. None of the four back-half priorities above is a surprise to this leadership team, and none requires new capital or a change in strategy; they require sequencing, ownership, and a set of explicit decisions — particularly on Charleston and on e-commerce spend — that should not wait for the normal quarterly review cycle.

We look forward to discussing this report and the recommended back-half priorities in detail at the upcoming executive review, and to preparing whatever additional detail Devon Marsh and the sponsor board require ahead of the August 18 board session.
