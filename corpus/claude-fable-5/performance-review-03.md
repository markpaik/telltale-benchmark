# Mesilla Valley Regional Medical Center
## Annual Performance Review — Progressive Care Unit

---

**Employee:** Rosa Elena Trujillo, BSN, RN, CCRN
**Title:** Charge Nurse, Progressive Care Unit
**Review Period:** July 1, 2025 – June 30, 2026
**Reviewer:** Adaeze Obi, MSN, RN, Nurse Manager, Progressive Care Unit
**Date of Review:** August 9, 2026

---

### Overview and Context

Rosa Elena Trujillo completed her fourteenth year of service to Mesilla Valley Regional Medical Center this review period, her sixth in the charge nurse role on the progressive care unit. This review covers a year in which the unit operated with an 18 percent registered-nurse vacancy rate, with eleven of 62 budgeted positions covered by travel nurses, and in which the unit came under monthly audit following the October accreditation finding on medication reconciliation. I note these conditions at the outset because Rosa Elena's performance cannot be fairly assessed without them. The charge nurse role on a stable unit is a demanding job. The charge nurse role on this unit, this year, was something else entirely, and she carried it.

### Assessment Against Annual Goals

**Goal 1: Maintain safe patient flow and staffing coverage on assigned charge shifts, including surge periods.**

Rosa Elena met and exceeded this goal, and the clearest evidence is the Fourth of July weekend. When the emergency department boarded 40 patients over that holiday weekend, Rosa Elena ran the unit's response. She reorganized assignments across three shifts, negotiated directly with the house supervisor for float support, personally took a patient assignment on the evening of July 5 when a traveler called out, and created a boarding-patient tracking sheet that the ED charge nurses adopted and continued using through the fall. No patient safety events were reported on the unit during that weekend despite census pressure that exceeded anything we saw in the prior two years. Two hospitalists separately emailed me afterward to name her coordination as the reason the weekend held together. I agree with them.

Beyond the July surge, Rosa Elena's routine charge shifts show the same pattern in smaller form. She balances assignments with attention to acuity and to the experience level of the nurse, which matters on a unit where at any given time a fifth of the staff are travelers unfamiliar with our workflows and another portion are new graduates. Her shift handoffs are thorough. When she is in charge, I get fewer calls.

**Goal 2: Precept and support the transition of new graduate nurses through the residency program.**

Exceeded. Rosa Elena precepted five new graduates this year, more than any other preceptor on the unit and, to my knowledge, more than any preceptor in the hospital. All five completed the residency. One of them, Sandeep Chaudhary, took his own assignment at week fourteen against a sixteen-week target, and while the credit for that belongs to him as well, his early progression reflects the structure and honesty of her precepting. Her preceptee feedback forms are consistently specific — she tells new nurses what they did, what she would have done, and why, and she documents it. When one of her preceptees needed remediation on drip titration, she flagged it early, framed it without drama, and stayed involved through the second remediation rather than handing the problem off. That is what precepting is supposed to look like.

I want to be direct about the cost of this goal. Five preceptees in one year, on top of charge duties, on a short-staffed unit, is a heavy load, and Rosa Elena absorbed it because the educator position she applied for was cut from the budget in January. She has effectively performed portions of the unit educator role without the title, the pay, or the protected time. I will return to this under compensation and goals.

**Goal 3: Support unit quality initiatives, including medication reconciliation compliance following the October accreditation finding.**

Met. After the October finding placed the unit on monthly medication reconciliation audits, Rosa Elena built the audit review into her charge shift routine, spot-checking reconciliation documentation at handoff and coaching nurses in real time rather than letting errors surface a month later in audit results. On shifts where she was in charge, the unit's monthly audit compliance averaged 96 percent from November through June, against a unit-wide average of 91 percent. She did not create the unit's corrective response — that was a shared effort — but she operationalized it at the bedside more consistently than anyone else.

**Goal 4: Advance professional development, including clinical ladder progression.**

Partially met, through no fault of her own. Rosa Elena maintained her CCRN certification, completed her required contact hours, and submitted a complete clinical ladder level four portfolio, which I reviewed and endorsed. The portfolio demonstrates the exemplars the ladder requires: unit leadership, precepting, quality improvement participation, and certification. Her application is pending before the clinical ladder council; the delay is on the council's calendar, not on her preparation. Her application for the unit educator position could not proceed because the position was eliminated in the January budget action. She applied for both things this organization asked its strongest nurses to pursue, and in both cases the institution, not the nurse, is the reason the outcome is unresolved.

### Patient and Colleague Feedback

Rosa Elena's patient experience scores lead the unit at the 84th percentile, against a unit average that sits meaningfully below that. Written patient comments this year name her by role ("the charge nurse") and by name, and the recurring themes are that she explains what is happening and that she follows up when she says she will. Colleague feedback gathered for this review was uniformly strong, with one theme I want to name honestly: two staff nurses independently observed that Rosa Elena takes on too much herself before delegating, particularly during surges. I read this as accurate and as partly a symptom of the staffing environment — delegation requires people to delegate to — but it is also a development edge, because a charge nurse who absorbs everything is a single point of failure, and I do not want this unit to be one Rosa Elena Trujillo away from trouble.

### Self-Assessment

Rosa Elena submitted a written self-assessment. Her words appear below in italics and are hers, not mine.

> *"I am proud of how the unit handled July. I am proud of my five preceptees. What I struggle with is that I applied for the educator role because I believe the unit needs one, and the answer I got back was that the position no longer exists but the work still does. I have kept doing the work. I want to be honest that I have thought about whether this is the right place to keep doing it. I am still here because of this team and these patients, but I need to see a path."*

I take this seriously. Her assessment of the situation is factually correct, and her frankness is a courtesy to me and to the organization. Retention of this nurse should be treated as a unit-level risk item, and I am treating it as one.

### Rating

**Overall Rating: Exceeds Expectations (Outstanding)**

Rosa Elena exceeds expectations in clinical practice, leadership, precepting, and quality performance. This is the highest rating available on the form and it is earned without reservation.

### Contract and Compensation

Under the current collective bargaining agreement, a satisfactory rating or above qualifies the employee for the annual step increase; Rosa Elena will receive her step. Ratings above satisfactory draw from the 2.4 percent merit pool. Given the size of the pool and the number of eligible staff, I am allocating Rosa Elena the maximum individual merit award available under my allocation, and I state plainly for the record that the amount does not reflect the value of her contribution — it reflects the size of the pool. I have separately submitted to the CNO a written recommendation that the unit educator position be restored in the FY27 budget and that Rosa Elena be considered for it, and a request that her clinical ladder level four application be expedited, as ladder advancement carries a differential outside the merit pool and is the one meaningful compensation lever currently available.

### Goals for the Coming Year

1. **Complete clinical ladder level four advancement** by end of Q2 FY27, with manager support in resolving the council backlog.
2. **Formalize the precepting infrastructure** she has built informally: document the preceptor tools, tracking sheets, and remediation escalation pathway so the work survives beyond one person, targeting a written unit precepting guide by January 2027.
3. **Develop delegation depth in charge role** by identifying and mentoring two relief charge nurses to independent competence by end of FY27, reducing single-point-of-failure risk during surges.
4. **Sustain medication reconciliation performance** at or above 95 percent on her charge shifts and co-lead the unit's effort to close the gap between her shifts and the unit average.
5. **Retention and career pathway conversation** with manager and CNO by October 2026 regarding the educator role and alternatives, with a documented outcome. This goal is as much mine as hers.

# Mesilla Valley Regional Medical Center
## Annual Performance Review — Progressive Care Unit

---

**Employee:** Denise Kowalczyk, RN
**Title:** Staff Nurse II, Progressive Care Unit
**Review Period:** July 1, 2025 – June 30, 2026
**Reviewer:** Adaeze Obi, MSN, RN, Nurse Manager, Progressive Care Unit
**Date of Review:** August 9, 2026

---

### Overview and Context

Denise Kowalczyk has served this hospital for 22 years, longer than any other nurse currently on the progressive care unit and longer than I have been here. This review period was, by any measure, the hardest of her career, and I want this document to be honest about the difficulties, honest about the recovery, and honest about the work that remains. It will do Denise no favors to soften any of the three.

The period under review includes two medication events in its first half, one of them serious; a twelve-week leave of absence from which Denise returned in March; and a corrective action plan completed in June. It also includes a documented and sustained turnaround in her documentation practice and a return to full clinical duty on a unit under exceptional strain. Both halves of that sentence are true, and this review holds them together.

### Assessment Against Annual Goals

**Goal 1: Deliver safe, competent patient care consistent with unit standards and scope of practice.**

Not met in the first half of the review period; met in the second half following corrective action, with continued monitoring.

Denise was involved in two medication events during the review period. The first, in September, was a missed-dose event caught on audit with no patient harm. The second, in November, involved a heparin drip administered at an incorrect rate; the error triggered a rapid response, and while the patient stabilized without lasting harm, the event was serious, was reportable, and occurred during the same window in which the October accreditation finding placed the entire unit's medication practices under monthly audit. I will not minimize this. A heparin error on a progressive care unit is among the events this profession works hardest to prevent, and it happened on Denise's watch.

The root cause analysis identified contributing factors that included interruptions during drip verification and a workaround on independent double-checks that, the review found, was not unique to Denise but was present in her practice. A corrective action plan was initiated in December. Denise then took an approved twelve-week leave beginning shortly after, and the plan resumed upon her return in March. The details of the leave are personal and protected, and they are not part of this evaluation; the timeline is noted only because it explains the plan's schedule.

What matters most for this review is what happened after March. Denise completed every element of the corrective action plan — medication safety retraining, supervised drip management, return demonstration on independent double-check procedure, and a documentation improvement component — and completed it on schedule, closing in June. She did not contest the plan, did not litigate the findings, and by the account of the two nurses who supervised her return-to-practice shifts, she engaged with the retraining seriously and without defensiveness. Since her return in March, Denise has had no medication events across four monthly audit cycles, and her double-check compliance on audited drips has been 100 percent.

**Goal 2: Achieve and sustain documentation compliance at or above the unit standard of 90 percent.**

Met, following significant improvement. Denise's documentation compliance stood at 71 percent when the corrective action plan was initiated. It now stands at 94 percent, above the unit standard and above the unit average. A 23-point improvement in documentation compliance, achieved during a period of personal leave and formal corrective action, is not a small thing, and I want it recognized in writing. It reflects deliberate change in practice, not luck.

**Goal 3: Contribute to unit operations as a senior member of the nursing staff.**

Partially met, and this is where I must raise the most difficult remaining issue.

Two colleagues, independently and without prompting, reported during this review cycle that Denise avoids the sickest patient assignments — that she requests assignment changes when her group includes the highest-acuity patients, and that on some shifts the charge nurse accommodates this to keep the shift moving. I reviewed assignment records for April through June. The pattern is not universal, but it is visible: Denise's assignments over that period skewed measurably lower in acuity than the unit's median for a nurse of her seniority, and there are documented instances of assignment swaps at her request.

I raised this with Denise directly before writing this review. Her response appears in the self-assessment section below, in her own words. I will say here what I said to her: I understand that a nurse returning from a serious medication event and from personal leave may reasonably want a graduated re-entry, and for the supervised period of the corrective action plan, lighter assignments were appropriate and were part of the plan. The plan closed in June. A 22-year nurse on a progressive care unit with an 18 percent vacancy rate cannot indefinitely carry a lighter acuity load than a first-year nurse; it is not sustainable for the unit, it is not fair to her colleagues, and — I believe this — it is not good for Denise, because avoidance left unaddressed hardens into incapacity. This is the central development issue for the coming year, and it is written into her goals with a timeline and with support attached.

I also want the record to reflect what Denise's colleagues did *not* say. No one questioned her kindness with patients or families. No one questioned her willingness to help with tasks, admissions, or new-nurse questions. The concern was specific and narrow, and the rest of her collegial contribution remains intact.

### Patient and Colleague Feedback

Denise's patient experience feedback remains solid, with several written comments this year noting her warmth and her attention to older patients and their families — a longstanding strength. Colleague feedback is as described above: broadly positive on teamwork and generosity, with the specific concern regarding high-acuity assignments documented and discussed.

### Self-Assessment

Denise submitted a written self-assessment. Her words appear below in italics and are distinguishable from my own assessment.

> *"This was the worst year of my career and I know it. I think about the heparin patient more than I will ever tell anyone here. I did everything the plan asked and I would have done more. On the assignments — my manager asked me about it straight, so I will answer straight. Yes, I have been asking off the heaviest patients. I lost my confidence in November and I have not gotten all of it back. I am not proud of that and I don't want a career that ends this way, hiding from the work. I need help getting back, not a pass, and not a pretend version where everyone acts like it isn't happening."*

I quote this at length because it changed how I approached this review. Denise named the problem herself before I finished naming it, and asked for structure rather than accommodation. That request is granted, and it shapes her goals below.

### Rating

**Overall Rating: Satisfactory (Meets Expectations)**

This rating reflects the full year. The medication events and the acuity-avoidance pattern preclude a rating above satisfactory. The completed corrective action plan, four clean audit cycles, the documentation turnaround from 71 to 94 percent, and Denise's honest engagement with the remaining issue preclude a rating below it. A satisfactory rating is not a courtesy here; it is my judgment that Denise, as she stands today, meets the standard for a staff nurse on this unit, with a defined development requirement for the year ahead.

### Contract and Compensation

Under the collective bargaining agreement, a satisfactory rating qualifies Denise for her annual step increase, and she will receive it. The 2.4 percent merit pool is contractually reserved for ratings above satisfactory; Denise is therefore not eligible for a merit award this cycle. I reviewed this with her directly. The path back to merit eligibility is explicit in her goals: a full year of safe practice at full acuity makes an above-satisfactory rating attainable next cycle, and I told her I expect to be writing that review.

### Goals for the Coming Year

1. **Graduated return to full-acuity assignments.** Beginning September 2026, Denise will take at least one high-acuity assignment per scheduled week, paired with a designated resource nurse for the first eight weeks; by January 2027, she will carry assignments at the unit median acuity for her seniority without accommodation. Progress reviewed monthly with the manager.
2. **Sustain medication safety performance:** zero preventable medication events and 100 percent double-check compliance across all monthly audit cycles.
3. **Sustain documentation compliance** at or above 92 percent for the full year.
4. **Confidence and competence support:** complete the hospital's peer support program intake by October 2026 (offered, and accepted by Denise in our pre-review conversation), and complete a drip-management skills refresher in the simulation lab by December 2026 — not as remediation, but as rehearsal.
5. **Re-engage seniority contribution:** by Q3 FY27, resume one senior-nurse function of her choosing — relief charge orientation, skills validation, or new-graduate mentoring — as a step toward restoring her standing as a senior clinical resource on the unit.

# Mesilla Valley Regional Medical Center
## Annual Performance Review — Progressive Care Unit

---

**Employee:** Sandeep Chaudhary, BSN, RN
**Title:** Staff Nurse I, Progressive Care Unit (Nurse Residency Program)
**Review Period:** January 5, 2026 – June 30, 2026 (initial partial-year review)
**Reviewer:** Adaeze Obi, MSN, RN, Nurse Manager, Progressive Care Unit
**Date of Review:** August 9, 2026

---

### Overview and Context

Sandeep Chaudhary joined the progressive care unit in January 2026 as a new graduate nurse in the hospital's residency program. This is his first performance review, covering roughly six months rather than a full year, and it should be read as what it is: an early portrait of a nurse still forming, on a unit that gave him an unusually hard place to form. He began three weeks before a February respiratory surge that stretched an already short-staffed unit, under the shadow of monthly medication audits, precepted by our strongest preceptor but surrounded by travelers who could offer him little continuity.

Against that backdrop, Sandeep's first six months contain genuinely exceptional early markers and two significant concerns. New graduate reviews sometimes flatten into either cheerleading or alarm. This one will try to do neither, because Sandeep has shown me he can hear direct feedback, and direct feedback is what a first-year nurse is owed.

### Assessment Against Residency Goals

**Goal 1: Complete residency milestones and progress to independent practice within the sixteen-week orientation target.**

Exceeded. Sandeep took his own patient assignment in week fourteen, two weeks ahead of the sixteen-week target. This progression was not a scheduling artifact; it was recommended by his preceptor, Rosa Elena Trujillo, validated by the residency coordinator, and based on demonstrated competency in assessment, prioritization, and documentation. For context, of the five new graduates who entered the residency on this unit in the past year, Sandeep was the only one to complete orientation ahead of target. His documentation compliance since taking his own assignment has averaged 93 percent, above the unit standard, which is notable for a first-year nurse writing under audit conditions.

His clinical strengths at this stage are pattern recognition and communication. On an April shift, he identified early signs of decompensation in a post-procedure patient — subtle vital sign drift and a change in mentation — escalated appropriately, and the patient was transferred to ICU before the situation became a rapid response. The intensivist noted the catch. For a nurse four months out of school, that is the right instinct arriving early.

**Goal 2: Demonstrate safe medication administration and titration competency.**

Partially met, with documented remediation. Sandeep required a second remediation on continuous drip titration after his first remediation, completed in orientation, did not fully resolve inconsistencies in his rate-change verification and documentation practice. To be specific about what this was and was not: there was no patient harm event, and the gaps were caught through the supervised practice structure and the monthly audit process working as designed. But on a unit that received an accreditation finding on medication practices in October and had a serious heparin event in November, drip titration is not an area where "mostly right" is acceptable, and I told Sandeep exactly that.

The second remediation was completed in late May with his preceptor and the simulation lab, and his subsequent supervised titrations and audit results have been clean. Two remediations on the same competency in a first year is a flag I will continue to watch, and it appears in his goals below with a defined validation schedule. It is also, I want to be fair, a flag on a competency that experienced nurses on this unit have also struggled with, and the fact that his gaps were surfaced, named, and worked twice — rather than papered over — speaks well of both Sandeep and the structure around him.

**Goal 3: Demonstrate reliability and professional accountability.**

Partially met. Sandeep called out for three shifts during the February surge, in weeks six through eight of his orientation. On a fully staffed unit, three call-outs in a month by a new employee would draw a conversation; on this unit, during that surge, each call-out meant a traveler reassignment or an unsafe ratio narrowly avoided, and his absences were felt by the team. I addressed this with him in March. He did not offer excuses; he acknowledged the impact, and his attendance from March through June has been perfect — no call-outs, no tardiness, and two occasions on which he picked up open shifts during coverage gaps, including one weekend shift in June that he took with four hours' notice.

I have formed a view on the February pattern that I shared with him and will record here: I believe those call-outs were, at least in part, a first-year nurse drowning quietly during a surge and lacking the vocabulary to say so. New graduates rarely announce that they are overwhelmed; they call out. I would rather Sandeep tell his charge nurse "I am at my limit" than disappear for a shift, and building that skill — asking for help before the breaking point — is written into his goals. The attendance standard itself is not negotiable, and his record since March suggests he understands that.

**Goal 4: Establish therapeutic relationships with patients and families.**

Exceeded. Sandeep was named, by name, in two unsolicited patient letters during a six-month period — a rate few nurses on this unit match over a full year. One letter, from the daughter of an 81-year-old patient, described him sitting with her father during a frightening night "explaining every alarm so the machines stopped being monsters." His patient interaction scores, to the extent they can be isolated for an individual on our survey instrument, are consistent with the letters. Colleagues describe him as eager, humble about what he does not know, and quick to ask questions — which, in a first-year nurse, is a safety behavior, not a weakness.

### Patient and Colleague Feedback

Summarized above: two named patient letters, strong family feedback, and colleague reports that are warm on attitude and teachability. His preceptor's final orientation evaluation rated him ready for independent practice ahead of schedule while explicitly flagging drip titration for continued validation — an assessment this review has borne out on both counts.

### Self-Assessment

Sandeep submitted a written self-assessment. His words appear below in italics and are his own.

> *"I am proud that I came off orientation early and proud of the letters, but I want to be honest about February. I was not as sick as I said I was. I was scared to come in. I had a patient almost code in week five and I did not sleep right for two weeks after. I should have told Rosa Elena or my manager instead of calling out, and I have not called out since and I will not handle it that way again. On the drips, I know two remediations is not a good look. I asked for extra sim lab time in July on my own and I will keep asking until nobody has to check behind me."*

I appreciate this honesty more than Sandeep may realize, and I told him so. A first-year nurse who can write that paragraph is a nurse who will be safe to work with for thirty years. His account of February is consistent with my own read of it, and his self-initiated July simulation sessions are confirmed by the lab schedule.

### Rating

**Overall Rating: Satisfactory (Meets Expectations)**

For a partial-year review of a residency nurse, this rating reflects: orientation completion ahead of target and exceptional patient feedback, weighed against a second remediation on a critical medication competency and an attendance lapse during the period when the unit most needed him. The trajectory since March points upward on every measure. Had the review period run a full year at his March-through-June standard, the rating would likely have been higher; the rating covers the period as it actually occurred.

### Contract and Compensation

Under the collective bargaining agreement, Sandeep's satisfactory rating qualifies him for his step increase upon completion of his first contractual year in January 2027, and it will be processed on that anniversary. The 2.4 percent merit pool is reserved for ratings above satisfactory, so no merit award applies this cycle; I have explained to Sandeep that residency-year reviews on this unit rarely rate above satisfactory and that the path to merit eligibility runs directly through the goals below. Retaining new graduates past the two-year mark is among this unit's most pressing needs, and I have flagged Sandeep to the residency coordinator as a retention priority.

### Goals for the Coming Year

1. **Drip titration validation:** complete quarterly competency validations on continuous drip management through FY27, with 100 percent audit compliance and zero titration events. Two consecutive clean quarters will close the remediation flag on his file.
2. **Attendance:** maintain the March-through-June standard — full attendance apart from legitimate illness or approved leave — through the full review year.
3. **Escalation before overwhelm:** practice and demonstrate proactive escalation, including at least one documented instance per quarter of appropriately requesting assignment support, resource help, or debriefing after a difficult event. Complete the hospital's resilience-in-residency debriefing series by December 2026.
4. **Broaden clinical exposure:** by end of FY27, achieve competency validation in unit specialty skills including post-procedure recovery and non-invasive ventilation management, positioning him for the full range of unit assignments.
5. **Begin giving back:** in the second half of FY27, serve as a peer resource for the incoming January 2027 residency cohort, sharing the first-year experience — including February — with the nurses who come after him.
