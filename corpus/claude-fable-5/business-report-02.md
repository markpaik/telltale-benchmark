# Tuscarora County, Pennsylvania

## Mid-Year Operations and Financial Report — Fiscal Year 2026

**Reporting Period:** January 1 – June 30, 2026
**Prepared by:** Elias Whitcomb, Budget Director
**Departmental submissions compiled by:** Danielle Ferraro, Chief Clerk
**Presented to:** Board of Commissioners, Bernadette Osei-Tutu, Chair
**Meeting Date:** August 6, 2026

## 1. Executive Summary

At the midpoint of fiscal 2026, Tuscarora County's $196 million general fund is under measurable strain from three directions at once: personnel costs running ahead of budget in public safety, revenue softness in fee-supported offices, and a nursing home operation whose losses have already exceeded the full-year budgeted subsidy in six months. None of these pressures is individually unmanageable. Taken together, and absent corrective action in the third quarter, they project to a year-end general fund operating deficit of approximately $8.4 million, which would draw the unassigned fund balance from $41.2 million to roughly $32.8 million — still above the Board's 15 percent reserve policy floor of $29.4 million, but with little margin remaining for the fiscal 2027 budget cycle.

The headline items, each addressed in detail in the departmental sections that follow:

- **Sheriff's office:** $2.1 million over budget at mid-year on overtime alone, driven by 31 vacancies among 214 deputy positions and a jail census averaging 388 inmates against a rated capacity of 340. Unchecked, overtime projects to a year-end overrun of $4.3 million against the $34 million departmental budget.
- **Roads and bridges:** 61 percent of the $27 million paving allocation spent to complete only 38 percent of planned centerline miles, the gap driven by a 14 percent rise in asphalt prices. The April emergency closure of the Route 4012 bridge adds an unbudgeted repair now estimated at $3.8 million.
- **Human services:** $71 million in state and federal pass-through funds drawn through June, with $1.9 million in unreconciled grant receivables — the specific item named in the June audit letter and the subject of the corrective action plan described in Section 4.
- **Tuscarora Manor:** a six-month operating loss of $4.6 million against a budgeted full-year loss of $3.1 million, with census at 142 of 180 beds and agency nurses covering 27 percent of nursing hours. Without intervention the Manor projects a year-end loss near $9.0 million.
- **911 center:** 214,000 calls answered with 9 of 46 dispatcher seats vacant; the center missed the state ninety-second answer standard on 8 percent of calls, exposing the county to a potential compliance finding from PEMA.
- **Register of deeds:** 11,400 documents recorded, down 22 percent with the collapse in mortgage refinancing volume, cutting fee revenue $940,000 below the mid-year target.

The second half of the report closes with the decisions the Board faces before the fiscal 2027 budget is built: whether to fund a corrections pay adjustment, how to finance the Route 4012 bridge, whether to commission an operational or disposition study for Tuscarora Manor, and how to close the grant reconciliation finding before the auditors return for fieldwork in October.

### 2.3 Fund Balance and Reserves

The county entered fiscal 2026 with an unassigned general fund balance of $41.2 million, equal to 21.0 percent of budgeted expenditures. The Board's reserve policy sets a floor of 15 percent, or $29.4 million against this year's budget. The uncorrected projection of an $8.4 million deficit would leave $32.8 million, or 16.7 percent — compliant, but for the first time in five years the county would be closer to the floor than to the 20 percent mark the rating agencies cited when the county's general obligation rating was affirmed at AA- in 2024. If the Board also cash-funds the bridge, the year-end balance falls to approximately $29.0 million, marginally below policy. That combination should be avoided; the bridge financing analysis in Section 3.2 outlines the alternatives.

## 3. Departmental Detail

### 3.1 Sheriff's Office and County Jail — Sheriff Marvin Delgado

The sheriff's office is the county's largest single budget pressure and the most structural. The department's $34 million budget assumed a full complement of 214 sworn deputy positions across patrol, court security, and corrections. As of June 30, 31 of those positions are vacant — a 14.5 percent vacancy rate, up from 22 vacancies at the start of the year. Fourteen deputies separated in the first half: six to municipal police departments offering starting salaries $6,000 to $9,000 above the county scale, four to the state corrections system, and four to retirement. The county hired seven, but the academy pipeline means new hires do not fill posts for months after their start dates.

The vacancies collide with a jail that is running well over capacity. The average daily census in the first half was 388 inmates against a rated capacity of 340 — 114 percent of capacity, with a peak single-day count of 411 in May. The census increase traces to two sources Sheriff Delgado's submission documents in detail: a 19 percent increase in pretrial detainees held on cash bail they cannot post, reflecting a backlog in the Court of Common Pleas that has stretched average pretrial stays from 41 to 58 days, and a state Department of Corrections backlog that left an average of 26 state-sentenced inmates awaiting transfer in the county jail each day, for which the state's per diem reimbursement covers roughly 60 percent of the county's actual cost per inmate.

The arithmetic of overcapacity plus vacancy is overtime. Post-staffing at the jail is not discretionary — housing units, medical escort, transport, and booking must be covered every shift regardless of who is on the roster — and every vacant post is covered at time-and-a-half. Overtime spending through June was $4.9 million against a full-year overtime budget of $2.8 million, putting the line $2.1 million over budget at the halfway mark. Mandatory overtime assignments (ordered holdovers) accounted for 44 percent of overtime hours, and 61 corrections officers worked more than 400 overtime hours in six months. That level of forced overtime is itself a retention problem: exit interviews cite mandatory holdovers as the leading reason for separation, ahead of pay. The department is caught in a loop in which vacancies drive overtime, overtime drives resignations, and resignations drive vacancies.

Projected without intervention, overtime finishes the year at approximately $9.9 million, a $7.1 million overrun on that line partially offset by roughly $2.8 million in salary savings from the vacant positions themselves — a net departmental overrun of $4.3 million.

Sheriff Delgado's submission requests three actions, which I have costed as follows:

| Requested Action | FY2026 Cost (half-year) | Annualized Cost | Projected Overtime Offset |
|---|---|---|---|
| Corrections officer pay adjustment ($4,500 base increase) | $0.6M | $1.4M | Retention-dependent; est. $1.2M/yr |
| $5,000 hiring bonus, corrections and patrol | $0.3M | $0.3M | Est. $0.8M/yr if 20 hires accelerate |
| Contract housing: transfer 40 inmates to Juniata County ($82/day) | $0.6M | $1.2M | Est. $1.5M/yr in post reduction |

I recommend the Board approve the contract housing agreement immediately — it is the only measure that reduces overtime in the current fiscal year rather than the next — and take up the pay adjustment as part of the fiscal 2027 budget with a possible November effective date. The county should also press the state on the transfer backlog; at the reimbursement gap, the 26 stranded state inmates cost the county approximately $340,000 per year beyond what the state pays.

### 3.2 Roads and Bridges — Public Works Department

The roads and bridges program illustrates how inflation can turn an on-budget program into an underperforming one. The department has spent $16.5 million, or 61 percent, of its $27 million paving allocation, a spending pace slightly ahead of the construction-season norm. But the program completed only 38 percent of the planned centerline miles — 47 of a planned 124 miles — because asphalt prices rose 14 percent between the budget's assumptions (built on October 2025 quotes) and the bids received in March. The department's per-mile cost came in at an average of $351,000 against a budgeted $302,000. Holding spending to the allocation, the program will finish the season at approximately 77 to 80 miles, a shortfall of 44 to 47 miles that rolls into the fiscal 2027 program and compounds: deferred resurfacing is not deferred cost, it is escalated cost, as road segments that miss their resurfacing window degrade into full-depth reclamation candidates at three to four times the price. The department estimates that ten of the deferred segments are at risk of crossing that threshold if pushed past 2027.

The larger and more urgent public works matter is the Route 4012 bridge over Killbuck Creek. The structure, a 1958 two-span steel girder bridge carrying an average of 4,100 vehicles per day, was closed on April 14 after a routine inspection found section loss in a girder bearing area beyond the tolerance for continued load posting. The closure imposes a 7.4-mile detour on through traffic and has drawn sustained complaint from residents of Killbuck Township and from two employers whose truck traffic uses the crossing. The emergency repair — girder end replacement, bearing replacement, substructure concrete repair, and deck work at the affected span — is now estimated at $3.8 million, up from the initial $2.9 million estimate after the design consultant's inspection found the second span's bearings in similar condition. Design is 80 percent complete; with an expedited letting, construction could begin in October and the bridge could reopen in the second quarter of 2027.

No funding source is budgeted. The options are:

1. **Cash from capital reserve.** The capital reserve holds $11.6 million, but $7.2 million is committed to the courthouse HVAC replacement and the human services building roof. A $3.8 million draw would leave the reserve at $600,000 of uncommitted balance — imprudent.
2. **County liquid fuels fund.** The fund's unallocated balance is $1.4 million; it could contribute but not carry the project.
3. **PennDOT emergency bridge funding / off-system bridge program.** The county has applied; an award of up to $1.5 million is plausible but not assured, and the timeline is uncertain.
4. **Short-term borrowing.** A five-year bank note for $3.8 million at current rates would cost approximately $850,000 per year in debt service, absorbable within the existing debt service structure as older issues roll off in 2027.

My recommendation is a blended approach: commit $1.4 million from liquid fuels, pursue the PennDOT award aggressively, and authorize a bank note for the balance, sized after the PennDOT decision. What the Board should not do is delay the letting while the funding question is resolved; every month of delay pushes reopening deeper into 2027 and holds the detour on 4,100 vehicles a day.

### 3.3 Human Services — Pass-Through Funds and the Grant Reconciliation Finding

The human services department administers the county's largest flow of money that is not, in the ordinary sense, the county's money. Through June 30 the department drew $71 million in state and federal pass-through funding across its major program streams — mental health and intellectual disabilities base funding, the HealthChoices behavioral health capitation, children and youth Act 148 reimbursements, drug and alcohol grants, early intervention, and the human services block grant. Programmatically, the department's first half was sound: children and youth caseloads held stable at an average of 412 open cases, placement costs tracked budget, and the mental health base program operated within its allocation.

The financial management of that money is the problem, and it is the specific subject of the June audit letter. As of June 30, the department carries $1.9 million in unreconciled grant receivables — amounts the county has spent and billed, or believes it has billed, against grant awards, where the county's records and the state's records of what has been drawn, what has been paid, and what remains available do not agree and have not been brought into agreement. The auditors' letter characterized the condition as a significant deficiency in internal control and warned that, if unresolved at fiscal year-end, it could escalate to a material weakness in the fiscal 2026 single audit — a designation with real consequences, since a material weakness in federal award administration can trigger enhanced monitoring by granting agencies and, in the worst case, cash-on-reimbursement restrictions that would force the county to front program costs.

The composition of the $1.9 million, the aging of the items, and the corrective action plan are detailed in Section 4. The department-level point for the Board is this: the receivables are not, on present evidence, money that is lost. The department's preliminary work suggests the large majority of the balance represents timing differences, unbilled adjustments, and state payment postings the county has not matched to its own invoices. But "probably collectible" is not a status an auditor will accept, and some fraction of the balance — the department's fiscal officer estimates $150,000 to $300,000 — is likely to prove unbillable due to missed submission deadlines on prior-period adjustments. Any such amount becomes a county cost, and I have included a $250,000 provision in the year-end projection.

The county's own share of human services spending — the required local match and county-funded services — stands at $9.6 million against a $19.4 million budget, precisely on pace, and I project no variance on the county share.

### 3.4 Tuscarora Manor — Administrator Sheila Rourke

Tuscarora Manor is, in dollar terms, the county's most serious operating problem, and unlike the sheriff's overtime it is not a problem that hiring alone can fix. The 180-bed skilled nursing facility budgeted a fiscal 2026 operating loss of $3.1 million, to be covered by a general fund subsidy transfer. Through six months, the Manor has lost $4.6 million — the full-year budgeted loss plus $1.5 million, in half the time. On its current trajectory the facility will lose approximately $9.0 million this year, nearly triple the budget, and the enterprise fund's cash position will require a supplemental transfer of at least $2.5 million before year-end simply to meet payroll and vendor obligations.

The loss has two engines, and Administrator Rourke's submission is candid about both.

**Census.** Average occupied beds in the first half were 142 of 180, a 78.9 percent occupancy rate against a budgeted 87 percent (157 beds). The Manor is not short of demand — the facility maintains a referral waiting list — it is short of the staffed capacity to admit. Two wings totaling 24 beds have been effectively offline since February because the facility cannot staff them within the state's minimum nurse staffing ratios. Every empty bed forgoes revenue (a blended rate of roughly $310 per day across the payer mix) while the building's fixed costs — plant, dietary, administration, debt service on the 2014 renovation bonds — continue unchanged.

**Agency nursing.** Contracted agency nurses covered 27 percent of nursing hours in the first half, at hourly rates averaging 2.1 times the fully loaded cost of employed staff. Agency spending through June was $3.4 million against a full-year budget of $1.8 million. The facility has 34 vacant nursing positions (RN, LPN, and CNA combined) out of a complement of 168; like the jail, it is losing staff to competitors — in this case hospital systems and, perversely, to the very staffing agencies whose nurses then work the Manor's own floors at double the cost.

The payer mix compounds everything. Medicaid residents are 71 percent of census, and the Pennsylvania Medicaid rate reimburses approximately $46 per day below the Manor's actual cost per resident day even at efficient staffing. At current staffing costs the gap is closer to $95 per day. The facility's Medicare and private-pay census — the payers that cross-subsidize Medicaid in every viable nursing home — has shrunk as the offline wings reduced the short-stay rehabilitation capacity that attracts Medicare admissions.

Administrator Rourke's stabilization plan requests a wage adjustment for nursing staff (estimated $1.1 million annually), a weekend shift differential, and a commitment to reopen one 12-bed wing by November. Her projections show that plan reducing the annual loss to the $5.5 to $6.0 million range by fiscal 2028 — an improvement, but still roughly double what the county budgeted this year. The honest framing for the Board is that the choice is not between the current loss and the budgeted loss; it is between funding a stabilization plan that still leaves a structural loss of $5 to $6 million a year, and commissioning a formal study of the alternatives — management contract, census reduction to a sustainable core, or sale to a nonprofit or private operator, a path a substantial number of Pennsylvania counties have taken over the past two decades. Section 5 recommends the Board authorize both: fund the near-term stabilization measures, because the facility's 142 residents require safe staffing regardless of the long-term answer, and commission the study, because fiscal 2027 cannot be budgeted around a $9 million open question.

### 3.5 911 Emergency Communications Center

The 911 center answered 214,000 calls in the first half — emergency and administrative combined — a volume essentially flat against prior year. The center's problem is not workload growth but staffing: 9 of 46 dispatcher seats are vacant, a 19.6 percent vacancy rate, and the operational consequence is now measurable in the center's core performance standard. Pennsylvania's standard requires 90 percent of 911 calls to be answered within ten seconds and, under the metric the county reports, calls answered within ninety seconds at a threshold the center has historically exceeded comfortably. In the first half, the center missed the ninety-second standard on 8 percent of calls — roughly 17,100 calls in which a caller in a potential emergency waited more than a minute and a half for a human voice. The misses cluster predictably: overnight shifts running minimum staffing, and surge periods during severe weather events in March and June when call volume spiked while the same thin roster was on the floor.

The compliance exposure is concrete. PEMA's 911 program office reviews answer-time performance in connection with the county's statewide interconnectivity funding, and sustained noncompliance can jeopardize a portion of the roughly $2.1 million the county receives annually from the state 911 surcharge distribution. Beyond the funding risk sits the liability and public-trust risk that needs no elaboration.

The staffing problem has a specific shape. Dispatcher training takes six to nine months from hire to independent console work, so the center's seven first-half hires will not relieve the floor until late 2026 at the earliest — and the center lost five dispatchers in the same period, three of them within eighteen months of hire. Starting pay of $41,800 trails the adjacent counties' centers by $3,000 to $5,500, and mandatory overtime (dispatchers averaged 11 hours of overtime per pay period in the first half) is producing the same burnout-driven attrition loop documented at the jail and the Manor. Overtime spending of $410,000 through June is running against vacancy salary savings and nets to a modest projected favorable variance of $200,000 — the only department in this report whose vacancy problem is not also a budget overrun, which is cold comfort given what the vacancy is doing to answer times.

The director's submission requests a market pay adjustment (approximately $290,000 annually) and a restructured trainee classification allowing hires to be paid during academy and console training at a rate that competes with entry-level positions elsewhere in county government. Both requests are reasonable in scale relative to the funding at risk, and I recommend the pay adjustment be considered together with the corrections adjustment in a single classification-and-compensation action rather than piecemeal.

### 3.6 Register of Deeds

The register of deeds recorded 11,400 documents in the first half, down 22 percent from 14,600 in the same period of 2025. The decline is not an office performance issue; it is the mortgage market. Refinancing activity, which drove recording volumes through the low-rate years, has largely evaporated, and purchase-money mortgage volume in the county is down with sales activity. Deed recordings held nearly flat (down 4 percent), while mortgage and mortgage-satisfaction recordings fell 31 percent — and mortgages carry the office's higher fee schedule, so the revenue decline outpaces the volume decline.

Fee revenue through June was $2.46 million against a mid-year target of $3.40 million, a shortfall of $940,000. The office's budget assumed a modest recovery in recording volume that has not materialized and, on current interest-rate expectations, will not materialize this year. I project a full-year revenue shortfall of $1.7 to $1.9 million on this line, and the fiscal 2027 revenue budget should be built on current volumes, not on a recovery assumption.

The register's operating costs are modest ($1.4 million budgeted) and on pace; the register has held two clerical vacancies open, saving approximately $60,000, which is the appropriate response to reduced volume. There is no corrective action available on the revenue side — recording fees are set by statute, and volume is set by the bond market. The item's significance is as a component of the countywide revenue shortfall and as a caution for 2027 budgeting: the county's fee-revenue base is more rate-sensitive than the budget has historically assumed.

## 4. The Audit Finding: Grant Reconciliation

### 4.1 The Finding

The June 12 letter from the county's independent auditors, issued at the conclusion of interim fieldwork for the fiscal 2025 single audit, identified a significant deficiency in internal control over grant financial management, centered on human services. The condition: the county does not perform timely, documented reconciliations between its general ledger grant receivable balances, its grant billing records, and the confirming records of the granting agencies (principally the state Department of Human Services). As a result, $1.9 million in grant receivables on the county's books cannot currently be tied, item by item, to amounts the granting agencies acknowledge as due.

### 4.2 Composition and Aging

The chief clerk's office and the human services fiscal division have completed a preliminary decomposition of the balance:

| Category | Amount | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| State payments received but not matched to county invoices | $0.7M | Timing/posting; resolvable through matching |
| Invoices submitted, unpaid, within normal state payment cycle | $0.5M | Collectible; requires confirmation |
| Prior-period cost report adjustments, not yet billed | $0.4M | Partially at risk if submission windows lapsed |
| Disputed or unsupported items | $0.3M | At risk; documentation being assembled |
| **Total** | **$1.9M** | Provision of $250K included in year-end projection |

Of the total, $1.1 million relates to fiscal 2025 and prior award periods — the aging that most concerned the auditors, since resolution windows narrow as award periods close out.

### 4.3 Root Causes

Three causes, in rough order of weight. First, the human services fiscal division lost its senior grant accountant to retirement in September 2025 and its reconciliation specialist to a state job in January 2026; the reconciliation function effectively stopped for two quarters, and no compensating control caught it. Second, the county's financial system requires manual re-keying between the grant billing module and the general ledger, generating mismatches that only reconciliation would surface. Third, the state's own remittance detail frequently arrives as lump-sum payments spanning multiple invoices and program streams, making matching genuinely laborious — an explanation, not an excuse, since other counties reconcile against the same remittances.

### 4.4 Corrective Action Plan

The plan, developed jointly by my office, the chief clerk, and the human services fiscal officer, has been shared with the auditors:

1. **Immediate reconciliation project (July–September).** Two temporary accountants engaged July 21, working under my office's supervision, to clear the $1.9 million item by item. Target: balance reduced below $300,000 by September 30, with every remaining item documented, before audit fieldwork resumes in October. Cost: approximately $85,000, absorbed in the budget office's existing appropriation.
2. **Permanent staffing.** Refill of the grant accountant position (posted, interviews in August) and conversion of one temporary position to a permanent reconciliation role in the fiscal 2027 budget.
3. **Monthly reconciliation control.** Effective October 1, monthly grant reconciliation with sign-off by the human services fiscal officer and quarterly review by the budget office, reported to the Board's finance committee. This is the control whose absence the auditors cited; instituting and documenting it is what closes the finding.
4. **System remediation.** An interface between the billing module and general ledger, scoped by IT at approximately $120,000, proposed for fiscal 2027 capital.

Any portion of the balance that proves unbillable will be written off in fiscal 2026 with full disclosure; the $250,000 provision noted above covers the fiscal officer's estimated range.

## 5. The Second Half: What the Board Faces

### 5.1 Closing the 2026 Gap

The uncorrected projection is an $8.4 million operating deficit. The measures available in the current year:

| Measure | Estimated 2026 Effect |
|---|---|
| Jail contract housing (40 inmates, Juniata County) | +$0.9M net overtime savings |
| Hiring freeze on non-critical general government vacancies (excludes sheriff, 911, Manor nursing) | +$1.1M |
| Third-quarter discretionary spending controls (travel, non-safety equipment, contracted services review) | +$0.8M |
| Unspent contingency held rather than allocated | +$0.6M |
| **Total mitigation** | **+$3.4M** |
| **Revised projected deficit** | **~$5.0M** |

A $5.0 million draw leaves the unassigned fund balance at approximately $36.2 million, or 18.5 percent — inside policy with room to spare, provided the bridge is financed rather than cash-funded. I request Board concurrence with the freeze and spending controls at the August 6 meeting so they take effect for the full second half.

### 5.2 Decisions Requested of the Board

1. **Approve the jail contract housing agreement** with Juniata County, effective September 1. This is the single largest current-year mitigation available and also relieves an overcrowding condition that carries its own liability.
2. **Authorize the Route 4012 bridge letting** in September and the blended financing approach (liquid fuels contribution, PennDOT application, bank note for the balance), with the note sized after the state's decision. Do not cash-fund from the capital reserve.
3. **Direct a single classification-and-compensation action** covering corrections officers, 911 dispatchers, and Manor nursing staff, for adoption with the fiscal 2027 budget and a possible November 2026 effective date for the most critical classes. The three worst vacancy problems in the county share one cause; they should share one solution, costed together (approximately $2.8 million annually at full implementation) rather than approved as competing piecemeal requests.
4. **Fund Tuscarora Manor's near-term stabilization** — the supplemental transfer of $2.5 million and the nursing wage measures — **and simultaneously commission an independent study** of the Manor's long-term options, with a report due in time to inform the fiscal 2027 budget. The county cannot responsibly budget 2027 with a $9 million annual loss as an unexamined assumption.
5. **Accept the grant reconciliation corrective action plan** and direct quarterly reporting to the finance committee until the auditors confirm the finding closed.

### 5.3 Looking Toward Fiscal 2027

The 2027 budget will be built on harder ground than 2026's was: recording revenue at current volumes rather than a recovery assumption; asphalt at 2026 prices; a jail overtime line sized to reality; a Manor decision, one way or the other; and a compensation structure that stops financing vacancies with time-and-a-half. Several of those items are cost increases the county cannot avoid recognizing; a preliminary 2027 gap estimate will accompany the September budget kickoff. The purpose of the actions requested above is to ensure the county enters that process with reserves intact, the audit finding closed, and its three staffing crises on a path to correction rather than compounding for another year.
