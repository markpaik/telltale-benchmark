## 1. Purpose and Scope

This report presents the full third-quarter results for all five Tidewater Opportunity Partners program lines, the agency's financial position through nine months of fiscal year 2026, the current staffing picture, the status of the Petersburg site corrective action plan, and the matters the committee should weigh regarding the fourth quarter and the pending reduction in early literacy grant funding effective October 1, 2026.

At the direction of Mr. Vasquez-Ortiz and Ms. Blanchard, this report is organized to present the quarter comprehensively rather than by exception. Following feedback after the second-quarter report, every program line is addressed on its own terms, with results, budget performance, and an explanation of drivers, regardless of whether that line met, missed, or exceeded its targets. The intent is to give the committee a complete and even-handed picture ahead of the May 12 meeting so that no trustee is learning about a material issue for the first time in the room.

Where a table more clearly conveys the data, a table is used. Narrative sections explain causes, context, and management's current thinking, which do not compress well into tabular form.

## 2. Program Line Results

### 2.1 Youth Workforce Development

Youth Workforce served 1,340 participants in the third quarter against a target of 1,800, an enrollment shortfall of 460 participants, or 26 percent below plan. Of those served, 47 percent were placed into jobs by quarter's end, compared with 58 percent in the same quarter of the prior fiscal year — an 11-percentage-point decline in placement performance layered on top of the enrollment shortfall.

Two separate problems are contributing to this line's underperformance, and it is important that the committee understand them as distinct rather than as a single trend.

The enrollment shortfall traces primarily to referral volume from the three public school divisions that historically account for roughly 70 percent of Youth Workforce intake. Two of those divisions changed their career-counseling referral protocols at the start of the school year, requiring a signed parental consent form to be submitted before a referral packet is forwarded to Tidewater rather than after, as had been the prior practice. Program staff did not identify the practical effect of this change — a slower and leakier referral pipeline — until January, when quarterly enrollment numbers were compiled and compared against target pacing. Ms. Nwachukwu's office has since met with counseling leadership in both divisions to discuss a joint intake event model that would reduce the number of steps a family must complete before a young person is enrolled. A pilot of that model is planned for the fourth quarter in one division, with results to be reported to the committee in the fourth-quarter report.

The placement decline is a separate matter and appears tied to sector demand. Youth Workforce's placement pipeline is weighted toward retail, hospitality, and food service employers in the Hampton Roads region. Program staff report that several long-standing employer partners reduced entry-level hiring in the January–March window, consistent with seasonal patterns but more pronounced this year than in the two prior years. Staff also note that average time-to-placement for those participants who were placed lengthened from roughly 5 weeks to roughly 8 weeks, suggesting a softer, not simply smaller, hiring market rather than a failure of program delivery. Program staff are expanding outreach to employers in logistics and warehousing, sectors that have shown more stable entry-level demand in the region, and will report on early results from that shift next quarter.

Youth Workforce's expenditures for the quarter were modestly under budget, reflecting lower-than-planned enrollment (fewer participants drawing on stipend and support-service funds) rather than a deliberate cost-management action. This should not be read as a favorable financial result; it is a byproduct of the enrollment shortfall described above and is expected to reverse somewhat in the fourth quarter if the enrollment pilot succeeds.

### 2.2 Adult Reentry Employment

Adult Reentry Employment served 410 participants in the quarter and held its job placement rate at 63 percent, consistent with the same period a year ago. On the surface, this is the most stable of the five program lines this quarter. The committee should understand, however, that this stability was achieved under meaningfully degraded staffing conditions and is not necessarily sustainable without intervention.

The program lost both of its senior case managers in February — one to a position with a state agency offering a higher salary band, and one to relocation outside the region. Neither position has been filled as of this writing. As a direct result, average caseloads for the remaining case management staff have risen to 71 participants per case manager, up from a target ratio the program has historically tried to hold at or below 45. Program leadership reports that remaining staff have absorbed the increased caseload through additional hours and some triage of lower-acuity cases, which is one reason placement performance has not yet visibly declined. Staff turnover risk among the remaining case managers is a live concern; two have raised workload concerns directly with the program director in the past six weeks.

Recruitment for both vacant positions is underway. Human resources reports that the senior case manager role has historically taken 10 to 14 weeks to fill given the specialized experience required (case managers in this program generally need direct experience with justice-involved populations and existing relationships with regional employers willing to hire individuals with criminal records). Absent a hire before the fourth quarter's midpoint, program leadership has flagged a real risk that placement performance will begin to decline in the fourth quarter even though it held steady this quarter. This is discussed further in Section 6.

Adult Reentry Employment's spending was on budget for the quarter. The two vacant senior positions produced modest salary savings that were largely offset by increased use of contracted per-diem support staff to help cover intake and documentation functions that would ordinarily fall to the case managers.

### 2.3 Emergency Housing

Emergency Housing is the program line requiring the most direct attention from the committee this quarter. The line spent $4.9 million against a $4.2 million quarterly budget, a variance of $700,000, or approximately 17 percent over budget for the quarter.

Two factors drove this variance, and both are structural rather than one-time in nature.

First, motel voucher costs — the largest single cost category within this program line — rose 34 percent compared with the same quarter last year. This reflects both rate increases from motel partners, several of whom renegotiated nightly rates upward at the start of the calendar year, and a shift in the mix of available inventory toward higher-cost properties as several lower-cost motels the program had relied on either closed or declined to renew participation agreements. Program staff have begun conversations with two additional lower-cost properties but have not yet secured signed agreements.

Second, and more significant to the underlying cost trajectory, average length of stay in emergency housing rose from 41 nights in the prior comparable period to 66 nights this quarter — a 61 percent increase. Longer stays mean more nights billed per household, which compounds the effect of higher nightly rates. Program staff attribute the lengthened stays primarily to a tighter regional rental market: case managers report that the supply of affordable permanent units to which they can transition households has visibly contracted since the fall, and that landlords who previously accepted program-subsidized move-ins are increasingly requiring credit and income documentation that many program participants cannot immediately provide. This is consistent with reporting from other regional housing providers and appears to reflect a market-wide condition rather than a program-specific delivery problem, though program leadership is also reviewing internal case management practices to confirm that exits to permanent housing are being pursued as actively and as early in each stay as program protocol requires.

The committee should understand that this is not a one-quarter anomaly that is expected to self-correct. If length of stay and voucher rates remain at current levels through the fourth quarter, this line is on a trajectory to finish the fiscal year materially over its annual budget, a matter addressed further in Section 3 and Section 6. Program leadership is preparing options for the committee's consideration, including a possible cap on voucher-eligible length of stay paired with expedited case management review at 45 days, and renewed outreach to secure lower-cost motel inventory. These options will be presented in fuller form at the May 12 meeting.

### 2.4 Behavioral Health Counseling

Behavioral Health Counseling billed $2.1 million in Medicaid revenue against a $2.8 million quarterly projection, a shortfall of $700,000, or 25 percent below projection. This is a revenue-side problem rather than a demand-side one: referral volume and waitlist figures indicate that need for services has not declined.

The proximate cause is staffing. Three of the program's 11 clinician positions were vacant for the entire quarter. Because Medicaid billing in this program is directly tied to billable clinical hours delivered, a 27 percent vacancy rate in clinician headcount translates roughly, though not precisely, into a comparable reduction in billable capacity. The gap between the vacancy rate and the somewhat larger revenue shortfall percentage reflects some additional drag from new-clinician ramp-up time among two staff hired late in the second quarter, who are still building caseloads to full productivity, and from one clinician's extended medical leave that began in February and was not separately counted among the three vacant positions.

Recruitment for behavioral health clinicians has been difficult agency-wide and is not unique to Tidewater; program leadership and human resources both point to a regional shortage of licensed clinicians, particularly those willing to work with the program's target population and at the program's compensation bands, which have not kept pace with several regional health systems' recent increases. Two of the three vacancies have been posted since November of last year without a qualified hire. Human resources is preparing a compensation benchmarking review for this job family, to be discussed in Section 4.

The revenue shortfall in this line does not immediately threaten cash operations because Medicaid billing operates on a reimbursement basis with an existing lag, and the program has sufficient unrestricted reserves allocated to bridge short-term timing gaps. It does, however, represent a real erosion of the program's ability to reach projected service volume and revenue for the full fiscal year if vacancies are not resolved. This is addressed further in Section 6.

### 2.5 Early Literacy

Early Literacy is the smallest of the five program lines by budget, at $1.6 million for the year, and it is the one line that met every performance target in the third quarter. The program enrolled 620 children against its enrollment target and met its associated targets for kindergarten readiness screening participation and family engagement session attendance. Program leadership reports no operational concerns for the quarter, and finances were on budget.

The committee's attention on this line should be directed not at the quarter just completed but at the quarter ahead. Early Literacy's primary federal grant, which funds approximately 38 percent of the program's annual budget, will be reduced by $600,000 effective October 1, 2026 — the start of fiscal year 2027's first quarter, but a date that falls within the planning horizon the committee should be considering now given lead time needed for staffing and enrollment decisions. This reduction and its implications are addressed in full in Section 6.

## 4. Staffing and Workforce Capacity

Agency-wide staff turnover ran at 29 percent on an annualized basis for the third quarter. This figure is elevated relative to the agency's three-year historical average of approximately 19 percent and represents the highest quarterly turnover rate the agency has recorded since Ms. Nwachukwu's office began tracking this metric on a consistent quarterly basis three years ago.

Turnover is not evenly distributed across the agency. The table below summarizes where the committee should focus attention.

| Area | Turnover Indicator | Notable Context |
|---|---|---|
| Adult Reentry Employment | Both senior case managers departed in February | Caseloads now at 71, versus target of 45 |
| Behavioral Health Counseling | 3 of 11 clinician positions vacant all quarter | Regional clinician shortage; compensation gap suspected |
| Petersburg Site | Not separately tracked this quarter, but under corrective action | See Section 5 |
| Agency-wide | 29% annualized turnover | Up from ~19% three-year average |

Human resources leadership has identified compensation competitiveness as the most likely single driver of elevated turnover, particularly in clinical and senior case management roles where regional competitors, including hospital systems and state agencies, have made compensation adjustments over the past 18 months that Tidewater's pay bands have not matched. A compensation benchmarking study covering clinical and case management job families has been commissioned and is expected to be completed before the fourth-quarter report, with preliminary findings to be shared with the committee at that time.

Exit interview data collected during the quarter, while limited in volume, cites workload and compensation as the two most frequently mentioned factors among departing staff, with a smaller number of respondents citing management or supervisory concerns. Ms. Nwachukwu's office does not consider the current exit interview sample large enough to draw firm conclusions about supervisory factors and recommends continued monitoring rather than immediate action on that specific point.

The immediate operational consequence of this turnover, beyond the direct effects already described in Sections 2.2 and 2.4, is increased reliance on remaining staff working above capacity and on contracted or temporary support in some functions. This is not a sustainable long-term operating model and increases the risk of further departures if not addressed. Management's near-term response includes accelerated recruitment for the vacant positions described above and the compensation review noted here; a longer-term response, including any request for board-level action on compensation strategy, will depend on the outcome of that review and will be brought to the committee separately.

## 5. Petersburg Site Corrective Action

The Petersburg site, under the direction of Thuy Pham, remains under a state corrective action plan following a January monitoring visit by the state oversight agency that found 22 incomplete case files. The committee should understand both the nature of the finding and the status of the agency's response.

The monitoring visit reviewed a sample of case files across the site's program lines and identified 22 files with incomplete documentation, meaning files that were missing one or more required elements such as updated service plans, signed consent forms, or documented case notes at required intervals. The state's finding did not allege service failures to individual clients or safety concerns; it was a documentation compliance finding. That distinction matters for how the committee should weigh the severity of the issue, but it does not diminish the obligation to correct it fully and promptly, since documentation compliance is itself a condition of continued state funding at the site.

Ms. Pham submitted a corrective action plan to the state within the required 30-day window following the finding. That plan, which Ms. Nwachukwu's office has reviewed, includes the following core elements:

- A full file audit of all active cases at the Petersburg site, not limited to the sampled files, to identify any additional documentation gaps beyond the 22 originally cited.
- Reassignment of file completion responsibilities with a revised weekly review checkpoint led by the site's program supervisor, intended to catch documentation gaps before they accumulate rather than relying solely on periodic audits.
- Additional training for Petersburg site staff on documentation requirements, delivered in February and March.
- A commitment to closing all 22 originally identified file gaps by a state-set deadline, which Ms. Pham's office reports has been met as of the date of this report, subject to state confirmation.

The state has not yet conducted a follow-up monitoring visit to confirm closure of the corrective action plan. Ms. Nwachukwu's office understands from informal communication with the state program officer that a follow-up visit is expected sometime in the fourth quarter but has not been formally scheduled. Until that visit occurs and the state confirms the corrective action plan is satisfied, the Petersburg site remains formally under corrective action status, which carries some risk to continued state funding at that site if the state is not satisfied with the remediation.

Ms. Nwachukwu recommends that the committee ask Mr. Vasquez-Ortiz's office to provide a direct update as soon as the follow-up visit occurs, rather than waiting for the next scheduled quarterly report, given the funding risk involved.

## 6. Fourth Quarter Outlook and the Coming Grant Reduction

The committee should carry three specific concerns into its consideration of the fourth quarter and beyond, in addition to continued monitoring of the Petersburg corrective action described above.

**Emergency Housing cost trajectory.** As discussed in Section 2.3, the drivers behind this quarter's $700,000 overage — elevated motel voucher rates and lengthened average stays — are structural conditions tied to the regional housing market, not one-time events. Absent a change either in program design (such as a length-of-stay cap with expedited case review, as program leadership is considering) or in available lower-cost motel inventory, the fourth quarter is likely to show a similar or larger overage. Management will bring specific options for committee consideration at the May 12 meeting, but the committee should be prepared to discuss whether any of those options require board-level approval, particularly if they involve a change to the annual budget for this line or a request to draw further on reserves.

**Adult Reentry Employment staffing risk.** The stable 63 percent placement rate reported this quarter was achieved with two senior case manager vacancies partially absorbed by remaining staff working caseloads of 71 against a target of 45. If both positions remain unfilled through the fourth quarter, program leadership has indicated a real risk that placement performance will decline in the next reporting period, and that remaining staff retention is itself at risk. The committee should treat this quarter's stable numbers as a lagging indicator that may not hold, not as evidence the staffing gap is being adequately managed on a sustained basis.

**Behavioral Health Counseling revenue.** With three clinician vacancies unfilled for the full quarter and a regional clinician shortage that recruitment efforts have not yet overcome, the fourth quarter is likely to show a continued revenue shortfall against projection unless at least some vacancies are filled. The compensation benchmarking study referenced in Section 4 may eventually address the underlying recruitment difficulty, but its findings will not be available in time to affect fourth-quarter hiring, meaning the committee should expect this line's revenue shortfall to persist through at least the end of the current fiscal year.

**The October 1 grant reduction for Early Literacy.** Although Early Literacy performed well this quarter and the $600,000 federal grant reduction does not take effect until October 1, 2026 — the start of fiscal year 2027 — the committee should begin discussing this now for two reasons. First, any decision about how to absorb the reduction, whether through reduced enrollment, replacement funding from other sources, or reallocation of agency general funds, has staffing and enrollment implications that require lead time; program staff and family commitments for the 2026–2027 program year are typically finalized well before October. Second, a $600,000 reduction against a $1.6 million total program budget represents a 38 percent cut to this line's funding, which is large enough that it cannot be absorbed through minor efficiencies alone without a corresponding reduction in the number of children served, absent replacement funding. Ms. Nwachukwu's office recommends that this matter be placed on the agenda for the committee's next two meetings as a standing item, separate from the routine quarterly report cycle, so that a funding and enrollment plan can be developed well ahead of the October effective date. Development staff have identified two foundation prospects that could potentially offset a portion of the reduction, but neither has been formally approached, and neither should be assumed as a certain source in any planning scenario the committee considers.

Taken together, these four matters — the Emergency Housing cost trajectory, the Adult Reentry Employment staffing risk, the Behavioral Health Counseling revenue shortfall, and the Early Literacy grant reduction — represent the agency's principal areas of financial and operational exposure heading into the fourth quarter. None of them is a surprise arising late in the quarter; each has been visible to management for at least several weeks and is presented here in full so that the committee has the same information management has been working with.

## Appendix E: Definitions and Reporting Methodology

To assist committee members who do not review program-level data on a regular basis, this appendix defines the key metrics used throughout the report and notes any changes in methodology that could affect quarter-over-quarter or year-over-year comparisons.

**Job placement rate (Youth Workforce and Adult Reentry Employment).** Calculated as the number of participants who obtained and held unsubsidized employment for at least 30 consecutive days during the reporting period, divided by the number of participants who reached program completion or exited the program during that same period. This definition has been applied consistently across the periods compared in this report. Program staff note that a participant placed in the final two weeks of the quarter may not yet have reached the 30-day threshold at the time this report was compiled and would be reflected in the following quarter's figures instead, which can create minor timing effects at period boundaries.

**Average length of stay (Emergency Housing).** Calculated as the average number of nights between intake and exit for all households that exited emergency housing during the quarter, regardless of exit destination. This figure does not include households still in emergency housing at quarter's end, whose eventual length of stay is not yet known and may be longer than the reported average once they do exit.

**Medicaid billing revenue (Behavioral Health Counseling).** Reflects revenue recognized on an accrual basis for billable clinical services delivered during the quarter, net of anticipated denials based on the program's historical denial rate. It does not reflect cash actually received during the quarter, which lags recognition by the program's standard Medicaid reimbursement cycle of approximately 45 to 60 days. Ms. Blanchard's office can provide cash-basis figures separately if the committee prefers that view.

**Annualized turnover rate.** Calculated by dividing the number of voluntary and involuntary separations during the quarter by the average number of filled positions during the quarter, then multiplying by four to annualize a single quarter's rate. This is a standard human resources convention and should not be read as meaning 29 percent of staff actually departed during the quarter itself; the actual quarterly separation count is provided in Appendix B for those who prefer that figure directly.

**Caseload ratios (Adult Reentry Employment).** Calculated as the number of active participant cases divided by the number of filled case management positions at period end. This ratio is sensitive to short-term vacancies and can move quickly when even one position turns over, as occurred this quarter.

No changes in methodology were made during the third quarter that would affect comparability with prior periods, with one exception: beginning this quarter, Emergency Housing length-of-stay figures exclude a small number of households (fewer than five) placed under a short-term emergency weather protocol activated in January, whose stays are capped by design at 14 nights and were previously blended into the general average. Program staff made this adjustment because blending the two populations was understated the general population's length-of-stay trend in prior periods. Had this quarter's figure been calculated using the prior methodology, average length of stay would have been 64 nights rather than 66 — a small difference, but the committee should be aware of the change in case it is reviewing multi-quarter trend lines independently.
