## Statement of Need

In the four-state region Sunflower Civic Code serves — Kansas, Missouri, Nebraska, and Oklahoma — the gap between eligibility for public benefits and actual enrollment remains one of the most consequential and least visible failures of the safety net. The people who use our tools have a median household income of $27,400, well below twice the federal poverty line, and the majority of them qualify for programs they are not receiving or are at risk of losing. The problem is rarely eligibility itself. It is process: paperwork that arrives at the wrong address, renewal notices written at a college reading level, court dates communicated once by mail, and application systems designed for desktop computers when 61 percent of our users reach us from a phone.

Two developments have made this need sharper since we last did work at this scale. First, the resumption of Medicaid renewals and the tightening of SNAP recertification schedules mean that continuous coverage is over and procedural churn is back. National data consistently show that most Medicaid disenrollments during renewal cycles are procedural — the person remains eligible but loses coverage because a form was not returned or a document was not uploaded in time. In the rural counties that make up most of our service area, county human services offices are understaffed and cannot do proactive outreach at the volume the renewal calendar demands. A family in Barton County, Kansas, or Nowata County, Oklahoma, that misses a renewal deadline may not discover the lapse until a pharmacy visit fails. Our screener currently handles initial eligibility screening well — 218,000 screenings last year — but it was not built for the renewal and recertification workflow, which is where the churn actually happens.

Second, failure to appear in court remains a quiet engine of escalating legal and financial harm for low-income people. A missed hearing on a traffic citation or an eviction answer date converts a manageable problem into a bench warrant, a default judgment, a suspended license, or a lost home. Before our reminder tool, the failure-to-appear rate across our three pilot counties averaged 22 percent across 14,000 hearings. With plain-language text reminders tied to court calendaring data, that rate fell to 9 percent — a 59 percent relative reduction. That result tells us two things: the problem is largely a communication and logistics problem rather than a compliance problem, and a modest, well-built tool can move an outcome that courts have struggled with for decades. But the tool currently reaches only three counties, and the underlying data integration must be rebuilt county by county.

There is also a language access gap our current tools do not close. Spanish is the primary language in a growing share of households across southwest Kansas, the Missouri bootheel, and Oklahoma's meatpacking corridor, and Vietnamese-speaking communities in Wichita, Kansas City, and Oklahoma City are substantially underserved by both benefits offices and court systems. Our tools today are English-only, which means the households most likely to lose benefits to procedural churn — those navigating notices in a second language — are the households we serve least well.

Finally, there is a need on the institutional side. Forty-six county agencies and seven legal aid organizations rely on our tools now, and demand from additional counties consistently exceeds our onboarding capacity. Counties want software that works, that they can afford, and that they do not have to procure from vendors whose per-seat pricing was designed for large urban jurisdictions. Open-source civic infrastructure fills that gap — but only if the organization maintaining it is durable, well-governed, and financially sustainable. This application addresses both the product need and the institutional one, and we address our own institutional history with this Foundation candidly in the Organizational Capacity section below.

## Project Description

Sunflower Civic Code requests $3,100,000 over three years to rebuild and extend our open-source benefits and courts tooling in four connected workstreams.

**Workstream 1: Rebuild the benefits screener for Medicaid renewal and SNAP recertification.** Our current screener answers the question "am I eligible?" This project rebuilds it to answer the harder operational questions: "when is my renewal due, what documents do I need, and how do I submit them before the deadline?" The rebuilt screener will ingest renewal timelines, generate a personalized document checklist, send deadline reminders by text, and — where county systems allow — pre-populate renewal paperwork. The architecture will separate the eligibility rules engine from the interface layer so that state-specific rule changes (which arrive constantly, especially in SNAP) can be updated without redeploying the application. The rules engine will be published as a standalone open-source library so that legal aid organizations and other civic technology groups can reuse it. Because 61 percent of our traffic is mobile, the rebuild is mobile-first: the target is a complete renewal-preparation session in under twelve minutes on a phone, over a cellular connection, without requiring an account or an app download.

**Workstream 2: Spanish and Vietnamese language versions.** Localization here means more than translation. Benefits and court language does not map word-for-word across languages, and machine-translated legal notices are a known source of error and mistrust. We will contract community-based translators and reviewers in each language, conduct usability testing with native speakers recruited through our legal aid partner, and build the localization framework into the codebase so that additional languages can be added later at marginal cost. All text-message reminders — both benefits deadlines and court dates — will be delivered in the user's selected language.

**Workstream 3: Expand from 46 to 76 county agencies.** Onboarding a county involves data integration (court calendaring feeds for the reminder tool; renewal-date feeds where available for the screener), staff training, and a support relationship. We will hire two implementation specialists and build a standardized onboarding kit — integration templates for the case-management systems most common in our region, a training curriculum, and a self-service configuration portal — to cut average onboarding time from the current eleven weeks to six. Expansion targets are set at ten counties per year, prioritized by a published rubric weighing population in poverty, current failure-to-appear rates, county readiness, and geographic equity across the four states. We address the February withdrawal of the Nebraska Department of Health and Human Services from a planned pilot directly in the Organizational Capacity section; for planning purposes, our Nebraska expansion in Year 1 proceeds through county courts and legal aid channels, which do not depend on the state agency, while we rebuild the state-level relationship.

**Workstream 4: Stand up a paid support tier.** The software remains free and open source. What counties and legal aid organizations have told us they will pay for is guaranteed support: service-level commitments on uptime and response time, priority onboarding, custom integration work, and hosted infrastructure for agencies that do not want to run their own. We will formalize this into a tiered offering — a hosted-plus-support tier priced for county budgets and a premium integration tier for state agencies and larger organizations — with pricing validated against county procurement thresholds so that most counties can purchase without a competitive bid process. This workstream is the backbone of the sustainability plan described later in this narrative.

**Partners.** Three partners carry defined roles with subawarded or contracted budgets. Kansas Legal Services will lead user testing, recruiting testers from its client base for both the renewal workflow and the localized versions, and will run quarterly structured feedback panels. The Office of Judicial Administration will supply hearing and disposition data under an existing data-sharing agreement, extended for this project, which is what makes both the reminder tool and its evaluation possible. The public policy center at the University of Kansas, led by Dr. Ramona Chee, will serve as independent evaluator, as described in the Evaluation Plan. Dr. Chee's team designed the difference-in-differences analysis behind our original failure-to-appear findings and will apply the same rigor here — importantly, as a party independent of our own outcome reporting.

**Timeline.** Year 1: screener rebuild in production by month 9; Spanish version by month 12; first 10 counties onboarded; support tier launched with 5 charter customers. Year 2: Vietnamese version by month 18; 10 additional counties; support tier reaches 20 paying agencies; midpoint independent evaluation delivered. Year 3: final 10 counties; support-tier revenue reaches sustainability targets; final evaluation and open-source documentation release so other regions can replicate the stack.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

The objectives below are stated against the Fund's required reporting measures — counties onboarded, screenings completed, applications submitted and approved, uptime, failure-to-appear rate, and cost per screening — plus objectives specific to this project's language-access and sustainability aims. Baselines are as of December 31, 2025, and each objective names its data source, because our credibility on measurement is something we intend to demonstrate rather than assert.

**Goal 1: Reduce procedural loss of benefits by rebuilding the screener around renewals and recertification.**

- **Objective 1.1:** Complete 300,000 screenings in Year 1, 380,000 in Year 2, and 450,000 in Year 3 (baseline: 218,000). *Source: application analytics, de-duplicated by session.*
- **Objective 1.2:** Of renewal-workflow users, 65 percent generate a completed document checklist, and 45 percent report submitting their renewal on time, rising to 55 percent by Year 3. *Source: in-tool completion data and opt-in follow-up text survey; submission and approval figures verified against county agency data where sharing agreements permit, with the verification method disclosed in each report.*
- **Objective 1.3:** Track applications submitted and approved with the assistance of the tool: 40,000 submitted and 28,000 approved by end of Year 3, cumulative. *Source: county partner data and user-reported outcomes, reported separately and never combined without labeling.*

**Goal 2: Close the language access gap.**

- **Objective 2.1:** Launch the Spanish version by month 12 and the Vietnamese version by month 18, each validated through at least 40 moderated usability sessions with native speakers before public release. *Source: testing records maintained by Kansas Legal Services.*
- **Objective 2.2:** By end of Year 3, 12 percent of all screenings occur in Spanish or Vietnamese. *Source: application analytics.*

**Goal 3: Expand reach and reliability of the court reminder tool.**

- **Objective 3.1:** Onboard 10 new county agencies per year, for a total of 76 by end of Year 3 (baseline: 46). *Source: signed onboarding agreements and live data integrations.*
- **Objective 3.2:** Reduce the failure-to-appear rate in newly onboarded counties from their documented baselines to at or below 12 percent within 12 months of go-live, benchmarked against the 22-to-9-percent result from our original three-county pilot. *Source: Office of Judicial Administration hearing data, analyzed independently by the KU policy center.*
- **Objective 3.3:** Maintain 99.9 percent uptime for all hosted services, measured by third-party monitoring and reported monthly. *Source: external uptime monitoring service; raw logs available to the Foundation on request.*

**Goal 4: Achieve financial and operational sustainability.**

- **Objective 4.1:** Reduce cost per screening from the current $8.10 to under $5.00 by end of Year 3, calculated as total program expenses attributable to the screener divided by completed screenings. *Source: audited financials mapped to program allocations.*
- **Objective 4.2:** Grow earned revenue from $190,000 to $1,100,000 by end of Year 3, per the plan in the Sustainability section, with quarterly milestones: $340,000 in Year 1, $680,000 in Year 2, $1,100,000 in Year 3. *Source: audited revenue statements.*
- **Objective 4.3:** Convert the interim chief technology officer role to a permanent appointment within six months of the grant start, and maintain fully staffed leadership across all four workstreams thereafter. *Source: board minutes and organizational chart, provided in each report.*

**Reporting commitment.** We will submit every required report at least five business days before its deadline, and every reported outcome figure will carry a stated data source and calculation method. Any restatement of a previously reported figure will be disclosed proactively, in writing, with an explanation, before the next scheduled report. The internal controls behind this commitment are described under Organizational Capacity.

## Evaluation Plan

Evaluation for this project is designed around a principle we learned the hard way: outcome claims must be independently verifiable, produced by documented methods, and separated from the organization's own advocacy. The evaluation has three layers.

**Layer 1: Continuous performance monitoring (internal, automated).** Our data infrastructure will produce a monthly operational dashboard covering all six of the Fund's required reporting measures: counties onboarded, screenings completed, applications submitted and approved, uptime, failure-to-appear rates by county, and cost per screening. Every metric on the dashboard is generated by a documented, version-controlled query; when a definition or method changes, the change is logged and disclosed. The Foundation will receive read access to this dashboard, not just quarterly snapshots. We are deliberately choosing transparency over presentation: the Foundation will see the same numbers we see, at the same time.

**Layer 2: Independent outcome evaluation (external, led by the KU policy center).** Dr. Ramona Chee's team will conduct the outcome evaluation under a contract that gives the center full access to underlying data and full publication rights, including the right to publish findings unfavorable to us. Two studies anchor this layer:

*Failure-to-appear study.* Using hearing-level data from the Office of Judicial Administration, the center will run a staggered difference-in-differences analysis comparing failure-to-appear rates in onboarded counties against matched non-onboarded counties, exploiting the phased rollout of ten counties per year as a natural stepped-wedge design. This replicates and extends the methodology behind the original 22-to-9-percent pilot finding, now at ten times the scale. The design will pre-register hypotheses and primary outcomes before Year 1 rollout.

*Renewal outcomes study.* For the rebuilt screener, the center will evaluate whether tool users are more likely to complete Medicaid renewals and SNAP recertifications on time, using county-level administrative data where sharing agreements permit and a matched-survey approach elsewhere. Because random assignment of a free public tool raises ethical and practical problems, the center will use propensity-matched comparison and will state the limits of causal inference plainly in its reports — we would rather report honest, appropriately hedged findings than overstated ones.

**Layer 3: User-centered evaluation (led by Kansas Legal Services).** Quarterly structured feedback panels with users — including dedicated Spanish-language and Vietnamese-language panels once localized versions launch — will assess comprehension, trust, and usability. Findings feed directly into development sprints, and panel summaries will be included in annual reports to the Foundation.

**Reporting cadence and quality control.** Quarterly reports to the Foundation will combine the operational dashboard, narrative progress against each objective, and a variance analysis explaining any objective tracking more than 10 percent off target — with a corrective plan, not just an explanation. Annual reports incorporate the independent evaluation's interim findings. Before submission, every report passes a two-person review: the Finance Director verifies all financial and cost-per-screening figures against the general ledger, and the evaluation lead verifies all outcome figures against source data. This review is documented and the sign-off sheet accompanies each report. The final evaluation, delivered in month 34, will include a public replication package — anonymized data structures, analysis code, and integration documentation — so the findings and the tools are usable beyond our region.

## Organizational Capacity

Sunflower Civic Code is a 501(c)(3) organization based in Lawrence, Kansas, with 24 staff and a $4.3 million annual budget. Our tools are used by 46 county agencies and seven legal aid organizations across four states; we ran 218,000 benefits screenings last year; and our court reminder tool, across 14,000 hearings in three pilot counties, cut failure-to-appear from 22 percent to 9 percent. Our engineering team of nine maintains the full stack in the open, our implementation team manages county relationships, and our operations team supports a user base whose needs — mobile-first, low-bandwidth, plain-language — shape every design decision.

We also owe this Foundation a direct accounting of three matters, because a capacity section that omitted them would not be credible, and because how an organization handles its failures says more about its capacity than how it describes its strengths.

**Our reporting history with the Ardith Lammers Foundation.** In 2024, the Foundation declined to renew a $900,000 grant to us after we missed two reporting deadlines and restated previously reported outcome numbers. Those failures were ours. The root causes were specific: outcome data was assembled manually from county spreadsheets by program staff without independent verification, reporting responsibilities were split across three people with no single owner, and when a data-definition error was discovered — screenings had been counted by page view in one quarter and by completed session in another, inflating the figure — we corrected it in a restatement rather than catching it before it was ever reported. We do not characterize this as a misunderstanding or a staffing accident. It was a controls failure, and we treated it as one.

Since then, we have made four structural changes. First, all grant reporting now runs through a single owner — the Finance Director, Hannah Steinmetz — with a reporting calendar reviewed monthly by the executive team and quarterly by the board's audit committee, and internal deadlines set ten business days ahead of funder deadlines. Second, every outcome metric now has a written definition and a version-controlled automated query; the manual spreadsheet pipeline that produced the 2024 restatement no longer exists. Third, we instituted the two-person sign-off described in the Evaluation Plan, separating the people who produce numbers from the people who verify them. Fourth, the board amended its charter to require that any restatement of previously reported figures be disclosed to the affected funder in writing before the next scheduled report, with a root-cause memo. Since these changes took effect in early 2025, we have submitted 14 reports to six funders, all on time, with zero restatements. We recognize that returning to this Foundation carries a higher burden of proof, and we have designed this application to meet it: the read-access dashboard, the independent evaluator with publication rights, and the documented reporting controls all exist so that the Foundation never again has to take our numbers on faith.

**The chief technology officer transition.** Our CTO departed in November 2025, and Zsofia Balogh, previously our lead engineer, has served as interim CTO since. We want to be precise about what this transition does and does not mean for execution risk. Ms. Balogh has been with the organization for five years, wrote substantial portions of both the screener and the reminder tool, led the court data integrations behind the failure-to-appear results, and has managed the engineering team without attrition or missed releases during her five months as interim — including shipping two county integrations and a major reliability upgrade in that period. The technical roadmap in this application is hers. The board opened a national search in January 2026 with an executive search firm experienced in nonprofit technology leadership; Ms. Balogh is a candidate, and the board expects a permanent appointment by the end of the third quarter of 2026 — within the six-month window committed to in Objective 4.3. In the interim, technical continuity does not rest on one person: architecture decisions are documented in public design records, the codebase is open source with distributed maintainer knowledge, and two senior engineers share deployment and incident-response authority. The realistic risk is not project failure but slowed hiring in Workstream 3 if the search runs long; our Year 1 timeline holds the two implementation-specialist hires until month 3 partly for this reason.

**The Nebraska withdrawal.** In February 2026, the Nebraska Department of Health and Human Services withdrew from a planned benefits-screening pilot. The department cited a statewide procurement and data-governance review that paused all new external data integrations, not performance concerns with our organization — and we can provide the department's correspondence to the Foundation. The practical effect is that state-level renewal-date data in Nebraska is unavailable for now. Our mitigation is structural rather than hopeful: the reminder tool in Nebraska runs on county court data through the judicial branch, which is unaffected, and the screener's eligibility and checklist functions do not require state data feeds. Year 1 Nebraska expansion therefore proceeds through county courts and Legal Aid of Nebraska, and our county-selection rubric weights the other three states more heavily until the state relationship is rebuilt. We are maintaining quarterly contact with the department, and if its review concludes favorably, Nebraska renewal integration returns in Year 2; if not, our objectives are achievable without it, and we have budgeted accordingly.

**Leadership and governance.** Executive Director Priscilla Ojeda has led the organization for seven years, through its growth from a three-county project to a four-state platform. Finance Director Hannah Steinmetz, a CPA who joined in 2023, oversees a finance function that has produced clean audits with no material weaknesses in each of the last two years. Our nine-member board includes a former county human services director, a legal aid executive, a retired district court judge, and a public-sector software executive, and its audit committee now reviews all funder reporting — an oversight layer created directly in response to the 2024 failures.

## Sustainability

The central sustainability question for this project — and the one Finance Director Hannah Steinmetz addresses here directly — is how earned revenue grows from $190,000 today to $1,100,000 by the end of Year 3, and why that number is a plan rather than a hope.

**What we sell, and to whom.** The software stays free and open source; that is a mission commitment and, as it happens, our best sales channel, because counties adopt the free tool first and buy support second. Earned revenue comes from the paid support tier in Workstream 4, structured in three products:

1. **Hosted + Support (county tier), $18,000/year.** Fully hosted infrastructure, guaranteed 99.9 percent uptime, priority support with response-time commitments, and included training. Priced below the small-purchase threshold in most county procurement codes in our region, so a county administrator can buy it without a competitive bid — a deliberate design choice validated with the county officials we already serve.
2. **Premium Integration (state agency and large-organization tier), $60,000–$120,000/year.** Custom data integrations, dedicated support, and configuration for statewide deployments.
3. **Implementation and integration services,** billed at cost-recovery rates for one-time custom work.

**The revenue build, year by year.** Current earned revenue of $190,000 comes from ad hoc integration contracts with four agencies — proof that agencies will pay us, but not yet a product. The plan:

- **Year 1 ($340,000):** Convert the four existing contract clients to the formal county tier and sign five charter customers from our current 46 agencies at a discounted charter rate, plus continuing services work. This year is deliberately conservative: nine paying customers out of 56 total agencies is a 16 percent attach rate among agencies that already depend on the tools.
- **Year 2 ($680,000):** Reach 20 county-tier customers ($360,000), sign two premium-tier agreements ($200,000) — our pipeline here includes the Office of Judicial Administration, which has indicated interest in a statewide hosted arrangement, and one Oklahoma state agency — plus $120,000 in services.
- **Year 3 ($1,100,000):** Reach 34 county-tier customers ($612,000) — a 45 percent attach rate across 76 agencies, in line with what comparable open-source civic software organizations achieve at maturity — plus three to four premium agreements ($360,000) and $128,000 in services.

**Why these conversion rates are credible.** Three reasons. First, we surveyed our current 46 agencies in late 2025: 31 said they would consider paid hosting and support at the price points above, and 11 said they would purchase in the next budget cycle. Second, the counties onboarded under this grant enter through the standardized onboarding process, which includes the support-tier offer from day one, so new counties convert at higher rates than legacy ones. Third, the alternative for these agencies is commercial court-notification and screening vendors whose regional pricing runs two to five times our county tier; we are not asking counties to find new money so much as to redirect less of it.

**What happens if we fall short.** The support tier at $1.1 million, combined with a diversified grant base (no single funder exceeds 30 percent of revenue in our Year 3 budget), covers core engineering, hosting, and support operations without further capital grants. If earned revenue reaches only 70 percent of target, core operations remain funded and the gap falls on the pace of new-county expansion — a growth slowdown, not an existential risk. Quarterly earned-revenue milestones are built into Objective 4.2 precisely so that the Foundation sees any shortfall early, alongside our corrective plan.

**Sustainability beyond revenue.** The code, the eligibility rules engine, the localization framework, and the integration templates are all published openly, so the public value of this investment does not depend on our organization's survival. The final-year replication package makes the full stack adoptable by civic technology organizations in other regions. And the independent evaluation record — whatever it shows — becomes part of the public evidence base on court reminders and renewal support, which outlasts any single grant.

## Budget Narrative

The total request is $3,100,000 over three years: $1,180,000 in Year 1, $1,040,000 in Year 2, and $880,000 in Year 3. The declining profile is deliberate — grant funds front-load the rebuild and expansion while earned revenue ramps to absorb recurring costs, so the grant buys durable capacity rather than a temporary operating subsidy.

**Personnel ($1,860,000; 60 percent).** Grant funds support the positions that execute the four workstreams, at allocations reflecting actual project time. Engineering: three engineer positions fully allocated to the screener rebuild and localization framework in Years 1–2, tapering to two positions in Year 3 as the rebuild moves to maintenance ($780,000 with benefits at 24 percent). Implementation: two new implementation specialists, hired in month 3 of Year 1, fully grant-funded in Years 1–2 and 50 percent grant-funded in Year 3 as support-tier revenue picks up their cost ($430,000) — this handoff is itself a sustainability test, visible in our financial reports. Leadership and management: 25 percent of the CTO role (permanent, once appointed), 20 percent of the Executive Director, and 30 percent of a program manager dedicated to grant deliverables and partner coordination ($410,000). Data and evaluation support: one data engineer at 50 percent, responsible for the automated metrics pipeline and the read-access dashboard — a position that exists in this budget specifically because of the reporting controls described above ($240,000).

**Partner subawards and contracts ($480,000; 15 percent).** University of Kansas policy center, $300,000 over three years for the independent evaluation, under a contract guaranteeing data access and publication independence. Kansas Legal Services, $120,000 for user-testing coordination, tester compensation, and quarterly feedback panels. Localization contractors, $60,000 for community-based Spanish and Vietnamese translation and review, including compensated review sessions with native-speaker panels. The Office of Judicial Administration's data provision is covered under our existing agreement and requires no grant funds.

**Technology and infrastructure ($370,000; 12 percent).** Hosting and infrastructure scaled for growth from 218,000 to 450,000 annual screenings and from 46 to 76 counties ($210,000 across three years, declining as support-tier revenue absorbs hosting for paying customers). Text-message delivery costs for benefits and court reminders in three languages ($90,000, at volume rates). Third-party uptime monitoring, security audits of the rebuilt screener before launch, and penetration testing ($70,000) — non-negotiable items for a tool handling benefits-related information for low-income users.

**Travel and county onboarding ($130,000; 4 percent).** On-site onboarding, training, and relationship management across a four-state rural service area at ten new counties per year. Budgeted at federal per diem rates; most training moves to remote delivery after initial on-site sessions, which is reflected in the declining annual figure.

**Other direct costs ($105,000; 3 percent).** Executive search costs for the permanent CTO appointment ($35,000, Year 1 only), reporting and audit costs attributable to the project ($40,000, including the annual independent audit's project allocation), and convening costs for an annual partner summit bringing county, court, and legal aid partners together ($30,000).

**Indirect costs ($155,000; 5 percent).** We apply a 5 percent indirect rate against direct costs, below our federally negotiated rate of 14 percent; the difference is absorbed as organizational cost share, which — together with staff time contributed by the Office of Judicial Administration — represents a total match of approximately $310,000, or 10 percent of the request.

**Cost per screening.** At full Year 3 volume, total screener-attributable costs against 450,000 screenings yield a cost per screening under $5.00, down from $8.10 today — the Fund's efficiency measure, tracked quarterly and calculated by a documented method included in every report.

**Financial controls.** Grant funds will be tracked in a dedicated fund with monthly budget-to-actual review by the Finance Director, quarterly review by the board audit committee, and full financial reporting on the Foundation's schedule — submitted, as with all reporting under this grant, ahead of deadline. Our last two audits were clean with no material weaknesses, and our auditors' reports are available to the Foundation on request.

Sunflower Civic Code is asking this Foundation to reinvest in an organization it declined to renew two years ago. We have not written around that fact; we have written through it, because the same discipline that rebuilt our reporting controls is the discipline this project's measurement plan is built on. The tools work — a 59 percent reduction in failure to appear across 14,000 hearings, 218,000 screenings for households earning a median of $27,400 — and the need at renewal time is urgent and growing. What this grant buys is the rebuilt product, thirty more counties, two more languages, an independent evidence base, and an earned-revenue engine that ends the project standing on its own. We welcome the Foundation's scrutiny at every step, and we have designed this project so that scrutiny is easy.
