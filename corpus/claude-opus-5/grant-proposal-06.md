## Statement of Need

Pine Bluff, Arkansas sits in a county where 21.4 percent of residents are food insecure. The state rate is 16.6 percent; the national rate is 13.5 percent. Jefferson County has lost population every year since 1990 and has lost supermarkets faster than it has lost people. When the regional chain closed its 28,000-square-foot store in 2018, it took with it the last full-line grocery in a census tract of 12,400 residents — the same tract this application proposes to serve.

The six-tract service area PBCGC defines as its primary market contains approximately 28,600 people. Seventy-six percent are Black. Thirty-one percent live below the federal poverty line. Twenty-two percent of households have no vehicle. Median household income in the target tract is $28,400, and 41 percent of households there receive SNAP. These are not overlapping abstractions; they compound. A household without a car in a tract without a grocery store is not shopping four miles away. It is shopping at one of the two dollar stores or four convenience stores inside the tract, none of which stocks fresh produce, none of which stocks fresh meat, and all of which price shelf-stable staples 18 to 34 percent above what PBCGC charges for the same items — a comparison the co-op has documented quarterly since 2023 using a 42-item market basket.

The alternative is transit. The nearest full-line grocery is 4.7 miles from the center of the target tract. By bus that is a 38-minute one-way trip with one transfer, on a route that runs hourly and does not run on Sundays. A shopper making that trip cannot buy frozen goods in July, cannot buy more than she can carry, and spends better than two hours on a routine errand. The predictable result is high-frequency, low-volume purchasing of shelf-stable calories, which is exactly the pattern the county's health data reflects. Adult hypertension prevalence in the service area is 34 percent. County diabetes prevalence is 19 percent against a national figure of 11.6 percent. Our hospital partner has documented 2,340 emergency department visits over 24 months originating in the target tract with a diet-related primary or secondary diagnosis.

There is also a supply side to this need, and it is the reason PBCGC believes a cooperative rather than a charitable model is the right instrument. Jefferson County and the four counties adjacent to it contain a substantial population of small farms — the farmers alliance partnering on this project has 61 members, 78 percent of whom farm fewer than 80 acres and 23 of whom are Black-owned operations. Thirty-nine of those 61 farms have no cold storage of any kind. Post-harvest loss among alliance members is estimated at 22 to 28 percent. These farms are within 45 minutes of a food-insecure population of 28,600 people and they cannot reliably move product to it, because there is no aggregation point, no cold chain, and no buyer with the volume to justify a farm-direct route. PBCGC bought $312,000 from 24 area farms last year — an average of $13,000 per farm. That is meaningful supplemental income for a small operation and it is nowhere near what the ground could support.

PBCGC has already demonstrated demand in this market. The 12,000-square-foot store opened in 2022 did $4.9 million in FY26, up 18 percent over the prior year, with 1,840 member-owners and 31 employees. SNAP redemption reached $610,000 — 12.4 percent of sales, against a national grocery average near 5 percent — and the co-op's produce incentive program matched an additional $88,000 in purchases. That $698,000 in combined federal nutrition spending stayed in Pine Bluff, recirculated through 31 payrolls and 24 farm accounts, rather than leaving for a chain's regional distribution center. The store serves roughly 4,600 unique households. It is at 92 percent of the customer count our layout and checkout capacity can absorb at peak, and it draws 31 percent of its traffic from outside the immediate neighborhood, including from the target tract — people who are already driving or riding past two dollar stores to reach us.

What the co-op cannot do from one store is reach the people who cannot get to it. The unmet need this application addresses is specific: 12,400 residents in a tract with no full-line grocery since 2018; approximately 4,900 residents across six rural communities 9 to 31 miles out, whose nearest grocery is 11 to 24 miles away; and 61 farms with no cold chain to any of them.

## Project Description

PBCGC proposes a three-part expansion that converts a single-store retailer into a small distributed food system with one hub and three points of delivery. We describe it as an enterprise model deliberately: every component is designed to generate earned revenue, and the grant and investment are structured to buy the assets and absorb the ramp, not to subsidize operations in perpetuity.

**Component 1 — Second Store (9,600 sq ft, target tract).** PBCGC holds a signed letter of intent on a 9,600-square-foot vacant retail box within the target tract, 0.6 miles from the site of the 2018 supermarket closure, with 71 parking spaces, two loading positions, and existing three-phase power. The lease is 15 years with two five-year options, at a base rent of $6.75/sf triple net with the first nine months abated — terms the landlord agreed to in exchange for tenant-funded roof and HVAC replacement, which is budgeted here. The store will carry a full assortment: produce, meat and seafood, dairy, frozen, full center store, bulk, and a small prepared-foods program. It will operate 12 checkstands' worth of throughput across four staffed lanes and two self-checkouts, accept SNAP and WIC from day one, and run the same produce incentive as Store 1. Projected mature sales are $3.86 million, or roughly $402 per square foot, which is the same productivity Store 1 currently achieves — we have not assumed the new store outperforms the store we already operate.

**Component 2 — Shared Refrigerated Warehouse (6,000 sq ft).** A leased 6,000-square-foot building 2.1 miles from Store 1 will be built out with three temperature zones (34°F, 50°F, ambient), two dock doors with levelers, a blast chiller, pallet racking, and a forklift. It is the hub. It will receive from farms, cross-dock to both stores and the mobile market, and — critically — offer paid storage and cross-dock service to farmers alliance members selling to buyers other than PBCGC, including school districts and the hospital's food service contractor. This is what turns 24 farm accounts into 40 and $312,000 in farm purchases into more than $1 million. The warehouse charges a 6 percent cross-dock fee on invoice value plus monthly storage rental, and is projected to cover its own operating cost in Year 3.

**Component 3 — Mobile Market (six rural sites).** A 24-foot refrigerated box truck, outfitted with reach-in coolers, dry shelving, an awning, a wireless EBT terminal, and a produce-prescription card reader, will run a weekly circuit to six rural sites across four counties. Sites will be co-selected with the county extension office and the farmers alliance based on distance to nearest grocery, transit availability, and the presence of an anchor host (three churches, one senior center, one clinic, one community center have given preliminary commitments). The mobile market carries approximately 240 SKUs weighted toward produce, protein, dairy, and staples, and prices at Store 1 shelf price with no distance markup.

**Enterprise model and cooperative structure.** PBCGC is a consumer cooperative owned by its shoppers. A share is $100, payable at $10 per month, and $25 for SNAP-participating households. Membership is never required to shop, and no member price tier exists — the co-op competes on absolute price, not on a members-only discount, because a two-tier price in a 31-percent-poverty market simply excludes people. Members elect a nine-seat board, receive patronage allocations in profitable years, and vote one member, one vote. Membership stands at 1,840 and is projected to reach 3,400 by Year 4, which itself contributes approximately $156,000 in additional paid-in equity beyond the capital campaign described in the budget narrative.

The economic logic of the expansion is scale against a fixed overhead base. Store 1 currently absorbs 100 percent of central costs — bookkeeping, IT, insurance administration, marketing, GM salary. Adding a second store and a warehouse spreads that base across roughly 2.1 times the volume, moves the co-op from the sub-$5 million tier to the $10 million tier of our primary wholesaler's volume discount schedule (a 1.4-point improvement in cost of goods on roughly 62 percent of purchases), and makes farm-direct sourcing economical at case volumes small farms can actually fill. Consolidated sales are projected to move from $4.9 million (FY26) to $10.34 million (FY31), with consolidated operating margin moving from 1.2 percent to 2.2 percent over the same period despite absorbing a full second-store opening.

**Partners.** Four partners hold defined, contracted roles rather than letters of general support:

- **The regional hospital system** will operate the produce prescription referral pipeline. Clinicians in its family medicine, endocrinology, and cardiology practices will enroll patients with diet-related diagnoses in a six-month benefit of $60 per month, redeemable at both stores and the mobile market. The hospital funds the benefit dollars from its community benefit budget and pays PBCGC a per-participant administration fee. Enrollment targets are 400 households in Year 2, 700 in Year 3, and 900 in Year 4. A HIPAA business associate agreement and data-sharing protocol are executed and attached.
- **The farmers alliance** (61 members) will serve as the aggregation counterparty for the warehouse, hold two non-voting seats on a joint sourcing committee, coordinate crop planning against PBCGC's forward commitments, and administer a GAP-certification cost-share for members selling into the warehouse.
- **The community development bank**, represented by loan officer Nikhil Sundaram, has issued a conditional commitment for a $600,000 term loan at 5.5 percent, 15-year amortization with a seven-year balloon, secured by store equipment, contingent on this award. The bank underwrote our FY24–FY26 statements and the Store 2 pro forma, and will hold a 1.25× debt service coverage covenant tested annually beginning FY31.
- **The county extension office** will co-select and staff mobile market sites, deliver nutrition education at all six stops and both stores, provide production and food-safety technical assistance to alliance farms selling into the warehouse, and — with the 1890 land-grant university in Pine Bluff — administer the food security survey described in the Evaluation Plan.

**Sequence.** Months 1–3: loan closings, lease execution, architect engaged. Months 4–9: warehouse build-out; Food Access Coordinator (month 4) and Warehouse Coordinator (month 6) hired; warehouse opens month 9. Months 10–12: mobile market vehicle delivered and outfitted; Mobile Market Coordinator hired month 8; mobile market launches month 12 at three sites. Month 13: Store 2 construction begins; Store 2 Manager hired. Month 15: mobile market at all six sites. Months 16–17: 22 Store 2 positions hired and trained, including four weeks of cross-training at Store 1. **Month 18 (September 2028): Store 2 opens**, with produce prescription enrollment beginning the same month. Months 18–48: operations, evaluation waves, and reporting.

## Evaluation Plan

**Design.** The evaluation uses a quasi-experimental pre/post design with a matched comparison group. The treatment panel comprises 450 households recruited from the target tract and the six rural mobile market sites. The comparison panel comprises 300 households recruited from a demographically matched census tract in an adjacent county that will not receive a store, mobile market stop, or produce prescription during the project period, matched on poverty rate, racial composition, vehicle access, and distance to nearest full-line grocery. Recruitment for both panels occurs in months 18–20, before treatment households have had meaningful exposure to Store 2. Target retention is 70 percent at 24 months; we will over-recruit by 15 percent against attrition and will pay a $25 incentive per completed wave.

**Primary outcome instrument.** The USDA Six-Item Short Form Food Security Module, administered at baseline (months 18–20), **12 months** (months 30–32), and **24 months** (months 42–44). Administration is by trained enumerators from the county extension office and the 1890 land-grant university, in person at the stores, at mobile market stops, and by telephone for non-responders. Scores will be reported both as the categorical classification (high/marginal, low, very low) and as the raw affirmative-response count, so that within-category movement is visible.

**Administrative and transactional data.** The evaluation does not rest on survey data alone. The following are captured continuously from operating systems:

| Indicator | Source | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Households served | POS loyalty/member records, EBT card tokens, mobile market accounts, deduplicated | Monthly |
| SNAP redemption | EBT settlement reports by store and by mobile market terminal | Monthly |
| Produce incentive redemption | Incentive tender type at POS | Monthly |
| Produce prescription redemption | Benefit card processor reports; hospital referral roster | Monthly |
| Farm sales to the co-op | Accounts payable ledger, coded by vendor and farm ID | Monthly |
| Third-party warehouse throughput | Cross-dock invoicing | Monthly |
| Store-level operating margin | 13-period store P&Ls with defined overhead allocation | Every 4 weeks |
| Basket composition and produce share | Scan-level category data | Monthly |
| Price comparison | 42-item market basket vs. four in-tract competitors | Quarterly |

**Secondary analyses.** With hospital consent and under the executed business associate agreement, the hospital will provide de-identified, aggregated utilization data for produce prescription enrollees — emergency department visits, HbA1c where drawn, and blood pressure readings — at enrollment and at six and twelve months post-enrollment, compared against a matched non-enrolled panel drawn from the hospital's own patient population. This analysis is exploratory; PBCGC will not represent it as causal and will report it separately from the primary food security findings.

**Independence and governance.** The 1890 land-grant university's community health research group will serve as third-party evaluator under a subcontract, with responsibility for instrument administration, data cleaning, analysis, and authorship of the 12-month and 24-month reports. Human subjects review will be obtained through the university's IRB before recruitment. PBCGC will hold no editorial control over the evaluator's findings; the subcontract expressly reserves the evaluator's right to publish. A three-person evaluation advisory group — one extension agent, one alliance farmer, one board-appointed member-owner from the target tract — will review instruments and reports before release.

**Reporting to the Foundation.** Quarterly dashboards on all five Foundation indicators within 30 days of period close, including a variance narrative from Finance Manager Lorena Escamilla whenever an indicator falls more than 10 percent below target. Annual narrative and financial reports within 60 days of fiscal year end, with audited statements. Dedicated food security reports at the 12-month and 24-month waves. A final evaluation report at month 48. Where results are behind plan, we will say so in the quarter it occurs — a practice we adopted after 2021 and describe in the next section.

**Evaluation budget.** $92,000 over four years, 2.6 percent of total project cost, of which $58,000 is the university subcontract, $19,000 is participant incentives, and $15,000 is POS reporting integration and dashboard development.

## Organizational Capacity

**The prior closure.** PBCGC's first store opened in April 2019 in this same neighborhood and closed in February 2021, after 22 months, owing $340,000 to 31 vendors. Any assessment of this application should begin there, so we will.

The store failed for reasons that were structural, identifiable at the time, and are documented in the post-mortem the board commissioned in 2021 and attached here in full:

1. **Undercapitalization.** The store opened with $410,000 raised and $22,000 in working capital — 5.3 percent of first-year sales, against an industry benchmark of 12 to 15 percent. It had no capacity to absorb a bad quarter, and it had several.
2. **Gross margin set without costing.** Prices were set by a volunteer committee. Realized gross margin was 26.4 percent against a cost structure that required better than 32 percent. The store sold more each month and lost more each month.
3. **No inventory control.** There was no scan-level POS and no perpetual inventory. Shrink ran 6.9 percent of sales and was not detected for eleven months.
4. **Governance failure.** There was no professional general manager. An eleven-member board directed operations directly; four different people had purchasing authority; no one owned the P&L.
5. **Supply.** Sixty percent of center store came in through direct-store-delivery at full jobber pricing. A primary wholesaler contract was not signed until month 14.
6. **COVID-19**, which arrived in month 12 and compressed the remaining ten months. There was no e-commerce, no curbside, and no staffing depth.

COVID is last on that list because it belongs last. The store was insolvent on a run-rate basis by month nine.

What the co-op did next is the part we ask the Foundation to weigh. The board voted against bankruptcy. It negotiated forbearance and repayment agreements with 28 of 31 vendors, and paid the other three in full immediately. **All $340,000 was repaid over 34 months, with the final payment made in November 2023.** No vendor took a write-off. Nineteen of those 31 vendors supply Store 1 today, including our primary wholesaler, and three have provided letters attached to this application. The repayment is visible in the audited statements for FY22, FY23, and FY24, all of which carry unmodified opinions.

**What changed before we opened again.** The 2022 store was not a second attempt at the same thing.

- **Professional management.** Bernadette Okafor was hired as General Manager in 2021 after fourteen years in cooperative grocery, most recently as operations director for a three-store cooperative in Memphis. She opened Store 1 four days ahead of schedule and $31,000 under budget. Her contract runs through 2030.
- **Professional finance.** Lorena Escamilla was hired as Finance Manager in 2022. The co-op closes its books by day twelve, reports on a 13-period calendar, and produces department-level gross margin and labor-to-sales reporting every four weeks.
- **Governance reform.** The board was reduced from eleven seats to nine and adopted a policy governance model in 2022. The GM reports to the board as a body; individual directors have no operational authority. All nine directors, including Board President Curtis Yamashita, have completed the national cooperative association's director training, most recently in 2024.
- **Systems.** Scan-level POS with perpetual inventory, cycle counting, and category-level margin targets. FY26 shrink was 2.3 percent.
- **Supply.** A full-line primary wholesaler contract with volume-tier pricing. DSD reduced to 18 percent of center store.
- **Policy.** A board-adopted working capital policy requiring a minimum of 45 days' cost of goods in liquid reserve, and a capital replacement reserve funded monthly.

**Current performance, stated plainly.** Store 1 did $4.9 million in FY26 at a 1.2 percent operating margin, and **lost money in two of the last four quarters.** Q1 was +2.1 percent and Q3 was +3.0 percent. Q2 was −0.4 percent: a compressor failure on the produce rack cost $41,000 in equipment and product, and a nine-week road closure on the primary approach cut traffic 11 percent. Q4 was −0.9 percent: a $58,000 write-off of remaining capitalized pre-opening costs, four days closed in a January ice storm, and a 3.1 percent wage adjustment we implemented two quarters ahead of budget because we were losing produce staff to a distribution center.

We are not going to characterize a 1.2 percent margin as strong. It is thin, it is below the 2.5 to 3.5 percent a store this size should produce, and two of the four causes above were avoidable. The corrective actions are in place: a preventive maintenance contract on all refrigeration, an equipment reserve now funded at $2,100 per month rising to $4,000 in FY29, a labor scheduling target of 20.5 percent of sales enforced weekly, and no remaining capitalized pre-opening costs on the balance sheet. FY27 is tracking to 1.7 percent through three quarters. Store 1 is projected at 2.0 percent in FY28 before Store 2 opens, and the Foundation should hold us to that number as a precondition — we would accept a disbursement milestone tied to it.

**Capacity to execute this project.** Thirty-one employees today, 24 FTE. Okafor commits 0.35 FTE and Escamilla 0.30 FTE to the project, backfilled through the budget. Four new positions carry the expansion: Food Access Coordinator (month 4), Warehouse Coordinator (month 6), Mobile Market Coordinator (month 8), Store 2 Manager (month 13, recruited nationally with a nine-month runway). Twenty-two Store 2 positions, 17 FTE, start at $15.75 with cross-training at Store 1 before opening. Total employment reaches 62 by Year 4. The co-op carries $2M/$4M general liability, product liability, and employment practices coverage; has completed a full financial audit each year since FY23; and has no unresolved audit findings, no delinquent tax filings, and no litigation pending or threatened.

## Sustainability

**When the second store carries itself.** This is the question the Foundation should ask hardest, so we answer it precisely, in four stages.

| Milestone | Store 2 month | Calendar | Definition |
|---|---|---|---|
| First positive operating month | Month 19 | March 2030 | Sales less direct COGS, labor, occupancy, and direct operating expense > 0 |
| Sustained operating breakeven | Month 22 | June 2030 | Four consecutive positive months |
| Fully allocated breakeven | Month 26 | October 2030 | Above, plus allocated share of central overhead |
| **Full self-sufficiency** | **Month 33** | **May 2031** | **Above, plus 100% of monthly debt service on the PRI and the bank loan** |

Store 2 reaches a **+1.3 percent store-level operating margin in FY31, the final year of the grant term**, on $3.58 million in sales. It reaches full self-sufficiency including debt service two months after the grant term ends, and **FY32 is its first complete fiscal year of standalone viability**: $118,000 in operating income against $82,630 in allocated debt service, a coverage ratio of 1.43×.

| Store 2 | FY29 (7 mo) | FY30 | FY31 | FY32 | FY33 | FY34 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sales | $1,640,000 | $3,120,000 | $3,580,000 | $3,860,000 | $4,190,000 | $4,410,000 |
| Store-level operating income | $(281,000) | $(104,000) | $47,000 | $118,000 | $176,000 | $221,000 |
| Operating margin | (17.1%) | (3.3%) | 1.3% | 3.1% | 4.2% | 5.0% |
| Allocated debt service | $32,500 | $74,830 | $82,630 | $82,630 | $154,970 | $154,970 |
| Coverage | — | — | 0.57× | **1.43×** | 1.14× | 1.43× |

Cumulative store-level operating losses through breakeven total $385,000. The $340,000 working capital reserve capitalized in this budget covers 88 percent of that; the remaining $45,000 is absorbed by consolidated operations. We have sized the reserve to the loss we actually project rather than to a round number.

**Debt service on the investment.** The $800,000 program-related investment is modeled at 2 percent over a 15-year term with **five years interest-only ($16,000 annually) followed by ten years of amortization at $88,340 annually**, retiring in FY42. Total interest across the life of the instrument is approximately $183,000. We request this structure specifically: a PRI that amortizes during the ramp would not be patient capital, and the five-year interest-only period is what allows Store 2 to reach fully allocated breakeven before principal begins. The community development bank's $600,000 loan at 5.5 percent runs twelve months interest-only, then 15-year amortization at $58,830 annually with a balloon at year seven. Member preferred shares of $260,000 carry a 3 percent non-cumulative dividend, $7,800 annually, payable only in years when consolidated operating margin exceeds 1.5 percent — a term the capital campaign subscribers accepted explicitly.

Consolidated debt service is $32,500 in FY29, $74,830 in FY30, and $82,630 in FY31 and FY32, rising to $154,970 in FY33 when PRI amortization begins. Consolidated coverage is 0.78× in FY30 — the trough, funded from the working capital reserve — then **2.75× in FY31, 3.93× in FY32, and 2.41× in FY33**, comfortably above the 1.25× covenant the bank will hold.

**The balloon.** The bank's seven-year balloon falls in FY36, at which point roughly $412,000 will be outstanding against equipment with meaningful remaining life and a co-op projected at $11 million in sales with a five-year margin history. Nikhil Sundaram has indicated the bank's normal practice is to refinance performing credits at maturity, and PBCGC will additionally begin accruing a refinance reserve of $1,500 per month beginning FY32. If refinance is unavailable, the reserve plus retained surplus covers a paydown to a level the co-op can amortize on its own balance sheet.

**Sustaining the non-store components.** The warehouse reaches operating self-sufficiency in FY30: $63,600 in cross-dock fees on projected throughput plus $18,000 in third-party storage rental against $71,000 in operating cost, producing a modest surplus that grows as third-party volume builds toward $245,000 by Year 4. The mobile market is the honest weak point. At Year 4 it generates approximately $280,000 in sales against roughly $318,000 in fully loaded cost — a structural gap of about $38,000 annually. We do not project it to close. It will be funded permanently by three sources: the hospital's produce prescription administration fee ($22,000 annually at 900 enrollees), federal produce-incentive administrative reimbursement, and consolidated operating surplus, which at FY32's projected $325,000 absorbs the remainder without strain. The board has adopted a written policy that the mobile market continues so long as consolidated operating margin exceeds 2.0 percent and that any suspension requires a two-thirds vote with 90 days' notice to host sites.

**Revenue mix.** Grants and contributions were 3.8 percent of PBCGC's FY26 revenue. During the grant term that rises to a peak of 11 percent in FY29 and falls to **6 percent by FY31 and under 4 percent by FY33.** This is not a program that becomes dependent on the Foundation; it is a capital event that a going concern absorbs.

**Reserves and policy.** Board policy requires 45 days' cost of goods in liquid working capital, tested quarterly, with an automatic expansion freeze if breached for two consecutive quarters. The capital replacement reserve funds at $2,100 per month now and $4,000 from FY29. Member equity grows with membership — 1,840 to a projected 3,400 by Year 4, adding roughly $156,000 in paid-in capital independent of the campaign.

**Downside case.** If Store 2 opens 15 percent below projected sales, FY31 store-level operating income falls to approximately $(62,000) and full self-sufficiency slips roughly seven months, to December 2031. The $340,000 reserve absorbs that scenario for 14 months. Under a 25 percent shortfall — a scenario we consider unlikely given demonstrated Store 1 demand from this tract — the co-op would reduce Store 2 hours from 84 to 70 weekly, cut two FTE, and defer the mobile market's sixth site, actions that close roughly $91,000 of the gap. We would notify the Foundation at the first quarter in which a 10 percent variance appears, not at fiscal year end.

**Application of Foundation funds.** The **$800,000 PRI** is applied entirely to Store 2 leasehold improvements — the hard-asset, revenue-generating component, appropriate for repayable capital and the basis for the co-op's leasehold interest. The **$1,400,000 grant** is applied as follows: warehouse build-out $395,000; warehouse equipment $118,000; mobile market vehicle $186,000; mobile market operating subsidy $64,000; Store 2 pre-opening labor, training and marketing $196,000; Store 2 working capital reserve $291,000; evaluation and data systems $92,000; project management and compliance $58,000. The grant funds precisely those components that cannot service debt — the shared warehouse, whose benefit accrues substantially to third-party farms; the mobile market, which never fully covers cost; the ramp-period reserve; and evaluation.

**Line detail.** *Leasehold improvements ($1,116,000)* covers roof replacement ($214,000), HVAC ($168,000), electrical and refrigeration infrastructure ($196,000), interior demolition and build-out ($291,000), restrooms and ADA compliance ($74,000), parking and lighting ($81,000), and architecture, engineering, and permitting ($92,000). Based on competitive bids from three general contractors, all attached; 7 percent contingency is embedded in the line items. *Equipment ($620,000)* covers refrigerated cases, walk-in cooler and freezer, meat and deli equipment, shelving, checkstands, and a four-terminal POS with scale integration, quoted by two vendors. *Pre-opening ($196,000)* covers 22 hires averaging 6.4 weeks of pre-opening wages and cross-training, plus $34,000 in launch marketing and $19,000 in signage. *Working capital reserve ($340,000)* is sized to 88 percent of projected cumulative ramp losses, held in a segregated account, drawn only against board-approved monthly variance reports, with any unspent balance at month 48 restricted to the capital replacement reserve. *Warehouse ($513,000 combined)* covers 6,000 square feet of insulated panel construction, three refrigeration systems, two dock levelers, a used electric forklift, pallet racking, and a farm-management and traceability platform. *Mobile market vehicle ($186,000)* is a 24-foot refrigerated box truck with a generator, awning, ramp, reach-in coolers, and dual EBT and benefit-card terminals; two quotes attached.

**Personnel.** Project-charged personnel total $58,000 across four years (project management and compliance) plus $64,000 of mobile market operations, which is primarily the Mobile Market Coordinator's partially subsidized salary. All other positions — Store 2 Manager, Warehouse Coordinator, Food Access Coordinator, and the 22 Store 2 staff — are carried on operating payroll from the date of hire, not on the grant. This is deliberate. Positions the co-op cannot afford to sustain from earned revenue are positions the co-op should not create.

**Indirect costs.** PBCGC requests no indirect cost recovery. Central administrative support to the project is contributed as match and valued at approximately $71,000 over four years (Okafor 0.35 FTE, Escamilla 0.30 FTE, plus IT, insurance, and audit allocation), reported but not charged.

**Disbursement.** PBCGC proposes tranched disbursement against milestones: 20 percent at closing; 25 percent at warehouse certificate of occupancy; 30 percent at Store 2 construction 50 percent complete, verified by the architect; 15 percent at Store 2 opening; 10 percent at month 30 conditioned on submission of the 12-month evaluation wave. We would additionally accept a condition precedent that Store 1 post an FY28 operating margin of not less than 1.5 percent before the Store 2 construction tranche releases.

**Fiscal controls.** Segregated project accounting by fund and component; dual signature above $5,000; board finance committee review of project draws monthly; annual audit with a supplementary schedule of Foundation funds; competitive bidding on every contract above $25,000, with three bids documented.
