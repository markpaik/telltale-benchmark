# Pine Bluff Community Grocery Cooperative
## Application to the Delta Provisions Foundation Food Access Enterprise Program

**Applicant:** Pine Bluff Community Grocery Cooperative
**Amount Requested:** $2,200,000 ($1,400,000 grant / $800,000 program-related investment at 2 percent)
**Project Period:** Four years
**Submitted by:** Bernadette Okafor, General Manager; Curtis Yamashita, Board President

---

## Statement of Need

Pine Bluff Community Grocery Cooperative operates the only full-line grocery store serving a significant portion of southeast Arkansas's Jefferson County population that identifies as Black, low-income, and disproportionately without personal transportation. Our current store, a 12,000 square foot cooperative grocery opened in 2022, serves 1,840 member-owners and generated $4.9 million in sales last year, an 18 percent increase over the prior year. The store operates in a service area that is 76 percent Black, where 31 percent of residents live below the federal poverty line and 22 percent of households have no vehicle. County-wide food insecurity stands at 21.4 percent, well above both state and national averages.

These figures describe a population for whom grocery access is not a convenience question but a health and economic security question. Federal and state data consistently show that food deserts in the Arkansas Delta correlate with elevated rates of diet-related disease, higher household transportation burden, and outmigration of retail investment. Our own store's performance is itself evidence of unmet demand: sales growth of 18 percent in a single year, against a backdrop of no comparable new retail entry, indicates that residents are directing food dollars to our cooperative not out of loyalty alone but because alternatives are scarce and often distant.

The specific gap this application addresses lies roughly three miles from our current store, in a census tract of approximately 12,400 residents that has had no full-line grocery store since a regional chain closed its location there in 2018. Residents in this tract currently travel to our existing store, to a small number of dollar-format stores that stock limited fresh food, or outside the county entirely. For a household without a vehicle, that trip can mean a two-hour round trip by bus with transfers, an expense in taxi or rideshare fares that erodes food budgets, or reliance on convenience-store pricing and selection. The 2018 closure of the prior grocery in this tract was not an isolated event; it followed a familiar pattern in which conventional retailers withdraw from low-margin, high-need markets once minimum profitability thresholds are not met, leaving residents with no full-service option for going on eight years.

Our cooperative's existing operations demonstrate both the need and the model's capacity to meet it. SNAP redemption at our current store reached $610,000 last year, reflecting substantial reliance on federal nutrition assistance among our customer base. A produce incentive program matched $88,000 in SNAP produce purchases, directly increasing fresh fruit and vegetable consumption among low-income shoppers. We currently purchase from 24 area farms, channeling grocery revenue into the regional agricultural economy rather than extracting it to distant corporate suppliers. These are not aspirational figures; they are our operating record.

At the same time, we are not asking the Foundation to evaluate this proposal in isolation from our history, and we address that history directly and without euphemism in the Organizational Capacity section below. Our first store, opened in this same neighborhood in 2019, closed after 22 months, leaving $340,000 in vendor debt. That debt has since been repaid in full. We raise it here because the underlying need that motivated that first attempt, and that motivates this proposal, has not diminished; if anything, seven additional years without a full-line grocery in the target tract have deepened it. The question this application must answer is not whether the need exists, but whether our organization has the operating discipline, the capital structure, and the partnerships to meet it sustainably this time. We believe the evidence assembled in this application demonstrates that it does.

## Project Description

This project has three integrated components, each designed to extend the reach of a now-stable cooperative grocery model into unserved and underserved parts of our service area, while sharing fixed costs across a growing enterprise rather than duplicating them.

**Second Store.** We will open a second full-line grocery store in the census tract of approximately 12,400 residents that has lacked a full-line grocery since 2018. The store will be similar in format to our existing location: a cooperative-owned, full-line grocery emphasizing fresh produce, culturally relevant staples, SNAP and produce-incentive acceptance, and integration with the regional farm supply chain we have already built. We will use market and demographic data specific to this tract, gathered in partnership with the county extension office, to size the store appropriately; current planning anticipates a footprint in the 9,000 to 11,000 square foot range, smaller than our flagship store, reflecting lessons learned from the 2019 closure about matching fixed costs to realistic first-three-year sales volume rather than to aspirational long-term volume.

**Shared Refrigerated Warehouse.** Rather than building duplicate cold storage and receiving infrastructure at each store, we will construct a shared refrigerated warehouse positioned between the two locations. This facility will receive deliveries from our 24 partner farms and other suppliers, consolidate orders, and distribute to both stores and to the mobile market described below. Centralizing refrigerated storage reduces per-store capital and operating cost, improves our bargaining position with farm suppliers by allowing larger consolidated orders, and reduces spoilage by allowing tighter inventory management across the enterprise rather than at each site independently. This warehouse is also the infrastructure that makes the mobile market financially feasible, since a stand-alone mobile market route could not otherwise justify its own cold-chain investment.

**Mobile Market.** A refrigerated mobile market vehicle, based at the shared warehouse, will run a fixed weekly route to six rural sites currently outside reasonable driving distance of either store. These sites will be selected in partnership with the county extension office based on population density, existing community gathering infrastructure such as churches and community centers, and absence of any grocery access within a defined radius. The mobile market will accept SNAP and produce-incentive benefits on the same terms as our stores, extending the reach of the produce incentive program described above to households who cannot reach either brick-and-mortar location.

**Partnerships.** This project depends on and is structured around four external partners, each with a defined operational role rather than a symbolic one. A regional hospital system will refer patients through a produce-prescription program, under which physicians and care coordinators identify patients with diet-related chronic conditions and issue vouchers redeemable for fresh produce at either store or the mobile market; this both increases produce-incentive volume and gives us a referral pipeline independent of general marketing. A regional farmers alliance, representing a portion of our 24 supplying farms, will coordinate aggregated ordering and delivery scheduling into the shared warehouse, smoothing the seasonal volume fluctuations that complicate direct farm-to-store purchasing. A community development bank, represented in this application by loan officer Nikhil Sundaram, is providing the $800,000 program-related investment structure alongside the Foundation's own PRI, and will hold a subordinate monitoring role on our overall capital structure, discussed further in the Sustainability section. The county extension office will provide site-selection data and technical assistance for the mobile market route and will support financial and operational education for member-owners and staff.

**Sequencing.** Year one will be dedicated to site acquisition, warehouse design and permitting, and finalization of partnership agreements, informed explicitly by the operational review described in Organizational Capacity. Year two will bring warehouse construction and second-store build-out, with mobile market vehicle procurement and route design proceeding in parallel. The second store and mobile market will open in year three, with a planned twelve-month ramp period before the store is expected to reach the operating margin defined in Sustainability. Year four is a stabilization year in which we expect the second store to approach break-even on a fully loaded basis, inclusive of its share of shared warehouse costs and debt service, as detailed below.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

**Goal 1: Expand physical access to full-line grocery service in unserved areas.**
- Objective 1.1: Open the second store, of 9,000–11,000 square feet, in the target census tract by the start of year three.
- Objective 1.2: Launch the shared refrigerated warehouse operational and receiving deliveries by the start of year three.
- Objective 1.3: Launch mobile market service to six rural sites on a fixed weekly schedule by the start of year three, with all six sites operating on schedule by the end of year three.

**Goal 2: Increase household-level food security and nutrition assistance utilization.**
- Objective 2.1: Serve at least 3,500 unique households across the second store and mobile market combined by the end of year three, rising to 5,500 by the end of year four.
- Objective 2.2: Achieve combined SNAP redemption across the second store and mobile market of at least $350,000 in year three, rising to $600,000 in year four.
- Objective 2.3: Achieve produce-prescription and produce-incentive redemption across the second store and mobile market of at least $60,000 in year three, rising to $110,000 in year four, in coordination with the regional hospital referral pipeline.
- Objective 2.4: Improve food security score, measured by the twelve- and 24-month instrument described in Evaluation Plan, among a tracked cohort of enrolled households, targeting a statistically meaningful improvement between the twelve-month and 24-month measurement points.

**Goal 3: Strengthen the regional farm economy through cooperative purchasing.**
- Objective 3.1: Increase the number of area farms supplying the cooperative from 24 to at least 32 by the end of year four, using the farmers alliance partnership to onboard smaller producers who could not previously meet single-store minimum order volumes.
- Objective 3.2: Increase total dollar volume of farm purchases by at least 40 percent by the end of year four relative to current single-store baseline, reflecting both new farm relationships and the second store's and mobile market's additional demand.

**Goal 4: Achieve sustainable store-level and enterprise-level financial performance.**
- Objective 4.1: Bring the second store to a store-level operating margin of at least 1.5 percent by the end of year four, consistent with the "when the second store carries itself" threshold defined in Sustainability.
- Objective 4.2: Maintain combined enterprise-level (both stores plus warehouse plus mobile market) operating margin at or above breakeven throughout the grant period, with no more than two quarters of negative margin at the enterprise level in any rolling eight-quarter period, an explicit and measurable improvement discipline informed by Finance Manager Lorena Escamilla's tracking of the flagship store's own margin history.
- Objective 4.3: Meet 100 percent of scheduled debt service payments on the $800,000 program-related investment and any additional financing described in Sustainability, with no missed or restructured payments during the grant period.

## Evaluation Plan

Evaluation responsibility rests jointly with General Manager Bernadette Okafor for operational data and Finance Manager Lorena Escamilla for financial data, with quarterly reporting to the Board and semi-annual reporting to the Foundation. We will track the following indicators, matched explicitly to the Foundation's stated tracking priorities.

**Households served.** Both stores and the mobile market will use point-of-sale and member-owner registration data to produce a de-duplicated count of unique households served monthly, aggregated quarterly. Mobile market sites will use a lightweight sign-in process, coordinated with site hosts such as churches and community centers, to capture household counts without requiring full point-of-sale integration on the vehicle at launch.

**SNAP and produce-prescription redemption.** SNAP redemption is captured automatically through EBT transaction processing at both stores and on the mobile market's point-of-sale unit, and is reported monthly by site. Produce-prescription redemption is tracked through voucher codes issued by the regional hospital's care coordinators, redeemed at point-of-sale and reconciled monthly against hospital-issued voucher counts, giving us both a redemption rate and a referral-to-redemption conversion rate that the hospital partnership can use to refine its own referral process.

**Farm sales to the co-op.** The shared warehouse's receiving log, maintained in coordination with the farmers alliance, will record dollar volume and farm count by supplier monthly, allowing us to report both total farm purchase volume and the specific farm-count growth objective above.

**Store-level operating margin.** Lorena Escamilla's office will produce store-level and enterprise-level profit and loss statements quarterly, with the second store's statement carrying its allocated share of shared warehouse fixed costs from the day it opens, so that the margin figure we report reflects true fully loaded performance rather than an artificially favorable figure that excludes shared infrastructure cost. This is a deliberate methodological choice responding directly to the fact that the first store's 2019 closure was accompanied by financial reporting that did not fully surface fixed-cost exposure until it was too late to correct course; we will not repeat that error.

**Food security score at twelve and 24 months.** We will administer a validated household food security survey instrument to a voluntary cohort of second-store and mobile-market customers, recruited at point of enrollment as member-owners or produce-prescription participants, at intake and again at twelve and 24 months. The county extension office will assist with survey administration and data entry, given its existing community relationships and its research support role in similar county-level programs. We anticipate an initial cohort of approximately 400 households, sized to allow a meaningful before-and-after comparison while remaining feasible to track without dedicated research staff.

**Internal review cadence.** Beyond the specific metrics above, the Board has adopted, in direct response to the lessons of the first store's closure, a quarterly financial review triggered automatically whenever any store or the enterprise as a whole records a negative operating margin, requiring Lorena Escamilla to present a written variance analysis and Bernadette Okafor to present a corrective action plan within thirty days. This review was not in place during the 2018–2019 period and is now a standing Board policy applicable to both existing and new operations.

## Organizational Capacity

Pine Bluff Community Grocery Cooperative is a member-owned cooperative with 1,840 member-owners, 31 employees, and $4.9 million in current annual sales from our existing store, which opened in 2022. General Manager Bernadette Okafor has led daily operations since the current store's opening and has direct experience managing the store's 18 percent year-over-year sales growth, its SNAP and produce-incentive program administration, and its farm-purchasing relationships with 24 area farms. Board President Curtis Yamashita has overseen governance through the current store's full operating history, including its transition from startup to its current growth trajectory. Finance Manager Lorena Escamilla maintains the cooperative's financial reporting and has direct, current visibility into store performance, including the fact that the current store has recorded losses in two of the last four quarters at a 1.2 percent margin.

We include that last figure deliberately and without minimizing it. The current store's margin performance over the past year has not been uniformly positive, and we do not want the Foundation to encounter that fact anywhere other than in our own words. Two quarters of loss at a 1.2 percent margin reflect a business operating close to breakeven, consistent with a young full-line grocery cooperative in a low-margin, high-need market, but it is not a record of comfortable stability, and we do not present it as one. It is, however, a materially different financial picture than the one that preceded the first store's closure, for reasons we describe below, and it is the picture against which the second store's projected path to a 1.5 percent margin, described in Sustainability, should be evaluated.

**The 2019 closure, directly addressed.** Our cooperative's first store opened in this same neighborhood in 2019 and closed after 22 months, leaving $340,000 in vendor debt. That debt has been repaid in full; the last payment was made before the current store's 2022 opening, and no vendor from that period carries an outstanding claim against the cooperative today. We do not present the repayment as erasing the closure, but as evidence that the Board and management treated the obligation as a continuing one rather than a discharged one, and that matters to how the Foundation should weigh our credibility on the debt-service commitments in this application.

The internal review the Board conducted after the 2019 closure identified three specific causes, and we describe our concrete responses to each because they bear directly on this proposal's design.

First, the 2019 store's footprint and initial inventory investment were sized to a projected sales volume that assumed near-immediate market penetration comparable to an established grocery chain, rather than to the realistic ramp curve of a new cooperative building member trust in a market that had just lost its prior grocery. Fixed costs, including staffing levels and lease terms, were set against that optimistic projection and did not flex downward when actual sales fell short in the first year. In response, the current store's 2022 opening used a more conservative initial staffing and inventory model, phased up as sales materialized rather than staffed to a target from day one, and this proposal's second store follows the same discipline: a smaller footprint than our flagship store, and a financial model, described below, that assumes a twelve-month ramp before approaching target margin rather than assuming immediate performance at scale.

Second, the 2019 store carried its own stand-alone refrigeration and receiving infrastructure, purchased new, which represented a disproportionate share of fixed capital cost relative to sales volume in the store's early months. This proposal's shared warehouse model is a direct structural response: cold-chain infrastructure cost is spread across two stores and a mobile market rather than borne entirely by the second store, and the warehouse's farm-consolidation function is expected to improve purchasing terms in a way the 2019 store, buying directly and independently, could not achieve.

Third, financial reporting during the 2019 store's operation was store-level only and reported monthly rather than continuously, which meant that mounting vendor payment delays were visible internally before they were fully reflected in formal financial statements, and the Board's awareness of the severity of the position lagged the operational reality by a period that, in hindsight, was long enough to foreclose corrective options. Lorena Escamilla's office now produces weekly cash-position reporting to the General Manager and monthly reporting to the Board's finance committee, and, as described in Evaluation Plan, any negative-margin quarter now triggers a mandatory variance review within thirty days. This reporting discipline has been in continuous use throughout the current store's operation, including through the two loss quarters noted above, which were identified, reviewed, and addressed under this process rather than discovered after the fact.

We ask the Foundation to weigh this history as evidence of an organization that has already been tested by a serious operating failure, has repaid the obligations that failure created, and has built specific, verifiable operating changes in direct response to specific, identified causes, rather than as evidence that the underlying model is unsound. The current store's three-plus years of continuous operation, its 18 percent sales growth, and its $610,000 in SNAP redemption are, we believe, the more reliable predictor of this organization's capacity than the 2019 closure alone, but we have not asked the Foundation to take that on faith without a full accounting of what went wrong and what has changed.

## Sustainability

**Debt service on the program-related investment.** The $800,000 program-related investment from the Foundation carries a 2 percent interest rate. Structured on a ten-year amortization with payments beginning in year two of the grant period, once the shared warehouse is operational and generating cost savings across both stores, annual debt service is projected at approximately $88,000, inclusive of principal and interest. This obligation is modeled explicitly into the enterprise-level financial projections referenced in Evaluation Plan and is treated as a fixed cost allocated across both stores and the warehouse, not as an obligation the second store alone must cover from its own revenue in its early, lower-volume years. The community development bank's parallel financing, coordinated through loan officer Nikhil Sundaram, is structured on compatible terms and will be formally subordinated in scheduling to ensure the Foundation's PRI payment obligations are met first in any period of cash-flow constraint; Mr. Sundaram's institution has reviewed and concurs with this priority structure as a condition of its own participation.

**When the second store carries itself.** We project the second store will not reach the 1.5 percent operating margin target described in Goals and Measurable Objectives until the end of year four, following a twelve-month ramp period after its year-three opening. During years three and its ramp period, the second store is expected to operate at a loss or at breakeven, consistent with the realistic ramp curve described in Organizational Capacity, and this shortfall is explicitly covered by the flagship store's existing operating surplus and by the grant funds themselves during the construction and ramp period, not by additional borrowing. We consider the second store to "carry itself," in the sense of covering its own fully loaded operating costs including its allocated share of warehouse fixed costs and its share of PRI debt service, beginning in year four, and we have built our internal cash-flow projections, reviewed quarterly per the Evaluation Plan process, on that conservative timeline rather than on an earlier and less certain breakeven date.

**Beyond the grant period.** Following the four-year grant period, the shared warehouse and mobile market are expected to be fully absorbed into ongoing enterprise operations, with their costs and revenues folded into standard store-level and enterprise-level financial reporting rather than tracked as a discrete grant-funded project. The farmers alliance relationship and hospital produce-prescription partnership are structured as standing operational partnerships, not grant-term arrangements, and both partners have indicated in writing their intention to continue past the grant period regardless of the specific funding mechanism. Continued SNAP and produce-incentive redemption revenue, which is federally funded and not dependent on the Foundation's grant, is expected to represent a growing and stable share of second-store and mobile-market revenue as the produce-prescription referral pipeline matures.

We recognize that the Foundation is, in effect, being asked to help fund a second attempt in a location where a first attempt by this same organization failed. We have tried, throughout this application, to make that fact visible rather than to minimize it, and to show the specific structural, financial, and reporting changes that distinguish this proposal from the 2019 store rather than asking the Foundation to trust that the outcome will simply be different this time. The shared warehouse, the smaller footprint, the twelve-month ramp assumption, the quarterly variance-review trigger, and the subordinated debt structure with the community development bank are the concrete mechanisms by which we intend to make that difference real.

## Budget Narrative

Total project cost is $2,200,000 over four years, funded by a $1,400,000 grant and an $800,000 program-related investment at 2 percent interest, amortized over ten years with payments beginning in year two.

**Year One — $650,000 (grant funds).** Site acquisition and due diligence for the second store location, $220,000. Architectural and engineering design for the second store and shared warehouse, $140,000. Permitting, environmental review, and legal costs associated with site acquisition and partnership agreements, including finalization of agreements with the regional hospital, farmers alliance, and county extension office, $60,000. Initial mobile market vehicle specification and procurement deposit, $80,000. Organizational capacity costs, including a dedicated project manager position to oversee construction sequencing and partnership implementation without diverting Bernadette Okafor's or Lorena Escamilla's attention from ongoing flagship-store operations, $150,000.

**Year Two — $850,000 ($550,000 grant funds; $300,000 program-related investment draw).** Shared warehouse construction, $420,000. Second store build-out and initial fixtures, $310,000. Mobile market vehicle final procurement and refrigeration outfitting, $70,000. Continued project management and partnership coordination costs, $50,000.

**Year Three — $500,000 ($250,000 grant funds; $250,000 program-related investment draw).** Second store initial inventory investment, staffed and phased consistent with the conservative ramp model described in Sustainability, $180,000. Mobile market launch costs, including route setup with the six rural sites identified by the county extension office, driver and staff hiring, and initial produce and grocery stock, $90,000. Second store staffing costs during the ramp period, budgeted above initial break-even staffing levels for the first several months consistent with a phased approach, $150,000. First-year debt service on the program-related investment, $30,000 (partial year, reflecting mid-year payment commencement). Marketing and member-owner recruitment specific to the second store's service area, $50,000.

**Year Four — $200,000 ($200,000 program-related investment draw, remaining balance).** Full-year debt service on the program-related investment, $88,000. Continued mobile market operating support during its own ramp toward self-sufficiency, $62,000. Evaluation costs, including the twelve- and 24-month food security survey administration in partnership with the county extension office, data analysis, and Foundation reporting, $50,000.

**Ongoing costs beyond grant funds.** Second store and mobile market operating costs beyond the amounts specified above, including the majority of staffing, utilities, and inventory replenishment from year three forward, are funded through store revenue and, during the ramp period, through the flagship store's operating surplus, as described in Sustainability. This structure ensures that grant and program-related investment funds are concentrated on capital costs, launch costs, and the specific debt-service and evaluation obligations tied directly to the Foundation's investment, while ongoing operations are supported by the cooperative's own revenue base from the earliest possible point in the project timeline.

**Contingency and cost-control provisions.** Recognizing that the 2019 closure was driven in part by a mismatch between fixed cost commitments and actual sales performance, this budget builds in explicit contingency rather than assuming best-case execution throughout. A contingency reserve of $75,000, drawn from year two and year three grant allocations at a rate of $37,500 per year, is held against construction cost overruns on the shared warehouse and second store build-out, the two line items most exposed to unpredictable material and labor cost movement in the current regional construction market. This reserve is not allocated to any specific line item in advance; release of contingency funds requires sign-off from both Bernadette Okafor and Lorena Escamilla and is reported to the Board at the following regular meeting, consistent with the variance-review discipline described in Evaluation Plan.

Staffing costs for the second store are budgeted on a phased hiring schedule tied to actual sales volume rather than to a fixed headcount target set in advance. The store will open with a core staff sized to a conservative first-quarter sales projection, with additional hires triggered by sustained sales performance above that projection rather than by the calendar. This is the single most direct budgetary lesson carried over from the 2019 closure, where staffing was set to a projected volume that did not materialize, and it is reflected in the year three staffing line as a range rather than a fixed figure, with the upper bound contingent on sales performance documented in the quarterly financial reporting described above.

Inventory investment for the second store is similarly phased. Initial stocking will emphasize high-turnover staples and produce, informed by the flagship store's three years of point-of-sale data on which categories move fastest in this demographic and income profile, rather than replicating the flagship store's full category breadth from day one. Category breadth will expand as the second store's own sales data justifies additional SKU investment, a lower-risk approach than the 2019 store's initial full-catalog stocking, which left slower-moving inventory as a cash-flow drag during the period when vendor payments began to lag.

**Program-related investment drawdown schedule.** The $800,000 program-related investment is drawn in three tranches rather than disbursed in full at closing: $300,000 in year two tied to executed construction contracts for the warehouse and second store, $250,000 in year three tied to store opening and mobile market launch, and $250,000 in year four tied to the second store's demonstrated progress toward the 1.5 percent margin objective, verified through the quarterly financial reporting shared with the Foundation. This tranched structure gives both the Foundation and the community development bank an ongoing checkpoint against the specific objectives in this application, rather than committing the full investment amount before construction risk and ramp performance are known. Nikhil Sundaram's institution has reviewed and structured its parallel financing on the same tranche schedule, so that both funders' disbursements remain aligned to the same verified milestones throughout the project.

**Reporting costs already embedded.** The evaluation costs listed under year four do not represent the full cost of the reporting and data collection described throughout this application; ongoing point-of-sale, EBT reconciliation, and warehouse receiving-log data collection are absorbed into existing administrative operations at no incremental cost, since these systems are already in place at the flagship store and are being extended rather than newly built. The year four evaluation line item covers the incremental cost of the food security survey instrument specifically, including printing, translation into languages spoken by farmworker households in the service area, data entry support from the county extension office, and the analysis and write-up required for the twelve- and 24-month reporting deliverables described in the Evaluation Plan.

**Summary.** Across all four years, grant funds are concentrated in years one and two, when capital construction and site costs are highest and before either the second store or the mobile market is generating revenue. Program-related investment funds are drawn later and in smaller tranches, tied to milestone verification, and debt service on that investment does not begin until year two and does not reach its full annual amount until year four, by which point the second store is projected to be approaching the margin performance that allows it to absorb that cost from its own operations rather than from the flagship store's surplus. This sequencing is designed so that the heaviest capital costs are covered by grant funds before any debt service obligation begins, and so that debt service scales up only as the underlying enterprise's revenue-generating capacity scales up alongside it.
