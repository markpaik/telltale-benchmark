## Executive Summary 1 of 4: Merchant Onboarding Service

**Program:** Merchant Onboarding Service ("Kestrel Fast Start")
**Review period:** Calendar year 2025
**Date of summary:** January 12, 2026

### Finding and Recommendation

The merchant onboarding service is the strongest performer in the 2025 portfolio and the clearest case this company has produced in several years of a program that did exactly what it was funded to do, at roughly the cost it said it would, in the time it promised. Median merchant onboarding time fell from nine days to 31 hours. The service brought 1,914 new merchants onto the platform during the year, and the cohort acquired through it is showing net revenue retention of 118 percent — meaningfully above the company average of 106 percent. The build cost $3.1 million against a $3.4 million approved envelope.

The recommendation is to convert this program from project funding to a standing product line with its own operating budget, and to approve a second tranche of $2.2 million in 2026 to extend the automated onboarding flow to the two merchant categories it currently excludes: high-risk verticals requiring enhanced due diligence, and multi-location franchise accounts. The Portfolio Review Board endorses this recommendation unanimously, and Compliance has reviewed and cleared the proposed extension into enhanced-due-diligence categories subject to conditions described below.

### What the Program Was Funded to Do

The board approved the onboarding program in January 2025 with a mandate that was deliberately narrow. Kestrel was losing prospective merchants during a nine-day onboarding cycle dominated by manual document review, sequential compliance checks, and a hand-built underwriting queue that processed applications in strict arrival order regardless of complexity. Sales estimated that between 20 and 30 percent of signed merchants abandoned before activation, and competitive intelligence indicated that the two competitors we most often lose deals to were activating comparable merchants in under 48 hours.

The funded mandate was threefold: reduce median onboarding time to under 48 hours for standard-risk merchants; hold or improve the compliance pass rate on post-activation audits, so that speed did not come at the cost of underwriting quality; and do it for $3.4 million over twelve months. The program explicitly did not promise revenue outcomes — those were treated as second-order effects — although Simone Adeyinka's sponsoring memo projected that faster activation would improve first-year retention because merchants who process transactions within days of signing form the habit of using the platform.

### What It Actually Delivered

Against the 48-hour target, the program delivered a 31-hour median by September and held it through the fourth quarter, including the holiday-season application surge, which historically doubled the onboarding queue. The tail also compressed: the 90th-percentile onboarding time fell from 19 days to 74 hours. The engineering work that made this possible — parallelized identity and sanctions screening, automated document classification, and a risk-tiered underwriting queue that routes simple applications around the manual review desk — came in at $3.1 million, roughly nine percent under budget, with Gustavo Peralta's team absorbing one scope addition (real-time bank account verification) without a change request.

The compliance question, which was the board's stated concern at approval, resolved favorably. Deborah Yankelovich's team ran quarterly post-activation audits on sampled cohorts throughout the year. The audit exception rate on merchants onboarded through the automated flow was 2.1 percent, against 2.6 percent for the legacy manual process — the automated document checks are more consistent than the human review desk they replaced. No regulatory findings or acquirer-bank exceptions were attributable to the new flow.

The business results exceeded the sponsoring memo's projections. The 1,914 merchants activated through the service in 2025 represent a 34 percent increase in gross merchant additions over 2024. The cohort's 118 percent net revenue retention, measured on the merchants activated in the first half and observed through year end, compares to 106 percent for the company overall and 101 percent for the 2024 onboarding cohort. Sales attributes at least part of a measurable win-rate improvement in competitive deals to the ability to promise next-day activation, though we caution the board that win-rate attribution is softer evidence than the retention figure, which is directly measured. Abandonment between signature and activation fell from an estimated 24 percent to 7 percent. On a contribution basis, Finance estimates the 2025 cohort will return the $3.1 million build cost by approximately the third quarter of 2026.

One qualification belongs in this summary. The 31-hour median applies only to standard-risk merchants, who represent about 78 percent of application volume. High-risk verticals and multi-location franchise accounts still route through the manual desk and still take seven to eleven days. These are, on average, larger and higher-lifetime-value accounts than the standard population. The program's headline numbers are real, but they describe the easier three-quarters of the funnel.

### What the Board Is Being Asked to Decide

Two decisions are requested. First, approve the conversion of the onboarding service from a funded project to a standing product line under the Chief Product Officer, with a 2026 operating budget of $1.6 million covering the retained engineering pod, model maintenance, and the residual manual review desk. Second, approve $2.2 million in new build funding to extend automated onboarding to the enhanced-due-diligence and franchise segments, with a target of bringing those categories under 96 hours by the fourth quarter of 2026.

Compliance supports the extension with two conditions: the enhanced-due-diligence flow must retain a mandatory human approval step for the final activation decision in designated high-risk verticals, and the quarterly audit sampling rate for those categories must double for the first year of operation. Both conditions are incorporated into the proposal and its budget.

### Exposure If the Decision Waits Another Quarter

The cost of a one-quarter delay is competitive rather than operational. The existing service runs and will keep running; nothing breaks in April that works in January. What is at risk is the segment we have not yet reached. High-risk and franchise merchants are the highest-value applications in the funnel, they currently wait seven to eleven days, and the same competitors who forced this program's creation are now marketing sub-72-hour activation to franchise operators specifically. Sales reports two franchise prospects in the fourth quarter, worth a combined estimated $410,000 in annual recurring revenue, who cited onboarding speed in choosing a competitor. A quarter's delay pushes the extended flow's delivery from the fourth quarter of 2026 into the first quarter of 2027, which means competing through the entire 2026 selling season with a nine-day answer to a three-day question in our most valuable segment. There is also a retention risk in team continuity: the engineering pod that built the service is intact and productive, and a funding gap between project close and the new tranche invites reassignment of people whose accumulated knowledge of the onboarding risk logic is genuinely difficult to replace.

The board should also weigh the signaling value of acting quickly on success. This portfolio contains two programs in serious difficulty. Funding the one that delivered, promptly and visibly, is how a portfolio process earns the credibility to make the harder calls that follow in this packet.

## Executive Summary 2 of 4: Terminal Firmware Modernization Program

**Program:** Terminal Firmware Modernization ("Project Meridian")
**Review period:** Calendar year 2025
**Date of summary:** January 12, 2026

### Finding and Recommendation

The terminal firmware modernization program is in serious trouble, and the nature of the trouble has changed in a way the board needs to understand clearly. Through the third quarter this was a schedule problem: the program had missed milestones and was running behind a fixed external deadline. As of this review it is a capacity problem: the lead firmware vendor, Corvid Embedded Systems, lost approximately half of its engineering team in November following an acquisition of its parent company, and its remaining staff cannot deliver the outstanding work on any schedule Kestrel can rely on. The program has completed two of its five 2025 milestones. Twenty-two percent of the installed base of roughly 41,000 terminals — about 9,000 devices in merchant locations — still runs a firmware build the manufacturer no longer supports and that will not pass the payment-card industry attestation due October 31, 2026. On the current trajectory, that deadline will be missed.

The recommendation is a structural restructuring of the program, decided now, not in April. Specifically: qualify and contract a second firmware integrator within 60 days; bring the update-orchestration layer — the system that schedules, delivers, and verifies firmware pushes to deployed terminals — in-house under Gustavo Peralta's organization; renegotiate the Corvid engagement down to the two components it has demonstrably staffed; and approve contingency funding of $1.9 million to cover the second vendor, the in-house build, and an accelerated field-replacement path for terminals that cannot take the new firmware over the air. Deborah Yankelovich additionally recommends, and the review board concurs, that Kestrel open a proactive dialogue with its acquiring banks and qualified security assessor in the first quarter about the attestation timeline, rather than waiting to disclose a miss.

### What the Program Was Funded to Do

The board approved Project Meridian in late 2024 at $4.6 million over eighteen months. The mandate was to migrate the entire installed terminal fleet — approximately 41,000 devices across three hardware generations — from a legacy firmware line to a modernized, supported build, ahead of the October 31, 2026 payment-card attestation deadline. The attestation matters because Kestrel's standing with its acquiring banks, and by extension its merchants' ability to process card transactions on our terminals, depends on it. The program plan set five 2025 milestones: completion of the new firmware core (Q1); certification lab validation (Q2); over-the-air update capability for the two newer hardware generations (Q3); fleet migration to 50 percent (Q3); and fleet migration to 85 percent (Q4), leaving the oldest-generation devices, some of which require physical replacement, for the first half of 2026.

The delivery model concentrated the specialized embedded work — the firmware core and the device-side update client — with Corvid, a vendor with a strong prior record on Kestrel's hardware platform. Kestrel retained merchant communications, field logistics, and program management. The structural risk in that model, flagged in the original approval papers, was single-vendor concentration in the scarcest skill set. That risk has now materialized in close to its worst form.

### What It Actually Delivered

Two of five milestones were met. The firmware core was completed in the first quarter, four weeks late but within tolerance, and lab certification completed in June. The three delivery milestones were all missed. Over-the-air update capability slipped from the third quarter and remains incomplete: it works for the newest hardware generation but fails intermittently on the middle generation, which represents about 40 percent of the fleet, due to a memory-partition constraint that Corvid identified in August and had not resolved when its staffing collapsed. Fleet migration stands at roughly 43 percent against the 85 percent year-end target. The 22 percent of the fleet on the unsupported build — the oldest devices, concentrated among small merchants who acquired terminals before 2021 — has not started migrating at all, because the field-replacement program that those devices require was sequenced behind the over-the-air work and never began.

Spending stands at $3.3 million of the $4.6 million envelope, which is to say the program has consumed 72 percent of its budget to complete 40 percent of its milestones, before accounting for the vendor failure. Corvid's staffing loss in November was abrupt: of a fourteen-person team, six remain, and the two engineers who owned the middle-generation memory-partition problem both departed. Corvid's management has proposed a recovery plan; Gustavo Peralta's assessment, which this review shares, is that the plan is not credible against the October 2026 deadline and that continued sole reliance on Corvid converts a probable attestation miss into a near-certain one.

The compliance dimension deserves plain statement. An attestation miss is not a fine-and-move-on event. It triggers remediation reporting to acquiring banks, potential restrictions on onboarding new terminal-based merchants, and in a prolonged scenario, pressure on existing merchant processing relationships. Roughly $31 million of Kestrel's annual recurring revenue touches terminal-based processing. Deborah Yankelovich's assessment is that a miss disclosed proactively, with a credible funded remediation plan, is manageable with our acquirers; a miss discovered by an assessor against a program in visible disarray is a materially worse conversation.

### What the Board Is Being Asked to Decide

The board is asked to approve four linked actions as a package:

1. **Second-vendor qualification and contract** for the middle-generation update client and the remaining device-side work, targeted within 60 days, at an estimated $850,000.
2. **In-house build of the update-orchestration layer**, moving scheduling, delivery, and verification of firmware pushes into Peralta's organization permanently, at an estimated $600,000 in 2026.
3. **Renegotiation of the Corvid engagement** to the newest-generation work it remains staffed to deliver, with milestone-gated payments.
4. **Contingency funding of $1.9 million** covering the above plus an accelerated field-replacement path for the estimated 6,000 oldest-generation devices that cannot be updated remotely.

The package raises total program cost to approximately $6.5 million against the original $4.6 million. It does not guarantee the October 31 deadline; it makes the deadline plausible, and it makes the fallback — a short, well-documented remediation period negotiated in advance with acquirers — defensible.

### Exposure If the Decision Waits Another Quarter

This is the program in the 2025 portfolio where a quarter's delay does direct, arithmetic damage. The October 31 deadline does not move. A decision in April instead of January consumes three of the ten months remaining, and the longest-lead items in the recovery plan — second-vendor qualification and the field-replacement logistics for 6,000 devices — are precisely the ones that cannot be compressed later by spending more. Field replacement alone, at realistic logistics throughput, requires roughly six months once started. Started in February, it finishes in advance of the deadline; started in May, it finishes after it. A quarter's delay therefore converts a recoverable position into a near-certain attestation miss on approximately 9,000 merchant devices, with the acquirer-relationship consequences described above, and it forfeits the option of the proactive disclosure that Compliance regards as the difference between a managed event and a damaging one. The board should treat this as the one decision in this packet that genuinely cannot wait.

## Executive Summary 3 of 4: Self-Serve Developer Tier

**Program:** Self-Serve Developer Tier ("Kestrel Open")
**Review period:** Calendar year 2025
**Date of summary:** January 12, 2026

### Finding and Recommendation

The self-serve developer tier attracted an audience and failed to build a business, and the honest reading of the 2025 evidence is that these were two different goals wearing one budget. The program drew 12,400 signups against a plan of 10,000 — top-of-funnel demand is real. But it converted 380 of those signups to paid accounts against a plan of 1,500, a 25 percent attainment on the number the business case actually depended on. It spent $2.1 million of its $2.3 million budget doing so, and it generates a support load of approximately 900 tickets per month from free-tier users, a cost currently absorbed by the support organization funded from paid-tier revenue. The unit economics as built do not work and will not be fixed by another quarter of the same activity.

The recommendation is not to kill the program outright, because the signup data contains a finding worth acting on, but to end it in its current form. Specifically: sunset the unrestricted free tier by the end of the second quarter of 2026, replacing it with a time-boxed sandbox that requires no support entitlement; retain and refocus a reduced team on the one conversion path that demonstrably worked, which was developers arriving from existing Kestrel merchant customers rather than from the open market; and cap 2026 spending at $700,000 against explicit conversion gates, with automatic wind-down if the gates are missed at mid-year. The review board is unanimous on ending the current model; the CPO's office supports the refocused continuation, and this summary presents the evidence for the board to weigh a full sunset against it.

### What the Program Was Funded to Do

The board approved Kestrel Open in January 2025 at $2.3 million, on a strategy argument rather than a near-term revenue argument. The thesis, laid out in Simone Adeyinka's sponsoring memo, held that Kestrel's payments APIs were sold exclusively top-down through an enterprise sales motion, that competitors with self-serve developer tiers were becoming the default choice for new software companies embedding payments, and that Kestrel needed a bottom-up channel before that default hardened. The funded plan promised a free tier with generous API limits, public documentation and onboarding, and a self-serve upgrade path to a paid tier at $299 per month. The plan's quantitative commitments were 10,000 signups and 1,500 paid conversions by year end, with paid-tier revenue of roughly $2.7 million annualized by the end of 2026. The memo acknowledged that free-tier support cost was a risk and committed to deflecting it through documentation and community channels rather than ticketed support — a commitment that, in the event, did not hold.

### What It Actually Delivered

The funnel tells the story compactly. Signups exceeded plan at 12,400. Activation — a developer making a first successful API call — reached about 5,600, or 45 percent of signups, which is respectable for the category. Sustained usage past 30 days fell to roughly 1,900. Paid conversion reached 380, one-quarter of plan, producing annualized paid revenue of approximately $1.4 million against the $2.7 million trajectory the business case required. Spending of $2.1 million is within budget; the problem is not cost discipline but the revenue the cost was supposed to produce.

Two failure mechanisms are visible in the data, and distinguishing them matters for the decision. First, the free tier's limits were generous enough that a large population of small integrations never needed to upgrade: analysis of sustained free users shows about 60 percent operating comfortably inside free limits with no growth trajectory that would push them out. The free tier is not a funnel for these users; it is the product. Second, the open-market signups the program was designed to attract converted at 1.8 percent, while the roughly 1,100 signups who came from developers at existing Kestrel merchant customers converted at 14 percent. The channel thesis — that self-serve would win new-to-Kestrel companies — is the part that failed. The incidental discovery — that existing customers' developers will self-serve their way into paid expansion — is the part that worked, and nobody planned for it.

The support load is the program's most corrosive feature. The commitment to deflect free-tier support did not survive contact with reality: free users generate approximately 900 tickets per month, about 30 percent of total support volume, from a population contributing under 15 percent of even the optimistic revenue projection. Support estimates the fully loaded cost at $60,000 to $70,000 per month, borne by a budget funded from paid customers. This is an unbudgeted cross-subsidy of roughly $800,000 annualized that appears in no program financials, and the board should read the program's true 2025 cost as closer to $2.7 million than the $2.1 million on the books.

### What the Board Is Being Asked to Decide

The board is asked to choose between two options, both of which end the current model. Option A is a full sunset: close the free tier by the end of the second quarter, migrate the 380 paid accounts to standard commercial terms, and redirect the team. Option B, recommended by the review board, sunsets the unrestricted free tier on the same schedule but retains a refocused program: a 30-day sandbox with no support entitlement replacing the open free tier; the paid tier repositioned as an expansion product marketed to developers inside existing merchant accounts, where conversion ran at 14 percent; a team reduced from eleven to four; and a 2026 budget of $700,000 gated on reaching 700 total paid accounts by June 30, with automatic wind-down to Option A if the gate is missed. Under either option, the support cross-subsidy ends in the second quarter, and finance should book the associated relief in the support budget.

### Exposure If the Decision Waits Another Quarter

The exposure here is not a cliff; it is a steady, quantifiable bleed and a compounding credibility cost. Each quarter of the status quo costs approximately $525,000 in direct program spend plus roughly $200,000 in unbudgeted support subsidy — three-quarters of a million dollars a quarter to sustain a model the evidence says does not convert. A quarter's delay also worsens the eventual sunset: the free-tier population is growing at 700 to 900 signups a month, and every cohort added is a cohort that must later be migrated or disappointed, with the developer-community friction that entails. Finally, delay costs the one asset the program produced. The 14 percent conversion finding among existing customers' developers is a live commercial opportunity in the 2026 selling year; deferring the decision to April means the refocused motion, if the board chooses it, misses the first half entirely. This is a decision that gets more expensive and less useful every month it is not made, without ever forcing itself onto the agenda. That is precisely why it belongs on this one.

## Executive Summary 4 of 4: Core Ledger Migration

**Program:** Core Ledger Migration ("Project Granite")
**Review period:** Calendar year 2025
**Date of summary:** January 12, 2026

### Finding and Recommendation

The core ledger migration is 51 percent over budget, 61 percent complete, and — most importantly for the board's purposes — no longer optional in either direction. The program was approved at $7.5 million and has spent $11.3 million. It has migrated 61 percent of accounts to the new ledger, which means the company now runs two ledgers in a dual-write configuration, and that configuration produced eleven reconciliation breaks in the fourth quarter alone, two of which required manual settlement totaling $1.4 million. The manual settlements were recovered without merchant loss, but the fact that a payments company had to hand-reconcile $1.4 million because its two ledgers disagreed should be understood as the program's most important single data point. The dual-ledger state is not a waypoint that can be safely inhabited. It is the most dangerous configuration this migration passes through, and the company is parked in it.

The recommendation is to finish — but not on the current program's terms. The review board recommends the board approve a recapitalized completion plan of $4.8 million, a re-baselined completion date of December 2026, and three governance changes as conditions of the funding: independent monthly reconciliation reporting from Finance directly to the audit committee, bypassing the program; a dedicated reconciliation-integrity workstream with authority to halt migration waves; and replacement of the program's single-cutover-per-quarter cadence with smaller, more frequent, individually reversible migration waves. What the board should not do is either fund the program as currently structured, which has twice produced estimates it then exceeded, or pause it, which is superficially prudent and substantively the most dangerous option available, for reasons this summary sets out.

### What the Program Was Funded to Do

The board approved Project Granite in mid-2023 at $7.5 million over thirty months. The mandate was to replace Kestrel's original transaction ledger — a system built in the company's first years, scaled far beyond its design assumptions, and dependent on a shrinking group of engineers who understand its settlement logic — with a modern double-entry ledger designed for the current transaction volume and for auditability requirements the old system meets only through manual compensating controls. The approval case rested on three arguments: the old ledger was approaching genuine scale limits, with settlement batch windows lengthening measurably year over year; the compensating controls around it were consuming increasing audit and finance effort and drawing pointed comments in successive external audits; and every future product the company wanted to build — including the onboarding and developer programs elsewhere in this packet — was constrained by the old ledger's integration surface. The plan called for a dual-write transition period in which both ledgers record every transaction, with accounts migrated in quarterly waves and continuous reconciliation between the two systems, followed by decommissioning of the old ledger. The dual-write period was planned at nine months. It has now run nineteen.

### What It Actually Delivered

The engineering achievement is real and should be stated before the overrun is. The new ledger exists, is in production, and correctly processes the 61 percent of accounts migrated to it — roughly $54 million of annual recurring revenue's worth of transaction flow — at latency and settlement performance materially better than the old system. The migration tooling works. The waves that completed, completed. This is not a program that built nothing; it is a program that built the right thing at the wrong cost and pace, and is now stalled at the worst possible altitude.

The overrun decomposes into three roughly equal parts. About a third came from underestimating the old ledger's undocumented behavior: settlement edge cases, some dating to individual merchant accommodations made a decade ago, that had to be discovered by reconciliation failure and then replicated or deliberately retired one at a time. About a third came from the extended dual-write period itself, which was costed for nine months and has run nineteen, with the full reconciliation and dual-infrastructure burden continuing every additional month. The final third came from scope the original estimate simply missed, principally the rework of downstream reporting and finance-close processes that consumed most of two quarters. The program produced a revised estimate at the start of 2025 and exceeded that too, which is why the governance changes attached to this recommendation are conditions rather than suggestions.

The reconciliation breaks require unvarnished description. Dual-write reconciliation compares every transaction's record in both ledgers daily. Through the first half of 2025 breaks were rare and trivially resolved. In the fourth quarter there were eleven, a step-change in frequency that correlates with the migration of more complex merchant accounts in the September wave. Nine were resolved by automated replay. Two were not: divergences in multi-party settlement logic that required Finance to manually determine and settle correct balances totaling $1.4 million. Root causes for both are identified and patched, and no merchant funds were lost or delayed beyond contractual windows. But the trend line matters more than the resolutions: break frequency is rising as account complexity rises, the remaining 39 percent of accounts are on average the most complex in the book, and the manual-settlement events consumed senior finance capacity during quarter close. Deborah Yankelovich's assessment is that the current state is defensible to auditors and regulators today precisely because it is temporary; a dual-ledger configuration that persists indefinitely, with recurring manual settlements, will not remain defensible.

### What the Board Is Being Asked to Decide

The board is asked to approve a recapitalized completion plan: $4.8 million of additional funding — bringing total program cost to $16.1 million against the original $7.5 million — a re-baselined completion of December 2026 for full migration and old-ledger decommissioning, and the three governance conditions named above: independent reconciliation reporting to the audit committee, a reconciliation-integrity workstream empowered to halt waves, and a shift to small reversible migration waves in place of quarterly cutovers. The estimate has been rebuilt bottom-up by Gustavo Peralta's organization with the remaining accounts individually complexity-scored, and reviewed by an external firm engaged by this review board specifically because the program's own prior estimates failed twice. The external review characterizes the new estimate as achievable with modest contingency, which is included in the $4.8 million.

The alternatives were examined and are presented for completeness. Continuing on the current structure and run rate is projected to finish later and cost more than the recapitalized plan, with the governance weaknesses intact. Pausing the migration does not pause its costs: the dual-write burden — the infrastructure, the reconciliation operation, and the break risk — continues in full during any pause, at roughly $340,000 a month, while delivering no progress toward ending it. Reverting to the old ledger would mean un-migrating 61 percent of accounts across a system boundary in the direction the tooling was never built to travel, into a system whose scale limits were the reason for the program. Neither pause nor reversal reduces risk; both extend the company's stay in the configuration that generates it.

### Exposure If the Decision Waits Another Quarter

A quarter's delay costs approximately $1 million in continued dual-state burn with nothing to show for it, but the money is the lesser exposure. The greater one is that every month in the dual-ledger state is another month of exposure to the failure mode that has already occurred twice at $1.4 million of manual settlement, against an account population whose remaining complexity makes further breaks more likely, not less. The fourth-quarter trend gives no reason to believe the current state is stable; it gives specific reason to believe it degrades. There is additionally a disclosure and audit dimension: the external auditors have observed the compensating controls around this configuration in two successive audits, and Compliance's judgment is that a funded, dated completion plan approved in January materially changes the character of that conversation, whereas an unresolved program status in April invites escalation of the audit language. The company chose this migration because the old ledger was becoming untenable. That judgment was correct. The only untenable position it has created is the one in the middle — and the exposure of waiting a quarter is a quarter longer in the middle.
