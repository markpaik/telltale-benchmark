# Hinge

A hinge has leaves. Not sides, not panels, not plates — leaves, the same word we hand to a book, a tree, and gold beaten thin enough to read a newspaper through. The leaves are drilled along one edge, and the drilled edge is rolled into knuckles, and the knuckles of one leaf interlace with the knuckles of the other, and down through the tunnel they make together you drive a pin.

Everything depends on the pin. Pull it out and you have two pieces of scrap that happen to fit.

I have been thinking for some time that a hinge is the only machine whose entire purpose is to permit separation without permitting departure. A wheel lets things leave. A lever lets things move. A hinge says: go, but not far, and come back along the same arc you left by. It is a device for controlled disagreement. The two leaves are never going to agree about which way to face; the pin is not asking them to.

---

There is a kind of hinge with no pin at all.

If you take polypropylene and mold a groove into it — thin, maybe half a millimeter — the plastic on either side stays rigid and the groove becomes a joint. This is a living hinge, and you have opened a thousand of them: the lid on a box of mints, the flip-top on a shampoo bottle, the clamshell that held some small hard thing you needed. They are called living because they are made of one continuous piece of material. There is no seam. The hinge is not attached to the object; it *is* the object, locally thinned.

Here is the part I love. The first few times you flex a living hinge, the polymer chains inside the fold, which had been lying every which way, are dragged into alignment by the strain. They line up along the axis of bending. The hinge gets *stronger* the first several times it is used. A well-made one will open and close a million times.

I want to be careful not to make too much of this. But I will make some of it.

---

Bivalves — mussels, clams, oysters, scallops — hinge along a ligament made of a protein called abductin, which is rubbery in a way that no other animal tissue quite manages. The ligament is compressed when the shell is shut, and like any compressed spring it wants to release. Which means that the resting state of a mussel is *open*.

The shell does not fall shut. It falls open. To be closed, the animal has to hold itself closed, with a muscle, continuously, using energy it has to get from somewhere. Closure is labor. That is why a pot of mussels comes to the table gaping: the heat kills the adductor, the muscle lets go, and the ligament does what it has been trying to do all along.

I first encountered this fact as trivia and have not been able to put it down since. Somewhere in the design of the animal, someone decided that being sealed against the world would be the expensive option — that it would cost something every second, and that the moment the creature stopped paying, it would open.

---

Your skull sits on the top vertebra, which is called the atlas, because it carries a world. The atlas sits on the second vertebra, the axis, which has a peg sticking up through it like a pin through a knuckle.

Yes lives in the upper joint. When you nod, the skull rocks on the atlas. No lives in the lower one. When you shake your head, the atlas and the skull turn together on that peg.

So assent and refusal are not opposites in the body. They are neighbors, a centimeter apart, using different hardware. You can perform them simultaneously if you want to, and people do, all the time, and we have a word for the resulting expression: rueful.

---

Astronomers looking at the moon do not look at the bright part. The bright part is a flat glare, a plate of light, geologically illegible. They look at the terminator — the line where day is passing into night — because there the sun is low and every ridge throws a shadow the length of a county. All the topography of the moon, its craters and rilles and the ruined rims of things that hit it, is visible only in that narrow band, and the band moves, so what you can see depends entirely on when you look.

The moon is best understood at the place where it is coming apart into light and dark. This is not a metaphor I am imposing. This is observational practice. Nobody points a telescope at the full moon expecting to learn anything.

---

In a Petrarchan sonnet the eighth line ends and the ninth begins and something turns. The Italians called it the *volta*, the turn, and it is not decoration — it is the structural fact of the form. Eight lines of problem, six of response; eight of weather, six of what the weather did to somebody. The poem swings on line nine like a door.

Every art form I can think of has located its hinge and made a fetish of it. The bridge of a song. The cut in a film, which Walter Murch argued we tolerate at all because we already blink, already chop our own vision into shots. The turn in a joke, where the setup's furniture gets rearranged in a quarter second. The pause before a name is read out.

We do not seem to be able to make a thing that is all one piece. Or rather: we can, and it is called drone, and it has its uses, but the meaning tends to arrive when something is allowed to be two things joined at a movable point.

---

The word *hinge* comes down from Middle English *heng*, and behind it stands the verb to *hang*. A hinge is a hanger. It is the thing from which the door depends — depends, from *pendere*, to hang, which is also where we get pendant and pending and suspense. Everything hanging is unresolved. Everything unresolved is hanging.

And a door that is neither open nor shut is *ajar*, which people assume is a fancy word for slightly and which is actually a fossil. It comes from *on char*: Old English *cerr*, a turn. Ajar means *on the turn*. A door left ajar has not been left in a position. It has been left in a verb. Somebody went through it and did not finish going through it, and the door is still, grammatically, in motion.

---

I find that I trust hinged things more than solid ones, and I have been trying to work out whether that is a real preference or a flattering one.

Here is what I can defend. A solid thing has no way of being wrong that isn't catastrophic; it holds, and then it snaps, and there is no third state. A hinged thing has a whole vocabulary of partial positions. It can be wrong by fifteen degrees and correct itself. Its weakest point — the thinned groove, the drilled edge, the ligament — is the exact point at which it does its work, and it is the only part of the structure that gets stronger from being used.

And a hinge is honest about what it is for. It does not pretend to be a wall. It admits, by existing, that this thing here and that thing there are going to have to move relative to each other, forever, and that the plan is not to prevent this but to give it an axis.

I would like to be the pin. Failing that, the groove. Failing that, a person going slowly through a door and stopping, so that for a while afterward the door is on the turn, and anyone in the hall can see that someone was here, and that they left the way open behind them.
