# Cimarron Valley Health System

## Proposal to the Board of Directors: Consolidation of Obstetric Services and Development of an Inpatient Behavioral Health Unit at Bison Ridge Critical Access Hospital

**Submitted to:** Board of Directors, Cimarron Valley Health System
**Submitted by:** Marisol Trevino, Chief Executive Officer
**Reviewed by:** Finance Committee, September 18, 2026
**Board action requested:** October 2, 2026
**Total capital requested:** $23,700,000

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Management proposes that the Board approve a $23.7 million capital program that (1) consolidates all inpatient obstetric services onto the Enid campus and closes the obstetric unit at Bison Ridge Critical Access Hospital, and (2) converts the vacated Bison Ridge space into a 14-bed licensed inpatient behavioral health unit, opening approximately 26 months after Board approval.

This proposal is brought to the Board now because three conditions have converged and none of them will improve with delay. First, the Bison Ridge obstetric program delivered 96 babies last year against a break-even volume of 240, and two of its four obstetricians will depart when their contracts end in June 2027. The unit is projected to lose $3.1 million next year even before accounting for the cost of replacing departing physicians in a recruitment market that has not produced a rural obstetrician candidate for this facility in three years. Second, a March 2026 CMS validation survey cited nurse staffing on the Bison Ridge medical-surgical floor, a deficiency directly connected to the thin, unpredictable staffing demands of a low-volume obstetric service that requires 24/7 readiness regardless of census. Third, the Enid emergency department logged approximately 1,400 psychiatric boarding hours last year — an average of 31 hours per patient held in an emergency bay awaiting an inpatient psychiatric bed, with the nearest program in Oklahoma City. Psychiatric boarding consumes emergency capacity, degrades care for every patient in the department, exposes the system to regulatory and liability risk, and is the single largest unmet inpatient need in our service area.

The system's operating margin is negative 2.8 percent. We do not have the financial capacity to sustain a $3.1 million annual loss on a service delivering fewer than two babies per week, and we do not have the clinical capacity to staff that service safely as its medical staff departs. At the same time, we have a fully built, licensed, staffed hospital shell at Bison Ridge and a documented regional demand for behavioral health beds. The proposal before the Board matches the asset to the need.

The plan would be financed by $14.0 million in tax-exempt revenue bonds, a $6.2 million foundation capital campaign of which $2.1 million is currently pledged, and $3.5 million from system operations phased over the construction period. Chief Nursing Officer Danielle Kirkpatrick has developed a redeployment plan under which all 31 Bison Ridge obstetric and cross-covering nurses are offered positions within the system, and 22 behavioral health positions, including a psychiatrist, are recruited over the 26-month development period. The behavioral health unit is projected to reach breakeven in its second full year of operation and to generate a positive contribution margin of approximately $1.4 million annually at stabilized census.

Management does not present this proposal as free of harm. Chief Medical Officer Rajiv Mehrotra estimates that after consolidation, 41 percent of the service area population would sit more than 45 minutes from a delivery room. The county commission and a citizens group have petitioned against closure, and Board Chair Pahsetopah has received those petitions and shared them with management in full. Section 10 of this document addresses the access consequences directly and describes the mitigation program — prenatal risk stratification, an emergency delivery protocol at Bison Ridge, EMS obstetric training and equipment, transportation and lodging support, and expanded prenatal clinic hours in the northwest service area — that management commits to fund and operate as a condition of this proposal.

The specific decision requested of the Board on October 2 is set out in Section 12. In summary: authorization of the $23.7 million program, authorization to issue up to $14.0 million in tax-exempt bonds, approval of the obstetric consolidation with closure of the Bison Ridge unit no earlier than completion of the mitigation program milestones, and direction to management to file the required licensure and regulatory applications for the behavioral health unit.

## 2. Background: The Situation That Requires a Decision

### 2.1 Obstetric volumes at Bison Ridge

Bison Ridge Critical Access Hospital delivered 96 babies in the most recent fiscal year. Volume has declined steadily for six years:

| Fiscal Year | Deliveries | Change |
|---|---|---|
| FY2021 | 178 | — |
| FY2022 | 161 | (9.6%) |
| FY2023 | 148 | (8.1%) |
| FY2024 | 127 | (14.2%) |
| FY2025 | 109 | (14.2%) |
| FY2026 | 96 | (11.9%) |

The break-even volume for the unit, calculated on fully loaded costs including 24/7 nursing coverage, anesthesia availability, surgical readiness for cesarean delivery, and physician call, is approximately 240 deliveries per year. The unit is operating at 40 percent of breakeven and the trend is not flattening. The decline reflects demographics — the childbearing-age population of the northwest service area has contracted roughly 2 percent annually — and patient choice: an increasing share of area families with commercial insurance are already electing to deliver in Enid or Oklahoma City. At 96 deliveries per year, the unit averages fewer than two births per week, with multi-day stretches of zero census followed by simultaneous deliveries that strain the on-call structure.

Low delivery volume is not only a financial problem; it is a safety problem. National evidence consistently associates very-low-volume obstetric units with worse outcomes in obstetric emergencies, because teams that manage a postpartum hemorrhage or shoulder dystocia twice a year cannot maintain the fluency of teams that manage them monthly. Dr. Mehrotra's assessment, which he will present to the Board in person, is that the current volume is below the threshold at which he can attest to the sustained competency environment the medical staff bylaws require.

### 2.2 Physician departures

Two of the four obstetricians on the Bison Ridge medical staff have given notice that they will not renew their contracts when they expire in June 2027. Their stated reasons are call burden — a 1-in-4 call schedule that becomes 1-in-2 with any vacancy — and professional isolation. The remaining two physicians cannot sustain the unit alone; a 1-in-2 perpetual call schedule is neither safe nor recruitable. The system has run a continuous rural obstetrician search for three years through two national firms and has not produced a single candidate willing to accept the position. Locum tenens coverage for a full obstetric call panel would cost an estimated $1.8 to $2.2 million annually above current physician costs, and locum-staffed obstetric units carry their own continuity and safety risks. Absent Board action, the practical reality is that the unit closes in June 2027 anyway — abruptly, without a transition plan, without mitigation, and without a productive reuse of the space.

### 2.3 Financial position

The system's consolidated operating margin is negative 2.8 percent. The Bison Ridge obstetric unit lost $2.7 million in FY2026 and is projected to lose $3.1 million in FY2027 as volume declines further and locum coverage begins. Over a five-year horizon, continuing the unit as currently configured would consume $15 to $18 million in operating losses — a sum comparable to the entire capital cost of this proposal, spent with no asset, no new service, and no improvement in the underlying trajectory. A system running a negative margin cannot absorb that. Every dollar of avoidable loss at Bison Ridge is a dollar unavailable for nursing wages, equipment replacement, and the clinic network.

### 2.4 The CMS validation survey

In March 2026, a CMS validation survey cited nurse staffing on the Bison Ridge medical-surgical floor. The finding, in summary, was that on multiple surveyed shifts, medical-surgical staffing fell below the levels required by the facility's own staffing plan because nurses were pulled to cover obstetric activity. This is a structural consequence of maintaining a 24/7 obstetric service on a 96-delivery volume: the unit cannot justify dedicated staffing at all hours, so it draws on the medical-surgical pool, and the medical-surgical floor absorbs the deficit. The system submitted an accepted plan of correction, and interim compliance has been achieved through premium-pay float coverage at an annualized cost of approximately $640,000. The plan of correction is a bridge, not a solution. A repeat finding would jeopardize the facility's Medicare participation, which for a critical access hospital is existential. The consolidation proposed here resolves the root cause: it removes the unpredictable obstetric staffing draw and allows Bison Ridge medical-surgical staffing to be planned and held stable.

### 2.5 Psychiatric boarding in the Enid emergency department

The Enid emergency department held psychiatric patients for approximately 1,400 boarding hours last year — roughly 45 patients averaging 31 hours each, with individual cases exceeding 90 hours. These are patients in mental health crisis, many under emergency detention orders, held in emergency bays because no inpatient psychiatric bed exists closer than Oklahoma City, and Oklahoma City facilities routinely decline transfers for capacity or payer reasons. The consequences are severe and measurable: emergency bays occupied by boarders are unavailable to medical emergencies; behavioral health patients receive holding rather than treatment; staff injuries and security costs rise; ambulance diversion hours increase; and the system carries regulatory exposure under EMTALA and accreditation standards for the safety of the boarding environment. Community stakeholders — law enforcement, the county mental health authority, schools, and primary care physicians across our clinic network — consistently identify inpatient behavioral health as the region's most acute service gap. Our own clinic data show more than 600 patients annually referred out of the area for psychiatric care.

## 3. The Proposal

### 3.1 Consolidation of obstetric services at Enid

All inpatient obstetric services would be consolidated on the Enid campus. The Enid hospital delivered 611 babies last year with capacity for approximately 850; absorbing Bison Ridge volume requires no bed construction, only modest renovation ($1.1 million, included in the capital budget) to add two labor-delivery-recovery rooms and refresh the triage area. The Enid unit operates with dedicated obstetric nursing, 24/7 in-house anesthesia, a level II nursery, and a five-physician obstetric group plus two certified nurse-midwives. Consolidated volume of approximately 700 deliveries places the combined program well above every recognized volume-safety threshold and creates a call schedule that is genuinely recruitable — the obstetric group has indicated it can recruit a sixth physician at consolidated volume, and one of the two departing Bison Ridge obstetricians has expressed interest in joining the Enid group rather than leaving the region.

The Bison Ridge obstetric unit would close no earlier than month 10 of the program and only after completion of the mitigation milestones in Section 10. Bison Ridge would retain its emergency department, medical-surgical beds, swing beds, laboratory, imaging, and outpatient services, and would retain the capability to manage an imminent emergency delivery under a formal protocol described in Section 10.

### 3.2 The 14-bed inpatient behavioral health unit at Bison Ridge

The vacated obstetric space — approximately 11,800 square feet — would be converted to a 14-bed adult inpatient behavioral health unit. The space is well suited: it is a discrete wing with controlled access points, its own nursing station, and adjacency to the emergency department. Conversion requires ligature-resistant fixtures throughout, anti-barricade doors, impact-resistant glazing, a secured outdoor courtyard, a dedicated intake and de-escalation suite, seclusion room meeting CMS Conditions of Participation, upgraded HVAC and nurse-call systems, and full furniture and equipment fit-out. Construction cost is estimated at $16.4 million including contingency, based on schematic-level pricing from two firms with recent Oklahoma behavioral health construction experience.

The unit would serve adults 18 and over with acute psychiatric conditions — the population currently boarding in the Enid emergency department. Average length of stay is projected at 6.5 days; at 80 percent stabilized occupancy the unit would serve approximately 620 admissions annually. Demand analysis based on Enid ED boarding volume, current out-of-area referrals, regional ED transfer data from neighboring counties, and Oklahoma Department of Mental Health and Substance Abuse Services need estimates indicates regional demand of 20 to 26 beds; a 14-bed unit will operate at or near capacity. Payer mix is projected at 48 percent Medicaid, 22 percent Medicare, 18 percent commercial, and 12 percent uninsured/other, with reimbursement modeled at current Oklahoma Medicaid inpatient psychiatric rates. Management has engaged the state authority regarding available bed-expansion incentive funding and community mental health block grant support; any award would reduce the operating subsidy in years one and two but is not assumed in the base projections.

**Regulatory note on critical access status:** Federal law permits a critical access hospital to operate a distinct-part psychiatric unit of up to 10 beds paid under the inpatient psychiatric facility prospective payment system, in addition to its 25 acute beds. A 14-bed unit at Bison Ridge therefore requires either (a) a 10-bed distinct-part unit configuration with 4 beds shelled for future use pending regulatory change, or (b) conversion analysis under the Rural Emergency Hospital or other designation. Management's base plan is the 10-bed distinct-part unit at opening with 4 beds built and shelled, and management will present the final licensure configuration to the Board for confirmation at the design-completion milestone in month 8. All financial projections in this document are presented for both the 10-bed opening configuration and the 14-bed full configuration; the 10-bed configuration reaches breakeven in year three rather than year two.

## 4. Staffing Plan

Chief Nursing Officer Danielle Kirkpatrick has developed and will oversee the workforce plan. Its governing principle, which management asks the Board to adopt as a commitment: **no involuntary layoff of any Bison Ridge nurse as a result of this consolidation.**

### 4.1 Redeployment of the 31 Bison Ridge nurses

The 31 nurses currently staffing or cross-covering the Bison Ridge obstetric unit will each receive an individual placement offer at current or better compensation, with a $5,000 retention-and-transition payment for those who remain through their unit's closure date. Projected placements, based on individual interviews already conducted with 27 of the 31:

- **10–12 nurses** to Bison Ridge medical-surgical and emergency positions, permanently resolving the CMS-cited staffing deficit without premium-pay floats.
- **8–10 nurses** to behavioral health positions on the new unit, with a fully funded psychiatric nursing transition program (12-week didactic and precepted curriculum developed with Northern Oklahoma College) and a $6,000 completion bonus.
- **5–7 nurses** to Enid obstetric positions, with mileage or relocation support.
- **2–4 nurses** to clinic and outpatient positions in the northwest service area, including the expanded prenatal clinic described in Section 10.

Attrition through retirement or relocation is expected to absorb any residual; if any nurse cannot be placed in a comparable role, severance at two weeks per year of service will apply, budgeted at $250,000 within the transition reserve.

### 4.2 Behavioral health recruitment: 22 positions

| Role | FTEs | Recruitment approach |
|---|---|---|
| Psychiatrist (medical director) | 1.0 | National search, launched at Board approval; income guarantee and loan repayment; telepsychiatry backup contract as bridge and for call coverage |
| Advanced practice provider (psych-certified) | 2.0 | Regional recruitment; OU-Tulsa and OSU program pipelines |
| Registered nurses (psychiatric) | 9.0 | 8–10 from OB redeployment plus external hires; transition curriculum |
| Behavioral health technicians | 6.0 | Local hire; paid training program with NOC |
| Licensed clinical social workers / therapists | 2.0 | Regional recruitment; loan repayment via HRSA shortage designation |
| Activity/occupational therapist | 1.0 | Regional recruitment |
| Intake/utilization coordinator | 1.0 | Internal posting |

The psychiatrist search is the critical path. The nearest inpatient program is in Oklahoma City, which cuts both ways: it is why the beds are needed, and it is why recruitment is hard. Mitigations: (1) the search begins at Board approval, giving 24 months before opening; (2) the compensation package is benchmarked at the 75th percentile with medical directorship stipend and federal loan repayment through the area's Health Professional Shortage Area designation; (3) a contracted telepsychiatry partnership — letters of interest are in hand from two established providers — supplies attending coverage capable of supporting the unit at opening if the on-site search runs long, and supplies night and weekend call permanently. The unit does not open without credentialed psychiatric attending coverage in place, whether on-site, hybrid, or telehealth-based; but the opening date is not hostage to a single hire.

### 4.3 Licensure and regulatory steps

1. **Oklahoma State Department of Health:** amendment of the Bison Ridge hospital license to add the distinct-part psychiatric unit; architectural plan review and approval before construction; life-safety and licensure survey before occupancy. Filed month 4; plan approval expected month 9.
2. **CMS:** distinct-part psychiatric unit certification under the IPF prospective payment system, including attestation of compliance with special Conditions of Participation for psychiatric hospitals/units (B-tag requirements for treatment planning, medical records, staffing). Application filed at licensure; certification survey after opening with retroactive payment provisions confirmed.
3. **Oklahoma Department of Mental Health and Substance Abuse Services:** certification for emergency detention and court-ordered treatment admissions — essential, since a substantial share of boarded patients are under detention orders.
4. **Oklahoma Health Care Authority:** Medicaid enrollment of the unit and rate confirmation.
5. **Accreditation:** extension of the system's accreditation to the new unit, including ligature-risk and suicide-prevention environment-of-care review.
6. **CMS notification** regarding the obstetric service closure and confirmation that the change does not affect Bison Ridge's critical access designation (it does not; CAH status does not require obstetric services), plus state-required public notice of service discontinuation, which management proposes to exceed with the community process in Section 10.

## 7. Alternatives Considered

The Board should approve this plan only against a fair comparison with the alternatives. Management evaluated five.

**7.1 Status quo — continue the Bison Ridge obstetric unit.** Requires replacing two obstetricians in a market that has produced zero candidates in three years, or perpetual locum coverage at $1.8–2.2 million annually on top of a $3.1 million projected loss. Five-year cost: $15–18 million with no asset created, declining volume, unresolved CMS staffing deficit, and unaddressed safety concerns at very low volume. Management's judgment is that this alternative is not actually available: the unit closes in June 2027 regardless, and the only choice is whether it closes with a plan or without one. **Rejected.**

**7.2 Freestanding birth center at Bison Ridge.** A midwifery-led birth center serving low-risk pregnancies would preserve some local delivery capability at lower cost than a hospital obstetric unit. Management examined this seriously. It fails on three grounds. First, safety: national birth center standards require reliable rapid transfer to a hospital with cesarean capability; a 52-mile transfer distance places Bison Ridge outside accepted transfer-time parameters for intrapartum emergencies, and Dr. Mehrotra cannot support a model in which a laboring patient with an emergent complication is nearly an hour from an operating room by design. Second, volume: only 40–55 of the 96 current deliveries would meet low-risk eligibility criteria, well below the roughly 200 births needed for a birth center's own sustainability, producing a projected loss of $700,000–900,000 annually. Third, staffing: Oklahoma's certified nurse-midwife supply is thin, and the model would recreate the recruitment problem it is meant to solve. **Rejected**, though the expanded prenatal clinic in Section 10 adopts the birth-center concept's strongest element — local midwifery-led prenatal and postpartum care — without the intrapartum risk.

**7.3 Telehealth obstetrics with local delivery.** Remote maternal-fetal monitoring and telehealth specialist consultation supporting continued local deliveries. Telehealth strengthens prenatal care and management adopts it fully for that purpose (Section 10). But telehealth cannot perform a cesarean section, manage a hemorrhage, or resuscitate a newborn; it does not resolve the physician departure, the call schedule, the volume-safety problem, or the staffing citation. As a means of sustaining the delivery unit, it addresses the wrong constraint. **Rejected as a substitute; adopted as a mitigation component.**

**7.4 Close the obstetric unit without the behavioral health project.** Saves $3.1 million annually with no capital outlay. But it leaves 11,800 square feet of hospital space dark, weakens the case for Bison Ridge's long-term viability as an institution, does nothing for 1,400 hours of psychiatric boarding, forfeits the strongest available answer to community concern about the facility's future, and abandons a service line projected to contribute $1.4 million annually. It is the cheaper decision and the worse one. **Rejected.**

**7.5 Build the behavioral health unit on the Enid campus instead.** Placing beds adjacent to the ED that generates the demand has logic. It fails on cost and consequence: Enid has no comparable vacant space, so the project becomes new construction at an estimated $21–24 million for the unit alone versus $16.4 million for renovation; and it leaves Bison Ridge with vacant space, a diminished mission, and a community that has lost a service and gained nothing. Locating the unit at Bison Ridge costs less, preserves 30-plus jobs in that community, anchors the facility's future, and serves a patient population — behavioral health admissions averaging 6.5-day stays — for whom a 52-mile distance is clinically manageable in a way it is not for a woman in labor. Transfer logistics from the Enid ED are addressed by a dedicated secure transport contract budgeted in unit operating expense. **Rejected.**

## 8. What Could Go Wrong

Management owes the Board a candid risk register, not only a plan.

**8.1 A patient is harmed by distance.** This is the gravest risk. With 41 percent of the service area more than 45 minutes from a delivery room, a precipitous delivery or obstetric emergency en route is a foreseeable event. The mitigation program in Section 10 exists for exactly this reason, and management proposes that the Board treat those mitigations as binding conditions of closure, with quarterly outcome reporting (transfer times, en-route deliveries, adverse events) to the Board's Quality Committee. It must also be said plainly: the status quo carries its own version of this risk — a very-low-volume unit facing an emergency it manages twice a year, soon staffed by locums or two exhausted physicians, is not the safe alternative it appears to be.

**8.2 Behavioral health recruitment fails.** The psychiatrist is the single point of failure, mitigated by the 24-month runway, the telepsychiatry bridge, and the rule that opening waits for credentialed coverage. Nursing risk is mitigated by the redeployment pipeline, which supplies up to ten of the nine budgeted RN positions from staff we already employ.

**8.3 The campaign falls short.** Addressed in Section 5.2: construction release is gated on a $4.0 million pledge threshold, and the bond authorization carries $2.0 million of upsize capacity within covered debt service.

**8.4 Construction overruns.** Behavioral health construction is specialized and ligature-resistant materials carry volatile pricing. Mitigations: 12 percent construction contingency plus $1.37 million owner's contingency (combined 18 percent of construction cost), guaranteed-maximum-price contracting, and two experienced bidders.

**8.5 Behavioral health census or reimbursement underperforms.** Demand analysis supports the unit, but Medicaid rate changes or competitor entry could compress margins. Downside sensitivity at 65 percent occupancy shows a stabilized contribution of approximately $250,000 — breakeven rather than $1.4 million — and the overall program still improves system performance by more than $3.5 million annually because the OB loss avoidance is independent of unit performance.

**8.6 Regulatory: the 10-bed CAH limitation.** Addressed in Section 3.2; the base financial case works at 10 beds, and 4 beds are shelled for expansion.

**8.7 Community opposition escalates.** Petitions from the county commission and a citizens group are before the Board. The risk is not only reputational; sustained opposition could affect the foundation campaign, county relationships, and workforce morale. Management's response is Section 10 and, more fundamentally, the substance of the plan itself: Bison Ridge does not shrink under this proposal — it gains a service line, retains its workforce, and secures its future. That case must be made in person, repeatedly, and honestly, including honesty about what is being lost.

**8.8 Interim staffing during transition.** Until closure at month 10, the obstetric unit must remain safe. The two remaining physicians have committed through the transition date; short-term locum coverage is budgeted at $400,000 within the transition reserve; and if physician coverage becomes unsafe before month 10, management will divert deliveries to Enid early with Board notification, prioritizing safety over schedule.

## 9. How the Combined Program Will Be Run

The behavioral health unit will operate as a distinct-part unit of Bison Ridge Critical Access Hospital under the Bison Ridge administrator, with the psychiatrist medical director reporting to Dr. Mehrotra and a dedicated nurse manager reporting through Ms. Kirkpatrick. A single systemwide transfer center will manage placement from the Enid ED and regional referrals, with a target of accepting Enid ED behavioral health patients within four hours of medical clearance — against a current average boarding time of 31 hours. The Quality Committee of the Board will receive quarterly dashboards covering: obstetric transfer and outcome metrics for the northwest service area; behavioral health census, boarding hours at Enid, restraint/seclusion rates, and 30-day readmissions; and Bison Ridge medical-surgical staffing compliance against the accepted plan of correction. A community advisory council for the northwest service area, described in Section 10, will receive the same obstetric access data.

## 10. Access, Community Impact, and Mitigation Commitments

### 10.1 The impact stated honestly

Dr. Mehrotra estimates that after consolidation, 41 percent of the service area population will live more than 45 minutes from a delivery room. Approximately 96 families per year who would have delivered at Bison Ridge will travel to Enid or elsewhere. For families without reliable transportation, and for the small number of precipitous or emergent labors, this is a real burden and a real risk. The county commission and a citizens group have petitioned the Board in opposition, and those petitions deserve a substantive answer, not a procedural one. The answer management offers is a funded, measurable mitigation program, proposed as a binding condition of closure.

### 10.2 Mitigation commitments

1. **Expanded northwest prenatal clinic.** Full prenatal and postpartum care at the Bison Ridge campus five days per week, staffed by a certified nurse-midwife and rotating Enid obstetricians, with telehealth maternal-fetal medicine consultation. Operational by month 6, four months before closure. No family loses local prenatal care; only the delivery itself moves.
2. **Risk stratification and delivery planning.** Every pregnancy in the northwest service area receives a documented delivery plan by 32 weeks, including transportation planning and, for higher-risk pregnancies, scheduled induction or planned early travel.
3. **Transportation and lodging support.** A funded program ($120,000/year within the mitigation budget) providing mileage reimbursement, ride arrangement, and — for patients beyond 45 minutes at 37-plus weeks who elect it — lodging near the Enid campus in the days before their due date, modeled on programs used in rural Western states.
4. **Emergency delivery capability at Bison Ridge.** The Bison Ridge ED will maintain and drill a precipitous-delivery and neonatal stabilization protocol, with delivery kits, neonatal resuscitation equipment and certification for all ED staff, and 24/7 obstetric telehealth consultation to Enid. This protocol will be certified complete before closure and drilled quarterly thereafter.
5. **EMS partnership.** $340,000 in capital for obstetric and neonatal transport equipment on northwest-area ambulances, plus funded EMS training in emergency delivery and neonatal care, and a direct-admission protocol so laboring patients transported by EMS bypass the Enid ED and go straight to labor and delivery.
6. **Community advisory council.** A standing council for the northwest service area — including county commission and citizens-group representatives — meeting quarterly with system leadership, receiving the obstetric access dashboard, and holding a standing agenda item before the Board's Quality Committee annually.
7. **Milestone-gated closure.** The obstetric unit will not close until items 1, 4, and 5 are certified complete by the Chief Medical Officer and reported to the Board.

The mitigation program is budgeted at approximately $700,000 annually in operations plus $340,000 in capital, and these amounts are already reflected in the financial projections in Section 3.3.

### 10.3 What Bison Ridge gains

The community conversation to date has centered on what closes. The Board and management must also be clear about what opens: a behavioral health service that northwest Oklahoma has never had; retention of all 31 nursing positions and creation of 22 new ones; permanent resolution of the staffing deficiency that threatens the hospital's Medicare certification; and a financial foundation that secures Bison Ridge's existence. The alternative future — the one that arrives by default in June 2027 — is an abrupt obstetric closure with no mitigation, an empty wing, and a critical access hospital with a weakening rationale. This proposal is, in management's judgment, the strongest available plan for Bison Ridge itself, and management commits to making that case in open community forums in the northwest service area during October and November regardless of the vote's outcome.

## 11. Summary of the Case

Why now: two of four obstetricians leave in June 2027 and cannot be replaced; the unit runs at 40 percent of break-even volume and will lose $3.1 million next year; a CMS citation traceable to the obstetric staffing draw threatens the facility's certification; and 1,400 hours of psychiatric boarding in the Enid ED represent the region's largest unmet need and this system's largest unmanaged risk. What it costs: $23.7 million, funded by $14 million in bonds, a $6.2 million campaign with construction gated on pledge progress, and $3.5 million from operations. What it returns: $3 to $5 million in annual operating improvement, restoration of the system to approximately breakeven, resolution of the survey finding, a recruitable and safer consolidated obstetric program, and 10 to 14 behavioral health beds where none exist within 90 miles. What could go wrong: distance-related obstetric risk, mitigated by a funded and milestone-gated program; recruitment, campaign, construction, and census risks, each with identified mitigations and a financial base case that survives the downside scenarios. What happens if the Board declines: the obstetric unit closes anyway in June 2027 — without mitigation, without redeployment, without reuse of the space, and without an answer for the patients boarding in the Enid emergency department.
