## Statement of Need

### The asset and the territory

The Cimarron Junction Rail District owns and holds all right-of-way, track, and structures on 38.0 route miles of short line railroad extending from the BNSF Railway interchange at Cimarron Junction (MP 0.0) northwest through Dunlap, Tolliver, Sherrard Flats, Aetna Wells, and Kepler to the end of track at Whitmore (MP 38.0), crossing portions of Woodward, Harper, and Woods Counties. The District is governed by a seven-member board appointed by the constituent counties and municipalities, employs four full-time staff, and operates on a $2.1 million annual budget. Freight service is performed under contract by Redbud Terminal Railway (RTR), a short line operator; the District is the infrastructure owner and is solely responsible for track and structure condition.

The line is the only rail access for nine active shippers: Tolliver Farmers Cooperative and Kepler Grain & Supply (grain elevators), Cimarron Gypsum Products (wallboard and stucco), High Plains Tower Works (wind-tower fabrication), Sherrard Ag Services (fertilizer), Whitmore Sand & Aggregate, Panhandle Pipe & Steel, Dunlap Feed & Nutrition, and Osage Salt & Chemical. These nine facilities employ 611 people directly. There is no competing rail line within 46 miles. The alternative to this railroad is US-270 and the county road network.

### Condition assessment

In August 2025 the District commissioned a full condition assessment of all 38 miles, performed by Chief Engineer Grigor Sarkisian, P.E., with an independent bridge review by a licensed structural consultant and a hi-rail geometry inspection. The findings are documented in the *2025 Cimarron Line Engineering Condition Assessment* (Attachment C) and are summarized here.

**Rail.** Fourteen and one-half miles of the main line remain on 85-pound ASCE section rail rolled in 1948 and laid in 39-foot jointed sticks. Measured vertical head loss on the high rail through curves exceeds 8/32 inch at 61 percent of the sampled locations, and gauge-face wear exceeds condemning limits at 14 locations. The rail is now in its 78th year of service against a design life of roughly 40 years under this tonnage. Over the trailing 36 months the District has recorded **41 service failures** — 23 broken joint bars, 11 broken rails or detected transverse defects, and 7 bolt-hole cracks — an average of one failure every 26 days. Each failure produces an unplanned outage averaging 9.4 hours.

**Slow orders.** As a direct consequence of rail and tie condition, the District maintains permanent 10 mile-per-hour restrictions (FRA Track Safety Standards Class 1) over **14.0 miles**, in two segments: MP 9.2 to MP 17.6 (8.4 miles) and MP 24.8 to MP 30.4 (5.6 miles). The balance of the line is Class 2 at 25 mph. The slow orders add 68 minutes to each one-way trip. Because RTR crews are subject to hours-of-service limits, the restrictions have forced the abandonment of same-day round trips beyond MP 24, and Kepler Grain & Supply and Osage Salt & Chemical are now served three days per week rather than five.

**Crossties.** Tie counts on the restricted segments show an average of 31 percent defective ties per mile against an FRA Class 2 requirement that no more than a defined number of non-effective ties occur in any 39-foot segment. Nine locations fail the clustering standard outright and are held out of service pending spot replacement.

**Ballast and drainage.** Ballast is fouled to 40 percent or greater by fines through 19.3 miles. Cross-section is deficient, shoulders are absent through eight miles, and three ditch reaches carry standing water after ordinary rainfall, producing pumping ties and repeat surfacing defects at the same locations each spring.

**Structures.** The line carries 11 bridges. Six timber pile trestles — at MP 10.3 (Coyote Creek), MP 13.9, MP 16.2 (Salt Draw), MP 25.1, MP 27.6, and MP 31.4 — were constructed between 1946 and 1952. All six are timber stringer spans on timber pile bents, with span lengths from 24 to 96 feet. Inspection under 49 CFR Part 237 documents pile section loss from decay at or above 30 percent at 47 of 208 piles, split and checked stringers on all six structures, and cap deterioration at 22 bents. **The governing bridge rating limits the entire line to 268,000 pounds gross weight on rail.** The North American interchange standard is 286,000 pounds.

**Grade crossings.** Thirteen crossings (nine public, four private) within the project limits have deteriorated timber or asphalt surfaces, degraded approaches, and inadequate flangeway depth. Three are on county roads maintained by the Woodward County Board of County Commissioners.

The cumulative judgment of the assessment is direct: the line is safe to operate at 10 mph under active restriction, but it is not capable of delivering competitive transit times or standard-weight cars, and the rate at which restrictions are spreading now exceeds the District's capacity to arrest them with routine maintenance funding.

### The volume case

Carloads have fallen from **3,400 in 2019 to 2,100 last year**, a decline of 38.2 percent. The District has analyzed this decline shipper by shipper rather than treating it as a general market condition, because the causes determine whether the traffic is recoverable.

| Cause of decline | Carloads lost, 2019 → last year |
|---|---|
| Light-loading of grain cars to respect the 268,000-lb limit | 385 |
| Transit-time loss / service-frequency reduction from slow orders | 344 |
| Closure of Aetna Wells Cooperative (2021, facility consolidation) | 260 |
| Drought-reduced hard red winter wheat receipts, 2022–2023 | 211 |
| Dimensional and heavy loads rerouted to truck by High Plains Tower Works | 100 |
| **Total** | **1,300** |

Two of these five causes — 729 carloads, or 56 percent of the total decline — are direct infrastructure failures that this project cures. The 268,000-pound limit forces the grain elevators to load approximately 4,600 fewer bushels per car than a 286,000-pound car permits, a payload penalty of roughly 15 percent that must be made up in additional cars or, more often, is simply diverted to truck. The wind-tower fabricator's heaviest nacelle and base-section loads cannot be routed over the timber structures at all.

Current carloads by shipper are: Cimarron Gypsum Products 610; Tolliver Farmers Cooperative 520; Kepler Grain & Supply 385; High Plains Tower Works 190; Sherrard Ag Services 145; Whitmore Sand & Aggregate 95; Panhandle Pipe & Steel 70; Dunlap Feed & Nutrition 55; Osage Salt & Chemical 30. Total 2,100.

### Highway and public consequence

Each rail carload displaces **3.5 truckloads**. The average truck alternative for freight originating or terminating on this line is a 137-mile one-way movement to the nearest Class I transload or market, or 274 vehicle-miles per truckload counting the empty return. Each carload therefore represents **959 avoided truck vehicle-miles**, of which the District's routing analysis assigns 78 percent to US-270.

At the current 2,100 carloads the line avoids 2,013,900 truck VMT annually. At 3,400 carloads it avoided 3,260,600. The 1,300 carloads lost since 2019 have placed approximately **1,246,700 additional truck vehicle-miles per year onto US-270 and the county road network** — roughly 4,550 additional heavy-truck trips through Woodward annually. Woodward County reports pavement distress on three farm-to-market routes that now carry grain traffic previously handled at Aetna Wells and Kepler.

### Consequence of no action

Without capital intervention, the District's engineering forecast projects that slow-order mileage grows from 14.0 to 21.5 miles by 2030 as the 85-pound rail on the adjacent segments reaches condemning limits, and that two of the six timber structures require load restriction below 268,000 pounds or embargo within four years. At 21.5 slow-order miles, RTR has advised in writing that service beyond MP 24 becomes uneconomic to provide at any frequency, which places 415 carloads and two shippers at immediate risk. The line's fixed cost structure does not scale downward; at approximately 1,600 carloads the District's operating budget no longer supports the maintenance program that keeps the remaining track in service, and the corridor enters the abandonment sequence familiar across the Great Plains.

This application is submitted at the point where capital investment still restores an asset rather than replaces one.

## Project Description

The Cimarron Corridor Restoration and Transload Initiative is a single, integrated capital program with four physical components, delivered over three construction seasons (2027–2029). All work occurs within existing District-owned right-of-way except the transload site, which is under an executed purchase option described below.

### Component 1 — Rail relay, 22.0 miles ($5,500,000)

The District will relay 22.0 miles of main line with 115-pound rail in two segments: **MP 8.0 to MP 19.2 (11.2 miles)** and **MP 23.0 to MP 33.8 (10.8 miles)**. These limits were chosen so that the relay envelope fully contains both slow-order segments (MP 9.2–17.6 and MP 24.8–30.4) with a running margin at each end, eliminating all 14.0 restricted miles and pre-empting the eight adjacent miles where the assessment projects the next restrictions.

Scope per mile includes: removal and disposal of 85-pound rail and other track material; installation of 115-pound relay-quality continuous welded rail in 1,440-foot strings, field-welded, with new elastic fasteners and tie plates; installation of 600 new 7-inch hardwood crossties per mile (13,200 total) plus 100 percent tie replacement in the nine clustered-defect locations; 2,000 tons of new AREMA No. 4 ballast per mile (44,000 tons total); shoulder construction and ditching through the eight miles now lacking cross-section; and full production surfacing, lining, and regulating with two post-construction stabilization passes.

Recovered 85-pound rail in serviceable condition will be reclaimed for use as yard and siding rail on the 16 miles outside the project limits, a use the District values at $214,000 and which is reflected as a credit in the budget.

**Design speed after relay is 25 mph (FRA Class 2) continuous over the 22 miles, eliminating 14.0 slow-order miles and 68 minutes of one-way transit time.**

### Component 2 — Bridge reconstruction, six structures ($1,140,000)

All six 1946–1952 timber trestles will be reconstructed to a Cooper E-80 live load rating, the standard required for unrestricted 286,000-pound operation. Following comparative design study, the selected approach at MP 10.3, MP 16.2, and MP 27.6 is full replacement with precast concrete box culverts and embankment, eliminating three bridges from the inventory entirely and removing their perpetual inspection and maintenance obligation. At MP 13.9, MP 25.1, and MP 31.4, where waterway opening requirements preclude culverting, the structures will be replaced in kind with steel-and-concrete spans on driven steel pile bents.

**On completion, the governing weight limit of the line rises from 268,000 to 286,000 pounds gross weight on rail**, and the District's Part 237 bridge inventory falls from 11 structures to 8.

### Component 3 — Cimarron Junction Transload Facility, 14 acres ($1,010,000)

The District will construct a 14-acre open transload facility on District-controlled land adjacent to the BNSF interchange at MP 1.4. Scope includes 4,900 track feet of new yard and loading track in three tracks with a run-around; 6.2 acres of compacted aggregate working surface; a 220-foot concrete bulk pad with containment; a 60-ton platform scale; site drainage and stormwater controls per Oklahoma DEQ permit; perimeter fencing, lighting, and a gate house; and a 2,400-square-foot equipment and office building.

The facility is deliberately **commodity-agnostic**. It is designed to handle bulk aggregate and fertilizer, palletized and bagged product, pipe and structural steel, and containerized freight without dedicated equipment. This design choice is the District's principal hedge against single-customer concentration and is discussed further under *Statement of Need* and *Sustainability* below.

The 14-acre parcel is held under a purchase option executed August 2025 with Hightower Land & Cattle, LLC. **Board Chair Wilbur Hightower is a member of the seller entity, has filed a written conflict-of-interest disclosure with the Board, recused himself from all deliberation and voting on the site selection and option, and will recuse himself from all matters relating to the facility.** The option price of $4,150 per acre was established by an independent MAI appraisal. Documentation is at Attachment K.

### Component 4 — Grade crossing rehabilitation, 13 crossings ($320,000)

Nine public and four private crossings within the project limits will receive new concrete crossing panels, reconstructed approaches, flangeway restoration, and drainage correction. The **Woodward County Board of County Commissioners** will perform approach paving, temporary road closure, detour signing, and traffic control on the three county-road crossings under an executed interlocal agreement, contributing $265,000 in force-account value as part of the non-federal match.

### Delivery method, schedule, and readiness

The District will deliver the project under a design-bid-build model with three prime construction packages (track, structures, site) competitively procured under the District's Procurement and Contracting Policy adopted March 2021 and reviewed by counsel for conformance with Oklahoma public bidding law and Trust requirements.

| Year | Principal work | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| 2027 | Final design; permits; ROW certification; rail and tie procurement; bridges at MP 10.3, 13.9, 16.2; transload site grading and drainage | $2,900,000 |
| 2028 | Relay MP 8.0–19.2 (11.2 mi); bridges at MP 25.1, 27.6, 31.4; transload track, pad, scale, buildings | $3,800,000 |
| 2029 | Relay MP 23.0–33.8 (10.8 mi); final surfacing; 13 crossings; commissioning; closeout | $2,585,000 |

**Readiness.** Preliminary engineering is complete to 30 percent for all components. All 22 relay miles and all six structures lie within existing District fee-owned or perpetual-easement right-of-way; title commitments covering the full corridor were obtained in 2024 and are current. The transload parcel is under option with a title commitment and a completed Phase I Environmental Site Assessment showing no recognized environmental conditions. A categorical exclusion determination has been requested from ODOT Rail Programs. **No construction notice-to-proceed will be issued on any component until right-of-way certification for that component is executed by the Executive Director and the Chief Engineer** — a control adopted directly in response to the 2020 lapse described under *Organizational Capacity*.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

The District states four goals and eleven objectives. Each objective specifies a baseline, a target, a deadline, and a data source, and each maps to a Trust scoring criterion.

### Goal 1 — Restore the physical asset to modern short line standard

**Objective 1.1.** Eliminate all 14.0 miles of 10 mph slow order and restore continuous 25 mph FRA Class 2 operation across MP 8.0–19.2 and MP 23.0–33.8 by **September 30, 2029**. *Baseline: 14.0 restricted miles. Target: 0. Source: District timetable, special instructions, and FRA Class certification by Chief Engineer.* [Trust criterion: slow-order miles eliminated]

**Objective 1.2.** Raise the governing gross weight limit from 268,000 to 286,000 pounds on rail across all 38 miles by **December 31, 2029**. *Baseline: 268,000 lb. Target: 286,000 lb. Source: Part 237 bridge load ratings, sealed by the structural engineer of record.*

**Objective 1.3.** Reduce rail and joint service failures from 41 in the trailing 36 months to **6 or fewer** in the 36 months ending December 31, 2032. *Source: District maintenance log and RTR incident reports.*

**Objective 1.4.** Reduce mean one-way trip time between Cimarron Junction and Whitmore from 4 hours 22 minutes to **3 hours 5 minutes or less** by March 31, 2030. *Source: RTR train sheets, sampled monthly.*

### Goal 2 — Recover and grow carload volume

**Objective 2.1.** Increase annual carloads from 2,100 to **3,200 in CY2029** and **3,850 in CY2031**. *Source: RTR interchange reports reconciled to BNSF waybill data.* [Trust criterion: carloads]

**Objective 2.2.** Achieve **430 annual carloads originating or terminating at the Cimarron Junction Transload Facility** by CY2031, from a minimum of four customers not served by the line today. *Source: transload gate and scale records.*

**Objective 2.3.** Increase average loaded weight on grain cars from 254,000 to **282,000 pounds** by CY2030, capturing the light-loading penalty. *Source: scale weights, Tolliver and Kepler elevators.*

The CY2031 target of 3,850 is built bottom-up from executed shipper commitments, not from trend extrapolation:

### Goal 4 — Deliver measurable highway and public benefit

**Objective 4.1.** Increase annual truck vehicle-miles avoided from 2,013,900 to **3,692,150** by CY2031, an increase of 1,678,250 VMT, of which approximately 1,309,000 are on US-270. *Method: carloads × 3.5 truckloads × 274 round-trip vehicle-miles, with the 137-mile one-way haul re-validated biennially by shipper survey.* [Trust criterion: truck vehicle-miles avoided]

**Objective 4.2.** Reduce heavy-truck trips on US-270 through Woodward attributable to line-served freight by **4,780 trips annually** by CY2031 relative to the current baseline.

## Evaluation Plan

### Design

The District will conduct a **pre/post evaluation with an interrupted time-series design and a comparison group**. Carload, service-failure, and transit-time series will be assembled monthly from January 2019 forward, establishing 96 pre-intervention observations by the time construction begins. Two peer Oklahoma short lines of comparable length, commodity mix, and Class I connection have agreed to share annual carload data, permitting a difference-in-differences check on whether observed volume change reflects the project or regional agricultural and construction cycles. This comparison is essential because the District's own history shows drought accounting for 211 carloads of decline; without a comparison series, the Trust cannot distinguish project effect from weather.

### The 2020 lapse, and what changed

The District must address directly a matter the Trust will find in its due diligence. **In 2020 the District returned $260,000 unspent to a State of Oklahoma rail program because it could not close on right-of-way required by the funded scope.** No funds were misspent; the award simply lapsed. The Board commissioned a review, and the findings are stated here without qualification:

1. The District applied for and accepted an award for a scope whose critical path ran through a single parcel for which **no title work had been performed** and no option or purchase agreement existed. Acquisition feasibility was assumed.
2. There was **no in-house real estate or acquisition capability** and no retained acquisition counsel. Negotiations were conducted informally by a board member without a documented offer, appraisal, or fallback.
3. **No alternative alignment or descoping option** had been developed, so when the parcel became unavailable the entire award became unusable rather than partially usable.
4. The lapse coincided with an executive director vacancy of seven months, during which no single person was accountable for grant milestones.

The Board adopted four corrective measures, all now in force for more than four years:

- **Right-of-way certification precedes notice to proceed.** Board Resolution 2021-04 prohibits issuance of a construction NTP on any component until the Executive Director and Chief Engineer jointly certify that all real property interests for that component are held. This is a condition the District requests the Trust incorporate into the grant agreement.
- **No award will be accepted whose critical path depends on unsecured property.** For this project, all 22 relay miles and all six structures are on existing owned right-of-way; only the transload parcel involves acquisition, it is under executed option with title commitment, and **the transload component is severable** — the relay and bridge scope can proceed to completion independently.
- **A standing Project Delivery Committee** of the Board, chaired by a member other than the Chair, receives monthly written milestone reporting on every active capital project. Minutes are public.
- **Retained acquisition counsel and a written acquisition procedure**, adopted 2021, requiring appraisal, written offer, documented negotiation, and a defined escalation path before any award application relying on acquisition is submitted.

The District offers this history not as background but as evidence. The failure was one of project readiness discipline, and the readiness posture of this application — complete title commitments, a completed Phase I, a severable acquisition component, and a certification gate written into the delivery plan — is the direct product of it.

## Sustainability

The Trust's central question is fair and is stated plainly in the application guidance: **how is the track maintained after the grant?** The District answers in four parts.

### 1. The operator arrangement

Redbud Terminal Railway's operating agreement expires this year, and RTR has requested a **22 percent increase** in the per-car rates it charges shippers. The District does not treat this as a dispute to be concealed from the Trust. It treats it as the clearest available evidence of the problem this project solves.

RTR's cost structure is dominated by crew time and equipment utilization, both of which are consumed by the 14 miles of 10 mph track. RTR's cost-of-service submission to the District shows that the slow orders and the three-day-per-week service pattern beyond MP 24 add $161 per car to its operating cost, against total revenue per car of $712. RTR is not seeking a windfall; at 2,100 carloads across a cost base sized for 3,400, it is seeking to remain solvent. **The rate request is a symptom of deferred maintenance, and unaddressed it will drive shippers off the line and further reduce volume — precisely the spiral the Trust's scoring criteria are designed to detect.**

The District's resolution, negotiated with RTR and endorsed by the Traffic Development Committee, is a new **ten-year operating agreement with two five-year renewal options**, to be executed before the Trust's award decision and provided as a post-submission attachment. Its material terms:

- **Volume-banded rate structure.** Rates step down as annual carloads rise through defined bands (below 2,400; 2,400–2,999; 3,000–3,599; 3,600 and above). This grants RTR relief at today's volume while returning the benefit of recovery to shippers automatically, without renegotiation. Modeled against the CY2031 target, the effective shipper-borne rate at 3,850 carloads is **4 percent below today's rate**, and the increase borne by shippers in 2027 is held to **7.4 percent** rather than 22 percent — satisfying Objective 3.2 and protecting the shipper retention score.
- **Maintenance standard covenant.** RTR is obligated to maintain the line to FRA Class 2 minimum on the 22 relayed miles and Class 1 elsewhere, with defined annual tie, surfacing, and vegetation programs and a liquidated-damages provision for failure to meet the standard.
- **Capital contribution.** RTR contributes $410,000 in crossties, other track material, and maintenance-of-way labor during construction, counted as match.
- **Per-car capital maintenance fee.** RTR remits **$26 per carload** to the District's Capital Maintenance Reserve, in addition to lease and access fees.
- **Performance termination and re-solicitation right.** The District retains the right to terminate for failure to meet defined service-frequency and maintenance standards and to re-solicit the operating rights. The District has confirmed with two other regional operators that they would bid on a rehabilitated 25 mph, 286,000-pound line; neither expressed interest in the line in its current condition. **Rehabilitation is what makes the operator relationship competitive rather than captive.**

### 2. Maintenance economics after construction

The dominant post-grant sustainability fact is that new track is dramatically cheaper to maintain than 78-year-old track. The District's five-year maintenance cost history on the 22 project miles averages **$19,400 per mile per year** — $426,800 annually — of which the largest components are spot tie replacement, repeat surfacing at fouled-ballast locations, and emergency response to the 41 service failures. Comparable relayed 115-pound CWR track on new ties and clean ballast maintains at approximately **$6,900 per mile per year**, or $151,800.

**Annual maintenance savings: $275,000.** By Board policy adopted in anticipation of this application, that savings is committed as follows: **$185,000 per year to the Capital Maintenance Reserve** and **$90,000 per year to an accelerated program on the 16 miles outside the project limits**, which prevents this project from creating a new deferred-maintenance problem on the segments it does not touch.

### 3. Revenue growth and the reserve

The District's current $2.1 million budget is composed of track access and lease fees from RTR ($840,000), an ad valorem levy ($610,000), ODOT maintenance assistance ($215,000), car storage and demurrage share ($145,000), permits and easements ($95,000), grant reimbursement ($150,000), and interest and other ($45,000).

The access fee component is per-carload ($268 per car on 2,100 cars) atop a fixed lease of $277,200. At the CY2031 target of 3,850 carloads, access and lease revenue rises to **$1,309,000, an increase of $469,000**. The transload facility is projected to generate **$145,000 in net annual revenue** at stabilization from handling fees, storage, and a per-ton throughput charge, on conservative volume assumptions of 430 carloads.

### 4. Traffic durability and the ethanol withdrawal

The second matter the District must address directly: **in April, an ethanol plant that had pledged 900 carloads annually withdrew when its parent company idled the facility.** That pledge was a material component of the District's earlier planning.

The District's response has three parts.

**First, the 900 carloads have been removed from this application entirely.** No portion of the 3,850-carload CY2031 target derives from that facility, its spur, or its parent. The District has not substituted an equivalent speculative volume. The target fell, and the application was rebuilt around a lower number. The Trust is asked to score this application on 3,850, not on 4,750.

**Second, the District changed how it counts volume.** Board policy now requires that any traffic in a funding application be supported by an **executed letter of intent specifying annual carload volume, commodity, and a term of not less than three years**, and that no single shipper exceed **35 percent** and no single commodity group exceed **45 percent** of a forecast. The 3,850 target complies: the largest shipper is 26.5 percent of the total, and grain — the largest commodity group — is 42.5 percent. Executed letters of intent covering **3,420 of the 3,850 carloads** are at Attachment D. The 430 transload carloads are supported by four letters of interest, deliberately identified as a lower evidentiary tier and carried at a 40 percent discount from the customers' own stated volumes.

**Third, the transload facility exists because of this lesson.** A single large captive shipper is a concentration risk that the District has now experienced twice — the 2021 closure of Aetna Wells Cooperative and the April ethanol withdrawal. A commodity-agnostic transload facility at the Class I interchange serves customers who are not on the line, are not committed to a single commodity, and can be replaced when one leaves. It converts the District from a captive-shipper railroad to a market-access railroad. It is the smallest component of the budget and the most important to the twenty-year durability of the asset.

The idled ethanol facility's rail spur remains connected and in place. If the plant restarts, the traffic returns. The District has not counted it, will not count it, and will report it as unanticipated volume if it materializes.

### Cost per carload

The Trust scores cost per carload. The District reports three formulations rather than selecting the most favorable.

- **Trust cost per incremental annual carload:** $6,499,500 ÷ 1,750 = **$3,714**
- **Total project cost per incremental annual carload:** $9,285,000 ÷ 1,750 = **$5,306**
- **Trust cost per carload moved over the 20-year asset benefit period:** assuming volume ramps to 3,850 by CY2031 and holds flat thereafter with no growth, the line moves approximately 73,900 carloads over 20 years, yielding **$88 per carload moved.**

For comparison, the District's 2022 tie program returned $1,312,000 against an estimated 240 incremental annual carloads, or $5,467 per incremental carload. This project is more efficient because relay and bridge work together remove two binding constraints — speed and weight — rather than one.
