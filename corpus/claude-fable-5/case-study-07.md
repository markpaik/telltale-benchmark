# Building Access in the Wiregrass: Ogeechee Valley Health and the Two-Year Struggle to Open Its Doors

## A Case Study in Rural Ambulatory Strategy, Execution Failure, and Operational Recovery

---

## Introduction

In the flat pine country of south Georgia, about fifteen miles north of the Florida line, Ogeechee Valley Health occupies the position that hundreds of rural health systems occupy across the American South: it is the largest employer in its county, the only hospital within a forty-minute drive for most of its patients, and an institution that the surrounding community cannot imagine losing — even as the economics of running it grow harder every year. Headquartered in Valdosta, Georgia, the system operates a 146-bed hospital, six outpatient clinics scattered across Lowndes and neighboring counties, and employs roughly 1,300 people. Its annual revenue in 2023 was approximately $210 million.

This case study follows Ogeechee Valley Health through a two-and-a-half-year arc: the recognition in 2023 that its outpatient access problem had become an existential financial threat; a $5.4 million board-approved investment in January 2024 to build two nurse-practitioner-led access clinics; a first year in which nearly everything that could go wrong did go wrong — staffing shortfalls, a location that patients could not find a reason to visit, and a credentialing error that silently destroyed four months of revenue; and a turnaround, beginning in November 2024, that within eight months converted the program from a cash drain into a modest contributor and helped pull the entire system's operating margin from minus 4.1 percent to minus 0.6 percent.

The story is worth telling not because it is unusual but because it is common. The strategy Ogeechee Valley pursued — same-day access clinics staffed by advanced practice providers, intended to decompress the emergency department and shorten primary care waits — is one of the most frequently recommended interventions in rural health strategy. What the recommendation rarely includes is an honest accounting of how difficult the execution is, how many ways the model can fail in its first year, and how much of the eventual success depends not on the strategic concept but on operational details: where the building sits, who answers the phone, whether the enrollment paperwork was filed correctly, and whether anyone is watching the denial reports.

---

## Part One: The Organization and Its Situation

### The system in 2023

Ogeechee Valley Health entered 2023 in a condition its own leadership described internally as "quietly deteriorating." The system was not in immediate danger of closure. It had modest reserves, a manageable debt load, and a payer mix that, while heavy on Medicare, Medicaid, and self-pay, was not catastrophic by rural Georgia standards. But the trend lines all pointed the same direction, and the 2023 operating results made the trajectory impossible to ignore.

The system posted an operating margin of minus 4.1 percent for the year — a loss of roughly $8.6 million on $210 million in revenue. For a rural system without a large endowment or a wealthy parent organization, a loss of that size is not sustainable for more than a few years. Chief Executive Officer Beatrice Nwachukwu, who had arrived in 2021 from a larger system in the Atlanta suburbs, told her board in the fall of 2023 that the system had "perhaps three years of runway at this burn rate before we are making decisions about which service lines to close rather than which ones to grow."

The losses had several sources — labor inflation, denials pressure, an aging inpatient plant — but when Nwachukwu and Chief Medical Officer Dr. Vikas Deshpande dissected the numbers, one theme kept surfacing: the system was failing at outpatient access, and that failure was bleeding money at both ends of the enterprise.

### The access problem, quantified

The 2023 data told a coherent and alarming story:

- **38 percent of emergency department visits were non-emergent.** Nearly four in ten patients arriving at the ED had conditions — urinary tract infections, sore throats, medication refills, rashes, minor sprains — that could have been handled in a primary care or urgent care setting at a fraction of the cost. These visits clogged the ED, lengthened waits for genuinely acute patients, generated poor patient experience scores, and lost money. A significant share of these patients were uninsured or on Medicaid, and the reimbursement for a low-acuity ED visit routinely failed to cover the fixed cost of delivering it in an emergency setting.

- **Nine of 31 budgeted primary care physician positions were vacant** — a 29 percent vacancy rate. Recruitment of primary care physicians to rural south Georgia had grown progressively harder since the pandemic. The system had made offers; it had lost candidates to Tallahassee, Jacksonville, and metro Atlanta, and in two painful cases to telehealth companies that offered comparable pay with no relocation at all.

- **The average wait for a new patient appointment in primary care was 47 days.** For a patient with a new symptom, a new diagnosis from an ED visit, or a new insurance card, the practical message was: you cannot get in. Many of those patients defaulted to the emergency department, completing the loop.

- **The no-show rate across the clinics was 24 percent.** Nearly a quarter of scheduled appointments went unfilled — a compounding tragedy in a system with too few appointments to begin with. Long waits and high no-shows fed each other: patients booked six weeks out forgot, found care elsewhere, or gave up; the resulting empty slots represented capacity the system desperately needed and could not use.

The financial arithmetic was straightforward. The system was simultaneously overspending to deliver low-acuity care in its most expensive setting and underdelivering the ambulatory care that generated downstream referrals, kept chronic disease controlled, and kept commercially insured families attached to the system rather than drifting to competitors in Tifton or across the Florida line.

Deshpande put it plainly in a memo to the board's finance committee in November 2023: "We do not have a demand problem. Demand is overwhelming. We have a front door problem. Patients cannot get in, so they come through the most expensive door we own, and we lose money on almost every one of them."

## Part Two: The Decision

### The proposal

In January 2024, Nwachukwu and Deshpande brought the board a proposal: invest $5.4 million over two years to open two "access clinics" — ambulatory sites explicitly designed around availability rather than continuity. The clinics would be staffed primarily by nurse practitioners, offer same-day and next-day scheduling with extended walk-in capacity, and be positioned geographically to intercept the patient populations most heavily represented in the non-emergent ED volume. One clinic would open in Hahira, a small town north of Valdosta whose residents accounted for a disproportionate share of low-acuity ED arrivals. The second would open in a growing area on the east side of Valdosta.

The financial model projected that the clinics would reach roughly 26 patients per provider-day within six months of opening, break even on operations within eighteen months, and generate system-level value in three ways: direct clinic revenue, reduced losses from non-emergent ED utilization, and downstream capture of imaging, laboratory, and specialty referrals.

The board approved the investment in January 2024. In retrospect, both the strategy and the approval were sound. The failures that followed were failures of execution — but they were rooted in assumptions embedded in the proposal that no one pressure-tested.

### The assumptions, examined

The plan rested on five assumptions, each of which seemed reasonable in January 2024 and each of which proved wrong or fragile in practice.

**Assumption one: staffing could be bridged with locums.** The system could not hire five nurse practitioners fast enough to open on schedule, so the plan called for contracting with Halcyon Clinical Staffing, a regional locum tenens agency, to fill the roles temporarily while recruitment of permanent employed NPs proceeded in parallel. The model budgeted for six months of agency coverage.

**Assumption two: "if we open it, they will come."** The demand analysis was correct at the population level — the patients existed, and their need was real. But the plan treated demand as automatic. It assumed that a new clinic, once open, would fill itself, and it invested almost nothing in community outreach, marketing, ED-to-clinic referral workflows, or the mundane machinery of making a new site visible to the people it was built for.

**Assumption three: location was a real estate decision, not a clinical one.** The east Valdosta site was selected largely on lease economics: a medical office park offered favorable terms, existing clinical build-out, and proximity to other health tenants. Nobody asked whether the target patient population — working families, hourly workers, patients without reliable transportation — ever had a reason to be in a medical office park.

**Assumption four: the revenue cycle would absorb the new sites routinely.** New providers, new locations, and new payer enrollments were treated as back-office formalities. The proposal contained no milestone for confirming that credentialing and payer enrollment were complete before providers began billing, and no early-warning process for monitoring denials at the new sites.

**Assumption five: the existing scheduling and reminder infrastructure was adequate.** The clinics would run on the same scheduling practices and (nonexistent) reminder systems as the legacy clinics — the same infrastructure that was already producing a 24 percent no-show rate.

None of these assumptions was interrogated with the rigor applied to the capital request itself. The board spent forty minutes debating the $5.4 million figure and less than five discussing how the clinics would actually fill their schedules.

## Part Three: The First Year — What Went Wrong

### Opening in Hahira

The Hahira clinic opened in April 2024 with genuine local goodwill — a ribbon-cutting attended by the mayor, coverage in the Valdosta Daily Times, and a first week that felt promising. Then the visits settled at a level that stunned everyone: approximately **six patients a day**, against a target of 26.

The reasons emerged slowly and painfully over the following months:

- **The clinic was invisible to its intended patients.** The population driving non-emergent ED use did not read the newspaper coverage, did not follow the system's social media, and had no established habit of seeking care anywhere but the ED. The ED itself — the single richest source of appropriate referrals — had no workflow for redirecting low-acuity patients to the new clinic. Discharge paperwork mentioned "follow up with primary care" generically. Emergency physicians, employed by a contracted group, had no incentive and no easy mechanism to book patients into the clinic before they left the department.

- **The schedule did not match patients' lives.** The clinic initially operated 8 a.m. to 5 p.m., Monday through Friday — precisely the hours when the working families it was designed for could not come. The ED, by contrast, was open at 7 p.m. on a Tuesday, which is when a parent finishing a shift could bring in a child with an ear infection.

- **Same-day access is meaningless if no one knows to call.** The clinic had same-day slots sitting empty every afternoon while the ED waiting room, eleven miles away, filled with the patients those slots were built for.

### The staffing shortfall

Halcyon Clinical Staffing, contracted to fill five nurse practitioner placements across the two sites, delivered three. The agency cited the same market conditions that had frustrated the system's own recruiting — and it was not wrong; the market was genuinely brutal — but the shortfall meant the second clinic opened late and understaffed, and the Hahira clinic could not extend hours even if leadership had wanted to.

The financial terms compounded the problem. Halcyon billed **$312 an hour** for NP coverage, roughly three times the fully loaded cost of an employed nurse practitioner. Over seven months, agency staffing consumed **$1.4 million** — more than a quarter of the entire two-year program budget — to staff clinics that were seeing a fraction of their projected volume. The system was paying premium rates for providers who spent much of each day waiting for patients who did not know the clinic existed.

Meanwhile, permanent recruitment stalled. The recruiting effort, split between an overstretched HR department and the same physician-recruitment firm that had failed to fill the primary care vacancies, produced few candidates. The "bridge" was becoming a permanent, ruinously expensive structure.

### The credentialing failure

The most expensive single failure was also the quietest. When the locum nurse practitioners began seeing patients in April 2024, their payer enrollment with the system's largest payers — including Georgia Medicaid and the dominant regional commercial plan — was incomplete. Claims for their visits were submitted, denied, and accumulated in the denial queue, where they sat unnoticed. Because the clinics were new cost centers with no historical baseline, the standard revenue cycle dashboards had no expected-revenue benchmark against which the missing payments would have stood out. The clinics' low volumes made the dollar amounts small enough, month by month, to escape the thresholds that triggered denial-management review.

It was not until August 2024 — four months in — that Revenue Cycle Director Emiliano Cortez, conducting a routine review of denial trends by location, noticed that the two new sites had denial rates approaching 100 percent for certain payers. The investigation took two weeks. The finding: a credentialing and enrollment error at program launch had left the locum providers unenrolled or incorrectly linked to the new site locations. **Four months of claims — $780,000 in billed charges — had been denied.**

Cortez's team began the appeals process immediately, but the damage was structural as well as financial. Timely-filing deadlines threatened a portion of the claims. Some payers required re-submission under corrected enrollment that could not be applied retroactively. And internally, the discovery shattered whatever confidence remained in the program. By early fall 2024, the access clinic initiative was, by any honest assessment, failing on every dimension: volume at a quarter of target, staffing at 60 percent of plan and triple the budgeted cost, and four months of revenue lost to an administrative error.

At the October 2024 board meeting, one director asked Nwachukwu directly whether the program should be shut down. Her answer would define the second act of the story: "The strategy is right and the execution is wrong. Before we abandon the strategy, I want to see what happens when we fix the execution."

## Part Four: The Turnaround

### New leadership, new mandate

In November 2024, Nwachukwu assigned the program to **Latrice Bonnaire**, the system's Director of Ambulatory Services. Bonnaire was an unglamorous choice by design. She was not a strategist; she was an operator — a former clinic manager who had spent fifteen years running outpatient practices and who was known inside the system for two qualities: she visited the sites she managed, constantly, and she measured everything weekly.

Bonnaire's mandate was explicit: full operational authority over both clinics, a direct reporting line to the CEO for the duration of the turnaround, and a standing weekly review with Nwachukwu, Deshpande, and Cortez. Her first act was to spend two weeks doing nothing but observing — sitting in the Hahira waiting room, listening to the front desk answer phones, riding along with the community paramedic program, and interviewing ED nurses about why they didn't refer patients to the clinics. ("Honestly?" one told her. "I forgot they existed.")

Out of that fortnight came a turnaround plan with five components, executed between November 2024 and March 2025.

### One: relocate the second clinic

The east Valdosta clinic, languishing in its medical office park, was moved to a **grocery-anchored retail plaza** on a major commercial corridor. The new site sat between a supermarket and a discount pharmacy, on a bus line, with abundant parking and signage visible from the road. The lease was more expensive per square foot; Bonnaire argued, correctly, that the cheapest lease in the market is worthless if patients never see the door. The relocation was completed in January 2025. The logic was simple and, in retrospect, obvious: the target patients went to the grocery store weekly. They went to medical office parks never. Foot traffic, visibility, and the psychological accessibility of a retail setting — no intimidating medical campus, no confusing suite numbers — mattered more than any marketing campaign the system could have bought. Walk-in volume at the relocated site tripled within eight weeks of the move.

### Two: convert locum roles to employed positions

Bonnaire, working with HR and a new recruiting approach that emphasized the NPs' autonomy, employed status, and community mission — and that recruited regionally from Georgia and north Florida NP programs rather than nationally — **converted two of the locum roles to permanently employed nurse practitioners** by early 2025. The conversions cut the hourly cost of those positions by roughly two-thirds, stabilized the clinical teams, and, just as importantly, gave the clinics providers who lived in the community, stayed long enough to build repeat relationships, and cared whether the program survived. The remaining Halcyon coverage was wound down as employed staffing came online, ending the $312-an-hour bleed.

### Three: match hours to patients' lives

Both clinics **added evening hours** — extending to 8 p.m. three nights a week — precisely targeting the after-work, after-school window in which non-emergent ED visits peaked. Evening slots filled faster than any other block on the schedule, confirming what the ED arrival-time data had been saying all along.

### Four: attack no-shows with text reminders

The system implemented **automated text message reminders** — first at the access clinics, then across the ambulatory network — with confirmations, easy rescheduling, and same-day waitlist backfill for cancelled slots. The technology was neither novel nor expensive; the system had simply never prioritized it. Combined with the shorter waits the clinics could now offer (patients booked two days out show up; patients booked six weeks out do not), the no-show rate began falling within the first quarter of deployment.

### Five: recover the denied claims

Cortez's team, working the $780,000 in denied claims through appeals, corrected enrollments, and payer negotiations, ultimately **recovered $520,000** — a 67 percent recovery rate, achieved through months of grinding appeals work. The remaining $260,000 was written off, most of it lost to timely-filing limits and non-retroactive enrollment rules. In parallel, Cortez built the control that should have existed from the start: a launch checklist requiring confirmed payer enrollment before any new provider's first billable visit, and a standing weekly denial report broken out by site and provider, reviewed in Bonnaire's operations meeting. No new-site claim would ever again age four months in a denial queue unnoticed.

### The missing piece: the ED referral loop

Alongside the five formal components, Bonnaire fixed the referral workflow that should have existed from day one. ED discharge staff gained the ability to book patients directly into access clinic slots before discharge — a scheduled appointment in hand, not a phone number on a printout. Triage nurses were given scripting for low-acuity walk-ins: the clinic could see them today, sooner and cheaper. The contracted emergency physician group, initially indifferent, came around when the data showed the redirection was shortening their own waiting room queue.

## Part Five: The Results

### The numbers, before and after

By June 2025 — eight months after Bonnaire took over, fourteen months after the Hahira clinic opened — the program's metrics had transformed:

| Measure | Baseline (2023–mid 2024) | June 2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Access clinic daily visits | 6 per day (vs. target of 26) | **31 per day** |
| New patient appointment wait | 47 days | **12 days** |
| No-show rate | 24 percent | **13 percent** |
| Non-emergent share of ED visits | 38 percent | **29 percent** |
| Access clinic financial contribution | Substantial operating losses | **+$210,000 for the quarter** |
| System operating margin | −4.1 percent | **−0.6 percent** |

Each number deserves a moment of interpretation.

**Thirty-one patients a day** exceeded the original target of 26 — a target the program had missed by 77 percent in its first months. The volume was driven by the relocation, the evening hours, the ED referral loop, and the compounding effect of word of mouth once patients discovered they could actually be seen the same day. Notably, volume growth accelerated after the employed NPs arrived; repeat patients asked for providers by name, something that never happened with rotating locums.

**The drop in new patient waits from 47 days to 12** reflected both the added capacity and the reduced waste. The no-show improvement alone effectively returned hundreds of appointment slots per month to productive use across the network.

**The no-show rate falling from 24 to 13 percent** validated a truth that clinic operators know and strategists often forget: no-shows are substantially a function of wait time and friction, not patient character. Patients seen within days show up. Patients reminded by text show up. The system had been blaming its patients for a failure of its own scheduling design.

**Non-emergent ED visits fell from 38 to 29 percent of volume.** This was arguably the most financially significant result, though the hardest to attribute cleanly. The decline represented thousands of annual visits redirected from the system's most expensive care setting to its least expensive one. Deshpande's team estimated the avoided-loss value at between $900,000 and $1.4 million annually, depending on assumptions about which visits would otherwise have occurred and their payer mix. The ED's own metrics improved in parallel: shorter door-to-provider times, fewer patients leaving without being seen, and modestly better staff retention in a department that had been running at a punishing pace.

**The clinics contributed $210,000 in the second quarter of 2025** — the program's first positive quarter. The figure was modest, but its direction and trajectory mattered more than its size. A program that had consumed cash for over a year was now adding to it, with volume still growing and the high-cost agency staffing fully unwound.

**System operating margin improved from minus 4.1 percent to minus 0.6 percent.** Honesty requires a caveat that Ogeechee Valley's own leadership was careful to state: the access clinics did not single-handedly produce a 3.5-point margin swing. Over the same period the system also renegotiated a payer contract, tightened supply chain spending, and benefited from moderating labor inflation. But the access program contributed on multiple lines — direct clinic contribution, reduced ED losses, recovered denials, downstream ancillary capture, and the elimination of the agency staffing drain — and, as Nwachukwu argued to the board, it contributed something less tangible: proof that the system could execute a turnaround, which mattered for physician recruitment, employee morale, and the board's willingness to fund the next initiative. By mid-2025, three of the nine vacant primary care physician positions had been filled, and two of the successful candidates cited the visible momentum of the ambulatory strategy in their decisions.

### The full cost

An honest accounting of what the program cost is essential to the case, because the headline results conceal how expensive the path to them was.

- **Board-approved investment:** $5.4 million over two years, substantially consumed by build-out, relocation, equipment, staffing, and operating losses during the ramp.
- **Agency staffing:** $1.4 million paid to Halcyon over seven months — roughly $900,000 to $1 million more than employed staffing for the same coverage would have cost, a pure premium paid for the failure to recruit ahead of opening.
- **Denied claims:** $780,000 billed and denied due to the credentialing error; $520,000 recovered after extensive appeals effort; **$260,000 permanently written off**, plus the unmeasured cost of the revenue cycle labor consumed in the recovery.
- **Relocation costs:** the abandoned medical office park lease and the second build-out at the retail plaza — expenses that a better initial site-selection process would have avoided entirely.
- **Opportunity cost:** roughly seven months during which the clinics operated at a quarter of capacity, delivering neither the patient access nor the ED decompression they were built for, while the underlying problems continued to compound.

Taken together, leadership estimated that avoidable execution failures consumed between $1.5 million and $2 million of the program's resources — roughly a third of the total investment spent on mistakes rather than mission. The program ultimately succeeded, but it succeeded despite its first year, not because of it, and a system with thinner reserves or a less patient board might not have survived long enough to reach the turnaround.

## Part Six: What Ogeechee Valley Would Do Differently

In a candid after-action review conducted in July 2025 — a review Nwachukwu insisted be written down and presented to the board precisely because the program had ended well and the temptation to forget the failures was strong — leadership identified what it would change if starting over.

**Do not open until enrollment is confirmed.** The single most expensive error, the $780,000 credentialing failure, was also the most preventable. The system now enforces a hard gate: no provider generates a billable encounter until payer enrollment is verified in writing for every major payer, and every new site's claims are monitored on a daily denial report for its first ninety days. Cortez's summary became an aphorism inside the organization: "Revenue you never collect costs exactly as much as revenue you never earn, and it's easier to lose."

**Recruit before you build.** The Halcyon experience taught the system that locum coverage is a legitimate bridge for a gap measured in weeks, and a financial catastrophe when the gap is measured in months with no credible end. In future launches, permanent recruitment begins six to nine months before opening, opening dates flex to staffing reality rather than the reverse, and any agency contract carries a defined, dated wind-down plan from day one.

**Choose locations the way retailers do, not the way medical real estate brokers do.** The relocation from the medical office park to the grocery-anchored plaza was, in Bonnaire's assessment, the single highest-impact operational decision of the turnaround. Access-oriented care is a retail business; visibility, parking, transit, foot traffic, and the daily habits of the target population outweigh lease rates and the comfort of clustering with other medical tenants.

**Build the demand pipeline before opening day.** "If we open it, they will come" is not a plan. The ED-to-clinic referral workflow, the discharge scheduling capability, the triage scripting, the community outreach — all of it should have existed on day one, not month nine. A clinic built to intercept ED volume must be wired into the ED before it sees its first patient.

**Design hours around patients, not staff.** Evening availability was among the cheapest changes made and among the most productive. The system now starts every access-oriented scheduling design from ED arrival-time data rather than from conventional clinic hours.

**Put an operator in charge from the beginning.** The program spent its first seven months as a strategy without an owner — governed by a steering committee, executed by no one. Bonnaire's weekly metric reviews, site visits, and direct authority produced more improvement in eight months than the committee structure produced in seven. Every subsequent initiative at Ogeechee Valley now launches with a single named operational owner and a weekly scorecard.

**Instrument new ventures more tightly than mature ones.** The credentialing failure hid for four months partly because new sites had no baseline against which anomalies stood out. The lesson generalized: new programs need more monitoring than established ones, not less, because their failure modes are unknown and their data has no history to betray a problem.

## Part Seven: What a Reader Should Take From This Case

For a leader at a rural health system — or any resource-constrained organization contemplating an access expansion — Ogeechee Valley's experience yields lessons that extend beyond its particulars.

**First: the strategy was never the problem.** The diagnosis made in 2023 was correct, the intervention chosen was the right one, and the final results vindicated both. Everything that went wrong went wrong in execution — staffing, siting, workflows, enrollment paperwork. Leaders reviewing struggling initiatives should be slow to conclude that the strategy has failed before asking whether the strategy has actually been executed. Nwachukwu's October 2024 distinction — "the strategy is right and the execution is wrong" — saved a program that a purely results-based review would have killed, and killed wrongly.

**Second: access problems and financial problems are the same problem.** Ogeechee Valley's 47-day waits, 24 percent no-shows, and 38 percent non-emergent ED share were not merely quality or experience issues sitting alongside the financial crisis; they were mechanisms of the financial crisis. Fixing access fixed money. Systems that treat patient access as a service amenity separate from margin recovery misunderstand the economics of their own front door.

**Third: the boring infrastructure is where programs die.** Credentialing, payer enrollment, denial monitoring, appointment reminders, referral workflows — none of this appears in a strategy deck, and any one of these can quietly destroy a well-conceived program. Ogeechee Valley lost $260,000 permanently and imperiled $780,000 because of an enrollment error no one thought to check. The most valuable page in any launch plan is the checklist.

**Fourth: agency staffing is a bridge, and bridges have two ends.** Locum coverage without an aggressive, dated, resourced plan for permanent hiring is not a bridge; it is a pier — expensive, extending into open water, ending nowhere. At $312 an hour against sixty percent fill, Halcyon consumed $1.4 million while the underlying recruitment problem went unsolved. The time to recruit for a new site is before the lease is signed.

**Fifth: convenience is a clinical intervention.** The turnaround's most powerful levers — a visible retail location, evening hours, same-day slots, text reminders — were interventions in convenience, not in medicine. They cut no-shows nearly in half, tripled and then quintupled volume, and redirected thousands of visits from the ED. Patients did not begin behaving differently because they were educated to; they behaved differently because the system finally made the right behavior the easy one.

**Sixth: put operators, not committees, in charge of turnarounds — and give them authority, cadence, and a scorecard.** The difference between the program's first seven months and its next eight was not resources, strategy, or market conditions. It was Latrice Bonnaire: one accountable owner, direct authority, weekly metrics, and a habit of standing in the waiting room to see what patients saw.

**Finally: count the full cost, including the cost of the mistakes.** Ogeechee Valley's story ends well — 31 visits a day, 12-day waits, a positive-contribution quarter, and a system margin within striking distance of break-even. But roughly a third of the investment was consumed by avoidable failures, and the system survived its first year of errors only because it had the reserves and the board patience to reach the turnaround. The honest lesson of this case is not that the model works — though it does. It is that the margin between this program's eventual success and its plausible failure was operational discipline, applied fourteen months later than it should have been. The systems that will replicate Ogeechee Valley's results are the ones that apply that discipline from day one, and keep the $2 million it cost Ogeechee Valley to learn it.
