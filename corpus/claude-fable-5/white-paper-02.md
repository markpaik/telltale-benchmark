# Underwriting the Enterprise: A Data-Driven Case for Flexible Funding and a Three-Year Transition Model for Regional Grantmakers

**A White Paper of the Headwaters Philanthropy Lab**

Dr. Rekha Balasubramanian, Research Director
Marisol Trevino-Guzman, Senior Fellow
Dr. Everett Kwiatkowski, Evaluation Lead

Headwaters Philanthropy Lab, Minneapolis, Minnesota
August 2026

---

## Abstract

Grantmakers in the Upper Midwest are financing nonprofit organizations in a way that systematically underprices the true cost of the work they commission and starves grantees of the reserves they need to survive routine volatility. Drawing on grant-level records covering 9,100 awards totaling $1.9 billion made between 2016 and 2024, audited financial statements for 620 grantees, and a 2025 survey of 480 nonprofit executive directors, this paper documents the scale of the problem: allowed indirect cost recovery across the regional portfolio averages 11.4 percent while audited true indirect costs run 26.8 percent; 41 percent of surveyed organizations hold under three months of operating reserve; and state contract payments in the region are running 94 days behind on average. Our data show that grantees receiving multiyear unrestricted support hold a median of 4.8 months of operating reserve against 1.9 months for project-restricted grantees, and report executive turnover of 14 percent against 26 percent. The paper compares five funding practices now in use among our 310 members—multiyear general operating support, tiered trust-based grantmaking, project grants paying full indirect costs, participatory pooled funds, and recoverable grants—and presents a three-year transition model for a foundation with a $40 million annual payout, including staffing implications, board policy changes, and risk controls. It closes by answering the three objections trustees raise most often: accountability, donor intent, and the capacity of small grantees.

---

## 1. The Problem: A Structural Underfinancing of the Nonprofit Enterprise

### 1.1 What the field is doing to itself

The nonprofit organizations that our 310 member foundations, corporate giving programs, and donor-advised funds support are not fragile because their leaders are poor managers. They are fragile because the dominant financing model in institutional philanthropy—annual, project-restricted grants with capped indirect rates—makes fragility a structural condition rather than a management failure.

Consider the arithmetic that a typical grantee in our region faces. A foundation awards a $100,000 project grant with a 10 percent indirect allowance. The organization's audited true indirect rate is 27 percent. The grant therefore delivers roughly $110,000 against approximately $127,000 of fully loaded cost. The organization must find the missing $17,000 somewhere—typically by drawing on unrestricted revenue, deferring maintenance and technology investment, underpaying staff, or quietly cross-subsidizing from another funder's grant. Multiply that gap across a portfolio of six or eight restricted grants and the organization is running a permanent structural deficit on the very work its funders most want done.

This is not a hypothetical. It is the median condition in our data.

### 1.2 The regional context makes it worse

Two regional conditions amplify the problem for grantees in Minnesota, Wisconsin, and the surrounding states.

First, **government payment delay**. State contract payments in the region are running 94 days behind on average. For an organization delivering human services under a state contract, this means floating a quarter's worth of payroll and program cost out of its own working capital. An organization with 1.9 months of reserve—the median for project-restricted grantees in our dataset—cannot float 94 days of receivables. It borrows on a line of credit if it can get one, delays vendor payments if it cannot, and in the worst cases misses payroll.

Second, **reporting burden**. Executive directors in our 2025 survey reported a median of 71 hours a year of reporting labor per restricted grant. An organization holding eight restricted grants is spending roughly 570 hours a year—more than a quarter of a full-time position—producing compliance documents, most of which our own member survey work suggests are read once or not at all. That labor is almost never reimbursed at its true cost, because it sits in the indirect pool that funders cap.

### 1.3 The stakes

When 41 percent of surveyed organizations hold under three months of reserve, the regional nonprofit sector has no shock absorber. A single delayed state payment, a lost contract, a leadership transition, or a facilities emergency can push an otherwise sound organization into crisis. Funders then respond with emergency grants—paying, at a premium and under duress, for resilience they declined to finance in ordinary times.

The question this paper addresses is not whether flexible funding is desirable in the abstract. National research from the Center for Effective Philanthropy, BDO's Nonprofit Standards benchmarking, and the trust-based philanthropy movement has made that case for a decade. The question is what our own regional data show, which practices deliver the strongest results, and how a foundation of realistic size and governance can actually get from here to there in three years without abandoning accountability, donor intent, or its smallest grantees.

## 2. The Evidence: What the Lab's Data Show

### 2.1 Data sources

The findings in this paper rest on three datasets held by the Headwaters Philanthropy Lab:

1. **Grant-level records** on 9,100 awards totaling $1.9 billion made by member funders between 2016 and 2024, coded by grant type, duration, restriction status, and indirect cost treatment.
2. **Audited financial statements** for 620 grantee organizations, from which we compute operating reserves, true indirect cost rates, and structural surplus or deficit positions.
3. **A 2025 survey of 480 nonprofit executive directors** in the member service region, covering reserves, reporting burden, cash flow stress, leadership tenure, and funder relationships.

### 2.2 Core findings

**Table 1. Financial and organizational health by grant structure (Headwaters Philanthropy Lab, 2016–2024 grant records; 620 audited statements; 2025 executive survey)**

| Indicator | Multiyear unrestricted recipients | Project-restricted recipients |
|---|---|---|
| Median operating reserve | 4.8 months | 1.9 months |
| Executive turnover (annual) | 14% | 26% |
| Median reporting labor per grant | Substantially lower (single annual report typical) | 71 hours/year per restricted grant |

**Table 2. The indirect cost gap and sector liquidity (portfolio-wide)**

| Indicator | Value |
|---|---|
| Average allowed indirect recovery across portfolio | 11.4% |
| Audited true indirect rate | 26.8% |
| Structural indirect gap | 15.4 percentage points |
| Organizations holding under 3 months of reserve | 41% |
| Average state contract payment delay in region | 94 days |

Three findings deserve emphasis.

**The reserve gap is large and consistent.** Grantees receiving multiyear unrestricted support hold a median of 4.8 months of operating reserve; project-restricted grantees hold 1.9 months. The commonly cited prudential floor is three months. Unrestricted recipients sit comfortably above it; restricted recipients sit well below it. The direction of causality plausibly runs both ways—stronger organizations may attract unrestricted support—but the gap persists when we compare organizations of similar size and subsector, and the mechanism is straightforward: unrestricted dollars can be retained; restricted dollars, by design, cannot.

**Leadership stability tracks funding structure.** Executive turnover among unrestricted recipients runs 14 percent annually, against 26 percent for restricted grantees. In our survey, executive directors of heavily restricted organizations describe a job dominated by cash flow triage and reporting compliance rather than mission leadership. A 26 percent annual turnover rate implies a median executive tenure under four years—shorter than the arc of most serious program strategies. Funders who restrict their grants are, in effect, financing a leadership churn that degrades the return on every dollar they give.

**The indirect gap is the engine of the problem.** The 15.4-point gap between allowed recovery (11.4 percent) and audited true cost (26.8 percent) means that, across a $1.9 billion portfolio, member funders have collectively shifted on the order of $250–290 million of real costs onto grantees over nine years. That figure is an estimate, not an audit finding, but its order of magnitude explains the reserve data: the sector's missing reserves are approximately the costs its funders declined to pay.

### 2.3 What the data do not show

Our data do not show that restricted funding produces worse *programmatic* outcomes grant by grant; we lack a common outcome measure across 9,100 awards, and we caution members against claims the evidence cannot carry. What the data do show, robustly, is that restricted funding is associated with materially weaker *organizational* outcomes—reserves, leadership stability, administrative burden—and that organizational weakness is the most common precursor to programmatic failure in our case files. Funders who care about program results should care about balance sheets, because programs do not outlive the organizations that run them.

## 3. Five Practices Compared

Five funding practices are now in active use among Headwaters members. Each addresses a different part of the problem, carries different governance demands, and suits different funder circumstances. We compare them here on six dimensions: effect on grantee balance sheets, administrative burden (both sides), accountability mechanics, fit with donor intent constraints, suitability for small grantees, and implementation difficulty.

### 3.1 Multiyear general operating support (MYGOS)

**What it is.** Unrestricted grants of two to five years' duration, sized to the organization rather than to a project, typically with a single annual report and renewal contingent on organizational health and mission alignment.

**Strengths.** MYGOS is the practice most directly supported by our data: it is the funding structure under which grantees hold 4.8 months of reserve and turn over executives at 14 percent. It eliminates the indirect gap entirely (there is no indirect calculation on an unrestricted grant), cuts reporting labor to a fraction of the restricted-grant norm, and gives leadership the planning horizon that annual grants deny. For the funder, it reduces grant administration volume—one three-year grant replaces three annual cycles.

**Limitations.** MYGOS demands the most rigorous *front-end* diligence, because the funder is underwriting an enterprise rather than a deliverable. It requires trustees to accept organizational-health metrics in place of project outputs, which some boards experience as a loss of control. It fits awkwardly with narrowly restricted endowments and with corporate giving programs whose internal justification depends on branded, bounded projects.

**Best fit.** Core grantees with proven leadership, mission alignment, and at least modest financial systems; funders with discretion over purpose.

### 3.2 Tiered trust-based grantmaking

**What it is.** A structured framework that assigns grantees to tiers—typically two or three—based on relationship depth and demonstrated capacity. Top-tier grantees receive multiyear unrestricted funding with streamlined reporting; newer or higher-risk relationships receive shorter, lighter-touch grants with proportionate diligence, and a defined pathway upward.

**Strengths.** Tiering answers the objection that trust-based practice treats a first-time applicant the same as a twenty-year partner. It gives boards a legible risk framework: trust is earned, documented, and reviewed. It also creates an on-ramp for small and emerging organizations without exposing the funder to concentrated risk. In our member interviews, tiered frameworks were the single most effective device for winning trustee approval of flexible funding, because they convert "trust" from a disposition into a policy.

**Limitations.** Tier assignment can harden into a caste system if criteria are opaque or if movement between tiers is rare in practice. The framework requires honest maintenance—annual tier reviews with real consequences in both directions—or it decays into labels.

**Best fit.** Funders with diverse portfolios spanning large anchor institutions and small emerging groups; boards that need a documented risk architecture before approving flexibility.

### 3.3 Project grants paying full indirect costs

**What it is.** Retention of the project-grant form, but with indirect recovery set at each grantee's audited true rate—in our region, 26.8 percent on average—rather than a capped 10 or 15 percent.

**Strengths.** This is the least disruptive reform on the list. It preserves everything trustees and donors recognize about project grantmaking—defined deliverables, project budgets, project reports—while closing the structural deficit that capped rates create. For funders bound by donor restrictions to specific program areas, it is often the *only* available reform, and it is a substantial one: closing a 15.4-point gap on a $100,000 grant returns roughly $15,000 of previously uncompensated cost to the grantee. It also has a disciplining effect on the funder's own budgeting, forcing honest conversation about what work actually costs.

**Limitations.** Full-cost project grants do not build reserves—every dollar is still spent on the project—and they do not reduce reporting burden or lengthen planning horizons. They stop the bleeding without restoring health. Verifying true indirect rates requires financial analysis capacity that some funders lack, though the grantee's audit typically supplies the necessary figures.

**Best fit.** Donor-restricted funds; corporate programs; any funder as a *floor* practice for whatever project grants remain in its portfolio.

### 3.4 Participatory pooled funds

**What it is.** Multiple funders contribute to a common pool whose allocation decisions are made wholly or partly by community members, practitioners, or grantee peers rather than by funder staff.

**Strengths.** Pooled funds spread risk across funders, reduce duplicative diligence and reporting (one application, one report, many funders), and reach organizations—especially small, informal, or culturally specific groups—that individual funders' pipelines miss. The participatory element improves information quality: allocators with lived proximity to the work often identify capacity and risk more accurately than external program officers. For a regional association like ours, pooled funds are also an infrastructure investment, building shared diligence that all members can use.

**Limitations.** Governance is genuinely harder. Trustees must delegate allocation authority, which some charters and some temperaments do not permit. Decision timelines can lengthen. Attribution is diluted, which matters to corporate funders and to some family donors. Pooled funds work best as a *component* of a portfolio—typically 5 to 15 percent—rather than its core.

**Best fit.** Reaching small and emerging organizations; place-based or issue-based collaboratives; funders seeking community accountability alongside board accountability.

### 3.5 Recoverable grants

**What it is.** Grants structured with a contingent repayment feature: if a defined event occurs—a delayed government contract pays out, an earned-revenue venture succeeds, a capital campaign closes—the grantee returns some or all of the funds, which the funder recycles.

**Strengths.** Recoverable grants are purpose-built for the region's 94-day state payment delay. A recoverable grant that bridges a state receivable is, functionally, a zero-interest working capital facility—precisely what a grantee with 1.9 months of reserve and a 94-day receivable needs, and precisely what commercial lenders either will not provide or will price punitively. Because funds recycle, a modest allocation supports a large volume of activity over time. Recoverable structures also appeal to trustees with finance backgrounds, and can draw on assets (including DAF balances) that might not otherwise move.

**Limitations.** Recoverable grants address liquidity, not solvency; they do nothing about the indirect gap or reserve accumulation, and if used carelessly they can load contingent liabilities onto weak balance sheets. They require legal documentation, receivable verification, and collections discipline that most program teams do not have. They should never substitute for grants where a grant is what is needed.

**Best fit.** Bridging government receivables; capital projects; a specialized sleeve of 3 to 8 percent of payout, managed with finance-office involvement.

### 3.6 Summary comparison

**Table 3. Five practices compared**

| Dimension | MYGOS | Tiered trust-based | Full-indirect project grants | Participatory pooled funds | Recoverable grants |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Builds grantee reserves | Strong | Strong (top tiers) | Weak | Moderate | None (liquidity only) |
| Closes indirect gap | Fully (moot) | Fully (top tiers) | Fully | Varies by pool policy | Not applicable |
| Reduces reporting burden | Strong | Strong (top tiers) | None | Strong (shared reporting) | None |
| Accountability mechanism | Organizational health metrics | Tier criteria and review | Project deliverables | Community allocators | Repayment trigger |
| Donor-intent compatibility | Requires purpose flexibility | Moderate | High | Moderate | High |
| Small-grantee suitability | Moderate (diligence bar) | Strong (on-ramp tiers) | Moderate | Strong | Weak |
| Implementation difficulty | Moderate | Moderate–high | Low | High | High (specialized) |

No single practice dominates. The portfolio question—how a real foundation combines them—is the subject of the next section.

## 4. A Three-Year Transition Model for a $40 Million Payout

Members asked the Lab for a concrete transition model: not principles, but a schedule. We present one here for a hypothetical but representative member—a private foundation with a $40 million annual payout, a portfolio of roughly 350 active grantees, a program staff of ten, and a board of twelve trustees meeting quarterly. Funders of different scale can prorate.

### 4.1 Design principles

The model rests on four principles drawn from member experience:

1. **Move the base, not the edge.** Pilots confined to 2 percent of payout change nothing and teach little. The model converts the *majority* of the portfolio by Year 3 while retaining project instruments where they genuinely fit.
2. **Sequence policy before money.** Board policy changes precede portfolio changes by at least one quarter, so that staff are never granting ahead of authority.
3. **Full indirect is the floor from day one.** Whatever else is phased, no grant issued after the transition begins should pay below audited true indirect cost. This is the cheapest, fastest reform available and it requires no restructuring of the portfolio.
4. **Capacity conversion, not capacity cuts.** Staff time released from grant administration is redeployed to diligence, financial analysis, and grantee relationship work—not eliminated.

### 4.2 The schedule

**Table 4. Portfolio allocation by year ($40M annual payout)**

| Instrument | Baseline (Year 0) | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 (steady state) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Multiyear general operating support | $4M (10%) | $12M (30%) | $20M (50%) | $22M (55%) |
| Project grants at full indirect | $2M (5%) | $16M (40%) | $10M (25%) | $8M (20%) |
| Project grants at capped indirect | $32M (80%) | $6M (15%) | $0 | $0 |
| Participatory pooled fund contributions | $1M (2.5%) | $2M (5%) | $4M (10%) | $4M (10%) |
| Recoverable grant sleeve | $0 | $1M (2.5%) | $2M (5%) | $2M (5%) |
| Transition and capacity reserve | $1M (2.5%) | $3M (7.5%) | $4M (10%) | $4M (10%) |
| **Total** | **$40M** | **$40M** | **$40M** | **$40M** |

The tiered trust-based framework is not a budget line; it is the *architecture* that governs which grantees move into MYGOS and on what schedule, adopted as board policy in Year 1.

**Year 1 — Foundation and floor.**
- *Q1:* Board adopts three policies: (a) full indirect cost recovery on all new grants; (b) a three-tier trust framework with published criteria (Tier 1: multiyear unrestricted eligibility; Tier 2: annual flexible grants with light reporting; Tier 3: project grants with standard diligence, and a defined promotion pathway); (c) delegated authority for staff approval of renewals within board-set parameters.
- *Q2:* Staff complete tier assignment for all 350 grantees using existing files, audits, and relationship history. Roughly 60–80 grantees—those with five-plus years of relationship, clean audits, and stable leadership—qualify for Tier 1.
- *Q3–Q4:* First cohort of approximately 50 Tier 1 grantees converts to three-year MYGOS. All remaining new grants issue at full indirect. Recoverable grant sleeve launches with $1 million focused on bridging state receivables, documented with counsel. Pooled fund contribution doubles.
- *Transition reserve ($3M)* funds one-time grantee capacity grants (financial systems, audit readiness for organizations approaching Tier 1), staff training, and legal and systems costs.

**Year 2 — Conversion at scale.**
- Second MYGOS cohort of approximately 70 grantees converts; total multiyear unrestricted book reaches $20 million across roughly 120 organizations.
- Capped-indirect grants are eliminated entirely; all remaining project grants pay audited true rates.
- Pooled fund allocation reaches $4 million, deliberately weighted toward small and emerging organizations for whom the pool serves as the primary access point.
- First annual tier review: promotions and, where warranted, demotions, with written rationale to the board.

**Year 3 — Steady state.**
- MYGOS stabilizes at 55 percent of payout. Because grants are multiyear, roughly one-third of the MYGOS book renews each year, smoothing workload.
- Project grants at full indirect settle at 20 percent, reserved for genuinely time-bound work: capital projects, research, pilots, and donor-restricted purposes.
- The 10 percent transition reserve converts to a permanent *organizational resilience allocation*: capacity grants, emergency bridge support, and reserve-matching grants (the foundation matches grantee reserve deposits up to a cap—directly attacking the 41-percent-under-three-months problem).

### 4.3 Staffing implications

The transition changes the shape of program work more than its size.

**Table 5. Staffing model, Year 0 vs. Year 3 (10 FTE program team)**

| Function | Year 0 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|
| Grant administration and reports processing | 4.0 FTE | 1.5 FTE |
| Program officers (relationship and diligence) | 5.0 FTE | 5.5 FTE |
| Financial analysis (audit review, indirect verification, recoverable structuring) | 0.5 FTE | 2.0 FTE |
| Evaluation and organizational-health monitoring | 0.5 FTE | 1.0 FTE |
| **Total** | **10.0** | **10.0** |

The volume drivers move in the funder's favor: 350 annual grants with quarterly or semiannual reports become roughly 200 active relationships, most on annual reporting. The released administrative capacity is converted into the financial analysis function the model requires—reading audits, verifying indirect rates, monitoring reserve trajectories, and structuring recoverable instruments. One new hire with nonprofit finance or audit background is typically needed; the remainder is retraining. Budget approximately $150,000–$200,000 annually for the added finance capacity, funded from the transition reserve in Years 1–2 and from administrative budget thereafter.

### 4.4 Board policy changes required

1. **Grant instrument policy** authorizing multiyear commitments (with an aggregate cap on out-year obligations, e.g., no more than 175 percent of one year's payout committed forward at any time).
2. **Indirect cost policy** setting audited true rate as the minimum for all project grants.
3. **Tier framework policy** with published criteria, annual review, and appeal process.
4. **Delegation policy** permitting staff approval of Tier 1 renewals within parameters, with board ratification.
5. **Recoverable grant policy** defining eligible uses, documentation standards, loss tolerance (we suggest budgeting for 10–15 percent non-recovery), and finance-office oversight.
6. **Spending policy note** confirming that multiyear commitments are compatible with payout requirements and investment policy (counsel should confirm treatment of pledged future grants).

### 4.5 Risk controls

- **Concentration limit:** no single grantee above 5 percent of annual payout, and MYGOS grants capped at 25–30 percent of a grantee's budget to avoid creating dependency the foundation cannot responsibly unwind.
- **Organizational health dashboard:** each MYGOS grantee monitored annually on four indicators—months of reserve, current ratio, leadership tenure, and audit findings—with defined thresholds triggering staff engagement rather than automatic termination.
- **Off-ramp protocol:** MYGOS grants that will not renew receive a minimum of twelve months' notice and, where appropriate, a final tapering grant. Abrupt exits from unrestricted support do more damage than restricted grants ever did.
- **Commitment ledger:** quarterly board reporting of all out-year obligations against the aggregate cap.
- **Recoverable grant reserve:** losses booked against the sleeve, not against program budgets, so that a failed recovery never punishes a program area.
- **Annual portfolio audit:** the evaluation function reports yearly on the same metrics this paper reports for the field—grantee reserves, turnover, reporting hours—so the board can see whether the transition is producing the outcomes that justified it.

### 4.6 Costs of the transition

Direct incremental costs are modest relative to payout: roughly $400,000–$600,000 over three years for staffing conversion, legal work, systems, and training, plus the transition reserve (which is grantmaking, not overhead). The larger "cost" is the shift of roughly $6 million per year (the 15.4-point indirect gap applied to the former project portfolio) from *nominal program purchasing* to *true cost payment*. Trustees should understand this clearly: the foundation will fund somewhat fewer nominal projects at honest prices rather than more projects at prices that were never real. The prior figure was an accounting fiction financed by grantee balance sheets.

## 5. Answering the Trustee Objections

Three objections recur in every board conversation our members report. Each deserves a serious answer.

### 5.1 "Unrestricted funding means less accountability."

The objection assumes that restriction produces accountability. Our data suggest it mainly produces *paperwork*. A median of 71 hours of reporting labor per restricted grant is generating compliance documentation, not insight; meanwhile, the organizations subject to the heaviest restriction regimes show the weakest reserves and the highest leadership turnover—outcomes that project reports never surface until they become crises.

The transition model does not remove accountability; it *relocates* it from outputs to outcomes and from documents to indicators. A funder that reviews audited reserves, current ratios, leadership tenure, and audit findings annually knows far more about whether its money is producing a durable institution than a funder that reads eight project reports. The tier framework adds a second layer: flexibility is earned against published criteria and reviewed annually, with real movement in both directions. Trustees who have adopted tiered frameworks among our members consistently report that their visibility into grantee health *improved* after the shift, because staff time moved from processing reports to reading financial statements and sitting with leadership.

Accountability for *what*, is the real question. If the answer is "that the money was spent as budgeted," restriction delivers it—at 71 hours per grant per year. If the answer is "that the organization is stronger and the mission is advancing," organizational-health monitoring delivers it better and cheaper.

### 5.2 "Our donors intended their money for programs, not overhead."

Three responses, in ascending order of force.

First, **the dichotomy is false**. There is no program without the organization that runs it. The 26.8 percent audited indirect rate in our data is not waste; it is rent, audit, insurance, technology, HR, and the finance staff who ensure that restricted funds are spent as restricted. A donor who intends to fund a food shelf's programs intends, whether or not the language says so, to fund a food shelf that can make payroll.

Second, **most restrictions are narrower than boards remember**. In our members' experience, the majority of donor instruments restrict *field or geography* ("for the benefit of children in Ramsey County"), not *instrument* ("only project grants at capped indirect"). A multiyear unrestricted grant to a children's organization in Ramsey County honors that intent completely. Boards should ask counsel to review actual instrument language before assuming a constraint exists; frequently the "donor intent" objection turns out to be institutional habit wearing the donor's clothes.

Third, **where genuine restrictions exist, the model accommodates them**. This is precisely why full-indirect project grants remain 20 percent of the steady-state portfolio. A restricted fund can honor its terms fully while paying honest prices. Nothing in fiduciary law requires a funder to pay 11.4 percent for what costs 26.8 percent; indeed, a trustee who knowingly underprices the work the donor commissioned has an awkward intent argument of their own to answer.

### 5.3 "Small grantees can't handle large flexible grants, and this model favors the big and established."

The concern is legitimate and the model is built around it rather than against it.

The tier framework exists precisely so that flexibility is matched to capacity: a two-year-old organization with a $200,000 budget does not receive a three-year $500,000 unrestricted commitment in Year 1, and should not. It receives an appropriately sized Tier 2 or Tier 3 grant—at full indirect, which for a small organization is often the single most valuable reform on the list, since small organizations have the least ability to cross-subsidize the indirect gap—plus a defined pathway upward and eligibility for capacity grants from the transition reserve to build the financial systems Tier 1 requires.

The participatory pooled fund, at 10 percent of steady-state payout, is the model's second answer. Pooled funds with community allocators reach small, emerging, and culturally specific organizations that individual funders' pipelines systematically miss, with a single lightweight application in place of many. For the smallest grantees, the pool is not a consolation prize; it is often better access than they had under the old regime, where a full proposal-and-reporting cycle for a $10,000 project grant cost them a meaningful fraction of the grant's value in labor.

Finally, the objection cuts the other way more than trustees realize. The *status quo* favors large organizations, which can absorb the indirect gap, float 94-day receivables, and staff 570 hours of annual reporting. It is small organizations that are being broken by capped indirect rates and compliance burden. Honest pricing and proportionate process are a small-grantee agenda, not a threat to one.

## 6. Conclusion

The evidence in the Lab's datasets tells a consistent story. Where regional funders provide multiyear unrestricted support, grantees hold healthy reserves (4.8 months at median) and retain their leaders (14 percent turnover). Where funders provide annual restricted grants at capped indirect rates, grantees hold 1.9 months of reserve, lose leaders at 26 percent a year, and spend 71 hours per grant per year on reporting—while absorbing, across the portfolio, a 15.4-point gap between what their work costs and what their funders pay. Layer a 94-day state payment delay onto a sector where 41 percent of organizations hold under three months of reserve, and the fragility our members observe in their grantees is not a mystery. It is a design outcome.

The remedy is neither radical nor unaffordable. Full indirect cost recovery can be adopted at the next board meeting. A tiered framework converts trust from a slogan into a policy that a fiduciary can approve. A three-year schedule of the kind modeled here moves a $40 million payout to a portfolio that is 55 percent multiyear unrestricted, 20 percent honestly priced project grants, 10 percent pooled and participatory, 5 percent recoverable working capital, and 10 percent resilience investment—with the same staff headcount, redeployed from paperwork to judgment, and with risk controls stronger than those most restricted portfolios carry today.

Trustees' objections—accountability, donor intent, small-grantee capacity—are serious, and each has a serious answer built into the model rather than bolted onto it. What has no serious answer is the status quo: a financing system in which funders pay 11.4 percent for work that costs 26.8 percent, and then wonder why their grantees have no reserves.

The Lab will publish annual updates of the indicators reported here so that members can benchmark their own transitions against the field. We invite member foundations undertaking transitions on this model to share portfolio data with the Lab under our standard confidentiality terms, so that the next edition of this paper can report not only the problem but the measured results of its repair.
