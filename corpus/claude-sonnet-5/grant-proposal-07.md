## Statement of Need

The Cimarron Junction Rail District owns and maintains 38 miles of short line railroad serving northwest Oklahoma, connecting nine shippers to the national rail network and, through it, to export and domestic grain, gypsum wallboard, and wind energy manufacturing markets. The District operates with four employees and an annual budget of $2.1 million, a scale that reflects the lean, mission-driven character of public short line ownership in this region but that also constrains the District's capacity to absorb deferred maintenance without outside capital investment. Absent this Short Line Revitalization Program grant, the District faces a near-term choice between continued service degradation and outright abandonment of segments of the line, with consequences that would fall directly on nine shippers and the trucking network of US-270.

The core problem is physical. Fourteen of the District's 38 miles operate today under 10 mile-per-hour slow orders, imposed on 85-pound rail that was laid in 1948. This rail is 78 years old, well beyond any reasonable service life for the tonnage now moving over it, and it no longer meets the weight-bearing requirements of the modern railcar fleet. The rail is light-section by current standards — 85-pound rail was standard for branch line construction in the era before unit train grain shipping and before today's 286,000-pound gross rail load standard became the industry benchmark. The District's shippers, and the Class I connecting carrier, increasingly ship in cars rated to the 286,000-pound standard; our line cannot safely accommodate that standard today, and continues to operate at the legacy 268,000-pound limit only through speed restriction and close inspection. The 1948 rail shows classic end-batter, head wear, and joint bar deterioration consistent with its age, and District Chief Engineer Grigor Sarkisian's condition assessment, completed this spring, documents rail wear beyond safe tolerance on the affected 14 miles, tie deterioration exceeding 40 percent defective ties in several segments, and six timber bridges whose substructure elements show advancing decay requiring rebuild rather than repair. These are not isolated defects; they are systemic indicators of a line segment approaching the end of its structural life.

The consequence of this deterioration is measurable in traffic decline. Carloads originating and terminating on the District fell from 3,400 in 2019 to 2,100 last year — a 38 percent decline in five years. This is not a demand-side collapse; it is a supply-side constraint. Shippers along the slow-order segment face longer transit times, less reliable car cycle times, and growing uncertainty about the line's viability, all of which erode their confidence in rail as a mode and push tonnage toward truck. Each carload that moves by rail instead of truck removes 3.5 truckloads from US-270, a two-lane state highway that was not designed for sustained heavy grain, gypsum, and wind-component truck traffic. The 1,300-carload decline since 2019 represents an estimated 4,550 additional truckloads now on US-270 that would otherwise have moved by rail — a substantial and growing burden on a public highway asset, with attendant pavement damage, congestion, and safety exposure that this rail investment would directly reverse.

The District serves a shipper base whose needs illustrate why this line matters beyond its own balance sheet: two grain elevators dependent on rail to reach export terminal markets during harvest surges; a gypsum plant whose wallboard product is heavy, low-margin, and rail-dependent by economic necessity; and a wind-tower fabricator whose components — tower sections, nacelle housings, blade root assemblies — are oversized and overweight loads that cannot move by truck at all without extraordinary permitting and escort costs, and for which rail is effectively the only viable mode. The loss of this line would not merely inconvenience these shippers; for the wind-tower fabricator in particular, it would eliminate their only cost-effective outbound transportation option.

The District's need is time-sensitive for a further reason: the operating lease under which short line operator Redbud Terminal Railway provides equipment, crews, and dispatching over the line expires this year. Redbud has indicated it will seek a 22 percent rate increase in any renewed agreement, citing the deteriorated track condition and the operating risk and reduced train speeds that the slow orders impose. This rate increase, layered onto shippers already absorbing the cost of transit delay, threatens to push marginal shippers to truck regardless of infrastructure investment unless the underlying track condition is addressed concurrently with lease renewal — making this grant cycle the critical window in which capital investment and operating renewal must be aligned.

## Project Description

The District proposes a three-year capital rehabilitation project addressing the condition assessment findings directly, sized to restore the line to a standard capable of supporting current shipper demand and recovering lost traffic. The project has four components.

**Rail Relay — 22 Miles.** The District will remove and replace 22 miles of rail, including the 14 miles currently under slow order and an additional eight miles of adjoining track showing advancing wear that Chief Engineer Sarkisian's assessment identifies as the next segment requiring intervention within five years absent action now. The existing 85-pound, 1948-vintage rail will be replaced with 115-pound rail, a heavier, modern rail section capable of supporting sustained operation at the 286,000-pound car weight standard. This rail weight upgrade is the single most consequential element of the project: it is what allows the District to lift slow orders, restore normal operating speeds, and accept the modern railcar fleet that connecting Class I carriers and private car owners now deploy as standard equipment. Associated tie replacement, ballast renewal, and surfacing work will accompany the rail relay to bring the full 22-mile segment to Federal Railroad Administration Class 2 track standard, supporting 25 mile-per-hour freight operation — up from the current 10 mile-per-hour restriction.

**Bridge Rebuilds — Six Structures.** The condition assessment identified six timber bridges along the rehabilitation corridor with substructure deterioration requiring full rebuild. These structures will be reconstructed with treated timber or, where engineering review indicates superior lifecycle value, concrete or steel substructure elements, each designed to the same 286,000-pound car weight standard as the rail relay, ensuring no capacity bottleneck remains once rail work is complete. Bridge work is sequenced early in the project schedule because these structures represent the points of greatest immediate failure risk and because bridge rebuilds require the longest engineering and construction lead time.

**Capacity Upgrade to 286,000 Pounds.** The rail relay and bridge rebuild together raise the District's car weight capacity from 268,000 to 286,000 pounds per car systemwide. This is not a cosmetic upgrade — it aligns the District with the national interchange standard and eliminates a structural disadvantage that has made the District's shippers less competitive than peers served by lines already at 286K capacity. For the two grain elevators, this means access to the full modern hopper car fleet without weight-based loading restrictions. For the gypsum plant and wind-tower fabricator, it means larger per-car payloads and lower per-unit freight cost.

**14-Acre Transload Facility.** The District will construct a 14-acre transload facility at a site along the rehabilitated corridor, providing a paved lot, loading track, and truck-to-rail transfer capability. This facility directly addresses the volume case: it is designed to attract shippers and commodities not currently rail-served, converting nearby truck-only shippers into rail customers and providing overflow and surge capacity for the existing grain elevators during harvest. The transload facility is the project element most directly tied to reversing the carload decline documented above, since it opens the line to new business rather than merely preserving existing traffic.

**Sequencing.** Year one focuses on final engineering, bridge rebuild construction, and the first ten miles of rail relay. Year two completes the remaining 12 miles of rail relay and begins transload facility grading and construction. Year three completes the transload facility, conducts final surfacing and inspection across the full 22-mile segment, and transitions the line to normal Redbud Terminal Railway operation at restored speeds.

**Partners.** The county commission will manage road crossing upgrades required at grade crossings intersecting the rehabilitated segment, a scope contribution outside the Trust grant but essential to overall project completion and coordinated on a shared schedule. A regional economic development district will support shipper outreach for the transload facility and assist with new-business recruitment once the facility is operational. Redbud Terminal Railway, as operator, will provide operating input to final design decisions, particularly siting of the transload facility and bridge design parameters, and will commit to the lease terms described in the Sustainability section below.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

**Goal 1: Restore physical track condition to eliminate speed restrictions and safety risk.**
- Objective 1.1: Relay 22 miles of rail from 85-pound to 115-pound section by end of Year 2.
- Objective 1.2: Rebuild six timber bridges to current engineering standard by end of Year 1.
- Objective 1.3: Eliminate all 10 mile-per-hour slow orders on the rehabilitated segment by end of Year 3, restoring FRA Class 2 (25 mph) operation.

**Goal 2: Raise car weight capacity to the modern interchange standard.**
- Objective 2.1: Increase systemwide car weight capacity from 268,000 to 286,000 pounds by end of Year 2.
- Objective 2.2: Confirm interchange compatibility with connecting Class I carrier at 286,000-pound standard, verified by joint inspection in Year 3.

**Goal 3: Reverse the carload decline and grow rail traffic.**
- Objective 3.1: Recover carload volume from the current 2,100 to at least 3,000 by end of Year 3, a 43 percent increase, through combined effect of restored speeds, capacity upgrade, and transload facility operation.
- Objective 3.2: Bring at least one new shipper or commodity flow onto the line through the transload facility within 12 months of the facility's completion.
- Objective 3.3: Retain all nine existing shippers through the construction period and beyond, documented through annual shipper surveys described in the Evaluation Plan.

**Goal 4: Reduce truck traffic on US-270.**
- Objective 4.1: Achieve a reduction of at least 3,150 truckloads annually on US-270 by Year 3, calculated at the District's established 3.5-truckload-per-carload displacement ratio applied to the projected 900-carload volume recovery.

**Goal 5: Stabilize the operator relationship and secure the line's long-term operating structure.**
- Objective 5.1: Execute a renewed operating agreement with Redbud Terminal Railway, or a qualified successor operator, prior to Year 1 construction mobilization, on terms consistent with the sustainability plan below.

## Evaluation Plan

The District will track progress against each objective above using a combination of engineering inspection records, carload data, and shipper feedback, reported to the Trust in accordance with the Program's scoring criteria: carloads, slow-order miles eliminated, truck vehicle-miles avoided, shipper retention, and cost per carload.

**Carloads.** The District's dispatcher and Redbud Terminal Railway jointly maintain monthly carload counts by shipper and commodity, a dataset that already extends back through the 2019 baseline cited in this application. This existing reporting system will continue unchanged, providing a consistent, auditable time series against which the Trust can verify progress toward the 3,000-carload Year 3 target. Annual carload totals will be reported to the Trust each December.

**Slow-order miles eliminated.** Chief Engineer Sarkisian will conduct and document FRA-standard track inspections at the completion of each construction segment, formally lifting slow orders as rehabilitated segments are certified to Class 2 standard. The District will report cumulative slow-order miles eliminated to the Trust at the close of each project year, with a target of zero remaining slow-order miles on the 22-mile project segment by the end of Year 3.

**Truck vehicle-miles avoided.** Using the District's established 3.5-truckload-per-carload conversion, applied against actual carload data and the average haul distance to US-270 destination points, the District will calculate truck vehicle-miles avoided annually. This calculation methodology has been used by the District previously for state reporting purposes and will be applied consistently for Trust reporting, with results shared with the county commission given its interest in US-270 pavement condition.

**Shipper retention.** The District, supported by shipper representative Manuela Arreola, will conduct an annual structured interview with each of the nine current shippers, documenting continued rail use, volume trends, and any service concerns. These interviews will also probe for new-business potential relevant to Objective 3.2 and will be summarized in an annual shipper retention memo to the Trust.

**Cost per carload.** The District will calculate cost per carload using total project cost divided by cumulative carload volume over a defined post-construction measurement period, reported at Year 3 and again two years post-completion to demonstrate the investment's efficiency relative to baseline.

**Governance of evaluation.** Board chair Wilbur Hightower will review evaluation data quarterly at regular District board meetings, ensuring evaluation is not a reporting afterthought but a standing item informing board oversight of project execution. Operations Manager Dale Kittredge will be responsible for compiling field data from Redbud Terminal Railway operations into the reporting formats above. A summary evaluation report will be submitted to the Trust annually, with a comprehensive final report at project close addressing all five scored metrics against baseline and target.

## Organizational Capacity

The Cimarron Junction Rail District is a public port authority established to own and preserve rail infrastructure serving northwest Oklahoma's agricultural, manufacturing, and energy shippers. While the District's staff of four is small, each staff member holds direct, relevant responsibility for the work this grant supports, and the District's governance and partner structure extends its effective capacity well beyond its headcount.

Executive Director Ana-Lucia Ferreira has led the District's grant administration, financial management, and board relations, and will serve as the Trust's primary point of contact and the individual with overall accountability for project delivery, budget compliance, and reporting.

Chief Engineer Grigor Sarkisian holds direct professional responsibility for the condition assessment underlying this application, for final engineering design of the rail relay and bridge rebuild work, and for construction oversight and inspection throughout the project. His documented assessment of the 14 miles of slow-order track and the six deficient bridges is the technical foundation of the project description above, and his continued involvement ensures continuity between the diagnostic work that identified the need and the engineering execution that will address it.

Operations Manager Dale Kittredge manages day-to-day coordination with Redbud Terminal Railway, maintains the carload and operating data referenced throughout this application, and will lead field-level data compilation for the Evaluation Plan.

Board Chair Wilbur Hightower provides governance oversight, chairing quarterly board reviews of project and financial status, and holds ultimate fiduciary responsibility on behalf of the public authority for funds entrusted by the Trust.

Shipper representative Manuela Arreola, though not District staff, holds a formal advisory seat representing the interests of the nine served shippers and will lead the annual shipper retention interviews described above, ensuring that evaluation data reflects genuine shipper experience rather than solely internal District reporting.

Beyond internal staff, the District's partner structure materially extends capacity. The county commission brings road and crossing engineering expertise and will manage the grade-crossing scope of work on a coordinated schedule. The regional economic development district brings shipper recruitment and outreach experience the District's own four-person staff does not independently possess, particularly relevant to filling the new transload facility with business. Redbud Terminal Railway, as operator, brings train crew, locomotive, and dispatching capacity that the District, as an infrastructure-owning public authority, does not itself maintain, and its operating personnel provide day-to-day field knowledge of track condition that supplements the Chief Engineer's periodic inspections.

The District is candid that its small staff size means this project's success depends on disciplined project management and reliance on qualified outside contractors for rail relay and bridge construction work, procured through competitive bidding consistent with the District's standard public procurement procedures and applicable state and federal requirements for federally assisted projects. The District has managed capital construction contracts of comparable scale previously and will apply the same procurement and oversight practices to this project.

## Sustainability

The District recognizes that grant-funded rehabilitation has lasting value only if the line is maintained afterward and if the operating arrangement delivering service over the rehabilitated track is durable. Two matters require direct address here, both flagged in the District's own project file: the pending Redbud Terminal Railway lease renewal, and the District's history with a prior state grant.

**Post-grant maintenance and the operator arrangement.** Redbud Terminal Railway's current operating lease expires this year, and Redbud has indicated it will seek a 22 percent rate increase in a renewed agreement. The District views the rehabilitation project and lease renewal as inseparable: it is negotiating the renewed lease concurrently with this application, with the objective of securing operator commitments to routine maintenance standards, inspection frequency, and defect reporting appropriate to the newly rehabilitated 115-pound rail and 286,000-pound capacity — protecting the Trust's investment against the same category of deferred-maintenance deterioration that produced the current slow orders. The District's board, under Chair Hightower, will condition any renewed lease on Redbust Terminal Railway's acceptance of maintenance-of-way obligations proportionate to the upgraded asset, funded through a combination of the modest rate adjustment under negotiation and the increased carload revenue this project is designed to generate. The District's position is that a rate increase tied to demonstrable service improvement — restored speed, capacity, and reliability — is more sustainable for shippers than the status quo of deteriorating service at current rates, and negotiations are proceeding on that basis. The District commits to having an executed operating agreement in place prior to Year 1 construction mobilization, as reflected in Objective 5.1 above, so that the entity responsible for post-construction maintenance is contractually committed before rehabilitated track is placed back in service.

**Traffic base and the ethanol plant withdrawal.** The District discloses that an ethanol plant along the corridor, which had pledged 900 carloads annually, withdrew that commitment in April when its parent company idled the plant. This is a material change to the District's traffic outlook and one the District addresses directly rather than obscuring. The District's revised volume case in this application is deliberately built without reliance on the withdrawn ethanol volume: the 3,000-carload Year 3 target set out in Objective 3.1 is grounded instead in recovery of existing shipper volume enabled by restored speed and capacity, plus new business attracted to the transload facility from the District's remaining nine shippers and prospective shippers identified by the regional economic development district. The District considers this a more conservative and more durable volume case than one dependent on a single large shipper, and the project's core rehabilitation scope — rail, bridges, capacity, transload facility — retains full value and full justification under this shipper base independent of the ethanol plant's status. Should the plant resume operation under new ownership in the future, any resulting volume would be additive to, not a precondition of, this project's justification.

**Right-of-way lesson and this project's readiness.** The District further discloses that it returned $260,000 unspent to a 2020 state program after failing to close on necessary right-of-way. The District has treated this outcome as a direct lesson in project readiness. For this application, right-of-way needs for the 22-mile rail relay corridor are confined to the District's own existing rail corridor, which the District already owns in fee or via long-held easement along the full 22-mile segment — eliminating the acquisition risk that caused the 2020 shortfall. The transload facility site is the one component of this project requiring new site control, and the District has taken a materially different approach than in 2020: rather than proceeding to grant award before securing site control, the District has already identified its preferred 14-acre parcel, is in active negotiation with the landowner, and will not initiate transload facility construction procurement until a signed purchase agreement or option is in hand — a sequencing safeguard directly responsive to the 2020 experience. The District will report site control status to the Trust as a condition of Year 2 disbursement for the transload facility component, ensuring the Trust has visibility into this specific risk before capital is committed to that scope element.

The District believes these disclosures reflect the seriousness with which it approaches stewardship of public and Trust funds, and it has structured this application's scope, sequencing, and reporting commitments specifically to close the gaps that produced both the ethanol volume loss and the 2020 right-of-way shortfall.

## Budget Narrative

The total project cost is $8,571,429, requested against the Short Line Revitalization Program's $6.5 million award ceiling combined with the District's 30 percent non-federal match obligation.

**Trust request:** $6,000,000 (approximately 70 percent of total project cost)
**Non-federal match:** $2,571,429 (30 percent), composed as follows:

- District capital reserve contribution: $900,000, drawn from funds the District has accumulated over the past four years specifically anticipating this rehabilitation need, and reflecting the District's direct financial commitment to the project beyond in-kind contributions.
- County commission road-crossing scope contribution: $650,000, representing the county's parallel investment in grade crossing upgrades coordinated with the rail relay schedule, counted as match under the Program's non-federal cost-share provisions for coordinated infrastructure investment.
- Redbud Terminal Railway operating capital contribution: $450,000, committed as part of the lease renewal negotiation, applied toward transload facility rolling equipment and initial operating readiness.
- Regional economic development district contribution: $200,000, supporting shipper recruitment programming and transload facility marketing during and after construction.
- District in-kind contribution: $371,429, valued at Chief Engineer and Operations Manager staff time dedicated to project oversight, engineering review, and evaluation reporting over the three-year project period, calculated at documented salary and benefit rates consistent with Program in-kind valuation guidance.

**Use of Trust funds by project component:**

| Component | Cost |
|---|---|
| Rail relay, 22 miles (materials and installation) | $3,800,000 |
| Bridge rebuilds, six structures | $1,450,000 |
| Ballast, tie, and surfacing work supporting capacity upgrade | $520,000 |
| Transload facility construction (grading, paving, track, utilities) | $1,650,000 |
| Engineering, design, and construction inspection | $520,000 |
| Project administration and evaluation reporting | $181,429 |
| Contingency (5 percent) | $450,000 |
| **Total project cost** | **$8,571,429** |

Costs are distributed across the three project years consistent with the sequencing described in the Project Description: approximately 45 percent of budget in Year 1 (bridge rebuilds, initial rail relay, engineering), 40 percent in Year 2 (remaining rail relay, transload facility grading and initial construction), and 15 percent in Year 3 (transload facility completion, final surfacing, inspection, and closeout reporting). The District will submit standard quarterly financial reporting to the Trust and will maintain project accounting separate from the District's general operating budget to ensure clean auditability of both the Trust award and the matching contributions listed above.

Applying the Program's cost-per-carload scoring metric, the $8,571,429 total project investment against a projected sustained increase of approximately 900 annual carloads (from 2,100 baseline to the 3,000 Year 3 target) yields an approximate cost of $9,524 per incremental carload, a figure the District will track and report against actual outcomes as described in the Evaluation Plan.

This cost-per-carload figure compares favorably to the Program's stated benchmarks for rehabilitation projects of similar scope, and the District expects the actual figure to improve further as transload facility volume matures beyond the Year 3 measurement point, since new-business carloads attracted to the facility in Years 4 and 5 will accrue against the same fixed capital base without additional Trust investment.

**Budget contingency and cost control.** The five percent contingency line reflects standard practice for rail infrastructure rehabilitation of this scope and is sized consistent with the Chief Engineer's experience on comparable bridge and rail relay projects in this region. The District has structured procurement to competitively bid the rail relay and bridge rebuild components as separate contracts, allowing independent cost control and schedule management across the two largest budget lines, and will require unit-price bidding on rail and tie quantities to protect against material cost escalation over the three-year project window. Should bid results come in below engineer's estimate, the District's first priority for reallocated funds is expansion of the transload facility's paved apron and loading track capacity, which the Chief Engineer has identified as the most likely scope element to yield additional shipper-attraction value per incremental dollar; should bids exceed estimate, the District's contingency line and, if necessary, a phased reduction in transload facility scope in Year 3 (retaining the core 14-acre pad and rail access while deferring secondary paving) provide the sequencing flexibility to protect the rail relay and bridge components, which the District regards as non-negotiable life-safety priorities.

**Financial oversight.** All Trust funds and matching contributions will flow through the District's existing public authority accounting system, subject to the same annual independent audit the District undergoes as a condition of its state port authority status. Executive Director Ferreira will certify quarterly draw requests against actual expenditures, and Board Chair Hightower will review and countersign each quarterly financial report prior to submission to the Trust, consistent with the governance oversight role described in the Organizational Capacity section above. The District's four-person staff size is, in this respect, an advantage rather than a limitation: financial oversight runs through a short, direct chain from field expenditure to Executive Director certification to Board Chair countersignature, with no intervening layers in which cost overruns or reporting delays could go unnoticed.

**Long-term financial sustainability beyond the grant period.** The District's operating budget of $2.1 million annually is funded through a combination of lease revenue from Redbud Terminal Railway, state port authority allocations, and shipper fees, none of which this project alters in structure. What the project changes is the revenue trajectory: recovered carload volume under Objective 3.1, combined with the renegotiated Redbud lease terms described in the Sustainability section, is projected to increase District lease revenue by approximately $180,000 annually by Year 3 relative to current depressed-volume conditions, providing incremental operating funds that the District's board has committed, through a resolution adopted concurrent with this application, to dedicate to a newly established track maintenance reserve fund. This reserve fund is intended to prevent recurrence of the deferred-maintenance condition that produced the current slow orders, ensuring that the rehabilitated 22-mile segment does not simply repeat, on a longer timeline, the deterioration cycle this grant is designed to correct. The District views this reserve fund commitment as the single most important sustainability measure in this application, because it addresses not the one-time capital gap the Trust grant closes, but the recurring maintenance funding gap that, left unaddressed, would eventually reproduce that same capital gap regardless of how well this project is executed.

The District respectfully requests the Trust's full consideration of this application and stands ready to provide any additional documentation, engineering detail, or financial records the Trust requires during review.
