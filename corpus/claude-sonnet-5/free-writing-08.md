# The Weight of Small Rooms

My grandmother kept a jar of buttons on the windowsill—
not for sewing, she never sewed after the war,
but because each one had belonged to a coat,
and each coat had belonged to a body,
and she liked knowing where the bodies used to be.

I used to think this was morbid.
Now I understand it was the opposite:
a woman refusing to let anything disappear twice.

*

There is a particular light that comes into a room
at 4pm in late October, sideways and apologetic,
the kind of light that makes you remember
you are inside a life, not just living one.

I have wasted so much of that light
looking at screens that promised me other rooms,
better light, elsewhere.

*

My father taught me to change a tire
in a gas station parking lot in Ohio,
not because he thought I'd need it
but because he wanted forty-five minutes
where the only thing between us
was a lug wrench and his patient hands
guiding mine toward the right amount of force—
enough to loosen, not enough to strip.

He never said *I love you* in that lot.
He said *righty-tighty, lefty-loosey*
and *good, now try the next one*
and *you're stronger than you think.*

I have thought about that sentence for twenty years.

*

What I mean is: attention is the last free thing.
No one can tax it, inherit it, take it from you
in a divorce or a recession or a diagnosis.
You can give it to a button jar,
to sideways light, to a stranger's hands
teaching you something small and permanent.

You can give it to a poem
that doesn't know what it's trying to say
until the very last line,
and then, suddenly, unbearably, does.
