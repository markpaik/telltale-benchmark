# LARKSPUR MARKETS
## Internal Memoranda — Digital Loyalty Program, Store Portfolio Actions

**TO:** All Store Directors; Corporate Staff, Greenfield
**FROM:** Deshawn Whitfield, President
**DATE:** February 9, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Launch of the Larkspur Rewards program and the closure of four stores

I am writing to tell you about two decisions the board approved on Friday. They are connected, and I want you to hear them from me at the same time rather than in pieces over the next several weeks.

The first decision is that Larkspur will retire the weekly paper circular as the center of our promotional model and replace it with a digital loyalty and targeted-pricing program called Larkspur Rewards. The second is that we will close four stores — Adams, Bellows Falls, North Hoosac, and Turners Falls Center — and reopen Bellows Falls and Turners Falls Center in 2028 at roughly 12,000 square feet under a smaller format we are calling Larkspur Neighborhood.

I want to explain why, because the reasoning matters more than the announcement.

Our gross margin was 26.8 percent two years ago. It is 24.1 percent now. That is 2.7 points on $610 million, which is about $16.5 million of gross profit that used to exist and no longer does. Nothing about our cost structure absorbed that. We did not become less efficient at moving product; our shrink numbers are better than they were three years ago and our labor productivity in the front end has improved every quarter since the scheduling changes Renata's team put in place. What happened is that we gave away margin through the circular, and we gave it away without knowing who we were giving it to.

The circular is a broadcast instrument. Every household in a trade area gets the same twelve-ounce coffee at $4.99 whether they were going to buy coffee at $8.49 that week or not. We have been paying full freight to discount a customer who did not need discounting, and we have been unable to reach the customer who would have shopped a second trip if we had given her a reason. Vendor funding papered over that for a long time. It does not anymore, because vendors have their own data now and they know what their circular dollars are buying.

Larkspur Rewards changes the unit of promotion from the item to the household. A customer enrolls, we learn what she buys, and we price to her. The pilot data Yuki's team will circulate separately shows the mechanism works. I will let her present it in full.

The store closures are a separate problem with the same root. Adams, Bellows Falls, North Hoosac, and Turners Falls Center lost money in all four quarters of 2026. Not a soft quarter, not a bad December — every quarter. Three of the four have been marginal since 2023 and we kept them because the trade areas have no other full-service grocery and because closing a store is a serious thing to do to a town. I still believe both of those things. But we cannot ask the twenty-four profitable stores to carry four losing ones indefinitely while our overall margin is contracting, and continuing to operate them at their current footprint is not a plan, it is a delay.

The footprint is the actual issue. Adams is 41,000 square feet. Bellows Falls is 38,000. North Hoosac is 44,000. Turners Falls Center is 36,000. Those were correct sizes when they were built. They are not correct now. A store that size in a trade area of eight to eleven thousand people has to do volume we cannot generate, and every square foot of it is heated, lit, staffed, insured, and stocked whether it earns or not. The perimeter departments in those four stores are actually fine — the delis and produce departments at Bellows Falls and Turners Falls Center perform at or above chain average per square foot. What is failing is 20,000 square feet of center store carrying slow-moving grocery at a turn rate that does not justify the space.

So we are not concluding that those towns cannot support a Larkspur. We are concluding they cannot support 38,000 square feet of Larkspur. Bellows Falls and Turners Falls Center will reopen at 12,000 square feet in 2028, built around the departments that work, with a center-store assortment cut to the items those households actually buy — which is precisely what Larkspur Rewards data will tell us. Adams and North Hoosac will not reopen. Both have lease and physical-plant problems that make a conversion uneconomic, and I would rather be honest about that now than hold out a possibility I do not think is real.

Here is what I want store directors to do.

Closing stores: your general managers heard from me directly this morning and Estelle's team will be on site at all four locations within seventy-two hours. Do not speculate with your teams about who transfers where. Estelle's memo, which follows this one, sets the actual policy, and it is a better policy than the rumor mill will invent. Send people to it.

Continuing stores: you will begin hearing from customers about the circular within days of the first announcement, and you will hear from your own associates before that. The message is not "we are cutting coupons." The message is that a customer who enrolls in Larkspur Rewards will get better prices on the things she actually buys than the circular ever gave her, and the pilot stores can prove it. Your enrollment numbers will be the single most-watched operating metric in this company for the next eighteen months, and I am telling you that now so it is not a surprise when it shows up in your reviews.

Everyone: the UFCW contract covering 2,400 of our people reopens in January. The closures are not a bargaining tactic and no one should characterize them that way, internally or externally. If you are asked, the answer is that the four stores lost money every quarter of last year, that we are reopening two of them, and that every affected employee has recall rights. That is the whole answer and it is true.

I have been at Larkspur eleven years. This is the hardest set of decisions I have been part of. I would ask you to give Estelle's team, Renata's team, and Yuki's team the benefit of the doubt over the next several months as they work through a great deal of detail under real time pressure. And I would ask you to remember that the 310 people affected by the closures are our colleagues, that most of them will still be our colleagues in six months, and that how we treat them now is the thing this company will be remembered for internally long after the margin recovers.

**TO:** Deshawn Whitfield, President; Board Finance Committee
**FROM:** Padraig Malloy, Chief Financial Officer
**DATE:** February 26, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Revised capital plan for Larkspur Rewards and restated FY28 gross margin forecast

This memo revises the capital plan I presented in October and restates the FY28 gross margin forecast that plan supported. Both numbers have moved materially and in the wrong direction, and I want the committee to have the full picture in one place rather than assembled from three sets of minutes.

### Capital

The October estimate was $5.4 million. The current estimate is $7.9 million. The variance is $2.5 million, or 46 percent, and I want to be direct that a variance of that size at this stage of a project is a planning failure and not a surprise event. I have broken it into four pieces.

The largest single item, $1.1 million, is point-of-sale integration at all 28 stores. Our original estimate assumed the loyalty platform would communicate with the existing POS through the vendor's standard middleware. It will not. Our POS is on a release the vendor no longer certifies against, and the choice is a middleware development project or a POS upgrade. We priced both. The middleware path is $1.1 million and leaves us on an uncertified release with a support risk we would carry indefinitely. The POS upgrade is $2.4 million and resolves the risk but adds nine months. I recommend the middleware path, with the POS upgrade moved into the FY29 capital plan as a scheduled item rather than an emergency one. The committee should understand that this is a deliberate acceptance of technical debt and that we will pay for it later.

The second item is $640,000 for network and in-store hardware — shelf-edge digital tags in the twelve highest-volume stores, wireless infrastructure upgrades in nineteen stores, and replacement scanning hardware at customer service desks. The October estimate covered tags in six stores and assumed existing wireless was adequate. Site surveys in November and December showed it was not; eleven of our stores have wireless coverage that fails in the back half of center store, which is precisely where the shelf-edge tags need to live.

The third item is $480,000 in additional professional services — data migration, customer file cleansing, and integration testing. Our customer file is worse than we represented to ourselves. We have approximately 214,000 records from three prior loyalty attempts, with duplicate rates I would estimate at 30 percent and address data that in many cases predates the 2019 system change. Migrating that file honestly requires work we did not scope.

The fourth item is $280,000 in extended vendor implementation and project-management costs associated with the schedule slip described below.

### Schedule

Vendor go-live has moved from March to August. The vendor attributes the slip to the POS integration scope described above and to resource constraints on their side arising from two other client implementations. I accept the first explanation and am skeptical of the second. Our contract has a service-credit provision for schedule failure and I have instructed our counsel to preserve that claim; I do not expect it to yield more than $150,000 to $200,000 and I have not built it into any forecast.

The practical consequence is that the program will contribute essentially nothing to FY28 first half. We had modeled a March go-live with ramp through the summer and a meaningful second-half contribution. We now have an August go-live, which given a realistic enrollment ramp means the first quarter in which the program materially affects margin is the third quarter of FY28.

### Vendor promotional funding

This is the more serious number and I want to spend the most time on it.

The six-store pilot cut vendor promotional funding by an annualized $1.2 million across those six stores. Extrapolated across 28 stores on a volume-weighted basis, the exposure is approximately $4.6 million annually against a current vendor promotional funding base of $14.2 million. That is a 32 percent reduction.

The mechanism is straightforward and, I would argue, was foreseeable. Vendor promotional funding in this industry is largely purchased circular placement. A vendor pays for the front page, the back page, an inside feature. When the circular goes away, the inventory that funding purchased goes away with it. Targeted digital offers are a different product, and the market for that product is neither as mature nor as generously priced. Vendors will pay for a targeted offer to a household with demonstrated category purchase history — they will pay reasonably well for it, in fact — but they will pay for far fewer of them, because targeting by definition reaches fewer households.

We are trading a large number of low-quality impressions for a small number of high-quality ones. That is the correct trade for gross margin and the wrong trade for vendor funding, and the two effects do not net out on the same timeline. The margin benefit accrues over eighteen to thirty months as enrollment builds and targeting improves. The funding loss is immediate and near-total in the first year.

I have had preliminary conversations with three of our five largest vendors. All three expressed interest in the targeted product and none would commit to a spend level. One was explicit that his 2028 trade budget was set on a circular assumption and that any change would come in the 2029 planning cycle. I take that as directionally representative.

### Restated FY28 gross margin forecast

The October forecast projected FY28 gross margin at 25.4 percent, a recovery of 1.3 points from the current 24.1 percent. That forecast assumed a March go-live, full-year program contribution of approximately 0.9 points, closure of the four losing stores contributing approximately 0.5 points through mix improvement, and vendor funding declining by $800,000.

I am restating FY28 gross margin to 24.3 percent. The bridge is as follows.

We begin at 24.1 percent. Store closures contribute a positive 0.5 points, unchanged; the four closing stores carried gross margins between 21.2 and 22.8 percent and removing them improves the blended rate mechanically. The Larkspur Rewards program contributes a positive 0.4 points rather than 0.9, reflecting five months of operation rather than twelve and a conservative enrollment ramp. Vendor funding contributes a negative 0.6 points, reflecting the $4.6 million exposure partially offset by approximately $1.0 million of funding we believe is genuinely portable to the digital product in year one. Incremental depreciation on the revised capital base contributes a negative 0.1 points.

That produces 24.3 percent. I want the committee to sit with that number, because it says something uncomfortable: after closing four stores, spending $7.9 million, and fundamentally rebuilding how we promote, FY28 gross margin improves by two-tenths of a point.

I do not think that makes the program wrong. FY29 is where the case lives. A full year of program contribution at maturity, a second year of enrollment depth, the two Neighborhood-format reopenings, and — critically — the 2029 vendor planning cycle in which we can sell the targeted product on its merits rather than as a mid-year substitution, together support a FY29 forecast in the 25.6 to 26.1 percent range. That is a real recovery and it is the recovery that justifies the capital.

But I want the record to be clear that we are asking the board to approve $7.9 million against a first-year return of two-tenths of a point, and that the FY29 case depends on a vendor funding recovery that I cannot yet evidence with a signed commitment from anyone. If the funding does not come back — if targeted digital simply monetizes at a structurally lower rate than circular placement did — then our steady-state margin ceiling is closer to 25.2 percent than 26.0, and the strategic case has to rest on basket growth and labor efficiency rather than on trade funding. I will bring the committee a sensitivity analysis on that question in April.

### Recommendation

I recommend the committee approve the revised $7.9 million capital plan and the middleware integration path. I recommend we hold $500,000 of the authorization in reserve against further scope discovery, releasable on my approval and reportable to the committee. And I recommend that we not communicate an FY28 margin recovery externally or to the store organization, because 24.3 percent will not feel like a recovery to anyone and we should not set an expectation we will spend the year defending.

**TO:** All Employees, Adams, Bellows Falls, North Hoosac, and Turners Falls Center; All Store Directors
**FROM:** Estelle Nwachukwu, Vice President of Human Resources
**DATE:** March 4, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Transfer, severance, scheduling, and recall policy for employees affected by store closures

This memo sets the complete policy for the 310 employees affected by the closures announced on February 9. It supersedes anything you have been told verbally by anyone, including me. If something in this memo conflicts with what a manager has said to you, this memo governs. If something in this memo conflicts with the collective bargaining agreement for employees covered by UFCW Local 1459, the agreement governs, and I have noted below where I believe those provisions apply.

I want to say at the outset that I know a memo is a poor instrument for this. My team will be at all four stores, in person, for at least two full shifts per week between now and the closing dates, and every one of you will have the opportunity to sit down with a member of my staff individually. This document exists so that you have the rules in writing and can hold us to them.

### Closing dates

Adams closes June 27, 2027. North Hoosac closes July 11, 2027. Bellows Falls closes August 8, 2027. Turners Falls Center closes August 22, 2027. These dates are firm. Employees will be paid through their store's closing date regardless of when merchandise operations wind down, and no one will be released early against their wishes.

### Transfer rights

Every employee at a closing store has the right to transfer to another Larkspur store. This is not a limited pool of openings that some employees will get and others will not. We have 24 continuing stores and, as of March 1, 187 posted and unposted openings across them. We will create positions where the openings do not naturally exist.

Transfers work as follows. Each of you will receive a transfer preference form by March 18. You will rank up to five stores in order of preference and indicate your preferred department, shift, and weekly hours. Forms are due April 10.

For bargaining-unit employees, placement follows the seniority and job-classification provisions of Article 14 of the collective bargaining agreement. Chain seniority carries in full. An employee with fourteen years at Bellows Falls transfers to Greenfield Main with fourteen years of chain seniority, and that seniority governs bidding, vacation selection, and layoff protection at the receiving store exactly as it did at the sending store. This is not a new policy; it is the existing contract, and I state it here because it is the question I have been asked most often.

For non-bargaining-unit employees, placement follows preference order with tenure as the tiebreaker where two employees seek the same position.

Where an employee's first-preference store cannot accommodate her, we will offer the highest-ranked alternative that can. Where none of the five ranked stores can accommodate an employee in her classification, my office will contact her personally before any assignment is made. No one will receive a placement letter for a store she did not rank without a conversation first.

### Wage protection

Any employee who transfers to a position at a lower base rate will be red-circled at her current rate for 24 months from the transfer date. Her rate will not decrease during that period and she will receive any general wage increases negotiated or granted during it. At the end of 24 months her rate will be the higher of her red-circled rate or the then-current rate for her classification. I want to be plain that this exceeds the 12-month protection in Article 14, Section 6, and that we are offering it because a closure is not an ordinary transfer.

Employees who transfer to a position at a higher base rate move to the higher rate immediately.

### Hours protection and scheduling

This is the provision I expect to generate the most difficulty, and I want to explain the mechanics carefully.

A transferring employee is guaranteed her average weekly scheduled hours over the 26 weeks preceding February 9, 2027, for a period of 26 weeks following her transfer. If you averaged 34 hours, you are guaranteed 34 hours. If you averaged 21, you are guaranteed 21. Your averaged figure will appear on your transfer preference form so you can verify it before you rank stores; if you believe it is wrong, tell us and we will recalculate.

The difficulty is that receiving stores have existing employees with existing schedules, and the collective bargaining agreement governs how hours are allocated among bargaining-unit employees at a store. We cannot guarantee a transferring employee 34 hours by taking hours from an incumbent employee who has bid them under Article 9. Where the arithmetic does not work, we will meet the guarantee by adding hours to the receiving store's labor budget rather than by reallocating existing hours. Renata Kovalenko and I have agreed a mechanism for this and the incremental labor cost sits in a corporate account, not in the receiving store's controllable expense. Store directors: your P&L will not be penalized for absorbing transferred employees. I want that understood clearly, because I do not want a single placement decision influenced by a store director's labor line.

Shift preference is not guaranteed. We will honor it where we can and we will be honest where we cannot. An employee who has worked days for nine years and is offered nights should tell us, and we will look harder before we finalize.

### Commuting

Several of you face a materially longer commute. Where a transfer increases one-way driving distance by more than 15 miles, we will pay a commuting differential of $0.42 per additional mile, each way, for each scheduled day worked, for 12 months. This is taxable and will appear on your paycheck. It is calculated from your home address of record; update it with my office if it has changed.

Where no continuing store is within a reasonable commute, an employee may elect severance instead of transfer without losing any severance benefit. Electing severance in that circumstance is not a resignation and does not affect unemployment eligibility, and my office will provide written confirmation of that to the relevant state agency in every such case.

### Severance

Any affected employee may elect severance instead of transfer, for any reason, without explanation.

Severance is two weeks of pay per year of service, with a minimum of six weeks and no maximum, calculated on average weekly earnings over the preceding 26 weeks including overtime and premium pay. An employee with 22 years of service receives 44 weeks. Health coverage continues at active-employee contribution rates for the length of the severance period or six months, whichever is longer, and we will pay the employer share of COBRA thereafter for an additional three months.

Severance elections are due May 1 for Adams and North Hoosac employees and June 15 for Bellows Falls and Turners Falls Center employees.

### Recall rights

Every affected employee retains recall rights for 24 months from her store's closing date, whether she transfers or elects severance. Recall rights mean that when a position opens in your classification at any Larkspur store, employees on the recall list are offered it in chain-seniority order before any external candidate is considered.

Bellows Falls and Turners Falls Center will reopen in the Neighborhood format in 2028. Former employees of those stores have first recall rights to positions there, ahead of the general recall list, in chain-seniority order. I want to be honest about scale: a 12,000-square-foot store will employ perhaps 35 to 45 people where the current stores employ 78 and 71. The reopenings are real and the recall right is real, and it will not accommodate everyone.

### Support services

An outplacement firm will be on site at all four stores beginning March 23 — résumé work, interview preparation, and a job board with regional employers who have told us they are hiring. Our employee assistance program is available to every affected employee and every member of her household at no cost, including for the next 12 months after separation. The number is on the back of your insurance card and calls are confidential; my office receives no report of who calls or why.

I will be at Adams on March 12, North Hoosac on March 13, Bellows Falls on March 19, and Turners Falls Center on March 20, for the full day each, and I will hold open sessions in the break room during every shift change. Come tell me what this memo gets wrong.

**TO:** Deshawn Whitfield, President; Executive Committee
**FROM:** Yuki Tanabe-Ortiz, Senior Vice President of Merchandising
**DATE:** March 30, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Larkspur Rewards six-store pilot — results at week 26 and implications for chain rollout

The pilot began September 28, 2026, at Greenfield Main, Northampton Pleasant, Brattleboro, Amherst Route 9, Pittsfield Dalton Avenue, and Springfield Boston Road. This memo reports results through week 26 and sets out what I believe they do and do not tell us.

### Enrollment

Enrollment across the six stores reached 61 percent of measurable transactions at week 26, against a target of 55 percent. Greenfield Main and Amherst Route 9 exceeded 70 percent; Springfield Boston Road reached 48 percent and is the outlier. The Springfield gap is a language and channel issue — enrollment there requires a smartphone app or a web form, and a meaningful share of that store's customers use neither. We have added an in-store kiosk and a paper enrollment path at Springfield and enrollment has moved four points in three weeks. Chain rollout must include the kiosk and paper path from day one; treating them as a remediation for underperforming stores was a mistake.

Enrollment skews younger and more affluent than our customer base overall, which is the expected pattern and the one that concerns me most. Households in our two lowest-spending quintiles enrolled at 44 percent against 68 percent for the two highest. If that persists at scale, we will have built a promotional system that systematically underserves the customers most sensitive to price, which is both a commercial problem and a problem I am not comfortable with on other grounds. Recommendations below.

### Basket

Average basket across the six pilot stores rose 4.1 percent against a control group of eight demographically matched stores. This is the headline number and it is real, but it requires decomposition.

Enrolled households increased basket 6.8 percent. Unenrolled households at pilot stores decreased basket 1.4 percent. The blended 4.1 percent reflects both, weighted by transaction share.

The unenrolled decline is important. Those customers lost the circular and gained nothing, and they responded by buying less. At 39 percent of transactions that is a drag we are carrying, and it will persist for whatever share of our customers never enrolls. If the terminal enrollment rate is 75 percent, we carry a 1.4 percent basket decline on a quarter of our business indefinitely. This is a strong argument for aggressive enrollment investment and for a residual non-targeted price presence — a limited set of genuinely good prices available to everyone — which I discuss below.

Within enrolled households, the basket gain came predominantly from trip frequency rather than trip size. Enrolled households shopped 4.4 percent more often and spent 2.3 percent more per trip. That is the better of the two outcomes; frequency is stickier than size and it reflects the offers doing what they are supposed to do, which is generate an occasion.

Category performance varied more than I expected. Center store grocery, which is where we targeted most heavily, gained 5.9 percent among enrolled households. Fresh meat gained 8.2 percent, which surprised us — we had assumed meat purchase was price-driven and destination-based rather than promotable to a targeted household, and that assumption was wrong. Produce was flat. Frozen gained 3.1 percent. Health and beauty declined 2.2 percent among enrolled households, which I believe reflects our having pulled circular support from a category that genuinely depended on it.

### Margin

Gross margin at pilot stores improved 82 basis points against control, before accounting for vendor funding loss. This is the number that matters and I want to explain where it comes from, because it is not primarily from charging more.

Roughly 55 percent of the improvement comes from promotional efficiency — we discounted 31 percent fewer units to generate more volume, because we stopped discounting to households that would have bought at full price. Roughly 30 percent comes from mix, as targeted offers moved households toward categories and brands with better margins. The remaining 15 percent comes from reduced markdown, because better demand signal improved our ordering on perishables.

Net of the vendor funding loss described in Padraig's February 26 memo, pilot-store margin improved 24 basis points. I want to state that plainly rather than lead with 82. The gross mechanism is strong. The net result in year one is modest, and it is modest for the reason Padraig identified: we lost the funding immediately and will earn the margin gradually.

### Vendor funding

The $1.2 million annualized reduction across six stores breaks down as follows. Circular-placement fees, which were $1.94 million annualized across the six stores, fell to zero. Digital targeted-offer fees, a new line, generated $610,000. Category-management and slotting arrangements, unaffected by the circular change, were flat at $2.1 million. Net reduction $1.33 million, partially offset by $130,000 of new performance-based funding tied to targeted-offer redemption, for a net $1.2 million.

The $610,000 in new digital fees came from eleven vendors, of whom four accounted for 78 percent. Those four are the vendors with the most sophisticated internal data capability, and the pattern suggests that funding recovery will track vendor analytical maturity rather than vendor size. I have asked my team to build a vendor-readiness segmentation and to concentrate 2028 selling effort on the second tier — vendors who have the interest but not yet the internal capability — with an offer to provide the analysis on their behalf. That is a merchandising service we have never provided and I think it is where the recovery lives.

### What the pilot does not tell us

Six stores are not 28 stores. The pilot stores skew toward our stronger, more urban, higher-income locations, chosen deliberately because we wanted the mechanism tested where enrollment would be easiest. That was the right choice for testing the mechanism and it means our enrollment and basket figures are optimistic for the chain. I would apply a discount of 15 to 20 percent to the enrollment projection and 20 to 25 percent to the basket projection when modeling the full chain.

The pilot ran 26 weeks. We do not know what happens in month 14, when the novelty of personalized offers has worn off and the question becomes whether the offers are actually good. Loyalty programs in this industry have a well-documented pattern of first-year enthusiasm followed by second-year indifference, and nothing in 26 weeks of data speaks to it.

The pilot ran without a competitive response. Two of our three principal competitors run mature loyalty programs and neither altered its promotional posture in our pilot markets. At chain scale they will notice.

### Recommendations

First, add the kiosk and paper enrollment path to the chain rollout scope from day one, and staff enrollment assistance at customer service in every store for the first twelve weeks. The Springfield experience is not an edge case; it is what will happen in Adams, Ware, Orange, and half our Berkshire County stores.

Second, establish a permanent non-targeted price tier — I would suggest 40 to 60 items, refreshed weekly, at genuinely competitive prices, available to every customer without enrollment. This costs margin and I am recommending it anyway. It addresses the unenrolled basket decline, it protects our price image with customers who will never enroll, and it gives store directors something to point to when a customer asks what happened to the circular.

Third, hold health and beauty on a circular-equivalent promotional program for at least two quarters past go-live while we determine whether the 2.2 percent decline is a targeting failure or a category characteristic.

Fourth, approve the vendor-readiness selling program described above, at an estimated cost of $340,000 in analytical staffing for FY28. Against a $4.6 million exposure I regard this as the highest-return spending in the entire program.

I will report again at week 39.

**TO:** Marisol Etchegaray, President, UFCW Local 1459
**CC:** Estelle Nwachukwu; Renata Kovalenko; Padraig Malloy
**FROM:** Deshawn Whitfield, President
**DATE:** April 20, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Response to the Local's formal objection dated April 6, 2027

Marisol,

I have your April 6 letter objecting to the notice period for the four store closures. I have read it carefully, I have discussed it with Estelle and with our counsel, and I am responding in writing because you asked me to and because I think the questions you raise deserve a real answer rather than a procedural one.

Your letter makes four arguments. I will take them in order and I will tell you where I think you are right.

**On the notice period.** You contend that the notice provided — beginning February 9 for closures ranging from June 27 to August 22 — is inadequate under Article 22, Section 3, which requires ninety days' written notice of store closure. The notice periods range from 138 to 194 days and therefore exceed the contractual requirement. I do not think that is in dispute and I do not read your letter as disputing it.

Your argument is that ninety days is a floor, not a standard, and that for a closure affecting 310 employees in four communities with limited alternative grocery employment, the practical standard should be longer. You are right that ninety days is a floor. You are right that this is a larger action than the provision contemplated when it was written in 2011, which followed a single-store closure. I accept both points.

Where I disagree is on remedy. You ask that we extend the closing dates by ninety days. I cannot do that, and I want to explain why rather than simply refuse.

Three of the four stores are on leases with fixed termination mechanics. Adams and North Hoosac have landlords demanding termination payments that currently total $2.1 million, and those figures are a function of remaining term. Every month we hold those leases increases our exposure and reduces our negotiating position. More directly: the four stores lost a combined $3.4 million in 2026 and are losing at approximately the same rate now. A ninety-day extension costs roughly $850,000 in operating losses plus the lease consequence. I am not willing to spend that when the alternative uses of the money — the hours guarantee, the wage protection, the commuting differential, the extended severance — go directly to the affected employees.

I would rather put money in your members' hands than in a landlord's, and I recognize you may regard that as a false choice. I do not think it is, but I understand why it looks like one from where you sit.

**On the sequencing of the announcement.** Your second argument is that the Company announced the closures and the loyalty program in a single communication in a manner that presented the closures as a consequence of the program, and that this framing was misleading and prejudicial to the Local's members.

You are right and I got this wrong. The closures and the program are separate decisions with a common cause — margin compression — but they are not causally linked to each other. Those four stores would have been closed if we had never contemplated a loyalty program, and the program would have proceeded if all 28 stores were profitable. Presenting them together in my February 9 memo created an impression I did not intend and should have anticipated. It also, as you note, gave rise to a reading in which employees at continuing stores might understand their job security as contingent on enrollment performance. That reading is wrong, it is not our position, and I will say so directly to store directors in writing within the week. I am grateful you raised it.

**On the adequacy of transfer opportunities.** Your third argument is that the Company's representation of 187 openings across continuing stores overstates genuine capacity, because a substantial fraction of those openings are part-time positions of fewer than 20 hours and cannot absorb full-time employees.

This is your strongest argument and it is substantially correct. Of the 187 openings, 71 are part-time positions averaging 16 hours. Of the 310 affected employees, 142 are full-time. The arithmetic in my February memo was accurate as far as it went and misleading in what it implied.

Estelle's March 4 memo addresses this through the hours guarantee — a transferring employee receives her pre-closure average weekly hours for 26 weeks regardless of the posted hours of the position she moves into, with incremental hours funded corporately rather than from the receiving store's labor budget. That mechanism is designed precisely for the gap you identify. But you are right that it is a 26-week bridge and not a permanent answer, and that a full-time employee bridged into a store with no full-time capacity faces a real problem in week 27.

I am prepared to do two things. First, I will extend the hours guarantee from 26 weeks to 52 weeks for any employee transferring into a position whose posted hours are more than eight hours below her pre-closure average. Second, I will commit that for the 24-month recall period, no continuing store may fill a full-time bargaining-unit vacancy from outside the recall list while any recall-list employee in that classification holds a position with fewer hours.
