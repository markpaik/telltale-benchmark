## Statement of Need

In Merced County, approximately 3,900 children are being raised by grandparents, aunts, uncles, older siblings, and other relatives rather than by their parents. They live in kinship care because of parental incarceration, substance use disorder, mental illness, death, deportation, or the intervention of the child welfare system. Kinship care is, by every measure the field has produced, the best available outcome for a child who cannot remain with a parent: children in kinship placements experience fewer moves, stronger cultural and family continuity, better school stability, and lower rates of re-entry into foster care than children placed with strangers. Yet the caregivers who make those outcomes possible are among the least supported adults in the child welfare system.

Merced County's kinship caregivers face a distinctive convergence of pressures. Among the families Los Robles Family Resource Network served last year, 64 percent of caregivers are over the age of 60 — grandparents and great-grandparents parenting a second or third time, often while managing their own chronic health conditions, fixed incomes, and the grief and family conflict that accompany a child's removal from a parent. Forty-three percent of the households we serve live below the federal poverty line, in a county where the cost of housing, food, and transportation has risen sharply while agricultural wages and Social Security payments have not kept pace. A grandmother in Planada raising three grandchildren on a monthly Social Security check is expected to absorb the cost of school clothes, counseling copays, court filing fees, and a larger apartment, frequently with no formal support at all, because the majority of kinship arrangements in Merced County — as nationally — exist outside the formal foster care system and its accompanying payments.

The families we serve reflect the county itself: 58 percent are Hispanic or Latino, 14 percent are Hmong, and 9 percent are Black. Each of these communities carries specific barriers to accessing support. Spanish-speaking caregivers frequently avoid public agencies out of fear related to immigration status, even when the children in their care are citizens and fully eligible for benefits. Hmong elders — many of whom arrived in the Central Valley as refugees and raised their own children through profound dislocation — face a service system with almost no Hmong-language capacity. There is currently no Hmong-language kinship support group anywhere in Merced County. Caregivers who cannot read an English-language court form, or who mistrust institutions that have historically failed them, do not seek guardianship, do not claim benefits the children are entitled to, and do not ask for help until a crisis forces the question.

The legal dimension of the need is acute. Without legal guardianship, a kinship caregiver may be unable to enroll a child in school, consent to medical treatment, add the child to health insurance, or prevent an unstable parent from removing the child on impulse. Probate guardianship in California requires filings, notice to parents, a court investigation, and at least one hearing — a process that is effectively impassable for a low-income, limited-English-proficient elder without counsel. Legal aid capacity in Merced County is thin and oversubscribed. When guardianship is not completed, placements are fragile: informal arrangements collapse under pressure from parents, landlords, schools, and benefits agencies, and children cycle back into instability or into the formal foster care system at far greater public cost.

The consequences of unsupported kinship care are measurable. Countywide, only 71 percent of kinship placements remain intact at twelve months. Every disrupted placement means a child changing homes, usually changing schools, often losing sibling contact, and entering a system where non-relative foster placement costs the county many times what preventive kinship support costs. Caregiver mental health drives much of this instability: national research consistently finds elevated depression among kinship caregivers, and our own intake screening confirms that a substantial share of the grandparents we serve arrive with clinically significant depression scores, compounded by isolation, financial strain, and the absence of even occasional respite. A caregiver who has not had a single day off in two years is a caregiver at risk of placement breakdown — not from lack of love, but from exhaustion.

Los Robles Family Resource Network has demonstrated that these outcomes are changeable. Among the 810 households we served last year, 88 percent of kinship placements remained intact at twelve months — seventeen percentage points above the county average. Our legal clinic completed 210 guardianship petitions, securing legal stability for children who would otherwise remain in limbo. But our capacity now stands at a crossroads. The $850,000 annual federal earmark that funded roughly one-third of our kinship program lapsed on September 30 and will not be renewed, and demand continues to grow: 810 households served against a county population of 3,900 kinship children means that the great majority of kinship families in Merced County still receive no support at all. The gap between demonstrated effectiveness and available resources — and the specific, unmet needs of Hmong-speaking caregivers, exhausted elders without respite, and families waiting for legal representation — is the need this proposal addresses.

## Project Description

Los Robles Family Resource Network requests $1,250,000 over three years from the Wendell Ruthers Foundation's Kinship Families Initiative to sustain, strengthen, and extend our comprehensive kinship support program. The project preserves the core model that produced an 88 percent twelve-month placement stability rate, and adds three targeted expansions that respond directly to the gaps identified above: Hmong-language support groups, a flexible respite fund, and two staff attorneys to expand the legal clinic.

### The Core Model

Our kinship program operates on a wraparound framework in which every enrolled household is assigned a kinship navigator who conducts a comprehensive intake assessment covering the child's legal status, school enrollment, benefits eligibility, caregiver health and mental health, housing stability, and family dynamics. From that assessment, the navigator and caregiver build a family support plan drawing on five integrated service lines:

**Kinship navigation and case management.** Navigators carry manageable caseloads and stay with families for the duration of enrollment, helping caregivers apply for CalWORKs child-only grants, CalFresh, Medi-Cal, and Social Security benefits for the child; coordinating with schools; and connecting families to housing, food, and health resources. Navigators are bilingual and bicultural, reflecting the Spanish-speaking, Hmong, and Black communities we serve.

**Legal clinic.** Our in-house legal clinic assists caregivers with probate guardianship petitions, educational rights, caregiver authorization affidavits, and benefits appeals. Last year the clinic completed 210 guardianship petitions. Demand substantially exceeds capacity; the current waiting list for full-representation guardianship cases runs several months.

**Caregiver support groups and peer networks.** Weekly and biweekly support groups in English and Spanish give caregivers a structured setting to reduce isolation, share practical knowledge, and normalize the emotional complexity of raising a relative's child. Groups are facilitated by trained staff and incorporate psychoeducation on child trauma, adolescent development, and caregiver self-care.

**Caregiver mental health screening and referral.** Every caregiver is screened at intake and at regular intervals using a validated depression instrument. Caregivers with elevated scores receive warm referrals to counseling, and navigators follow up to confirm connection to care.

**Emergency and concrete supports.** Small flexible grants cover the crisis expenses — a bed, a car repair, a utility arrears — that most often precede placement disruption in low-income kinship households.

### Expansion One: Hmong-Language Support Groups

With Kinship Families Initiative funding, we will launch the county's first Hmong-language kinship caregiver support groups, developed and delivered in partnership with the Hmong community center that has served Merced's Hmong community for decades. The community center will co-design the group model with Hmong elders to ensure cultural fit — including attention to clan structures, intergenerational communication, and culturally grounded understandings of mental health and family obligation — and will host groups at its facility, a trusted setting where elders already gather. We will hire a Hmong-speaking caregiver support specialist to facilitate groups, conduct culturally adapted depression screening, and serve as the bridge between Hmong caregivers and our navigation and legal services. We project two ongoing Hmong-language groups by the end of Year 1, growing to three by Year 3, reaching at least 60 Hmong caregiver households over the grant period — households that are currently almost entirely disconnected from formal support.

### Expansion Two: Respite Fund

We will establish a flexible respite fund enabling caregivers — with priority to those over 60 and those with elevated depression screening scores — to purchase short-term child care, weekend camps, after-school programming, and in-home respite care. Navigators will authorize respite vouchers as part of the family support plan, with a simple approval process designed for speed rather than bureaucracy. Respite is among the most requested and least available supports for kinship caregivers, and the research base links regular respite to reduced caregiver depression and improved placement stability. We project 150 households receiving respite support annually by Year 2.

### Expansion Three: Legal Clinic Expansion — Two Staff Attorneys

We will hire two staff attorneys, doubling the legal clinic's guardianship capacity and eliminating the current waiting list. Our legal aid partner will provide co-counsel on complex cases, training, and coverage for conflicts, extending the reach of both organizations. With expanded capacity, we project growing annual guardianship completions from 210 to at least 300 by Year 3, and adding capacity for related matters — benefits appeals, educational rights disputes, and record clearance for caregivers whose old convictions block resource family approval — that currently go unserved.

### Partnerships

The project is built on four formal partnerships, each governed by a memorandum of understanding:

- **Merced County Human Services Agency** refers kinship families at the point of placement — the moment of maximum need and maximum preventive opportunity — and shares administrative data on placement stability, enabling rigorous outcome measurement against county baselines.
- **Our legal aid partner** provides co-counsel, attorney training and supervision support, and conflict coverage for the expanded legal clinic.
- **The Hmong community center** co-designs and hosts Hmong-language support groups, provides cultural consultation across the program, and conducts trusted outreach to Hmong caregiver households.
- **UC Merced** serves as external evaluator, as detailed in the Evaluation Plan.

### Timeline

**Year 1 (Months 1–12):** Hire two staff attorneys and the Hmong caregiver support specialist by Month 4; launch first Hmong-language group by Month 6 and second by Month 12; stand up respite fund policies and begin vouchers by Month 5; execute data-sharing agreement with the county and finalize evaluation design with UC Merced by Month 3; serve 850 households.

**Year 2 (Months 13–24):** Full operation of all expansions; respite fund reaches 150 households; guardianship completions reach 260; first annual evaluation report delivered; begin 24-month stability tracking for the Year 1 cohort; serve 900 households.

**Year 3 (Months 25–36):** Third Hmong-language group launches; guardianship completions reach 300; summative evaluation completed, including 24-month stability findings; sustainability transitions executed as described below; serve 950 households.

Across the three-year grant period, the project will serve approximately 2,000 unduplicated kinship households — more than half of the county's kinship families — while producing evidence on the five outcomes the Foundation prioritizes: placement stability at twelve and 24 months, caregiver depression scores, school stability, guardianship completion, and cost per family.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

The project pursues three goals, each with measurable objectives aligned to the Kinship Families Initiative's outcome framework. Baselines reflect the most recent full program year; targets are annual unless otherwise noted.

### Goal 1: Kinship children in Merced County remain safely and stably in the care of their relatives.

- **Objective 1.1 — Twelve-month placement stability.** At least 88 percent of kinship placements among enrolled households remain intact at twelve months in Year 1, rising to 90 percent by Year 3, against a county baseline of 71 percent. *(Measured through county administrative data and navigator case records.)*
- **Objective 1.2 — 24-month placement stability.** At least 80 percent of placements among the Year 1 enrollment cohort remain intact at 24 months, measured in Year 3. This will establish the program's first formal 24-month benchmark. *(Measured through matched county administrative data.)*
- **Objective 1.3 — School stability.** At least 85 percent of school-age children in enrolled households experience no non-promotional school change during each program year. *(Measured through caregiver report verified by school enrollment records.)*
- **Objective 1.4 — Household reach.** The program serves 850 unduplicated households in Year 1, 900 in Year 2, and 950 in Year 3, from a baseline of 810.

### Goal 2: Kinship caregivers have the legal authority, mental health, and respite they need to sustain their caregiving.

- **Objective 2.1 — Guardianship completion.** The legal clinic completes 230 guardianship petitions in Year 1, 260 in Year 2, and 300 in Year 3, from a baseline of 210, and reduces the average wait from intake to filed petition to under 45 days by Year 2.
- **Objective 2.2 — Caregiver depression.** Among caregivers with clinically elevated depression screening scores at intake, at least 60 percent show a clinically meaningful reduction at the six-month rescreen, and at least 70 percent by the twelve-month rescreen. *(Measured using a validated instrument administered at intake, six months, and twelve months, with a culturally adapted Hmong-language protocol.)*
- **Objective 2.3 — Respite.** By Year 2, at least 150 households annually receive respite vouchers, with priority to caregivers over 60 and those with elevated depression scores; at least 80 percent of respite recipients report reduced caregiving strain on follow-up survey.

### Goal 3: Underserved caregiver communities — particularly Hmong-speaking elders — access support at rates proportional to need.

- **Objective 3.1 — Hmong-language groups.** Two Hmong-language support groups operate by the end of Year 1 and three by Year 3, with at least 60 unduplicated Hmong caregiver households participating across the grant period.
- **Objective 3.2 — Equitable enrollment.** By Year 3, program enrollment demographics reflect the county's kinship population within five percentage points for Hispanic/Latino, Hmong, and Black households, monitored quarterly with corrective outreach when gaps emerge.
- **Objective 3.3 — Cost per family.** Fully loaded program cost per household served remains at or below $4,500 annually, declining as household volume grows, with cost-per-family reported annually alongside a comparative estimate of the county cost of a disrupted placement. *(Calculated and verified by the UC Merced evaluation team.)*

Progress on all objectives will be reviewed quarterly by program leadership and the board's program committee, and reported annually to the Foundation.

## Evaluation Plan

Evaluation will be led by an external team from UC Merced, under a formal agreement providing evaluation design, instrument selection, data analysis, and annual reporting. The plan combines process and outcome evaluation, uses the county's administrative data to benchmark results against a real comparison population, and is structured explicitly around the five outcomes the Kinship Families Initiative measures.

**Placement stability at twelve and 24 months.** Under our data-sharing agreement, Merced County Human Services Agency will provide de-identified placement records enabling the UC Merced team to track whether each enrolled child remains with the same kinship caregiver at twelve and 24 months post-enrollment, and to compare enrolled families' stability rates with those of kinship families countywide who did not receive services. The 24-month measure — new to our evaluation practice and central to the Foundation's framework — begins with the Year 1 cohort and reports in Year 3. While this design is quasi-experimental rather than randomized, the countywide comparison group and the consistency of administrative data provide a credible counterfactual.

**Caregiver depression screening scores.** All caregivers are screened at intake, six months, and twelve months using a validated depression instrument. The UC Merced team will analyze score trajectories overall and by subgroup — age, language, race and ethnicity, and receipt of respite — allowing us to test whether the respite fund and Hmong-language groups are associated with greater improvement. The Hmong-language screening protocol will be adapted with guidance from the Hmong community center and reviewed by the evaluation team, given documented challenges in translating Western depression instruments for Hmong elders; the evaluation will document this adaptation as a contribution to the field.

**School stability.** Navigators will record school enrollment at intake and verify at each six-month review whether the child has changed schools for any non-promotional reason, cross-checked against caregiver report and, where releases permit, school records. School stability will be analyzed in relation to placement stability and guardianship status, testing the hypothesis that completed guardianship reduces mid-year school moves.

**Guardianship completion.** The legal clinic's case management system tracks every matter from intake through disposition, capturing petitions filed, guardianships granted, time from intake to filing, and case outcomes. The evaluation will report completion volumes against targets and analyze the relationship between guardianship completion and subsequent placement and school stability — a linkage of direct relevance to the Foundation's learning agenda.

**Cost per family.** Using audited financials and program service records, the UC Merced team will calculate a fully loaded annual cost per household served, disaggregated by service intensity, and will construct a comparative estimate of avoided public cost using county foster care placement rates. This analysis will give the Foundation, the county, and future funders a defensible answer to the question of what kinship prevention costs relative to what placement disruption costs.

**Process evaluation and continuous improvement.** Alongside outcome measurement, the evaluation includes implementation tracking (hiring milestones, group launches, respite fund utilization, waitlist length), annual caregiver satisfaction surveys in English, Spanish, and Hmong, and two focus groups per year — including at least one conducted in Hmong at the community center — to surface caregiver experience that numbers miss. Quarterly data reviews with program leadership will convert findings into course corrections in real time rather than waiting for annual reports.

**Reporting.** The evaluation team will deliver annual reports in Years 1 and 2 and a summative report in Year 3 including the 24-month stability findings, subgroup analyses, cost analysis, and recommendations. All reports will be shared with the Foundation, the county, and our partners, and a public-facing summary will be published to support replication in other Central Valley counties.

## Organizational Capacity

Los Robles Family Resource Network has served Merced County families for more than two decades and is the county's principal provider of kinship support services. The organization operates with 38 staff and an annual budget of $6.1 million, maintaining clean annual audits, a functioning system of internal financial controls, and current registration and good standing with all state and federal regulators. Our staff is bilingual and bicultural by design, drawn substantially from the communities we serve, with Spanish- and Hmong-speaking capacity across navigation, intake, and administrative functions.

Our track record in kinship services is the strongest evidence of capacity: 810 households served last year; an 88 percent twelve-month placement stability rate against a 71 percent county average; 210 completed guardianship petitions through a legal clinic we built and operate in-house; and durable, formalized partnerships with the county human services agency, legal aid, the Hmong community center, and UC Merced. We are experienced stewards of restricted funding at scale, including federal, state, county, and philanthropic awards, each with distinct compliance and reporting requirements that our finance team manages routinely.

We owe the Foundation a candid account of a period of organizational transition, because we believe transparency is itself a marker of capacity. Three facts define this moment:

**Leadership transition.** Our founding executive director retired in January after nineteen years of service. The board appointed Chue Vang Lor — previously our deputy director, with more than a decade inside the organization and deep roots in Merced's Hmong community — as Interim Executive Director. Under Mr. Lor's leadership, service delivery has continued without interruption, all funder obligations have been met, and the Hmong-language expansion in this proposal reflects his direct knowledge of an unserved community. The board, chaired by Ravi Chandrasekaran, has retained a search firm and adopted a transition plan targeting a permanent executive director within the first year of the grant period; Mr. Lor is a candidate. Program continuity does not depend on the outcome: day-to-day management of this project rests with Program Director Antoinette Boudreaux, who has led the kinship program through its strongest performance years and who designed the wraparound model that produced our stability results.

**Board transition.** The board has contracted from thirteen members to eight, reflecting term completions and departures that clustered around the founder's retirement. The remaining eight members are engaged and meeting regularly, and the board has adopted a recruitment plan to return to at least twelve members within eighteen months, with explicit priorities for kinship caregiver representation, Hmong community representation, and legal and finance expertise. Two candidates are currently in the pipeline. Mr. Chandrasekaran has committed the board to quarterly review of this project's outcomes and finances as a standing agenda item.

**Funding transition.** The $850,000 annual federal earmark that funded approximately one-third of the kinship program lapsed on September 30. We disclose this plainly because it is the central financial fact of this proposal: the Foundation's investment is not incremental funding layered on a stable base, but the bridge that preserves a demonstrably effective program while we rebuild a diversified revenue structure, as detailed in the Sustainability section. Development Director Siobhan McAllister leads that effort with a revenue plan already in motion. We have managed the immediate gap without service cuts through reserves and expense discipline, but that posture is not sustainable beyond the near term.

Organizations are tested not by the absence of transition but by how they manage it. Through the founder's retirement, the board's contraction, and the loss of a major funding stream, Los Robles has maintained its service volume, its outcomes, its partnerships, and its audit quality. The team named in this proposal — Mr. Lor, Ms. Boudreaux, Mr. Chandrasekaran, and Ms. McAllister — has the experience, the community trust, and the governance discipline to execute this project and to emerge from the transition period stronger than the organization entered it.

## Sustainability

The Foundation rightly asks how this work — and specifically the legal clinic — survives past year three. Our answer rests on a diversified revenue strategy with named mechanisms and a timeline, led by Development Director Siobhan McAllister with active board engagement under Chair Ravi Chandrasekaran.

**Public revenue replacement.** The lapsed federal earmark will not return in its prior form, and our plan does not assume it will. Instead, we are pursuing three public funding streams with structural durability that an earmark never had. First, California's investment in kinship navigator and family resource services — including federal Family First Prevention Services Act dollars flowing through the state and county — creates a contracting pathway for precisely the navigation model we operate; our county human services agency partnership and shared data infrastructure position us as the natural contractor, and the UC Merced evaluation is designed to produce exactly the outcome and cost evidence such contracting requires. We will open these negotiations with the county in Year 1, targeting a contract commencing in Year 3. Second, we will pursue competitive federal kinship navigator and children's bureau funding opportunities as they cycle, now with 24-month outcome data no competitor in our region can match. Third, our respite fund model aligns with state caregiver respite funding streams we have not previously accessed.

**Legal clinic sustainability past year three.** The legal clinic's post-grant plan has four components. (1) By Year 2, we will apply jointly with our legal aid partner for Legal Services Trust Fund, Equal Access Fund, and IOLTA-related support for which the expanded clinic's caseload will strengthen eligibility, with the partnership structure allowing either organization to serve as lead applicant depending on program fit. (2) The county contracting strategy above will include guardianship legal services as a line item, on the strength of the evaluation's evidence linking completed guardianship to placement and school stability — evidence that converts the clinic from a charitable service into a documented placement-preservation intervention with a public cost case. (3) We will establish a Kinship Legal Fund within our development program, seeded by a board-led campaign in Year 2 and cultivated among regional law firms, the local bar, and Central Valley funders, with a target of covering one attorney position from philanthropic and bar sources by the end of Year 3. (4) The clinic will pilot modest court-authorized fee recovery where appropriate and pursue pro bono panel development with our legal aid partner so that attorney capacity is leveraged, not merely funded.

**Philanthropic diversification.** Ms. McAllister's three-year development plan targets growth in foundation, corporate, and individual revenue so that no single source exceeds 25 percent of the kinship program budget by the end of the grant period. The board's recruitment plan directly supports this: new members are being sought in part for fundraising capacity, and the board has adopted a give-or-get policy taking effect this fiscal year.

**Structural sustainability.** Some elements of this project become self-sustaining by design. The Hmong-language support groups, once established and co-owned with the Hmong community center, carry modest ongoing costs that the partnership is structured to share. Navigator and group-facilitation capacity built during the grant remains in the organization and community. And the evaluation itself is a sustainability instrument: the 24-month stability data, depression outcomes, and cost-per-family analysis are the evidence base on which every subsequent public contract and philanthropic ask will stand.

By the close of Year 3, our target is a kinship program budget in which county and state contracts cover the navigation core, legal services funding streams and the Kinship Legal Fund cover the clinic, and diversified philanthropy covers innovation and flexible supports — a structure more durable than the earmark it replaces.

## Budget Narrative

Los Robles Family Resource Network requests $1,250,000 over three years, allocated at approximately $430,000 in Year 1, $415,000 in Year 2, and $405,000 in Year 3. The request represents a strategic share of the kinship program's total cost; the balance is carried by the organization's $6.1 million budget through existing public and philanthropic revenue and the diversification plan described above. Amounts below are three-year totals.

**Personnel and benefits — $795,000 (63.6 percent).** The largest category funds the positions that deliver the expanded model. Two full-time staff attorneys ($430,000 over three years, salaries and benefits) double the legal clinic's capacity and drive the growth in guardianship completions from 210 to 300 annually; attorney salaries are set to be competitive for the Central Valley legal market, where recruitment for public-interest positions is difficult. One full-time Hmong caregiver support specialist ($195,000 over three years) facilitates the Hmong-language groups, conducts culturally adapted screening, and links Hmong caregivers to navigation and legal services. The remaining $170,000 supports partial FTE allocations for a legal clinic paralegal and for the Program Director's supervision time attributable to the grant-funded expansions. All other program staff — navigators, group facilitators, intake, and administration — are funded through the organization's existing budget and are not charged to this grant.

**Respite fund — $175,000 (14.0 percent).** Direct respite vouchers for caregivers, phased at $45,000 in Year 1 and $65,000 in each of Years 2 and 3 as utilization matures, supporting at least 150 households annually by Year 2 at an average of roughly $400–$450 per household per year. Vouchers purchase short-term child care, camps, after-school programming, and in-home respite from vetted providers, authorized by navigators under written fund policies with priority criteria for caregivers over 60 and those with elevated depression scores. One hundred percent of this line is direct assistance to families.

**Evaluation — $120,000 (9.6 percent).** Contract with UC Merced for evaluation design, instrument adaptation (including the Hmong-language screening protocol), administrative data analysis under the county data-sharing agreement, annual and summative reporting, and the cost-per-family analysis. At 9.6 percent of the total request, this investment reflects the Foundation's emphasis on rigorous measurement — particularly the new 24-month stability benchmark — and doubles as the evidence engine for the sustainability plan.

**Program operating costs — $70,000 (5.6 percent).** Court filing fees and legal case costs for guardianship petitions where fee waivers are unavailable ($30,000); interpretation, translation, and Hmong-language materials development ($15,000); support group costs including facility contributions to the Hmong community center, child care during groups, and refreshments ($15,000); and staff mileage for home visits and court appearances across a large rural county ($10,000).

**Technology and training — $26,000 (2.1 percent).** Legal case management software licenses for the expanded clinic, laptops and equipment for three new staff, and training including guardianship practice updates, trauma-informed caregiver support, and joint training with the legal aid partner.

**Indirect costs — $64,000 (5.1 percent).** Charged at a modest rate well below our federally negotiated rate, covering the finance, human resources, facilities, and compliance functions that support the project — including grant accounting, audit, and the payroll administration of three new positions. We deliberately hold indirect recovery low on this request to maximize direct service; the organization absorbs the difference.

**Cost effectiveness.** Combined with the organization's existing investment, this budget delivers services to approximately 2,000 unduplicated households over three years at a fully loaded cost at or below $4,500 per household per year — a figure the evaluation will verify and compare against the substantially higher annual public cost of a single non-relative foster placement. Each disrupted kinship placement prevented represents avoided county expenditure several times the program's per-family cost, before accounting for the human cost to the child. The Foundation's $1.25 million investment thus purchases three things at once: sustained services for the county's most effective kinship program during a critical funding transition, three targeted expansions reaching caregivers no one else serves, and a rigorous evidence base — including the field's rare 24-month stability measurement — that converts this program from grant-dependent to contract-anchored by the end of the grant period.

We welcome the Foundation's questions and would be glad to host a site visit with our staff, our partners, and — most importantly — the grandparents and relative caregivers whose steadiness this project exists to sustain.
