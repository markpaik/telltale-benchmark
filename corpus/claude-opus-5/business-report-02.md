## 1. Purpose and Scope

This report presents the operating and financial condition of Tuscarora County at the midpoint of fiscal year 2026. It responds to the Board's standing requirement for a mid-year review and to Chair Osei-Tutu's specific direction, conveyed in her memorandum of June 24, that this cycle's report carry department-by-department detail rather than the consolidated fund-level presentation used in prior years. That direction followed the independent auditor's interim management letter of June 12, which identified a material weakness in the reconciliation of grant receivables within the Department of Human Services.

The scope of this report covers the six departments the Board identified as carrying the greatest financial or operational exposure at mid-year: the Sheriff's Office, Roads and Bridges, Human Services, Tuscarora Manor, the 911 Center, and the Register of Deeds. It also presents the county's aggregate position against the adopted budget, a full accounting of the audit finding and the remediation now underway, and an assessment of the decisions facing the Board between August and the close of the fiscal year.

Departmental submissions were collected by the Chief Clerk between June 30 and July 17. Financial figures reflect the general ledger as of the June 30 close, with the exception of the Route 4012 bridge repair estimate, which was revised by the county engineer on July 21 and is presented at the revised figure with the prior estimate noted. Grant receivable balances in Human Services are presented as recorded and are, as discussed in Section 8, of contested accuracy.

## 2. Summary of Findings

The county entered fiscal 2026 with an adopted general fund budget of $196 million, balanced on paper by a planned draw of $2.4 million from unassigned fund balance. At mid-year the county is not on track to hold that plan. Aggregate general fund expenditures through June 30 total $101.7 million, or 51.9 percent of appropriation, against a straight-line benchmark of 50 percent. Aggregate general fund revenues total $94.3 million, or 48.1 percent of estimate. The gap between the two is $7.4 million, of which $2.4 million is the planned fund balance draw and $5.0 million is unplanned.

The unplanned gap is concentrated. Four departments account for essentially all of it: the Sheriff's Office at $2.1 million over on overtime, Tuscarora Manor at $1.5 million worse than its budgeted loss, the Register of Deeds at $940,000 short on fee revenue, and the emergency bridge repair on Route 4012 at $3.8 million against no appropriation. Offsetting these are a $1.2 million favorable variance in personnel costs across departments carrying vacancies, $0.9 million in unspent contingency, and $0.7 million in timing differences on capital equipment purchases that will reverse in the second half.

Three of the four adverse variances are structural rather than timing. Sheriff's overtime is driven by a vacancy rate and a jail population that will not correct without deliberate intervention. The Manor's loss is driven by census and agency nursing costs that have persisted for six consecutive quarters. Recording fee revenue is driven by a mortgage market the county does not control. Only the bridge repair is a discrete event, and it is the largest single number in the report.

The audit finding on grant reconciliation is separate from the budget variance but compounds it. The county cannot presently state with confidence that $1.9 million in recorded human services receivables is collectible. If a material portion is not, the county will absorb the write-off in fiscal 2026 and the unplanned gap widens accordingly.

Absent Board action, the projected fiscal 2026 general fund result is a deficit of $7.4 million against a planned deficit of $2.4 million, drawing unassigned fund balance from $31.2 million at January 1 to approximately $23.8 million at December 31. That would place the balance at 12.1 percent of expenditures, below the 15 percent floor in the Board's fund balance policy for the first time since 2019.

### 3.2 Reading the Aggregate

Two lines in the table above deserve comment before the departmental sections, because they are easy to misread.

Real property tax collections at 52.1 percent are not a favorable trend. Tuscarora County bills in two installments, with the first due March 31 and the second due September 30, and the first installment historically produces 51 to 53 percent of annual collections. The county is within its normal band. The $1.9 million favorable variance to the straight-line benchmark is an artifact of the billing calendar and will disappear by the third quarter.

The $2.75 million unfavorable variance in "other and miscellaneous" is likewise largely timing. It includes $1.9 million in reimbursements from the Commonwealth for the 2025 winter maintenance season that were expected in the second quarter and are now expected in September, and $600,000 in a delayed sale of surplus county property. Neither is at risk. The remaining $250,000 is genuine shortfall spread across small accounts.

What is not timing is the $1.21 million unfavorable variance in fees, fines, and charges. Of that, $940,000 is the Register of Deeds shortfall discussed in Section 9, and the balance is spread across court costs, permit fees, and Manor private-pay revenue. This line will not recover on its own.

On the expenditure side, the $2.6 million unfavorable variance in personnel is the net of two large and opposite movements. Departments with vacancies — the Sheriff's Office at 31, the 911 Center at 9, and smaller counts elsewhere totaling 43 across the county — are underspending base salary by $3.8 million. Overtime, agency staffing, and shift differential across those same departments is overspending by $6.4 million. The county is paying a substantial premium to cover work it budgeted at straight time.

Capital outlay's $1.34 million favorable variance is a timing difference that will reverse. Three vehicle orders and one radio infrastructure purchase were placed in the second quarter with third-quarter delivery. The county has committed the money.

Contingency at 22.9 percent expended is the one genuinely favorable line, and it is the Board's principal uncommitted resource for the second half. The remaining balance is $3.7 million.

The department reports $2.1 million over budget at mid-year. That figure is the net position — overtime overspending of $2.1 million against the pro-rata overtime budget, partially offset by base salary savings from vacancies. The gross overtime overrun against the full-year appropriation is larger, and the full-year projection above shows the trajectory if nothing changes: a $5.85 million departmental deficit.

The projection assumes vacancies remain at current levels and jail population remains at current levels. Neither assumption is safe in either direction, which is why Section 4.4 presents scenarios.

### 4.2 Staffing

Thirty-one of 214 authorized deputy positions are vacant, a rate of 14.5 percent. The vacancies are not evenly distributed. Corrections assignments carry 22 of the 31, a vacancy rate of 19.1 percent against 115 authorized corrections posts. Patrol and court security carry the remaining 9 against 99 authorized posts, a rate of 9.1 percent.

The concentration matters because corrections posts cannot be left unfilled. A housing unit requires a defined number of officers per shift under the county's post orders and under Department of Corrections inspection standards. When a post is vacant, it is filled by holdover or callback, both at time-and-a-half. Patrol posts have more elasticity; a patrol sector can run thin for a shift without a regulatory violation.

Sheriff Delgado's submission reports that the department ran 62,400 overtime hours in the first six months against a budgeted annual figure of 47,000. At the department's blended overtime rate of $78.50 per hour including benefits, that is the $4.9 million recorded. Of the 62,400 hours, the department attributes 44,100 to mandatory corrections post coverage, 9,800 to inmate transport and outside hospital watch, 5,200 to court security backfill, and 3,300 to patrol.

Recruitment has not kept pace with separation. The department hired 14 deputies in the first half and lost 19, a net loss of 5. Of the 19 separations, 8 were resignations to other law enforcement agencies, 6 were retirements, 3 were terminations, and 2 were resignations from the profession. Exit interviews conducted by Human Resources on 14 of the 19 cite mandatory overtime as a contributing factor in 11 cases.

This is the mechanism the Board should understand clearly. Vacancies generate mandatory overtime. Mandatory overtime generates resignations. Resignations generate vacancies. The department entered the year with 24 vacancies and now has 31.

### 4.3 Jail Population

The jail averaged 388 inmates against a rated capacity of 340, an occupancy rate of 114 percent. The daily count ranged from 361 to 419. The population has exceeded rated capacity on every day of the reporting period.

| Population Component | Average Daily | Percent of Total | Change from FY2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pretrial, county charges | 231 | 59.5% | +18 |
| Sentenced, county | 84 | 21.6% | −3 |
| State parole detainers | 41 | 10.6% | +12 |
| Federal and other holds | 22 | 5.7% | +4 |
| Awaiting state transfer | 10 | 2.6% | +7 |
| **Total** | **388** | **100.0%** | **+38** |

The population grew by 38 average daily inmates from fiscal 2025. Three components account for the growth: pretrial detention on county charges, state parole detainers, and inmates awaiting transfer to state facilities after sentencing.

The pretrial increase of 18 corresponds to an increase in median time to disposition in the Court of Common Pleas from 118 days in fiscal 2025 to 147 days at mid-year fiscal 2026. The court reports a judicial vacancy from February through June and a backlog in the public defender's office. The jail holds the consequence.

The parole detainer increase of 12 and the state transfer backlog of 10 are both Commonwealth-driven. The county houses these inmates and bills the Commonwealth at the statutory per diem of $71.00, against an actual cost per inmate-day of $148.60. The county absorbs $77.60 per day on 51 inmates, or approximately $1.44 million annualized. The Sheriff has raised this with the county's legislative delegation.

Overcrowding drives cost beyond the direct per-inmate expense. Housing units operate at double-bunking levels that require additional supervision posts. Two dayrooms have been converted to temporary housing, each requiring a dedicated officer. The medical unit is running at capacity, and inmate medical spending is at 57.9 percent of appropriation at mid-year.

## 5. Roads and Bridges

### 5.1 The Paving Program

The department was appropriated $27 million for the fiscal 2026 paving program, with a target of 214 centerline miles. At mid-year it has spent $16.47 million, or 61 percent, and completed 81.3 centerline miles, or 38 percent.

| Measure | Plan, Full Year | Plan, Mid-Year | Actual, Mid-Year | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Appropriation | $27,000,000 | $16,200,000 | $16,470,000 | −$270,000 |
| Centerline miles | 214.0 | 128.4 | 81.3 | −47.1 |
| Cost per centerline mile | $126,168 | $126,168 | $202,583 | −$76,415 |
| Tons of asphalt placed | 148,000 | 88,800 | 61,200 | −27,600 |
| Cost per ton, delivered | $94.40 | $94.40 | $107.60 | −$13.20 |

The gap between 61 percent of dollars and 38 percent of miles has two causes, and they are not of equal size.

Asphalt cost is the smaller cause. Delivered price rose 14 percent from the $94.40 per ton assumed in the budget to $107.60 at mid-year. Applied to the 61,200 tons placed, the price increase accounts for $807,840 of additional cost, which translates to approximately 6.4 centerline miles of foregone work. Public Works Director Anton Kowalczyk's submission notes that the increase tracks liquid asphalt cement indices and regional plant consolidation, and that the department's supply contract contains an escalator clause it cannot renegotiate mid-season.

The larger cause is the reallocation of crews and equipment to the Route 4012 bridge closure. Beginning April 14, the department assigned two of its six paving crews, both of its milling machines, and its traffic control complement to the bridge site and to the detour route maintenance the closure requires. The detour adds 11.4 miles to the affected route and carries approximately 4,200 vehicles per day, including school bus and heavy truck traffic. The detour roads were built to a lower standard than the route they now carry and have required continuous patching, shoulder stabilization, and two culvert repairs.

Direct paving-program labor and equipment diverted to bridge and detour work totals $2.31 million against the paving appropriation, and the department estimates the diversion cost it 34 centerline miles of production. That is the dominant explanation for the mileage shortfall.

The remaining 6.7 miles of variance are attributed to weather. The department lost 19 working days to precipitation in the second quarter against a ten-year average of 12.

### 5.2 The Route 4012 Bridge

The bridge carries Route 4012 over Sinking Creek. It was built in 1961, last rehabilitated in 1994, and rated in fair condition at its 2024 inspection. On April 9, 2026, a routine inspection prompted by scour observed after March high water found section loss in two of the four steel girders at the east abutment bearing, together with abutment undermining. The county engineer closed the bridge on April 11.

The revision follows subsurface investigation completed in June. Borings found the east abutment founded on spread footings bearing on material that has been undermined more extensively than surface inspection suggested. The abutment cannot be underpinned and must be reconstructed on driven piles, which accounts for the largest single increase.

The department has applied for federal emergency relief funding. Preliminary indications from the district office are that approximately $222,000 in eligible costs will be reimbursed, reflected in the table. The county should not plan on more.

Construction is scheduled to begin September 8 with substantial completion targeted for May 2027. The bridge will remain closed through the winter. The detour will require continued maintenance, budgeted at $340,000 for the balance of fiscal 2026 and an estimated $520,000 in fiscal 2027.

### 5.3 Second-Half Outlook

The department projects completing 58 additional centerline miles in the second half for a full-year total of 139.3 miles, 65 percent of plan. This assumes the two diverted crews return to paving by August 25, that asphalt prices hold at current levels, and a normal fall weather pattern.

The $27 million paving appropriation will be fully expended. The department is requesting that the Board recognize the $3.8 million bridge cost as a separate appropriation rather than absorbing it within the paving line, on the grounds that continued absorption will reduce full-year mileage to approximately 108 miles, 50 percent of plan, and will leave 23 road segments scheduled for fiscal 2026 treatment in a condition that will require reconstruction rather than resurfacing within three years. The department estimates the deferred-maintenance cost of that outcome at $4.10 for every $1.00 of resurfacing foregone.

## 8. Audit Finding: Grant Reconciliation

### 8.1 The Finding

The independent auditor's interim management letter dated June 12, 2026, states in relevant part that the county's controls over grant receivable recognition and reconciliation within the Department of Human Services do not permit management to determine, on a timely basis, whether recorded receivables represent amounts due under executed grant agreements for allowable costs incurred within the applicable period of performance. The auditors classified the condition as a material weakness in internal control over financial reporting and noted that it may also constitute a finding under the Uniform Guidance for federal awards, subject to their single audit fieldwork in the fall.

The specific condition: $1.9 million in grant receivables recorded on the county's books cannot be traced to supporting documentation sufficient to establish collectibility.

### 8.2 Why This Matters Beyond the Dollar Amount

The $1.9 million is material but not, by itself, threatening to a $196 million operation. Three consequences extend beyond the amount.

First, a material weakness in internal control is a reportable condition that will appear in the county's audited financial statements. The county has two general obligation bond series outstanding totaling $47.3 million and expects to issue approximately $12 million in 2027 for the capital program. Rating agencies read management letters.

Second, if the condition is confirmed as a single audit finding, the Commonwealth and federal awarding agencies may designate the county a high-risk grantee. That designation carries specific consequences: reimbursement-only payment rather than advance drawdown, additional reporting, and in some programs a withholding percentage. The county draws $71 million annually through these programs. A shift from advance to reimbursement-only payment across even a portion of that portfolio would create a working capital requirement the general fund would have to bridge.

Third, the practice that produced the condition — estimated drawdowns trued up at year-end — may have produced drawdowns in excess of allowable costs in some programs. If so, the county owes money back. The auditors have not quantified this and neither can we; it is the open question that makes the finding serious.

|---|---|---|---|
| Fill grants accountant vacancy | Human Services / HR | Sept 15, 2026 | Posted; 3 candidates in process |
| Engage third-party firm for receivable reconstruction | Budget / Human Services | Aug 20, 2026 | Scope drafted; requires Board approval |

The third-party engagement is the item requiring Board action on August 6. The Office of Budget and Finance has scoped the work at $185,000 to $240,000 depending on the extent of the allowability review. This is not a discretionary expenditure. The county does not have internal capacity to perform the reconstruction while also operating the grant programs, and the auditors will return for fieldwork in October.

The Office of Budget and Finance recommends that the Board authorize a reserve of $600,000 against potential write-off of the receivable balance. That figure represents the sum of the categories assessed above as uncertain or likely uncollectible, plus the unidentified suspense balance, plus a margin. It is an estimate made without the substantiation work that would make it reliable.

The center answered 214,000 calls and missed the Commonwealth's ninety-second answer standard on 8 percent, or 17,120 calls. The standard requires 95 percent compliance. The center is at 92 percent and is therefore out of compliance.

Non-compliance is not merely a reporting matter. The Pennsylvania Emergency Management Agency conditions a portion of 911 surcharge distribution on performance standards. The county receives $3.94 million annually in surcharge revenue. PEMA has issued a notice of deficiency dated July 2 requiring a corrective action plan by September 30. Sustained non-compliance can result in withholding of up to 15 percent of the distribution, approximately $591,000.

### 9.2 Staffing

Nine of 46 dispatcher seats are vacant, a rate of 19.6 percent. The center operates three shifts with minimum staffing of 8, 11, and 7 positions respectively. With 37 working dispatchers against 46 authorized and accounting for leave, training, and scheduled time off, the center is meeting minimum staffing through overtime on approximately 61 percent of shifts.

Overtime expenditure is $684,000 against a $410,000 annual appropriation, 167 percent of budget at mid-year. Projected full-year overtime is $1.31 million, a variance of $900,000.

Director Rosalind Achebe's submission reports that the answer-time degradation correlates directly with staffing. On shifts running at or above minimum staffing plus one, the center meets the 90-second standard on 96.4 percent of calls. On shifts running at exactly minimum staffing, compliance falls to 89.1 percent. On the 214 shifts run below minimum during the period, compliance was 78.3 percent.

Training pipeline is the constraint on filling seats. A dispatcher requires 14 weeks of academy and console training before independent operation. The center graduated 6 in the first half and lost 8. Two trainees are currently in the pipeline with October completion dates.

The center's starting wage is $21.40 per hour. Comparable positions in the two adjacent counties start at $23.75 and $24.60.

The office recorded 11,400 documents, down 22 percent, with fee revenue down $940,000 against the budgeted figure.

The decline is concentrated in mortgage instruments, down 35.5 percent. Recorder Harlan Petrosky's submission attributes this to the interest rate environment and specifically to the collapse of refinancing volume, which fell from 1,940 instruments in the comparable fiscal 2025 period to 410. Purchase-money mortgages fell more modestly, from 3,350 to 3,000, a decline of 10.4 percent that roughly tracks the deed count.

The office reduced expenditures by $84,000 through attrition of one position and reduced supply purchases, which offsets less than 9 percent of the revenue shortfall.

The office projects full-year revenue of $8.6 million against a budget of $10.5 million, a full-year shortfall of $1.9 million. The Office of Budget and Finance concurs with this projection. Recording volume typically strengthens modestly in the third quarter and weakens in the fourth; there is no basis for expecting recovery.

For fiscal 2027 budget development, the Office of Budget and Finance recommends that recording fee revenue be estimated at $8.4 million rather than at a recovery to prior levels. The fiscal 2026 estimate of $10.5 million was built on a projection of rate declines that did not materialize.

### 11.2 Decisions Required on August 6

Three items require Board action at the August 6 meeting and cannot be deferred.

**Authorization of the third-party grant reconciliation engagement, $240,000.** The auditors return in October. Without the reconstruction work, the county will face the single audit without the documentation to respond to inquiries, and a material weakness becomes a repeat finding with a high-risk designation attached.

**Recognition of the Route 4012 appropriation, $3.8 million.** The Board can appropriate this from contingency and fund balance, or it can leave Roads and Bridges to absorb it against the paving line. The second course costs 31 additional centerline miles of resurfacing this year and, on the department's estimate, $4.10 in future reconstruction for every dollar of resurfacing foregone.

**Direction on the Manor nursing scale, $1.34 million annualized.** The facility cannot recruit at current rates and cannot reduce agency utilization without recruiting. Deferral to the fiscal 2027 budget cycle means six additional months at 27 percent agency coverage, at approximately $2.16 million in continued premium.

### 11.3 Decisions the Board Should Take Up Before October

**Jail population.** The county is spending $9.4 million in projected overtime substantially because it holds 388 inmates in a 340-bed facility with 22 corrections vacancies. The three components driving the increase — pretrial detention length, parole detainers, and state transfer backlog — have different remedies. Pretrial length is addressable through the county's own criminal justice coordinating structure. Parole detainers and transfer backlog are Commonwealth matters requiring legislative engagement. Neither will move without the Board's initiative.

**Compensation across the three understaffed operations.** The Sheriff's Office, the Manor, and the 911 Center each report that their pay scales are below the market they recruit from, and each is paying a large overtime or agency premium as a result. The combined premium across the three is running at approximately $8.3 million annualized. A coordinated market review, rather than three separate departmental requests, would let the Board see the whole of what it is buying with premium pay.

**Fiscal 2027 revenue assumptions.** Recording fees will not return to fiscal 2026 budget levels. The 911 surcharge distribution is at risk pending the PEMA corrective action plan. State pass-through terms may change if the high-risk designation is applied. These belong in the September budget instructions.

**Fund balance policy.** At the projected result, the county breaches its own 15 percent floor. The Board should decide whether to close the gap in fiscal 2026 through additional expenditure reduction, accept the breach and adopt a documented multi-year replenishment plan, or revise the policy. The Office of Budget and Finance recommends the second course. Closing a $13.0 million gap in five months would require reductions of a depth that would damage the operations described in this report, and several of those operations are already degrading.

### 11.4 A Closing Observation

Five of the six departments examined here report a version of the same problem. The Sheriff's Office cannot fill 31 posts and pays $9.4 million in overtime. The Manor cannot fill its nursing lines and pays $7.2 million to agencies. The 911 Center cannot fill 9 seats and misses the state answer standard. Roads and Bridges has the crews but redeployed them and lost 34 miles of paving. Human Services lost its grants accountant in October and cannot substantiate $1.9 million.

These are presented in this report as six departmental variances because the Chair asked for department-by-department detail, and the detail is real. But the Board should not leave the August 6 meeting having addressed six problems. The county has a labor problem with six expressions, and the premium it is paying to work around that problem — in overtime, in agency contracts, in foregone production, in unreconciled receivables — is larger than any single line item in this report.
