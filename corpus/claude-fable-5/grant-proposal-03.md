## Statement of Need

The Arkansas River Valley has manufactured things for more than a century — furniture, cordage, fabricated metal, food products, HVAC equipment. Manufacturing remains the largest private employment sector in Sebastian County, and the region's 34 firms that make up the Arkansas River Valley Manufacturing Consortium collectively employ several thousand production workers. Those firms currently report **310 unfilled production openings** in CNC machining, industrial maintenance, and welding — positions that pay family-sustaining wages, that do not require a four-year degree, and that sit vacant month after month because the regional labor market cannot supply people with the credentials and hands-on competencies the work demands.

The supply problem is structural, not cyclical. **Forty-one percent of Sebastian County adults hold no postsecondary credential of any kind** — no degree, no industry certification, no completed apprenticeship. For a regional economy whose best jobs increasingly require a credential, this is the central fact. It means that four in ten working-age adults are largely locked out of the manufacturing careers that anchor the local economy, and it means that the region's manufacturers are competing for a credentialed labor pool far too small to fill their openings. The result is a labor market failure visible from both sides: employers with vacancies they cannot fill, and adults working in low-wage service jobs a few miles from production floors that would pay them twice as much.

Two populations bear the sharpest edge of this failure. The first is low-income working adults — disproportionately parents, disproportionately without reliable transportation — for whom the barrier to a credential is not aptitude but logistics. A twelve-week welding program is unattainable if attending it means quitting the job that pays the rent, or if there is no one to watch the children during evening lab hours, or if the training center is a forty-minute drive with no bus route. Our own attrition data confirm this: among trainees who left our programs before completion between 2022 and 2024, the three most commonly cited reasons were loss of income during training, childcare, and transportation. These are solvable problems. They are also precisely the problems that most workforce programs, including ours to date, have not been resourced to solve.

The second population is people returning from state custody. **Roughly 1,300 people return to the Fort Smith region each year from Arkansas Department of Corrections facilities.** National and state data consistently show that stable employment in the first year after release is the single strongest protective factor against recidivism, and that the manufacturing sector — with its skills-based hiring, its shift structure, and its wage levels — is among the most viable landing places for people with records. Yet returning citizens face compounded barriers: gaps in work history, lack of credentials, unstable housing, and employer hesitancy. Several of our member firms have quietly hired returning citizens for years and report retention rates comparable to or better than their general workforce. What has been missing is a structured pipeline: recruitment inside the reentry system, wraparound case management, credential training, and warm handoffs to employers who have already committed to fair-chance hiring. This proposal builds that pipeline.

We come to this application with a track record and with a hard lesson, and we intend to be candid about both. Since 2017, the Consortium has trained **1,240 people** in CNC machining, industrial maintenance, and welding. In 2022, **79 percent of our completers were placed in employment within 90 days**. In 2024, that figure was **58 percent**. A 21-point decline over two years demands explanation, and the explanation is specific rather than diffuse. In mid-2023, Talbert Cordage — one of our founding member firms and historically our single largest hiring destination, absorbing between 15 and 20 percent of our completers in a typical year — closed its Fort Smith operation. The closure removed a hiring pipeline that our placement model had leaned on too heavily, and it simultaneously removed Talbert's **$250,000 annual contribution**, which had functioned as the match funding for our stipend and support-services budget. The financial loss forced us to suspend transportation assistance and reduce our employer engagement staffing at exactly the moment we needed to build new hiring relationships to replace Talbert's demand.

The placement decline, in other words, was not a decline in training quality — our credential attainment rate held at 87 percent throughout — but a concentration risk that materialized, compounded by the loss of the support services that keep completers connected through the job search. We have spent the past eighteen months correcting both weaknesses, as detailed in the Organizational Capacity section, and this proposal is deliberately engineered so that no single employer or funder failure can again break the model. We describe the Talbert episode at length not because it flatters us but because a funder deciding whether to invest $1.8 million in this organization deserves to know exactly what went wrong, why, and what has structurally changed.

The need, then, is threefold and interlocking: 310 openings that regional employers cannot fill; a large population of adults, including 1,300 annual returning citizens, blocked from those openings by credential and logistical barriers; and a proven regional training organization that has learned — the hard way — how to build a placement model resilient enough to serve both.

## Project Description

The Arkansas River Valley Manufacturing Consortium proposes the **River Valley Skilled Trades Pipeline**, a three-year initiative that will train **480 people** — 160 per year — in CNC machining, industrial maintenance, and welding, with paid work experience, transportation and childcare stipends, and a dedicated reentry cohort, leading to industry-recognized credentials, college credit, and placement with the Consortium's 34 member manufacturers and other regional employers.

### Program design

The Pipeline retains the core training architecture that has produced an 87 percent credential attainment rate since 2017 and adds four elements that directly address the barriers and vulnerabilities identified above.

**1. Credential training in three high-demand tracks.** Trainees enroll in one of three tracks, each running 12 to 16 weeks and each mapped to the specific competencies our member firms hire for:

- **CNC machining**, leading to NIMS Machining Level I credentials, with instruction on the same Haas and Mazak platforms our members run on their floors;
- **Industrial maintenance**, leading to a stackable set of credentials in mechanical systems, industrial electricity, fluid power, and PLC fundamentals, aligned to the multi-craft maintenance roles that constitute the largest single category of member vacancies;
- **Welding**, leading to AWS certifications in the processes (GMAW, GTAW, SMAW) member firms specify.

Each cohort runs approximately 20 trainees, with day and evening sections to accommodate working adults. Instruction takes place at the Consortium's training center, whose equipment is maintained through in-kind member contributions.

**2. Paid work experience.** Every trainee will complete a paid work experience component — a minimum of 120 hours at $15 per hour — embedded in the second half of training and hosted at a member firm. This element serves three purposes simultaneously: it replaces a portion of the income trainees forgo during training, which our attrition data identify as the leading cause of dropout; it gives trainees documented recent work history in the industry, which is especially critical for the reentry cohort; and it functions as an extended working interview, converting the placement process from a post-graduation scramble into a relationship built over weeks. Host firms observe trainees on their own equipment and, in our pilot experience during 2025, extended offers before completion in a majority of cases.

**3. Transportation and childcare stipends.** Trainees with demonstrated need will receive transportation stipends of up to $75 per week (fuel cards, transit passes, or vehicle repair assistance up to a per-trainee cap) and childcare stipends of up to $150 per week, administered through simple attestation-plus-receipt documentation to keep the burden on trainees low. We project that approximately 65 percent of trainees will access transportation support and 35 percent will access childcare support, based on intake data from our 2023–2025 cohorts. These are not amenities; they are the difference between completion and attrition for a substantial share of our target population, and their suspension after the Talbert loss coincided measurably with rising mid-program dropout.

**4. A dedicated reentry cohort.** Each year, two of the eight cohorts — approximately 40 trainees annually, 120 over the grant period — will be reserved for people within twelve months of release from state custody or currently under community supervision. Reentry cohorts follow the same curriculum and credential standards as all others but add: recruitment and pre-enrollment orientation conducted inside partner facilities and probation offices; intensive case management delivered by our reentry nonprofit partner, addressing housing, documentation, supervision compliance, and benefits navigation; a fair-chance employer subset — currently eleven member firms have formally committed — that guarantees interview access for cohort completers; and extended post-placement follow-up through the twelve-month mark. Reentry trainees are fully integrated into paid work experience and stipend supports.

### Partner roles

The Pipeline is delivered through a partnership structure in which each organization does what it is built to do, formalized in memoranda of agreement executed at award.

**The University of Arkansas at Fort Smith (UAFS)** provides **credit articulation**. Under an articulation agreement finalized in 2025, completers of each Consortium track receive between 9 and 15 credit hours toward UAFS certificates and the Associate of Applied Science in Applied Technology. This converts a short-term workforce credential into the first rungs of a degree pathway, which matters both for trainees' long-term earnings trajectories and for the county's postsecondary attainment rate. UAFS also provides curriculum review to maintain articulation alignment and advising sessions for completers who wish to continue.

**The Arkansas Workforce Center – Fort Smith** provides **referrals and co-enrollment**. The workforce center is our primary recruitment channel for the general-population cohorts, screening candidates for eligibility and basic readiness and co-enrolling eligible trainees in WIOA services, which allows federal individual training account and supportive-service dollars to supplement — never supplant — grant-funded stipends. The center also connects completers who are not immediately placed to its full job-search infrastructure.

**Our reentry nonprofit partner** provides **case management for the reentry cohorts**: pre-release recruitment in coordination with the Department of Corrections and Community Correction, individualized reentry plans, weekly check-ins during training, crisis intervention, and post-placement support through twelve months. The partner brings an established caseload relationship with the reentry population and existing trust with supervision officers that the Consortium could not replicate internally. Grant funds support 1.5 FTE case managers housed at the partner organization under subaward.

**Member firms** provide the demand side: curriculum validation through the Employer Advisory Committee, paid work experience placements, hiring commitments, equipment and consumables in-kind, and — critically — the cash contributions that constitute the majority of the required match, as detailed in the Budget Narrative.

### The redesigned placement model

Because the 2022–2024 placement decline is the central question this proposal must answer, we describe here how placement now works. Under the leadership of Employer Engagement Manager Rosa Delgadillo, the Consortium has rebuilt placement around three principles adopted after the Talbert closure:

- **No concentration.** No single employer may absorb more than 10 percent of a program year's completers in our placement planning. Ms. Delgadillo maintains active hiring relationships with all 34 member firms plus 18 non-member regional employers, and cohort scheduling is now driven by a quarterly demand survey of actual anticipated openings rather than historical hiring patterns.
- **Placement begins at enrollment.** The paid work experience component means every trainee is inside an employer's building weeks before completion. Ms. Delgadillo matches trainees to host firms based on the demand survey, so work experience sites are, by design, sites with imminent openings.
- **Support continues through placement.** Transportation and childcare stipends now extend 30 days past completion, because our data showed completers abandoning job searches for the same logistical reasons trainees abandoned training.

Early results validate the redesign: the two cohorts completing in the second half of 2025, the first to run under the full new model, achieved a 74 percent 90-day placement rate — not yet back to 2022 levels, but a 16-point recovery, achieved without grant support and before stipends were fully restored.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

The Pipeline is organized around three goals. Objectives are stated as measurable targets over the three-year grant period, and each maps directly to the Foundation's required reporting metrics.

### Goal 1: Expand the credentialed manufacturing workforce of the Arkansas River Valley.

- **Objective 1.1 — Enrollment.** Enroll 480 trainees over three years (160 per year across eight annual cohorts), of whom at least 120 are members of the reentry cohorts.
- **Objective 1.2 — Completion.** Achieve a program completion rate of at least 82 percent (394+ completers), against a 2022–2024 baseline of 76 percent, with the improvement attributable to restored stipends and paid work experience.
- **Objective 1.3 — Credential attainment.** At least 85 percent of completers (335+) earn one or more industry-recognized credentials (NIMS, AWS, or the industrial maintenance stackable set). At least 60 percent of completers have UAFS credit articulated to their transcripts.

### Goal 2: Place completers into quality manufacturing employment and keep them there.

- **Objective 2.1 — 90-day placement.** At least 75 percent of completers placed in training-related employment within 90 days in Year 1, rising to 80 percent in Years 2 and 3 as the rebuilt employer network matures. (Baseline: 58 percent in 2024; 74 percent in late-2025 pilot cohorts.)
- **Objective 2.2 — 180-day placement.** At least 82 percent of completers employed at 180 days, capturing later placements and confirming that early placements hold.
- **Objective 2.3 — Wages.** Median wage at placement of at least $19.50 per hour, and median wage at twelve months of at least $22.00 per hour, reflecting probationary-period increases and shift differentials standard among member firms.
- **Objective 2.4 — Employer retention.** At least 70 percent of placed completers retained by their hiring employer at twelve months, measured through the semiannual employer verification survey described in the Evaluation Plan.
- **Objective 2.5 — Reentry parity.** Reentry cohort placement and retention rates within 8 percentage points of general cohort rates, a target our fair-chance member firms' historical experience suggests is achievable.

### Goal 3: Operate efficiently and build a placement engine that outlasts the grant.

- **Objective 3.1 — Cost per placement.** Total program cost per placement (all sources) at or below $9,800 by Year 3, against a 2024 baseline of $11,400 — the improvement driven by higher completion and placement rates spreading fixed costs across more successful outcomes rather than by cuts to per-trainee support.
- **Objective 3.2 — Employer diversification.** No single employer accounts for more than 10 percent of any program year's placements; at least 40 distinct employers hire at least one completer over the grant period.
- **Objective 3.3 — Match and sustainability milestones.** Full dollar-for-dollar match documented each year; the fee-for-service and diversified-revenue milestones specified in the Sustainability section met on schedule.

All objectives are reported annually, disaggregated by track, by cohort type (general/reentry), and by demographic characteristics.

## Evaluation Plan

The Foundation's five required metrics — credential attainment, placement at 90 and 180 days, median wage at placement and at twelve months, employer retention, and cost per placement — form the spine of our evaluation design. Our approach combines rigorous internal performance measurement with independent external evaluation, and it is built to answer not only "did the program hit its targets" but "why or why not, and what should change."

### Data systems and collection

The Consortium tracks every trainee from application through twelve months post-placement in a case management database (Salesforce-based, implemented 2024) shared under data-use agreements with the workforce center and reentry partner, so that all three organizations work from a single record. Collection methods for each required metric:

- **Credential attainment** is verified directly against certifying-body records (NIMS and AWS online verification; UAFS registrar confirmation for articulated credit), not self-report.
- **Placement at 90 and 180 days** is verified through a two-source standard: employer confirmation (offer letter, hire verification, or work experience conversion record) plus trainee confirmation at structured 30-, 90-, and 180-day follow-up contacts. Where direct verification fails, we supplement with Arkansas UI wage-record matching through our data-sharing arrangement with the Division of Workforce Services, which also captures completers who take jobs outside our employer network.
- **Median wage at placement and twelve months** is drawn from employer-verified starting wage at hire and from the twelve-month follow-up contact cross-checked against UI wage records, reported as median and interquartile range by track and cohort type.
- **Employer retention** is measured through a semiannual verification survey administered by the Employer Engagement Manager to every hiring employer, confirming continued employment, current wage, and any promotion, for each placed completer through the twelve-month mark. Our member relationships make response rates above 90 percent realistic; 2025 pilot survey response was 94 percent.
- **Cost per placement** is calculated quarterly by our analyst, Tomasz Wierzbicki, as total program expenditures (all funding sources, including the value of in-kind match) divided by verified 90-day placements, reported both as a program-wide figure and by track, so the Foundation can see the true unit economics rather than a grant-dollars-only figure.

### Analysis and learning

Mr. Wierzbicki produces a quarterly performance dashboard for the Executive Director, the Board (chaired by Sung-Min Park), and the Employer Advisory Committee, showing each metric against target with cohort-level detail. The dashboard governs a quarterly program review at which Training Director Jamal Whitfield and Ms. Delgadillo are accountable for corrective action on any metric more than five points off target — a discipline instituted in 2024 specifically because the 2022–2024 placement decline unfolded across multiple quarters before it triggered structural response. Attrition is analyzed monthly with exit-reason coding, and stipend utilization is tracked against completion outcomes to test, with our own data, the causal premise that support services drive completion.

### External evaluation

We will engage an independent external evaluator (budgeted at $30,000 per year; we anticipate contracting with a university-affiliated evaluation group, with the College of Business at UAFS among the candidates, selected through competitive process to avoid conflict with UAFS's programmatic role). The external evaluator will: audit our metric definitions and verification procedures in Year 1; conduct annual completer and employer interviews and focus groups; produce a comparative analysis of reentry versus general cohort outcomes; and deliver a summative Year 3 report assessing outcomes against targets and documenting the model for replication. Given the reentry cohort's policy significance, the evaluator will also explore feasibility of a matched-comparison recidivism analysis using Department of Corrections data.

### Reporting to the Foundation

The Consortium will submit annual reports covering all required metrics with disaggregation, narrative analysis of variances, match documentation, and the external evaluator's annual findings, along with interim data at whatever frequency the Foundation prefers. Our position on transparency is the one this application itself demonstrates: we report the numbers we have, including the ones that are hard to explain, because a funder that discovers problems from us is a partner and a funder that discovers them otherwise is a former funder.

### Track record — and what the last two years taught us

Our headline outcomes: 1,240 trained since 2017; 87 percent credential attainment sustained across the full period; 79 percent 90-day placement in 2022. And then 58 percent in 2024 — a number we will not ask the Foundation to look past, because how an organization responds to a bad number is a better capacity indicator than a string of good ones.

The diagnosis, established through a Board-commissioned review completed in early 2024: the Talbert Cordage closure eliminated our largest hiring destination and our largest single funding source in the same quarter. The late-2025 recovery to 74 percent placement, achieved on the Consortium's own resources, is the evidence that the corrections work. This grant funds the model at full strength and full scale.

The Consortium maintains audited financials (unqualified opinions in each of the last five audits), Board-approved internal controls, and experience administering restricted public and philanthropic funds with subrecipients. Mr. Wierzbicki and our contracted fiscal services provider will maintain separate cost-center accounting for the grant and the match.

## Sustainability

The sustainability question this proposal must answer is direct, and Board Chair Sung-Min Park has instructed us to answer it directly: **member dues currently cover only 30 percent of the Consortium's operating cost. How does this program continue?**

The honest starting point is that the current model, as historically financed, was not sustainable — the Talbert episode proved it. A structure in which one member's contribution functions as the match for an entire support-services budget is a structure with a single point of failure, and it failed. The sustainability plan below, developed by the Board's financial sustainability committee under Mr. Park with analysis by Mr. Wierzbicki, is therefore not a set of aspirations appended to a grant application; it is the organization's response to a near-death experience, partially executed already.

### The four-part revenue strategy

**1. Broadened and restructured member investment.** The 30 percent dues figure understates member commitment — members also contribute equipment, consumables, instructor release time, and work experience wages — but the committee concluded the cash structure itself was the problem: dues were flat and modest, while large voluntary contributions (like Talbert's) were concentrated and fragile. In 2025 the membership approved a restructured dues schedule, phasing in over three years, that scales dues to firm employment size and incorporates a per-hire training investment fee (see below). Modeling by Mr. Wierzbicki projects member cash revenue rising from 30 percent to approximately 42 percent of operating cost by 2029, spread across all 34 firms with no single firm exceeding 8 percent of total revenue. The Board has adopted a standing policy that **no single revenue source — member, philanthropic, or public — may exceed 20 percent of the operating budget**, precisely so that no future closure or grant cycle can do what the Talbert loss did.

**2. Fee-for-service placement economics.** The strongest sustainability argument for this program is arithmetic our members already accept: their average cost to recruit, onboard, and reach productivity with an externally hired skilled worker — or to leave a position vacant — substantially exceeds what a per-hire fee to the Consortium would cost them. Beginning in Year 2 of the grant, member firms hiring Pipeline completers will pay a **per-hire training investment fee of $1,500**, rising to $2,500 in Year 3 and to a market-tested rate thereafter; non-member employers hiring completers pay a higher fee or join the Consortium. Fourteen member firms have already signed letters of commitment to the fee structure. At steady state (roughly 130 placements per year post-grant), per-hire fees are projected to generate $325,000–$400,000 annually. This converts the Consortium's core product — a placed, credentialed, retained worker — into earned revenue tied directly to the outcome the Foundation is funding us to produce.

**3. Public workforce funding integration.** Co-enrollment with the Arkansas Workforce Center allows WIOA individual training accounts and supportive service funds to cover a share of per-trainee costs on an ongoing basis; we project WIOA and related state workforce funds sustaining 15–20 percent of program cost post-grant. The reentry cohort, once its outcomes are documented by the external evaluation, positions the Consortium for Department of Corrections reentry contracts and federal reentry employment grants, for which three-year outcome data — placement, retention, wages, and prospectively recidivism — will be the decisive credential. This is a core purpose of the evaluation investment: the data the Foundation requires is the same data that unlocks public funding.

**4. Philanthropic diversification, honestly scoped.** We will continue to seek philanthropic support, but under the 20-percent policy, philanthropy's post-grant role is defined as funding innovation and expansion — new tracks, new populations — not core operations. We will not present the Foundation with a sustainability plan that consists of finding another foundation.

### What the grant period must accomplish

The three-year Dorsey-Vachon investment is structured as a bridge with milestones, reported annually under Objective 3.3: dues restructuring fully phased in and per-hire fee live by end of Year 2; per-hire fee revenue covering at least 20 percent of program delivery cost in Year 3; at least one public reentry funding award or contract secured by end of Year 3; and Year 3 grant dependence for core program delivery below 35 percent, with a Board-approved budget for the first post-grant year demonstrating continuation of at least 130 training slots annually without Dorsey-Vachon funds. The facility decision milestone (mid-2027) is incorporated here as well, since long-term facility economics are part of the post-2028 cost structure.

The program continues, in short, because by Year 3 the people who benefit most measurably from it — the employers — will be paying for it in proportion to that benefit, through a structure no single firm's fortunes can break.

## Budget Narrative

**Total three-year project budget: $3,600,000 — $1,800,000 requested from the Dorsey-Vachon Foundation, matched dollar-for-dollar by $1,800,000 in documented cash and in-kind contributions.** The grant request averages $600,000 per year; per-trainee project cost across all sources is $7,500, consistent with the Year 3 cost-per-placement target of $9,800 at projected placement rates.

### Personnel — $1,215,000 total ($690,000 grant / $525,000 match)

Grant funds support: the new Trainee Support Coordinator (1.0 FTE, three years, $186,000 with fringe); a second instructor (1.0 FTE, $234,000 with fringe); 75 percent of the Employer Engagement Manager ($171,000); and 50 percent of the Analyst ($99,000). Match covers the Executive Director's 25 percent time, the Training Director's 50 percent time, existing instructor time devoted to Pipeline cohorts, and fiscal/administrative support, funded from restructured member dues revenue. Fringe is calculated at the Consortium's actual composite rate of 28 percent.

### Trainee supports — $1,062,000 total ($852,000 grant / $210,000 match)

The heart of the budget, and deliberately so.

- **Paid work experience wages: $432,000.** 480 trainees × 120 hours × $15/hour = $864,000 total; grant funds cover $432,000, and **host member firms cover the remaining $432,000 as documented cash match** — wages paid directly by employers to trainees on our verified timekeeping, counted at 50 percent toward this line and 50 percent toward the employer match pool below to avoid double-counting. (Presented conservatively: $432,000 grant here; the employer-paid $432,000 appears once, in the match summary.)
- **Transportation stipends: $234,000 grant.** Approximately 312 trainees (65 percent) × average 12 weeks × $60 average weekly utilization, plus the 30-day post-completion extension.
- **Childcare stipends: $186,000 grant.** Approximately 168 trainees (35 percent) × average 12 weeks × $110 average weekly utilization, plus post-completion extension. Both stipend lines are budgeted from actual 2023–2025 intake and utilization data, with the Support Coordinator authorized to flex between lines up to 15 percent based on cohort need.
- **Credential testing and licensure fees: $144,000** ($120,000 grant / $24,000 match): NIMS, AWS, and stackable-credential exam fees for all trainees, including retakes, plus documentation fees (IDs, driving records) disproportionately needed by reentry trainees.
- **Emergency barrier fund: $66,000** ($60,000 grant / $6,000 match): capped micro-grants ($400 per trainee) for completion-threatening emergencies — a car repair, a utility shutoff — administered under Board-approved criteria. Our attrition data show small, fast interventions at moments of crisis are among the highest-leverage dollars in the program.

### Reentry partner subaward — $312,000 grant

Supports 1.5 FTE case managers with fringe, mileage for facility-based recruitment, and the partner's direct administrative costs, under a subaward agreement with quarterly deliverables and pass-through of all data and reporting requirements.

### Training materials and consumables — $438,000 total ($126,000 grant / $312,000 match)

Welding consumables, machining stock, maintenance-trainer components, and PPE for 480 trainees. Member firms supply the majority as documented in-kind match at fair market value — a longstanding practice with established valuation procedures accepted in prior audits.

### Evaluation — $102,000 total ($90,000 grant / $12,000 match)

External evaluator at $30,000 per year, plus data system licensing attributable to the project; match reflects UAFS-contributed articulation and transcript verification services.

### Facilities and equipment maintenance — $351,000 match (in-kind and cash)

The Pipeline's allocable share of training center lease, utilities, insurance, and equipment maintenance through the 2028 lease term, funded from Consortium operating revenue (member dues) and member in-kind maintenance services. No grant funds are requested for facilities.

### Administration — $120,000 total ($42,000 grant / $78,000 match)

Grant-funded administration is held to 7 percent of the direct grant request — audit allocation, fiscal services, and compliance — with the balance of true administrative cost absorbed as match.

That is the arithmetic of this program and the argument for it: the Foundation's $1,800,000, doubled by the region's employers and institutions, purchases roughly 370 credentialed, employed, retained skilled workers, a rebuilt and diversified regional training engine, and a financing structure designed to still be running long after this grant closes.
