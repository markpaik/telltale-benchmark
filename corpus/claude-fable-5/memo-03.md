# RIVERBEND COMMUNITY SERVICES

## Internal Memorandum Packet — Sycamore Street Absorption and FY28 Restructuring

---

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** All Riverbend Community Services Staff
**FROM:** Ayesha Nkemelu, Executive Director
**DATE:** April 20, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Board Vote on the Absorption of Sycamore Street Family Center; What This Means for Our Organization and for Sycamore's Employees

On Friday evening, April 16, the Riverbend board of directors voted 9 to 4 to absorb Sycamore Street Family Center of Manchester into Riverbend Community Services. I am writing to tell you what that vote means, what it does not mean, and what happens next. I would rather you hear all of it from me than assemble it from hallway conversation, and I will not pretend the picture is simpler than it is.

Sycamore Street is a 40-person agency serving roughly 620 active clients in family stabilization, early childhood home visiting, and emergency assistance. In late March its leadership informed us, and then informed the state, that it was two weeks from missing payroll. The alternatives at that point were a managed closure, which would have stranded 620 families in the middle of open cases, or absorption by a larger agency. The board debated both paths seriously — the vote was not unanimous, and the four members who voted no raised concerns I take seriously and will address with the full board separately. But the majority concluded, and I agree, that Riverbend is the only organization in the region with the program overlap and the administrative capacity to take this on, and that walking away from 620 families two towns over is not who we are.

I want to be equally direct about what we are taking on. Along with Sycamore's clients and programs, Riverbend assumes two facility leases in Manchester that run through 2030 and approximately $480,000 in deferred payables — bills Sycamore owed and could not pay. Hector Bonilla will circulate a revised FY28 budget memo this week that lays out those numbers in full, and I encourage everyone to read it. You have a right to see the arithmetic behind the decisions that affect you.

That brings me to the hardest part of this memo. As most of you know, in March the federal government changed its reimbursement methodology for refugee resettlement, cutting $3.1 million from that program line with 90 days notice. That loss is not related to the Sycamore decision — it would be with us either way — but the two events now land in the same fiscal year. After exhausting every alternative Hector and I could identify, including a hiring freeze already in effect, the elimination of most discretionary spending, and the bridge fundraising effort Charlene Whitcomb is leading, we must eliminate 14 positions across the combined organization to present a balanced FY28 budget. I will not soften that. Fourteen colleagues will lose their jobs at a time when this work has never been more needed, and no framing makes that acceptable to the people affected.

Here is what I can commit to about how it will be done. First, the reductions will be identified by function, not by legacy organization. This is not a case of Riverbend staff paying for Sycamore's rescue, nor of Sycamore staff being absorbed only to be cut. Hector's memo identifies the affected positions by role. Second, every affected employee will be notified individually and privately by their supervisor and by Human Resources before any list circulates, and each will receive severance consistent with our personnel policy, continuation-of-coverage information, and priority consideration for any Riverbend vacancy for twelve months. Third, no position that carries an active client caseload will be eliminated without a documented transition plan for every case on it. Sunny Trinh is building that plan now.

For Sycamore's employees: you become Riverbend employees effective July 1, 2027, the start of our fiscal year, with service credit for purposes of leave accrual. Sycamore's direct service staff are represented by a union; Riverbend's staff are not. The union has written to us asserting successorship — that is, asserting that Riverbend inherits Sycamore's collective bargaining obligations. We have retained labor counsel, we are reviewing that letter carefully, and we will meet with the union's representatives in good faith. I will not characterize the legal question in an all-staff memo, but I will say this plainly: no Sycamore employee will be treated worse in this transition because they are represented, and no one at Riverbend should speak for the organization on this subject except me, Hector, or counsel. Two of Sycamore's program directors have resigned in recent weeks; Sunny's memo will describe interim coverage for their programs.

One more thing. Our FY26 single audit identified a material weakness in subrecipient monitoring. That finding is ours, it predates Sycamore, and it must be fixed before we take on a larger and more complicated operation — our funders will accept nothing less, and neither should we. Sunny's memo to program managers will introduce a new monitoring procedure that is mandatory, immediately, for everyone who touches a subaward.

I will hold all-staff sessions at the Hartford office on April 23 and at the Manchester sites on April 24 and will answer every question I am able to answer. Where I don't know something yet, I will say so and tell you when I will. Thank you for the work you do. It is about to get harder before it gets steadier, and I would not attempt this with any other group of people.

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Ayesha Nkemelu, Executive Director; Riverbend Finance Committee; Senior Leadership Team
**FROM:** Hector Bonilla, Chief Financial Officer
**DATE:** April 24, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Revised FY28 Budget — Federal Resettlement Reduction, Position Eliminations, and Assumed Sycamore Liabilities

This memo presents the revised FY28 budget for the combined organization and the assumptions behind it. It replaces the preliminary FY28 budget distributed to the finance committee in February, which is now obsolete on both the revenue and expense sides. I have tried to write this so that a program manager can follow it as easily as a committee member; the numbers belong to everyone.

**The starting position.** Riverbend entered this planning cycle with a $22 million operating budget and 180 employees. Two events have restructured that base. On March 9, the federal Office of Refugee Resettlement notified us of a change in its reimbursement methodology, moving from cost reimbursement to a fixed per-capita rate that does not recognize our actual cost of service in the Hartford market. The effect is a $3.1 million annual reduction in our resettlement line, effective with the new federal period beginning in June — 90 days notice on a loss equal to 14 percent of the entire agency budget. There is no scenario in which a cut of that size on that timeline is absorbed by efficiencies. Second, on April 16 the board voted to absorb Sycamore Street Family Center, which adds roughly $2.9 million in annual program activity, 40 employees, and the liabilities detailed below.

**Revenue.** The FY28 revenue budget for the combined organization is $23.4 million: Riverbend's continuing base of $18.9 million after the federal reduction, plus $2.9 million in Sycamore contracts that the state and the two principal foundation funders have confirmed will transfer with the programs, plus $1.6 million in development revenue above the amounts already committed. I want the committee to see clearly that the development number carries the plan's largest risk. Charlene's campaign is $900,000 behind a $4.2 million annual goal with four months remaining in the fiscal year, and her bridge request to three family foundations, described in her memo of May 1, is built into this budget at a probability-weighted $600,000. If the bridge request fails, we reopen this budget in September.

**Assumed liabilities.** Riverbend assumes approximately $480,000 in Sycamore deferred payables. I have now reviewed the payables aging directly. Roughly $210,000 is owed to landlords and utilities on the two Manchester facilities; $155,000 to program vendors, including a home-visiting curriculum licensor whose materials we need in order to keep the program running; $70,000 in unremitted retirement plan contributions, which is the item that most concerns me and which we will cure within 30 days of closing because the exposure there is not negotiable; and roughly $45,000 in miscellaneous trade payables where I believe negotiated settlements at 60 to 70 cents are achievable. The FY28 budget carries the full $480,000 as a one-time charge; anything we save through negotiation improves the position. The two Manchester leases run through June 2030 at a combined $340,000 per year. I have asked counsel whether either lease can be consolidated or sublet; until we know, both are budgeted in full.

**Position eliminations.** To balance FY28 at these revenue levels, the budget eliminates 14 positions from the combined complement of 220, for annualized savings of $1.24 million including benefits. The eliminations were built by function across the merged organization, per the executive director's direction, and break down as follows: four positions in resettlement operations, where the federal reduction directly eliminates the funding that supported them; three in administrative support, where the merger creates genuine duplication (one finance, one HR, one reception/facilities); two in the Manchester emergency assistance intake function, which will consolidate with Riverbend's Hartford intake under a shared model; two vacant positions currently frozen, which will simply not be filled; and three in program coordination roles that sit between direct service and management, where Sunny Trinh and I concluded the combined structure can operate with a flatter span. No direct home-visiting, literacy instruction, or eviction prevention caseload position is eliminated. Severance and transition costs of approximately $185,000 are budgeted in FY27 year-end, not FY28.

I want to be candid with the committee about what this budget does not do. It does not rebuild the operating reserve, which will end FY27 at roughly 45 days of cash — below our 60-day policy floor — after absorbing the payables and severance costs. It does not fund the systems investment our subrecipient monitoring corrective action will eventually require beyond a $60,000 allocation for a monitoring specialist position and software, which I regard as the minimum credible response to the audit finding and which is protected in this budget; cutting it would be a false economy that risks federal funding far larger than the savings. And it assumes labor costs at current Sycamore contract rates pending the successorship discussion; a materially different bargaining outcome would require a budget amendment, and I have flagged the range of exposure to counsel and the executive director separately.

I will walk the finance committee through the detailed schedules on April 29 and am available to any program manager who wants to sit down with the numbers for their own area before then. My door is open on this. A budget this tight only works if the people executing it believe it.

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** All Program Managers, Hartford and Manchester Sites
**FROM:** Sunny Trinh, Director of Programs
**DATE:** April 28, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Assignment of 620 Transferred Sycamore Cases; New Subrecipient Monitoring Procedure Effective May 1

This memo does two jobs. The first half tells you how the 620 active Sycamore cases will be distributed and supervised beginning May 5. The second half sets out the new subrecipient monitoring procedure that every manager holding a subaward must follow starting May 1. Both halves are mandatory reading, and I will need written acknowledgment of the monitoring section from each of you by May 4.

**Case transfer and assignment.** My team spent last week on site in Manchester reviewing the Sycamore case management system with their remaining supervisors. The 620 active cases break down approximately as follows: 285 in family stabilization case management, 190 in the early childhood home visiting program, and 145 in emergency assistance, of which about 60 are episodic and will likely close within one intake cycle. The records are in better shape than I feared — Sycamore's direct service staff kept working through the financial crisis, and their documentation shows it — but there are gaps in supervisory sign-off from roughly February forward, which corresponds to the period when the organization was coming apart at the leadership level.

Assignments will follow three principles. First, continuity of worker wherever possible: a Sycamore family keeps their Sycamore caseworker in the great majority of cases, because those workers are becoming Riverbend employees and the relationship is the intervention. What changes is supervision and infrastructure, not the person at the kitchen table. Second, no caseload above standard: transferred cases will be counted against our existing caseload standards (1:25 for stabilization case management, 1:15 for home visiting) from day one, and where Sycamore workers are currently carrying above standard — and several are, some at 1:35 — we will redistribute the excess to Hartford-based staff with capacity rather than normalize an unsafe number. I have identified capacity for approximately 70 redistributed cases across the Hartford stabilization team; managers receiving them will get the list by May 2. Third, every open case gets a documented transition review within 60 days: a supervisor and worker sit down with the file, confirm the service plan is current, confirm consents and releases are valid under Riverbend's forms, and flag anything requiring escalation. The review template is attached. Sixty days times 620 cases is a real burden and I know it; it is also the only way to say honestly that we know what we inherited.

On supervision: as Ayesha noted in her April 20 memo, two Sycamore program directors have resigned. Effective immediately, I am directly supervising the Manchester family stabilization program on an interim basis, and Delia Marsh from the Hartford home visiting program will serve as interim director of the Manchester home visiting program, splitting her week between sites. We will post both permanent positions in June, and internal candidates from either legacy organization are encouraged to apply. Manchester emergency assistance intake consolidates with Hartford intake under Rob Casella's team on a timeline he will circulate; until then, nothing changes at the Manchester front desk and no client is redirected.

**Subrecipient monitoring procedure — effective May 1.** Now the second half, and I need your full attention here. Our FY26 single audit found a material weakness in subrecipient monitoring. In plain language, the auditors found that when Riverbend passes federal funds through to partner organizations — and we currently hold eleven subawards across resettlement, literacy, and eviction prevention — we did not consistently perform risk assessments before issuing the subaward, did not consistently review subrecipient financial reports before approving payment, and could not document site monitoring for four of the eleven subrecipients. A material weakness is the most serious category of finding short of questioned costs. If it recurs in the FY27 audit, we can expect designation as a high-risk grantee, which means payment delays, special conditions, and a credible threat to funding streams worth far more than anything else discussed in this packet. This is not a finance department problem. The managers in this memo's address line are the people who hold the relationships with these subrecipients, and the fix runs through you.

Effective May 1, the following procedure applies to every subaward, existing and new. Each subrecipient must have a completed written risk assessment on the attached form — covering audit history, financial systems, prior performance, and staff turnover — before any new subaward is executed, and for existing subawards, by June 15. Each subrecipient invoice must be reviewed and signed by the responsible program manager, confirming that the costs billed match the approved budget and the reported activity, before finance releases payment; finance will no longer pay on a program manager's email alone, and Hector's team has been instructed to return unsigned invoices. Every subrecipient will receive at least one documented site visit per year, scheduled on a calendar I will maintain, with a written report filed within ten business days using the attached template; high-risk subrecipients, as scored on the risk assessment, get two visits and quarterly desk reviews of their financial reports. Finally, the new subrecipient monitoring specialist position Hector's budget protects will, once filled, coordinate this system and maintain the central file — but the position supports the procedure, it does not replace your responsibility under it. Signed monitoring responsibility rests with the program manager named on each subaward.

Sycamore holds two small subawards of its own, both in the emergency assistance program. Those relationships come under this procedure immediately upon transfer, with risk assessments due July 15.

I will hold a required training on the monitoring procedure on May 6, offered twice, morning in Hartford and afternoon in Manchester. Bring your subaward files. I recognize that this memo hands you a heavy sixty days on top of an already hard spring, and I will not insult you by pretending otherwise. What I can tell you is that the case transition reviews and the monitoring procedure are the two things that determine whether this absorption becomes a story about an agency that took on too much or one that did a hard thing well. I intend it to be the second.

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Development Committee of the Board
**FROM:** Charlene Whitcomb, Director of Development
**DATE:** May 1, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Campaign Position at Eight Months; Bridge Funding Request to the Calloway, Bergstrom-Ives, and Two Rivers Foundations

I owe the committee a clear-eyed account of where the annual campaign stands, why it is behind, and what I propose to do about it in the four months remaining in the fiscal year. I also need decisions from you on the bridge funding strategy, because the asks go out the week of May 11 and I want the committee's fingerprints on them before they leave the building.

**Where we stand.** Through April 30, the campaign has raised $2.4 million against a $4.2 million annual goal, leaving us $900,000 behind the pace we need with four months to go; on a straight-line basis we should be at $3.3 million by now. The shortfall is not evenly distributed. Individual giving is essentially on plan at $1.1 million, and I want the committee to register that, because it means our donor base has not lost confidence in us. The gap sits in two places: institutional grants, where two anticipated renewals totaling $520,000 slipped when program officers paused decisions pending "clarity on the federal funding environment" — a phrase I have now heard from four different foundations this spring — and the spring benefit, which came in $180,000 under budget after two corporate sponsors reduced commitments, with the remaining $200,000 of the gap spread across smaller lapses I detail in the attached schedule. I will be plain about the environment: every human services fundraiser in Connecticut is having some version of this year. That context explains the shortfall; it does not excuse me from closing it.

**What the merger changes.** The Sycamore absorption cuts both ways for development, and I have thought hard about which way it cuts on net. On the cost side, we inherit an agency whose donors have just watched it nearly fail, and some of them will need persuading that their giving didn't disappear into a hole; we also inherit Sycamore's modest but real donor file of about 1,400 households in Manchester and Tolland County, a geography where Riverbend has historically raised very little. On the opportunity side — and this is the heart of my strategy for the next four months — the merger gives us a story that is urgent, specific, and true. We did not manufacture a crisis to fundraise against. Six hundred twenty families were two weeks from losing their services, Riverbend stepped in, and it costs real money to do it right. In fifteen years of this work I have learned that donors respond to institutions that run toward a problem, provided the institution is honest about the cost. I intend to be honest about the cost.

**The bridge request.** I propose formal bridge funding requests to three family foundations with which we hold long relationships: the Calloway Family Foundation, where I recommend an ask of $400,000 over eighteen months; the Bergstrom-Ives Foundation, $300,000 over twelve months; and the Two Rivers Fund, $200,000 in a single-year grant. Total ask: $900,000, matching the campaign gap deliberately, though Hector's FY28 budget prudently carries these requests at a probability-weighted $600,000 and I endorse that discount. Each request is framed the same way: one-time transition funding for the Sycamore absorption — case transition costs, the assumed payables, the systems investment behind our audit corrective action — explicitly not a request to plug an operating deficit, because these funders can tell the difference and respect being told the truth. Calloway's trustees have twice told us they prefer to fund "moments of institutional consequence" over annual operating support; this is the most consequential moment in Riverbend's history and I intend to say exactly that. Bergstrom-Ives funded Sycamore's home visiting program for nine years and has a direct stake in its survival under our roof; their program officer called me, not the reverse, after the board vote became known. Two Rivers is the most speculative of the three, but their new executive director has signaled interest in "merger and sustainability" grantmaking and we should be first in that door.

I need three things from the committee. First, review and approval of the three request letters, which are attached in draft, at or before our May 8 meeting. Second, a board member paired with me on each ask: I request Miriam Rothstein for Calloway, given her twenty-year relationship with the family; Dr. Osei for Bergstrom-Ives; and I will take Two Rivers with Ayesha. Foundations of this size fund leadership they can see, and they need to see the board leaning in, not just staff. Third, candor in the other direction: if any committee member believes the $4.2 million goal should be formally revised downward rather than bridged, say so on May 8, because the worst outcome is an unspoken consensus that the goal is fiction. My own recommendation is that we hold the goal, pursue the bridge, and report to the full board in September with either the money or a revised plan — but I would rather lose that argument in the open than win it by default.

Two housekeeping notes. I am adding a fourth-quarter direct appeal to the combined Riverbend and Sycamore files in June, themed on the transition, with a modest $85,000 target that is not in Hector's budget and is pure upside if it lands. And I will present a stewardship plan for Sycamore's legacy donors at the May meeting, because the fastest way to lose 1,400 households is to send them a Hartford-postmarked letter that pretends nothing happened.

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Board of Directors, Riverbend Community Services
**FROM:** Ayesha Nkemelu, Executive Director
**DATE:** May 5, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Response to the Concerns of the Dissenting Board Members and to the Union's April 27 Letter Asserting Successorship

**CONFIDENTIAL — BOARD ONLY**

At the April 16 meeting, four members of this board voted against the absorption of Sycamore Street Family Center, and each of the four spoke to their reasons on the record. Miriam has asked me, and I agree it is owed, to respond in writing to those concerns rather than let a 9-to-4 vote paper over a real disagreement. I will take the four objections in turn, as candidly as I can, and then address the union's April 27 letter, because the successorship question connects to the dissent in ways the whole board should understand.

**The first objection: that Riverbend is absorbing another organization's crisis while managing two of its own.** This was Mr. Delgado's argument, and it is the strongest of the four. We lost $3.1 million in federal revenue on 90 days notice, we carry a material weakness finding from our own auditors, and into that environment we have taken on $480,000 in someone else's debts, two leases through 2030, and 40 employees. Ramon's question at the meeting — "would we advise any other nonprofit in our position to do this?" — deserves a straight answer, and my straight answer is: probably not, if the only measure were institutional self-protection. But that is not the only measure, and I do not believe it is the measure this board has ever used. The 620 families were not a hypothetical. A managed closure of Sycamore would have taken four to six months under the best assumptions, during which caseworkers would have left, records would have degraded, and the families most dependent on continuity — home visiting families with infants, households mid-way through eviction diversion — would have absorbed the damage. What I can commit to Ramon and the board is that we will manage the risk he identified with the seriousness it deserves: Hector's budget carries the payables and the leases in full with no optimistic assumptions, the monitoring corrective action is funded and protected, and I have asked the finance committee to receive a monthly, not quarterly, dashboard through at least December so that if this is going wrong, this board learns it in weeks, not quarters.

**The second objection: that the diligence period was too short.** Two board members made this point, and they are factually correct — we conducted in three weeks a review that would ordinarily take three months, because Sycamore had two weeks of payroll left when it came to us. I will not pretend the diligence was complete. What I can report is what we have found since closing the review: the payables aging Hector examined came in at $480,000 against the $450,000-to-$500,000 range we presented on April 16, so no surprise there; the case files are in better condition than we modeled, per Sunny's on-site review; and the one genuinely adverse discovery is the $70,000 in unremitted retirement contributions, which carries fiduciary exposure and which Hector will cure within 30 days. If further diligence surprises emerge, the board will hear about them in my monthly report, affirmatively, whether or not anyone asks.

**The third objection: that the position eliminations sever the moral logic of the rescue** — that we cannot claim to be saving jobs and services in Manchester while eliminating fourteen positions across the combined agency. I understand why this lands hard, and I want to untangle it, because I think the objection conflates two events. The 14 eliminations are driven overwhelmingly by the federal resettlement cut, which predates and is independent of Sycamore; had the board voted no on April 16, we would still be eliminating roughly eleven or twelve positions, and Sycamore's 40 employees would be losing their jobs entirely in a closure. The absorption adds perhaps two to three eliminations attributable to genuine administrative duplication, while preserving on the order of 35 Sycamore positions that would otherwise vanish. That arithmetic does not make the fourteen notifications easier for the people receiving them, and I will not use it as anesthesia. But it does mean the board should not carry the idea that the merger caused the layoffs. The federal government caused the layoffs. The merger changed who survives them.

**The fourth objection: that absorbing a unionized workforce commits Riverbend to a labor relationship this board never chose.** This objection belongs with the union's letter, so let me take them together. On April 27, Riverbend received a letter from the union representing Sycamore's direct service staff — approximately 26 of the 40 employees — asserting that Riverbend is a successor employer obligated to recognize the union and to bargain, and requesting a meeting within 30 days. I have shared the letter with counsel, and I want the board to understand both the legal posture and the position I recommend, because they are not the same thing and the board should choose with eyes open.

Counsel's preliminary view is that the successorship question is genuinely contestable. The doctrine turns on continuity of the workforce and the enterprise: where a majority of the new unit's employees come from the predecessor's represented workforce and the work continues substantially unchanged, successorship obligations generally attach; where the workforce is substantially restructured or merged into a larger non-union operation, the analysis is more complicated. Riverbend could litigate this. We could restructure assignments to blur the unit, contest the union's majority in the merged workforce, and quite possibly prevail, at a cost counsel estimates in the low six figures and twelve to eighteen months of proceedings.

I recommend against that course, and I want to give the board my reasons plainly rather than bury them in legal advice. First, the practical one: we are asking 26 caseworkers to carry their clients through the most disruptive transition of their professional lives, while their employer of record litigates against their chosen representation. We would win the legal question and lose the workforce, and Sunny's entire transition plan depends on those workers staying and functioning. Second, the financial one: the litigation cost approaches the annual cost of any plausible first-contract improvement, and Hector's budget already carries Sycamore labor at current contract rates. Third, the reputational one: Charlene is at this moment asking three family foundations for $900,000 on the strength of the argument that Riverbend runs toward hard things honorably. A public unfair labor practice fight in the middle of that ask is not a risk, it is a certainty of damage. Fourth, and I will say this as a matter of conviction rather than strategy: an agency whose eviction prevention counselors spend their days defending working people's stability should not spend its legal budget contesting its own employees' representation on a technicality of corporate form.

My recommendation, concurred in by Miriam, is that we respond to the union by May 15 agreeing to meet, recognize the union for the transferred unit as it exists, and bargain in good faith over the terms of the transition — while being clear at the table that Riverbend's finances are what Hector's memo says they are, and that the eliminations already identified proceed on their announced timeline subject to any bargaining obligations counsel confirms. This does not commit us to extending representation beyond the transferred unit; whether Riverbend's Hartford workforce ever organizes is their decision, not ours and not the union's, and we will conduct ourselves lawfully and neutrally on that question. I ask for the board's endorsement of this approach at the May 12 meeting, and I have asked counsel to attend to answer questions I cannot.

I want to close with a word to the four members who voted no. A 9-to-4 vote on the largest decision in this organization's history is not a mandate to stop arguing; it is an obligation on me to prove the majority right, and an invitation to the four of you to hold me to every commitment in this memo. Ramon has agreed to join the finance committee's monthly dashboard review through December, and I hope the other three will accept similar roles in oversight rather than distance. If this goes the way I believe it will, a year from now the vote count will be a footnote. If it goes the way you feared, this board will need to have been watching closely — and I would rather be watched by skeptics than reassured by friends.
