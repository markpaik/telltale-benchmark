# CITY OF ASHMONT FALLS

## Lead Service Line Replacement Program — Internal Memorandum Packet

---

**TO:** Department Heads; Members of the City Council
**FROM:** Yolanda Priestley, City Manager
**DATE:** November 12, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Initiation of the Lead Service Line Replacement Program and Program Schedule Through 2037

---

The service line inventory that the Water Division filed with the New York State Department of Health on October 15 identified 6,140 service lines in the Ashmont Falls system that are either confirmed lead, confirmed galvanized requiring replacement, or of unknown material. Our planning estimate, carried in the capital improvement program since the 2023 update, was 2,800. The inventory number is 119 percent higher. This memorandum opens the replacement program formally, assigns responsibility, and sets the schedule we will work to. I want every department head to read it in full, because the program is going to touch permitting, code enforcement, communications, legal, and the tax office, not only Public Works and Water.

I will be direct about how we arrived at a number this far off. The 2,800 figure was built from a 1988 distribution system map, a sample of 340 curb box inspections performed in 2019, and an extrapolation from housing age. The extrapolation assumed that homes built after the 1961 city plumbing code amendment would not have lead goosenecks or lead service lines. The inventory field work, which involved 1,910 physical excavations and predictive modeling validated against those excavations, found that the 1961 amendment was not enforced with any consistency until roughly 1969, and that a significant number of connections in the Cordell Heights and Riverbend sections carry lead goosenecks on otherwise copper lines. Approximately 2,240 of the 6,140 remain classified as unknown, meaning we have neither excavated them nor been able to model them at a confidence level the state will accept. Every unknown must be treated as lead until proven otherwise. That is the rule, and it is not negotiable.

The federal Lead and Copper Rule Improvements require that we replace all lead and galvanized-requiring-replacement lines by 2037, at a mandatory pace of no less than ten percent of the remaining inventory per year averaged over rolling three-year periods. Against 6,140 lines, that pace works out to an average of 558 replacements per year if we distribute evenly across the eleven remaining years. We are not going to distribute evenly. The health department has asked us to prioritize the 411 households with a child under six, and there are engineering and cost reasons to front-load work in areas where we can replace lines in contiguous runs rather than one at a time. Our schedule therefore targets 720 lines per year in 2027 through 2029, stepping down to roughly 500 per year thereafter, with a compliance cushion built into the later years.

### Program Organization

Faisal Chaudhry, as Public Works Director, holds overall program management responsibility, including construction contracting, inspection, restoration, and the property owner consent process. Grace Muriuki, as Water Superintendent, holds responsibility for the inventory itself, the water quality monitoring program, flushing and filter distribution, and the technical adequacy of every replacement. Nadia Petrosyan holds the financing plan, the rate structure, draw requests against the state revolving fund loan, and the accounting treatment of the private-side charge if the council adopts one. Each of the three reports to me on this program directly, and I expect the three of them to meet weekly. Disagreements that cannot be resolved in that meeting come to me within forty-eight hours, not at the end of a quarter.

I am creating a standing program status item on the public utilities committee agenda. Councilman DuBois has asked for monthly reporting, and I have agreed to it. The report will carry, at minimum: lines replaced to date against schedule, lines by classification remaining, consent requests issued and returned, refusals, average cost per line for the trailing sixty days, and the results of every post-replacement water quality sample taken in the reporting period. I would rather this program be criticized on a full record than defended on a partial one.

### Schedule

Construction package one, covering 340 lines in the Cordell Heights section, was designed over the summer and advertised February 3. I will address the bid result only briefly here because Nadia's memorandum takes it up in detail: the low bid came in 38 percent above the engineer's estimate at $14,900 per line, against an estimate of $10,800. Package one is being held pending the council's action on the revised financing plan. I am not prepared to award it until the funding is real, and I am not prepared to rebid it without understanding why the market priced it where it did.

Packages two and three, covering the Riverbend section and the eastern half of the Millgate section, are in design now and will be advertised in May and August 2027 respectively. Package four, which will be the first package built around the health department's under-six prioritization rather than around geography, will be designed in the fall of 2027 for spring 2028 construction. The reason it comes fourth rather than first is that the 411 addresses are scattered across all six sections of the city and mobilizing crews to work them individually costs roughly 2.4 times what the same work costs in a contiguous run. Grace has proposed, and I have accepted, an interim approach: every household with a child under six that is on a lead or unknown line receives a certified filter and replacement cartridges beginning December 1, 2026, regardless of when their line is scheduled. That is a materially better protection than a two-year-earlier replacement date, and it costs us about $61,000 a year rather than several million.

Verification of the 2,240 unknowns proceeds on a separate track, funded from the operating budget rather than the loan, using vacuum excavation at the curb box. Grace's crews are completing about forty per week. At that rate the unknowns are resolved by mid-2028. Each unknown that comes back non-lead reduces the replacement obligation, and I want that work pushed as hard as staffing allows, because it is the only lever we have that reduces the total scope rather than the annual pace.

### What I Need From Departments Not Named Above

Code Enforcement will need to process an estimated 720 plumbing permits per year associated with private-side work, and I am directing that these be issued at no fee and on a same-day basis when the application comes from a contractor on our prequalified list. The Clerk's office will handle recording of consent forms and refusal notices, and Legal will need to give me a written opinion by January 15 on the city's authority to enter private property to complete a replacement where the owner has refused, and on whether a refusal can be made to run with the property on transfer. The Assessor and the Receiver of Taxes should be prepared, if the council adopts a private-side charge with a deferral option, to administer that deferral as a lien. Nadia will bring you the mechanics.

This is the largest infrastructure obligation the city has taken on since the wastewater plant upgrade in 1994, and unlike that project it happens on 6,140 individual private properties, one homeowner at a time. The engineering is straightforward. Everything else is not.

**TO:** Yolanda Priestley, City Manager; Members of the City Council
**FROM:** Nadia Petrosyan, Finance Director
**DATE:** March 6, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Revised Rate and Financing Plan Following the February 24 Bid Opening

---

The single low bid on construction package one was submitted by Vandermeer Underground of Newburgh at $5,066,000 for 340 replacements, or $14,900 per line. The engineer's estimate was $3,672,000, or $10,800 per line. The bid is 38.0 percent over estimate. Two other firms took plans and did not bid. This memorandum revises the financing plan I presented in November and recommends a course of action on package one.

### What the Bid Means for Total Program Cost

If $14,900 holds across the program, 6,140 replacements cost $91.5 million in 2027 dollars. The November plan assumed $10,800 and a program cost of $66.3 million. The gap is $25.2 million before escalation. Applying a three percent annual escalation across an eleven-year build, the nominal program cost at the bid unit price is approximately $108 million, against $78 million in the November plan.

I do not believe $14,900 will hold, and I want to explain why before anyone builds a budget on it. I asked Faisal to have the design engineer debrief Vandermeer and reach the two firms that took plans and did not bid. Four things came back. First, package one is in Cordell Heights, which has the narrowest right-of-way in the city, mature street trees on both sides, and a documented gas main conflict on eleven blocks; it is the most expensive geography we own and we chose it first because it has the highest lead density, not because it was cheap. Second, the package is small. Three hundred forty lines does not amortize mobilization, and both non-bidders cited package size. Third, we required full-depth pavement restoration curb to curb on any street where the trench exceeded thirty percent of the width, which the contractors priced as a near-certainty rather than a contingency. Fourth, and least fixable, the regional market for this work is genuinely tight; three counties around us are bidding the same work under the same federal deadline, and Vandermeer told the engineer plainly that they are choosing which jobs to bid.

The first three are ours to fix. My recommendation, developed with Faisal, is to reject the single bid and rebid package one in June combined with package two, for a total of approximately 810 lines, with the restoration specification revised to allow trench-plus-two-feet restoration on streets rated fair or better in the 2025 pavement survey, and with a defined unit price for gas main conflict work so that bidders are not pricing that risk blind. We expect a combined package to draw at least three bidders and we are targeting $12,200 to $12,900 per line. That is still well above the engineer's estimate and I am not going to pretend otherwise. The estimate was built on 2024 bid tabs from a market that no longer exists.

For planning purposes, the revised financing plan carries $12,600 per line for 2027 through 2029 and $13,000 escalating at three percent thereafter. Total nominal program cost: $94.6 million. That is the number I am asking the council to plan against, and I will revise it in September after we see the combined package result.

### The State Revolving Fund Loan

We hold an $18 million loan commitment from the New York State Environmental Facilities Corporation with $6.2 million in principal forgiveness, leaving $11.8 million repayable at zero interest over thirty years. The forgiveness is tied to our designation as a disadvantaged community under the state's affordability criteria, which we qualified for on median household income and the ratio of the average water bill to that income.

Two things about this loan need to be understood clearly. It covers 19 percent of the revised program cost. And the forgiveness component is not renewable at the same ratio; EFC's guidance for the 2028 and 2029 intended use plans anticipates a lower forgiveness percentage as the statewide demand curve steepens. I am modeling future EFC draws at 25 percent forgiveness rather than the 34 percent we received, and I would rather be wrong in that direction.

Our financing plan through 2037 therefore assumes: the current $18 million loan drawn down by mid-2029; a second EFC application in the 2029 intended use plan cycle for $24 million with $6 million forgiven; a third application in 2033 for $22 million with $5.5 million forgiven; approximately $9 million in federal Bipartisan Infrastructure Law supplemental funds passed through EFC over the period, all of which I model as forgiven; and the balance from rate revenue and, if the council directs, from a private-side property owner charge.

### The Rate Increase

In November I proposed 11 percent phased over two years — 6 percent effective July 1, 2027 and 5 percent effective July 1, 2028. That was priced against the $10,800 unit cost. It is no longer sufficient.

The revised plan requires 15.5 percent over three years: 6 percent on July 1, 2027, 5 percent on July 1, 2028, and 4 percent on July 1, 2029, compounding to 15.5 percent. For the median residential account using 5,400 gallons per quarter, the current quarterly bill of $94.20 becomes $99.85 in year one, $104.85 in year two, and $109.05 in year three. The full increase is $14.85 per quarter, or $59.40 per year.

I want to note something the council should weigh. Our affordability designation under EFC criteria depends on the ratio of the average annual water bill to median household income staying above a threshold, and raising rates moves us further above that threshold, which is favorable for future forgiveness. But the same increase falls on households for whom $59.40 a year is not trivial. Ashmont Falls has 2,910 households below 200 percent of the federal poverty line. I am recommending, separately, that the council adopt a low-income water rate discount of 30 percent for households enrolled in HEAP, funded from the water fund at an annual cost of approximately $131,000. It is not a large program and it does not solve the affordability problem, but it makes the rate increase defensible.

I am not recommending a larger rate increase in place of the private-side charge, and I want to be explicit about why, since I understand the committee has been discussing exactly that trade. Absorbing the private-side cost into rates requires approximately $19.0 million over the program period — 6,140 lines at $3,100 average. Funding that from rates alone requires an additional 11 to 12 percentage points of increase on top of the 15.5 percent, bringing the total to roughly 28 percent. That is a policy choice the council is entitled to make. It is not a small one, and I do not want it made on the assumption that spreading the cost across the ratepayer base makes it disappear. It moves the cost from 6,140 property owners to 14,800 accounts, most of which are tenants who pay it through rent whether or not their own line is replaced.

### Cash Flow and the Package One Decision

We can award package one at the bid price today. We have the loan capacity. What we cannot do is award it at the bid price and hold the schedule, because $14,900 per line against an $18 million loan buys 1,208 lines, and we need the loan to carry us to the 2029 application cycle. Rejecting and rebidding costs us roughly four months of construction season. I judge that acceptable. If the combined June package comes back above $13,500 per line, I will recommend awarding it anyway and coming back to the council with a further rate revision, because at that point we will have established that the market price is the market price and the delay serves no purpose.

**FROM:** Faisal Chaudhry, Public Works Director
**DATE:** April 20, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Property Owner Consent Policy and Procedure for Handling Refusals

---

This memorandum establishes the policy and operating procedure by which the city obtains property owner consent to replace the private portion of a lead service line, and how the city responds when consent is withheld or cannot be obtained. It takes effect on adoption of the program ordinance and applies to all packages from the combined package one/two forward. I have reviewed it with Legal, whose January 15 opinion is attached as Exhibit A, and with the Clerk.

### The Underlying Problem

A service line runs from the water main in the street to the meter inside the building. The city owns the portion from the main to the curb stop. The property owner owns the portion from the curb stop to the meter. Federal rule requires replacement of the full line. A partial replacement — public side only — is worse than no replacement at all in the short term, because disturbing the line and creating a dissimilar-metal junction reliably produces a spike in lead release that persists for weeks to months. We will not do partial replacements. This means that where an owner refuses, we do not replace the public side either, and that line stays lead until the refusal is resolved.

Legal's opinion is that the city has no general authority to enter private property to perform this work over an owner's objection. We can condition the continuation of water service on compliance with plumbing code requirements, and we can require full replacement at the time of any service disconnection, meter replacement, or transfer of title where a permit is pulled. We cannot simply enter and dig. Any policy that assumes we can is going to fail in court and, more immediately, is going to poison the voluntary compliance we need from the other 6,100 owners.

### Consent Process

Every affected property receives four contacts before we treat it as a refusal.

The first is a program notification letter mailed 120 days before scheduled construction, in English and Spanish, explaining that the property has a lead or unknown service line, what the health implications are, what the city proposes to do, what it will cost the owner if the council adopts a private-side charge, and what the deferral options are. It includes the consent form, a postage-paid return envelope, and a phone number that reaches a person during business hours.

The second is a follow-up mailing at 75 days to any property that has not returned a form.

The third is a door hanger and attempted in-person contact at 45 days, performed by a two-person team from the Water Division. These teams are the most important part of the process and I have told Grace I want her most patient people on them, not her fastest. The single most common reason we get for non-return of the form is not opposition; it is that the owner did not understand the form, or did not open the letter, or is not the person who lives there.

The fourth is a certified letter at 21 days stating that absent a returned consent form the property will be classified as a refusal, that the public-side portion will not be replaced, that the lead line will remain in service, and that the property will be reported to the state as a refusal in the city's annual compliance filing. That letter also states — and this is the sentence that produces the most return calls — that the refusal will be recorded with the county clerk against the property and disclosed on any future title search.

### Rental Properties

Twenty-nine percent of affected addresses are rental. This is the hardest category and I want to describe the problem honestly rather than pretend the procedure above handles it.

The consent must come from the owner, who in many cases does not live at the property and in some cases does not live in the county. The tenant, who bears the health risk, has no legal ability to consent. The tenant is also frequently the only person who opens mail addressed to the property, and our door-hanger team is talking to someone with no authority to sign anything.

Our procedure for rentals is therefore: we identify the owner of record through the Assessor's file rather than relying on the service address, and all four contacts go to the owner's mailing address of record. Simultaneously — and this is a departure from how we would handle an owner-occupied property — we send a separate tenant notification to the service address at the 120-day and 45-day marks. That notification does not ask the tenant for anything. It tells the tenant the property has a lead line, that the city has contacted the owner, that the tenant is entitled to a free certified filter regardless of what the owner decides, and how to obtain one. Grace's division stocks the filters and the pickup point is the Water Division counter at the Municipal Building with no appointment and no proof of tenancy required.

I raised with Legal whether a landlord's refusal to permit replacement could be made actionable under the state's warranty of habitability. The opinion is unsettled and Legal declined to advise that we build policy on it. I am noting it here because I expect the question to be asked and I would rather the record show we looked at it.

For properties with five or more units under common ownership, and there are 61 such owners covering 890 units, we are not relying on mail at all. I have assigned a single staff member as the account contact for each of those owners, with instructions to reach a negotiated schedule covering the owner's entire portfolio. Two of those owners hold more than sixty units each. Getting those two to yes is worth more than a thousand letters.

### Handling Refusals

A property is classified as a refusal when either the owner returns a form declining, or the four-contact sequence completes without a returned form. Refusals are recorded, reported to the state annually as the rule requires, and revisited on the following schedule.

The refusing property receives a renewed consent request every twelve months. The property is flagged in the utility billing system, the code enforcement permit system, and the Assessor's file. When any of the following occurs, replacement becomes a condition: application for a plumbing permit of any kind at the property; meter replacement or upgrade; disconnection and reconnection of service; or transfer of title where the buyer applies to open a new water account. On transfer, we will not open a new account without either a returned consent or a signed acknowledgment from the new owner that the property carries a lead service line and a recorded refusal. Legal advises this is within our authority as a condition of service and I am prepared to defend it.

Where the refusal is at a rental property with a household containing a child under six, the file goes to the health department with the tenant's consent, and the health department pursues it under its own authority. That authority is broader than ours. I am not going to characterize what the health department will do with those referrals; that is their determination.

I want to set an expectation about volume. Utilities that have run full-replacement programs report refusal rates from four percent to more than thirty percent, and the variable that most predicts the low end is whether the owner pays anything. Programs that charge the owner nothing run at four to eight percent. Programs that charge full cost run at twenty to thirty-five percent. If the council adopts a $3,100 private-side charge without a deferral mechanism, I expect a refusal rate above twenty percent, which is roughly 1,230 properties, which would put us out of compliance with the ten percent annual pace requirement by 2029 and expose the city to state enforcement. I am not making a recommendation on the charge — that is Nadia's memorandum and the council's decision — but the operational consequence belongs in this one.

### Restoration and the Owner's Experience

Every consent form we send carries a written restoration commitment: interior work restricted to the wall penetration and the meter area, drywall patched and primed but not painted to match, exterior lawn restored with topsoil and seed within ten working days, hard surfaces replaced in kind, and any damage beyond the scope of the commitment addressed by the contractor at no cost to the owner under a warranty we will hold for one year. Half of the refusals other systems report are not about money and not about lead. They are about a stranger digging up a lawn and a bathroom. We will lose more consents to a bad restoration reputation in the first hundred properties than to any amount of mail.

**TO:** Yolanda Priestley, City Manager; Faisal Chaudhry, Public Works Director
**CC:** Public Utilities Committee; Orange County Department of Health
**FROM:** Grace Muriuki, Water Superintendent
**DATE:** October 8, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Program Status After First 400 Replacements — Productivity and Water Quality Results

---

The combined package one/two was awarded to Vandermeer Underground on July 6 at $12,410 per line and the notice to proceed issued July 19. As of October 2 the contractor has completed 400 full service line replacements. This memorandum reports crew productivity, cost, consent performance, and the water quality sampling results, which are the part I want read most carefully.

### Productivity and Cost

Vandermeer is running two crews. Through the first six weeks the combined output was 6.8 lines per week. Over the most recent five weeks it has been 21.4 lines per week. The learning curve was steeper than either of us expected and I want to record what drove it, because it will inform how we structure the next package.

The initial constraint was not excavation. It was interior access. Each replacement requires a plumber inside the building at the meter, and the contractor's original scheduling assumed the excavation crew and the interior crew could work the same address on the same day. In practice about forty percent of appointments failed on the first attempt — nobody home, wrong contact, tenant unaware of the appointment, dog in the yard, meter behind a finished wall or a freezer. The contractor now confirms interior access by phone two days ahead and again the evening before, and works a rolling list rather than a fixed street sequence. First-attempt success is now at 84 percent.

The second constraint was the gas main conflict Nadia flagged in her March memorandum. We found conflicts requiring hand excavation on 71 of the first 400, higher than the 40 the design assumed. The unit price we wrote into the rebid has been invoked 71 times at $1,840 each, which is $130,640 above base. That is real money and it is why the trailing cost per line is $12,940 rather than the $12,410 bid — 4.3 percent over. I have no complaint about the mechanism; writing that unit price into the contract instead of leaving the risk unpriced is the single reason the rebid came in 17 percent below the original bid.

Directional boring, which we allowed as an alternate on lots with a straight run and no obstruction, has been used on 118 replacements and is averaging $9,700 against $13,900 for open cut. Restoration cost on bored lines is a fraction of open cut. Where soil and obstruction permit it, it should be the default in future packages, and I would like the package three design to identify bore candidates explicitly rather than leaving it to the contractor's election.

Full-line replacement time on a routine open-cut property is now averaging 5.5 hours from first cut to water restored, with customers out of water for an average of 3.2 hours. We have not exceeded eight hours on any property. Every household is given bottled water on the morning of the work.

### Consent Performance

We issued 620 consent requests to build a working inventory of 400 completed replacements. Of those, 468 returned consent on the first or second mailing, 71 more after door-hanger contact, and 14 after the certified letter, for 553 consents against 620 requests — an 89.2 percent consent rate and a 10.8 percent refusal rate. That is materially better than Faisal projected, and I attribute most of it to two things: the council's adoption of the deferred-payment option in June, and the door-hanger teams.

Of the 67 refusals, 44 are at rental properties. That is 65.7 percent of refusals against a 29 percent share of the affected inventory, and it confirms the concern in the April memorandum. Nine of the 44 involve one owner holding 23 units in the Riverbend section who has not responded to any contact including two attempted in-person visits at his listed address in Rockland County. Faisal and I have referred that file to Legal.

Six of the 67 refusals are at properties with a child under six. Those six have been referred to the county health department with tenant consent in four cases and owner-occupant consent in two. All six households have filters.

### Water Quality Sampling

This is the section I want the committee to focus on.

Every replacement property receives a flush protocol at restoration of service, a certified pitcher filter with a six-month cartridge supply, and written instructions in English and Spanish. We then sample at the kitchen tap on the following schedule: immediately post-flush, at day 3, at day 30, at day 90, and at day 180. Sampling is first-draw after six hours minimum stagnation, analyzed by Keystone Analytical under our existing contract.

Through October 2 we have complete results for 400 properties at the post-flush and day-3 marks, 341 at day 30, and 196 at day 90.

The pre-replacement baseline, drawn from the 400 properties before work, had a 90th percentile of 21 parts per billion and a median of 7 ppb. Nineteen properties exceeded 15 ppb, and the highest was 74 ppb at a Cordell Heights property with a lead gooseneck and a lead interior line.

Post-flush, the 90th percentile was 12 ppb and the median 5 ppb. That is an improvement but it is not the result people expect, and it is not the result the contractor's flushing was supposed to produce. At day 3, the 90th percentile rose to 18 ppb and the median to 7 ppb. Thirty-one properties exceeded 15 ppb at day 3 — more than exceeded it at baseline.

This is expected behavior and it is why the filters matter more than anything else in this program. Physical disturbance of the line, even in a full replacement, dislodges scale from the interior plumbing that we do not touch and that continues to release particulate lead for weeks. The literature is consistent on this. What the literature does not agree on is duration, and our numbers help.

At day 30, the 90th percentile fell to 6 ppb and the median to 2 ppb, with four properties above 15 ppb. At day 90, the 90th percentile was 3 ppb and the median below the reporting limit of 1 ppb, with one property above 15 ppb. That property, on Halstead Avenue, sampled at 22 ppb at day 90 against 31 ppb at baseline. Investigation found a lead-soldered joint and a brass fixture of pre-1998 manufacture in the kitchen supply, both downstream of our work. The owner has been notified, the filter supply extended indefinitely, and the county health department informed. I want that case in the record because it is the honest answer to the question of whether replacing the service line fixes the problem at every house. It does not. It fixes the largest source at nearly every house.

The operational conclusions I draw are these. First, the six-month filter supply is not generous, it is the minimum defensible period, and I am recommending we extend it to twelve months at an additional program cost of about $34 per property, or $209,000 across the program. Second, our flush protocol at restoration of service is not aggressive enough; I am revising it from the current fifteen-minute high-flow flush at the nearest fixture to a thirty-minute flush at multiple fixtures with the aerators removed and cleaned, and the contractor will perform aerator cleaning as part of the work. Third, we must stop describing the day-3 and day-30 period to residents in reassuring terms. The current door hanger says water may contain elevated lead for a short period after the work. It should say that lead levels are likely to be higher for approximately thirty days than they were before the work, that this is normal and temporary, and that the filter must be used for every drop of water consumed by anyone during that period and for a year after. Residents who are told the truth use the filter. Residents who are told it is probably fine do not.

### Schedule Position

At the current 21.4 lines per week, the combined package completes its remaining 410 lines in approximately nineteen weeks, finishing in late February 2028 with a winter shutdown from mid-December to mid-March. Realistic completion is late April 2028. Package three should be advertised in January so that the contractor can move directly from one to the next without demobilizing.

We remain 4.3 percent over the awarded unit price and 15.0 percent below the March planning figure of $12,600 per line. Against the 2027 target of 720 lines we will finish the year at approximately 480, which puts us behind on the first year of a rolling three-year average. It is recoverable in 2028 and 2029, but only if package three is on the street in January.

**FROM:** Yolanda Priestley, City Manager
**DATE:** November 3, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Response to Your Written Objection of October 21 Concerning the Private-Side Replacement Charge

---

I have your memorandum of October 21 objecting to the $3,100 private-side charge adopted by the council in June, and requesting that the committee recommend its repeal and the absorption of private-side costs into the rate base. I have read it carefully, I have circulated it to the three department heads running this program, and I am responding to each of your five arguments in turn. I want to say at the outset that I think two of your five are correct, and I am recommending changes on both. On the central question — whether the charge should be repealed — I disagree with you, and I will explain why in terms of what I think happens to the program if it is.

### On your first argument, that the charge falls on people who did not create the problem

You are right, and it is not a small point. No property owner in Ashmont Falls chose to have a lead service line. The lines were installed under a city plumbing code that permitted them, in many cases by city-approved contractors, up to 1961 and in practice later than that. The city's own inventory work established that our enforcement of the 1961 amendment was inconsistent for most of a decade. There is a real argument that the city's regulatory history is a cause of the current condition.

Where I part from you is on the conclusion. The same argument applies with equal force to the public-side cost, which we are absorbing entirely, and to the 2,240 unknowns we are investigating at ratepayer expense. It applies to the rate increase, which falls on 14,800 accounts, of which roughly 8,660 have no lead line and no benefit from this program beyond a general public health interest. If the standard is that cost should follow fault, then the largest cohort being asked to pay under either version of this policy is the cohort with the least connection to the problem — the ratepayer whose line is copper. Repealing the private-side charge does not resolve the fairness objection. It relocates it onto a larger and less identifiable group.

### On your second argument, that the charge will produce refusals and defeat the program

This was the strongest argument in October, and Grace's October 8 data has now largely answered it. Our refusal rate through 620 consent requests is 10.8 percent. Faisal projected above twenty percent in April for a charge without a deferral mechanism. The council did not adopt the charge without a deferral mechanism; it adopted the charge with the option to defer payment as a zero-interest lien satisfied at sale or transfer, plus a full waiver for households below 200 percent of the federal poverty line and for any household enrolled in HEAP. Of the 553 consents obtained, 214 elected deferral and 96 qualified for waiver. That is 56 percent of consenting owners who paid nothing at the time of the work.

I want to be careful about what this proves. It proves that a charge with a well-used deferral is compatible with an 89 percent consent rate in the first 620 properties, which were in Cordell Heights and Riverbend and are not representative of the whole city. It does not prove the rate holds at 6,140. I have directed Faisal to report the refusal rate by section and by owner-occupancy status monthly, and I have told him and I am telling you that if the refusal rate crosses fifteen percent on a trailing six-month basis, I will come back to this council recommending changes to the charge myself, without being asked.

### On your third argument, that the burden falls on renters who have no say

You are right, and this is the change I am recommending most strongly.

Twenty-nine percent of affected addresses are rental, and Grace's data shows they are 65.7 percent of refusals. The structure we adopted in June treats a rental property identically to an owner-occupied one: the owner is billed $3,100, may defer as a lien, or may qualify for waiver. But the waiver is means-tested on the owner's income, not the tenant's. A landlord with substantial income who rents to a household at 130 percent of poverty gets no waiver, and the tenant, who bears the entire health risk, gets no protection and no voice. Worse, nothing in our policy prevents that landlord from passing the $3,100 through as a rent increase, which in a market with our vacancy rate is what will happen.

I am recommending three changes and will bring them to the committee on November 17.

First, that the private-side charge be waived entirely for any rental property where the household includes a child under six, without regard to the owner's income, on the theory that we are buying consent for the properties where consent is most urgent. There are 411 such households citywide and 119 of them are rentals. The cost is approximately $369,000 over the program.

Second, that the deferred lien for rental properties carry a recorded covenant prohibiting pass-through of the charge as a rent increase for thirty-six months, with the covenant enforceable by the tenant. Legal advises this is defensible as a condition of the deferral benefit, since the owner may always decline the deferral and pay.

Third, that where an owner of a rental property refuses and the household includes a child under six, the city offer to perform the private-side replacement at no cost to the owner in exchange for a recorded access easement. This makes the city's position on such properties simple: we will pay for it ourselves before we will leave a lead line in a house with a small child in it. Grace estimates fewer than sixty properties will use this and I would authorize it for two hundred.

### On your fourth argument, that $3,100 is arbitrary

It is an average, and you are right that averages are a poor basis for billing individuals. The actual private-side cost across the first 400 replacements ranged from $1,240 to $7,850, driven almost entirely by setback distance and whether the interior line runs through finished space. Under the current policy an owner with a twelve-foot setback subsidizes an owner with an eighty-foot setback.

I am not recommending we bill actual cost. Billing actual cost means the owner does not know what they owe when they sign the consent form, which Faisal's April memorandum correctly identifies as the thing most likely to produce a refusal, and it means we spend staff time adjudicating disputes over trench length. I am recommending instead that the flat charge be tiered by setback distance into three brackets — under twenty-five feet at $1,900, twenty-five to sixty feet at $3,100, over sixty feet at $4,400 — with the bracket stated in the consent letter and fixed regardless of actual cost. That preserves the certainty that makes consent work and removes the most visible unfairness. Net program revenue is approximately unchanged.

### On your fifth argument, that the city should absorb the cost and raise rates further

This is the recommendation I decline, and I want to give you the arithmetic rather than a position.

Absorbing the private-side cost is $19.0 million over the program, or $16.2 million after the waivers and tiering above. Nadia's March analysis puts the rate impact at an additional 11 to 12 percentage points, bringing the cumulative increase to approximately 27 percent rather than 15.5 percent. For the median residential account that is a quarterly bill moving from $94.20 to roughly $120 rather than to $109, and an annual increase of $103 rather than $59.

Three consequences follow. The first is distributional and it is the one I keep returning to. That increase falls on all 14,800 accounts. The 8,660 accounts with copper lines pay it in full and receive no service. A substantial share of our lowest-income households are tenants in buildings with copper lines whose landlords pay the water bill and recover it in rent. Under the private-side charge, those households pay the 15.5 percent through rent. Under absorption, they pay 27 percent through rent, to fund replacements at properties they do not live in. I have not found a way to describe that outcome as more equitable than what we adopted, and I have tried.

The second is that our EFC forgiveness depends on an affordability calculation, and while a higher bill improves the ratio, EFC has signaled that it will scrutinize applications where a system's rate structure has outrun what its median household can bear. I do not want to be in the position of arguing to the state that our rates are simultaneously affordable enough to be sustainable and unaffordable enough to warrant forgiveness at 34 percent.

The third is practical. A 27 percent increase adopted in a single ordinance in this city will not survive a budget hearing. I have been through three water rate cases here. The 15.5 percent phased over three years is at the edge of what I believe passes. If the committee recommends 27 percent and the council rejects it, we do not end up with absorption funded some other way. We end up with no rate increase, no private-side charge, no funding, and a federal deadline in 2037 that does not move. I would rather defend an imperfect charge than lose the program to a vote we did not have to take.

### Where this leaves us

I am recommending we retain the private-side charge, tier it by setback, waive it entirely for rental properties housing a child under six, bar pass-through on deferred rental accounts, and offer no-cost replacement with an easement where a refusal at such a property would otherwise leave a lead line in service. I estimate the package costs the program $610,000 against the current adopted structure and I believe it buys back more than that in avoided refusals and avoided litigation.

I want to close by acknowledging something. You have been the most consistent voice on this council for the position that a household should not be handed a four-figure bill for a hazard it did not create and cannot see. That position is right in principle. My disagreement with you is entirely about what happens when it meets a $94.6 million obligation, an $18 million loan, and a statutory deadline. If you bring the repeal to a vote I will not oppose it publicly beyond stating what I have stated here, and if the council adopts it, I will implement it and Nadia will bring you a rate ordinance to fund it within thirty days.

I would like to walk the November 17 recommendations through with you before the meeting. My office will call yours.
