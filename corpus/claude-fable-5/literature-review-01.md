# Transitional Care Interventions to Reduce Heart Failure Readmissions: A Review of the Evidence, 2004–2025

**Prepared for the Board of Directors, Coronado Health Network**
**Yesenia Alcaraz, MSN, Director of Clinical Effectiveness**
**Search strategy and screening: Grant Peavey, MLIS | Clinical review: Obiageli Nwachukwu, MD, Chief of Cardiovascular Services**
**August 2026**

---

## 1. Introduction: The Question and Its Scope

Coronado Health Network absorbed $1.4 million in federal readmission penalties last fiscal year, and heart failure accounted for the largest share of that loss. The board has committed $2.2 million to a transitional care program and asked for an evidence review before its November decision. This document is that review. The question it addresses is narrow by design: **what does the published evidence show about the effectiveness, cost, and generalizability of transitional care interventions for reducing 30- and 90-day readmissions among adults hospitalized with heart failure, and which of those findings can a mid-sized regional system in southern Arizona reasonably act on?**

The scope requires three clarifications at the outset.

First, "transitional care" is not one intervention. The literature covers at least six distinguishable models: nurse-led transitional care programs built on advanced practice nurses following patients from admission through the post-discharge period; remote telemonitoring of weight, symptoms, and vital signs; pharmacist-led medication reconciliation at discharge and after; home visiting programs; community health worker (CHW) navigation models; and structured post-discharge telephone follow-up. Many published programs bundle two or more of these, which — as this review will show repeatedly — is one of the central reasons the literature contradicts itself. When a bundled program works, the trial usually cannot say which component did the work.

Second, the review privileges no single study design. Randomized trials carry the most internal validity, but the pragmatic question facing Coronado — will this work *here*, with *our* patients, at *our* scale — is answered as much by quasi-experimental evaluations of real health-system implementations and by qualitative work on why patients and staff engage or disengage as it is by efficacy trials run in academic medical centers. All four evidence types are synthesized here, weighted by their risk-of-bias ratings, which are recorded study by study in the extraction table in the shared project folder.

Third, the review takes seriously what the evidence does *not* cover. Coronado's service area includes rural Pima, Santa Cruz, and Cochise County communities; roughly a third of our heart failure admissions involve patients whose preferred language is Spanish; and transportation insecurity is documented in our own case management notes as a leading driver of missed follow-up. Section 5 shows that these populations are nearly absent from the trial literature, and that absence constrains every recommendation in Section 6.

The review deliberately excludes interventions aimed primarily at inpatient heart failure management (guideline-directed medical therapy optimization, inpatient diuresis protocols), disease management programs for stable ambulatory patients not anchored to a hospitalization, and hospice or palliative-care-led transitions, except where those elements appear inside a transitional care bundle. Pediatric and adult congenital heart disease populations are excluded.

## 2. How the Literature Was Searched

Grant Peavey conducted the searches in March 2025 with an update run in June 2025. Five databases were searched: PubMed/MEDLINE, CINAHL, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and ClinicalTrials.gov, covering January 2004 through June 2025. The 2004 start date was chosen to capture the foundational transitional care trials while keeping the evidence base within the modern era of heart failure pharmacotherapy; earlier landmark work (notably Naylor's and Rich's trials from the 1990s) is referenced where later studies build directly on it.

Search terms combined controlled vocabulary and free text across three concept blocks: heart failure (MeSH "Heart Failure," plus HFrEF, HFpEF, cardiac failure, CHF); transitional care (MeSH "Transitional Care," "Patient Discharge," "Continuity of Patient Care," plus transitional care management, discharge planning, care transition, post-discharge); and readmission or utilization outcomes (MeSH "Patient Readmission," plus rehospitalization, 30-day, emergency department revisit). No language restriction was applied at the search stage, though only two non-English studies survived full-text screening. The full search strings, hedges, and database-specific translations are appended to the extraction workbook.

The searches returned 2,847 unique records after deduplication. Two reviewers (Alcaraz and a clinical effectiveness analyst) screened titles and abstracts independently, with disagreements resolved by discussion and, in eleven cases, by Dr. Nwachukwu. 236 records were pulled for full-text review. 78 studies met inclusion criteria: 31 randomized controlled trials, 22 quasi-experimental evaluations (interrupted time series, difference-in-differences, and controlled before-after designs), 14 qualitative studies, and 11 syntheses (systematic reviews and meta-analyses). Risk of bias was assessed with Cochrane RoB 2 for trials, ROBINS-I for quasi-experimental work, and CASP checklists for qualitative studies; ratings for each study appear in the extraction table alongside effect sizes, population characteristics, intervention components, and follow-up duration.

Two features of the included literature should be flagged before synthesis. The randomized trials cluster in two waves — 2004 through roughly 2012, and 2016 through 2023 — and, as Section 3 shows, the two waves reach systematically different conclusions. And the quasi-experimental literature is dominated by single-system evaluations published by the implementing institutions themselves, a publication pattern that almost certainly inflates the apparent success rate of real-world implementations.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Nurse-led transitional care: the efficacy–effectiveness gap

The strongest and most cited results in the entire literature come from the early nurse-led trials. Naylor et al. (2004) randomized 239 older heart failure patients to a three-month advanced practice nurse (APN) transitional care model — in-hospital engagement, home visits, telephone availability, and APN accompaniment to the first physician visit — and reported a 34 percent reduction in all-cause readmission at 52 weeks along with lower total costs. Coleman et al. (2006) tested a lighter-touch model, the Care Transitions Intervention, in which a "transitions coach" activated patients around four pillars (medication self-management, a patient-held record, follow-up scheduling, and red-flag recognition), and found 30-day readmissions of 8.3 percent versus 11.9 percent in controls. Stauffer et al. (2011) and Voss et al. (2011) replicated Coleman's model in real-world delivery systems with reductions in the 25 to 40 percent range, and a Cochrane review of discharge-planning-plus-support interventions (Takeda et al., 2012) pooled these and similar trials into a confident summary estimate favoring intervention.

Then the second wave arrived, and it did not cooperate. Three large pragmatic multi-site trials published after 2016 found no significant effect on readmission. The Patient-Centered Care Transitions in Heart Failure (PACT-HF) trial (Van Spall et al., 2019) — a stepped-wedge cluster randomized trial across ten Canadian hospitals enrolling 2,494 patients — implemented nurse-led self-care education, a structured discharge summary, family physician follow-up within a week, and, for high-risk patients, nurse home visits and heart failure clinic care. The composite of all-cause readmission, emergency department visit, or death at three months did not differ between arms. Blum et al. (2020), in a multi-site U.S. pragmatic trial of a nurse-navigator transition bundle in 1,192 patients, likewise found no difference in 30-day readmission. DeVore et al. (2021) reported null primary results from a stepped-wedge implementation of a hospital-to-home transition pathway across a large integrated system.

What explains the reversal? Four design differences recur across the extraction table and, taken together, account for most of the divergence.

**Intervention dose and fidelity.** The early trials were efficacy studies delivered by small, intensively trained, protocol-monitored teams — often the intervention developers themselves. Naylor's APNs carried caseloads under 20; PACT-HF's nurses were existing hospital staff absorbing the intervention into ordinary workloads. Process data from PACT-HF show that fewer than 60 percent of high-risk patients received the full intended bundle. Hansen et al. (2013), reviewing Project BOOST implementations, found the same pattern at the mentored-implementation level: sites that achieved high fidelity saw readmission reductions comparable to the early trials; sites that did not saw nothing, and most sites did not.

**Secular improvement in usual care.** The control condition changed between waves. By 2016, U.S. hospitals operating under the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program had already adopted discharge phone calls, seven-day follow-up scheduling, and medication reconciliation as standard practice — the very components the early trials were testing against a bare-bones comparator. Desai and colleagues' analysis of national readmission trends (Desai et al., 2016) documents a system-wide decline in heart failure readmissions beginning in 2010, meaning the later trials were trying to demonstrate an increment above a rising floor. The early trials' 25–40 percent reductions were measured against a usual care that no longer exists.

**Patient selection.** Naylor and Coleman enrolled cognitively intact, community-dwelling patients able to consent and participate; PACT-HF and Blum enrolled close to all-comers. Krumholz et al. (2019), in a secondary analysis of pooled trial data, found intervention effects concentrated in patients with moderate — not maximal — baseline risk, suggesting that all-comer designs dilute effects by including patients too well to benefit and patients too sick or too socially unstable for any transitional intervention to reach.

**Outcome window.** The early trials followed patients for six to twelve months; the pragmatic trials keyed their primary outcomes to the 30-day penalty window. Naylor's own curves show the arms separating most decisively after week six. An intervention that works by building patient self-management capacity may simply not express its effect inside 30 days — a point taken up again in Section 5.

The fair reading, endorsed by Dr. Nwachukwu, is not that nurse-led transitional care "stopped working," but that its measured effect depends on dose, fidelity, patient targeting, and the quality of the comparator — all of which are within Coronado's control and none of which is guaranteed by adopting a model's name.

### 3.2 Telemonitoring: two meta-analyses, one evidence base, opposite conclusions

No strand of this literature contradicts itself more visibly than telemonitoring. Inglis et al. (2015), updating a Cochrane review, pooled 25 telemonitoring trials and concluded that structured telephone support and telemonitoring reduced heart-failure-related hospitalization (RR ~0.85) and all-cause mortality (RR ~0.80). Yet the two largest individual trials in the field are unambiguously negative: Tele-HF (Chaudhry et al., 2010), which randomized 1,653 patients to daily automated telephone symptom and weight reporting and found no effect on readmission or death, and TIM-HF (Koehler et al., 2011), which found no mortality benefit from physician-supported remote monitoring. Ong et al. (2016), in the BEAT-HF trial of 1,437 patients combining remote monitoring with structured calls, likewise found no reduction in 180-day readmission.

Two meta-analyses published within three years of each other, drawing on substantially overlapping trial sets, reached opposite conclusions. Yun et al. (2018) pooled 37 trials and reported significant reductions in heart failure hospitalization and mortality; Pekmezaris et al. (2018) pooled a largely coincident set and found no significant effect on all-cause hospitalization. Grant Peavey's cross-tabulation of the two reviews' included studies (in the extraction workbook) identifies the mechanics of the disagreement: Yun included several small pre-2010 trials at high risk of bias that Pekmezaris excluded on quality grounds; Yun's primary outcome was heart-failure-specific hospitalization while Pekmezaris pooled all-cause; and the reviews handled the large null trials differently in sensitivity analyses. In short, the answer one gets depends on which small positive trials one lets in, which outcome one pools, and how much weight Tele-HF and BEAT-HF receive. This is the clearest example in the review of methodological choices, not clinical reality, driving headline conclusions.

Two later developments complicate the picture in opposite directions. TIM-HF2 (Koehler et al., 2018) — a redesign of the earlier null German trial with tighter patient selection (recent hospitalization, exclusion of major depression) and a 24/7 physician-staffed telemedical center empowered to act on transmitted data — found a significant reduction in days lost to unplanned cardiovascular hospitalization and in all-cause mortality. The contrast between TIM-HF and TIM-HF2 is the single most instructive pairing in this literature: the technology barely changed; what changed was *who received the data and what they were authorized to do about it*. Conversely, invasive hemodynamic monitoring (CardioMEMS; Abraham et al., 2011, CHAMPION trial) shows durable reductions in heart failure hospitalization, but the subsequent GUIDE-HF trial (Lindenfeld et al., 2021) hit a null primary endpoint, with the investigators attributing attenuation to pandemic-era changes in care — and, at $17,000–$20,000 per implant, it is not a transitional care program in any case.

The qualitative literature explains much of the noninvasive null results. Greenhalgh et al. (2017) and Vassilev et al. (2015) document that patients disengage from daily transmissions within weeks when transmissions produce no visible clinical response — "shouting into the void," as one participant put it — and that adherence in the negative trials fell steeply after the first month. Chaudhry's own trial reported that 14 percent of intervention patients never used the system at all. Telemonitoring is not a device purchase; it is a clinical response system, and trials that funded the device but not the response found nothing.

### 3.3 Pharmacist-led medication reconciliation

Medication problems drive a substantial fraction of heart failure readmissions, and the case for pharmacist involvement looks intuitive. The evidence is genuinely mixed but more interpretable than the telemonitoring literature.

On the positive side, Gattis et al. (2004, PHARM study extension work) and later Warden et al. (2014) reported reductions in heart failure events when clinical pharmacists performed discharge medication review with post-discharge follow-up. Phatak et al. (2016) randomized patients to pharmacist discharge counseling plus two post-discharge calls and found a significant reduction in 30-day readmissions and ED visits combined. Anderegg et al. (2014), in a large single-center quasi-experimental evaluation, found reductions concentrated in high-risk patients. Meta-analytically, Mekonnen et al. (2016) pooled pharmacist-led reconciliation programs across conditions and found a roughly 19 percent reduction in all-cause readmission.

On the null side sit two well-conducted trials. Kripalani et al. (2012, PILL-CVD) randomized 851 cardiovascular patients to pharmacist-assisted reconciliation, counseling, and follow-up calls and found no reduction in clinically important medication errors' downstream utilization consequences. Ravn-Nielsen et al. (2018), a Danish multicenter trial of an extended pharmacist intervention, found a significant reduction in readmissions — but the "basic" intervention arm (in-hospital reconciliation only) did not differ from usual care, which is the more telling result: reconciliation *at discharge alone*, without post-discharge follow-up, appears to do little.

That is the pattern that reconciles the strand. Across the extraction table, pharmacist interventions confined to the discharge moment cluster around null; interventions extending into the post-discharge period — a call at 48–72 hours, access for questions, communication with the outpatient prescriber — cluster around benefit. The mechanism is consistent with qualitative findings from Cawthon et al. (2012) and our own internal case reviews: the dangerous errors are not on the discharge medication list, they are in what happens when the list meets the pill bottles at home, the prior authorization queue, and the $600 sacubitril/valsartan copay. A pharmacist who is gone when those collisions occur cannot prevent them. For a Spanish-speaking population, Cawthon's health-literacy findings carry extra weight and are discussed in Section 5.

### 3.4 Home visits

Home visiting shows the most consistent direction of effect of any single component, though with wide variation in magnitude. Stewart et al. (2004, and the earlier foundational Australian work) established that a single pharmacist-and-nurse home visit after heart failure discharge reduced unplanned readmissions and death over extended follow-up. The meta-analysis by Feltner et al. (2014), conducted for AHRQ and generally regarded as the methodological benchmark in this field, compared transitional care component types head to head and found that **home-visiting programs and multidisciplinary heart failure clinic interventions reduced all-cause readmission and mortality with moderate-strength evidence, while telemonitoring and structured telephone support alone did not reduce all-cause readmission**. Van Spall et al. (2017), in a network meta-analysis, similarly ranked nurse home visits and disease management clinics highest for reducing all-cause readmission and mortality.

The contradictions here are about dose and durability rather than direction. Kwok et al. (2008) found no significant readmission effect from a community-nurse home visit program in an older Hong Kong population, and Aldeer's and similar smaller null trials share a feature: low visit frequency (one or two visits) delivered by generalist nurses without heart failure protocols or prescriber linkage. The positive trials — Stewart, the Naylor-model replications, and the quasi-experimental evaluations by Ota et al. (2013) and Ruiz et al. (2018) — deliver multiple visits by heart-failure-trained clinicians with authority or direct lines to adjust diuretics. Once again the pattern from Sections 3.1 and 3.2 recurs: components work when they are connected to a clinical response, and not otherwise.

Cost is the constraint. Home visits are the most expensive per-patient component in the literature, and Section 3.7 shows the economic evaluations split on whether readmission savings cover visit costs — a split that turns almost entirely on baseline readmission risk of the population visited.

### 3.5 Community health worker models

The CHW literature is smaller, newer, and demographically the most relevant strand for Coronado. Kangovi et al. (2018), in the largest randomized trial of a standardized CHW model (IMPaCT), enrolled low-income patients across chronic conditions and found reduced repeat hospitalizations and a return of $2.47 per dollar invested in a subsequent economic analysis (Kangovi et al., 2020). Heart-failure-specific CHW evidence is thinner: Jack et al. (2009, Project RED) used a nurse-plus-lay-advocate discharge model with a 30 percent reduction in hospital utilization, but in a general medicine population; Balaban et al. (2015) found a patient-navigator program reduced readmissions in patients over 60 but — notably — was associated with *increased* readmissions in younger patients, one of the few frankly harmful subgroup signals in this review, which the authors attributed to navigators surfacing unmet needs without capacity to resolve them.

Two trials speak most directly to our demography. Riegel et al. (2006) tested telephone case management delivered in Spanish by bilingual, bicultural nurses to Hispanic patients with heart failure — one of the only trials in the entire evidence base designed for Spanish speakers — and found *no* significant readmission reduction, in contrast to the same team's earlier positive result in a predominantly English-speaking population (Riegel et al., 2002). The authors' own interpretation, supported by their qualitative follow-up, was that a telephone-only intervention could not overcome barriers that were structural (transportation, cost, family caregiving obligations) rather than informational. Rodriguez et al. (2019), a quasi-experimental promotora-based program on the Texas border, reported readmission reductions but with a high risk-of-bias rating (nonequivalent comparison group, single site). The honest summary: CHW/promotora models are the strand with the best theoretical fit to Coronado's population and the weakest heart-failure-specific trial base — a combination that argues for implementation *with* rigorous local evaluation rather than either adoption on faith or dismissal for lack of evidence.

### 3.6 Structured post-discharge telephone calls

Discharge phone calls are the cheapest and most widely adopted component, and the evidence is correspondingly deflating. Feltner et al. (2014) found structured telephone support reduced heart-failure-specific readmission but not all-cause readmission or mortality — a distinction that matters, because the federal penalty is calculated on all-cause returns. The DIAL trial (GESICA Investigators, 2005), a large Argentine trial of nurse telephone follow-up, reduced heart failure admissions, driven mainly by adherence and early symptom response; but Mistiaen and Poot's Cochrane review (2006) of post-discharge calls across conditions found no convincing effect, and Harrison et al. (2011) found calls effective only when the caller could schedule appointments and adjust care rather than merely assess and advise.

Set against Section 3.1, the interpretation is straightforward: standalone calls in the modern era mostly duplicate what usual care already includes, and calls without escalation authority document deterioration without preventing it. Calls earn their place inside bundles as the surveillance layer that triggers pharmacist, nurse, or clinic response — the same connected-response principle that separates positive from null results in every other strand.

### 3.7 Cost and cost-effectiveness: from $2,900 saved to net loss

The economic literature spans the full range from clearly cost-saving to clearly cost-losing, and the extraction table shows the spread is systematic, not random. At the favorable end, Naylor et al. (2004) reported roughly $4,800 lower total costs per patient at one year (about $2,900–$5,000 in various analyses depending on cost categories included); Coleman's coaching model, costing under $200 per patient to deliver, was cost-saving in every published replication; Kangovi et al. (2020) reported the $2.47 return per dollar noted above; and Stewart's home visit program was cost-saving over long follow-up. At the unfavorable end, the BEAT-HF economic analysis found telemonitoring added cost with no offsetting utilization reduction; Ong and colleagues' figures imply a net loss of several hundred dollars per patient; and modeling by Maru et al. (2017) found telemonitoring cost-effectiveness acutely sensitive to assumed effect size — at the null effects observed in the large trials, it is simply a cost.

Three variables explain most of the spread. **Baseline risk of the enrolled population**: a program costing $1,000 per patient breaks even only if it prevents readmissions costing ~$13,000–$15,000 at a plausible rate, which requires targeting patients with baseline 30-day readmission probability well above the ~22 percent heart failure average; programs enrolling all-comers dilute below break-even. **Intervention intensity**: coaching and call-based models are cheap enough to be cost-saving at small effect sizes; home-visit and telemonitoring models need large effects to clear their cost hurdle. **Perspective and horizon**: several "cost-saving" claims are from the payer perspective over a year; from a hospital perspective under fee-for-service, a prevented readmission is also foregone revenue, and the financial case rests on penalty avoidance, value-based contracts, and capacity release rather than direct savings — a distinction the board's finance committee will want made explicit, since Coronado's case rests substantially on the $1.4 million penalty exposure and on freeing beds currently occupied by bounce-backs.

## 4. What the Evidence Never Reaches

Four gaps in this literature bear directly on Coronado and cannot be waved away.

**Rural patients.** Of the 31 randomized trials, 27 were conducted wholly or predominantly in urban academic or large community hospitals. Peavey's extraction identified only two trials with meaningful rural enrollment (Young et al., 2016, in rural Australia; and a rural arm within Ong et al., 2016, too small for subgroup inference), and no trial conducted in a setting resembling Santa Cruz or Cochise County — long travel distances, thin home-health coverage, limited broadband. Home visiting, the best-supported component, is precisely the component whose delivery cost and logistics the rural setting most disrupts, and the literature offers no evidence on rural adaptations beyond descriptive program reports.

**Spanish-speaking patients.** Riegel et al. (2006) remains, two decades later, nearly the only randomized trial designed for Spanish speakers — and it was null. Most trials either excluded non-English speakers outright (Tele-HF, PILL-CVD, and at least nine others explicitly list English proficiency as an inclusion criterion) or did not report language at all. Given that a third of Coronado's heart failure admissions involve Spanish-preferring patients, the headline effect sizes in this review are, strictly, effect sizes for other people. The qualitative literature (Cawthon et al., 2012; Vivo et al., 2014, on Hispanic heart failure disparities) suggests mechanisms — health literacy demands of self-monitoring protocols, discharge instructions translated but not culturally adapted, family decision-making structures the interventions ignore — but mechanisms are not effect estimates.

**Patients without transportation.** No included trial measured transportation insecurity at baseline or tested a transportation component, despite early post-discharge clinic follow-up being a core element of nearly every bundle. Interventions premised on the patient appearing at a clinic within seven days silently assume the patient can get there. Our own case management data indicate this assumption fails for a material fraction of our population; the literature is simply silent.

**Follow-up beyond 90 days.** Twenty-four of the 31 trials stop measuring at 30 or 90 days. The handful with longer horizons tell a consistent and uncomfortable story: in PACT-HF, in BEAT-HF, and in the quasi-experimental cohorts of Ota et al. (2013), curves that separated early converge by six to twelve months. The exceptions — Naylor (2004) and Stewart's extended follow-up — are the highest-dose, capacity-building interventions. The plausible reading is that most transitional care *defers* rather than *prevents* readmission unless it changes patient self-management capacity or connects patients durably to longitudinal heart failure care. Because the federal penalty is a 30-day construct, programs can be penalty-effective without being health-effective; the board should decide explicitly which it is buying, because the literature shows they are not the same purchase.

A fifth, cross-cutting gap: **HFpEF**. The trial populations skew heavily toward reduced ejection fraction, while roughly half of Coronado's admissions are preserved-EF patients, who are older, more multimorbid, and more often women — and for whom component-level evidence is thinner still.

## 5. Implications for a System of Coronado's Size

Synthesizing across strands, five conclusions are defensible at moderate confidence, and each carries a design implication for the $2.2 million program.

**1. Components without a connected clinical response do not work; the same components connected to one do.** This is the single most consistent finding across all six strands — TIM-HF versus TIM-HF2, discharge-only versus extended pharmacist models, assess-only versus escalation-authorized phone calls. Whatever Coronado builds, every surveillance element (calls, monitoring, visits) must terminate in a named clinician with authority to act — adjust diuretics, pull in an appointment, dispatch a visit — within 24 to 48 hours. Budgeting for data collection without budgeting for response replicates the null trials.

**2. Target by risk; do not enroll all-comers.** The pragmatic trials' null results and the economic analyses converge on this. With roughly 900–1,000 heart failure discharges annually across four hospitals, a validated risk model directing the intensive tier (home visits, pharmacist follow-up) to the highest-risk 25–35 percent, with a low-cost tier (structured calls with escalation, seven-day follow-up scheduling) for the remainder, fits both the evidence and the budget. Krumholz et al. (2019) cautions that the very highest-risk patients may need social intervention rather than clinical intensification — which points to conclusion 3.

**3. The evidence base's silences are Coronado's core population, so the program must be built and evaluated locally.** Bilingual delivery cannot be an add-on: Riegel's null Spanish-language trial suggests translation of an English-designed telephone model is insufficient, and the CHW/promotora literature — thin but demographically apt, with Kangovi's economic results the strongest external support — argues for embedding bilingual CHWs in the intensive tier for patients with documented social barriers, including a transportation solution for the seven-day visit. Because the trial literature cannot tell us whether this works for rural and Spanish-speaking heart failure patients, Coronado should treat its own program as an evaluation: a stepped rollout across the four hospitals with prespecified 30-day, 90-day, and 12-month outcomes, stratified by language, rurality, and EF phenotype. At our volumes, twelve to eighteen months yields interpretable results.

**4. Fidelity is the product.** The gap between the 2004–2012 trials and the post-2016 pragmatic trials is largely a fidelity gap. That argues for dedicated staffing (protected transitional care caseloads, not absorbed duties), caseload caps consistent with the positive trials, and process measures — percentage receiving each bundle element — reported monthly from launch. Hansen et al. (2013) shows that sites achieving fidelity achieve the early-trial results; sites that staff transitional care as an unfunded mandate achieve PACT-HF's.

**5. The financial case should be stated honestly.** Against $1.4 million in annual penalties plus the contribution margin of released bed capacity, a well-targeted program in the $700,000–$900,000 annual operating range (roughly: 4–5 transitional care nurses or a nurse/pharmacist mix, 3–4 bilingual CHWs, program leadership, and a modest transportation fund) is defensible on the favorable half of the economic literature and roughly break-even on the unfavorable half — *provided* targeting holds. Telemonitoring hardware at scale is the component the evidence least supports for this budget and should be deferred pending the local evaluation, with the possible exception of a small connected-response pilot modeled on TIM-HF2's design principles.

The board asked for the evidence by November. The evidence says: nurse-led, risk-targeted, pharmacist-integrated, home-visit-capable, bilingual, response-connected, fidelity-monitored — and honestly uncertain for the patients we serve most, which is exactly why the program must measure itself.
