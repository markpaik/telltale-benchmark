## Performance Review 1 of 3

**Employee:** Emmanuel Owusu-Bempah
**Title:** Dispatch Supervisor
**Review Period:** August 1, 2025 – July 31, 2026
**Reviewer:** Hala Nasrallah, Terminal Manager
**Date of Review:** August 9, 2026

### Summary of Role and Context

Emmanuel supervises all dispatch operations for the San Antonio hub, covering 34 city drivers and roughly 2,400 shipments a day across pickup and delivery. This was his sixth year in the role. The year presented three significant headwinds that frame this assessment: driver turnover reached 68 percent, terminal tonnage fell 9 percent year over year, and our largest retail account — a 90-store chain worth $3.8 million annually — placed the terminal on formal notice over a 12 percent late-delivery rate on their freight. The operations manager position Emmanuel applied for was frozen by corporate in July, and I address that directly in this review because it would be dishonest not to.

### Assessment Against FY26 Goals

**Goal 1: Maintain on-time performance at or above 95 percent terminal-wide.**
Result: **Achieved.** Emmanuel held terminal on-time performance at 96.4 percent for the full year. I want to be precise about what that number means. He did it through a year in which two out of every three driver seats turned over. A dispatch supervisor's on-time number is usually the first casualty of turnover — new drivers miss windows, misread dock instructions, and burn hours learning routes. Emmanuel absorbed that churn by pairing new drivers with route sheets he rewrote himself, staging the hardest delivery windows on veteran drivers, and personally working the dispatch board on 14 Saturdays this year when coverage fell short. The 96.4 percent figure is the strongest single operational result at this terminal this year.

**Goal 2: Reduce empty miles by 5 percent through route optimization.**
Result: **Exceeded.** Emmanuel rebuilt the city route plan from scratch in the fall, consolidating 38 legacy routes into 34 and resequencing pickup windows around the I-410 loop. Empty miles fell 11 percent against a 5 percent target. At current fuel and equipment costs, our controller estimates the annualized savings at approximately $210,000. The route rebuild also reduced average driver duty time by 22 minutes per shift, which matters for the hours-of-service exposure discussed below.

**Goal 3: Support driver retention and onboarding in partnership with Safety.**
Result: **Partially achieved.** Turnover at 68 percent is a terminal-level and industry-level problem, and I do not lay it at Emmanuel's feet. Within his control, results were mixed. Exit interviews show dispatch was rarely cited as a reason for leaving — drivers who quit named pay, the nine-day wait for road tests before they could start earning full route pay, and competitor offers. However, three departing drivers in the spring cited "inflexible dispatch" regarding requested schedule changes. Emmanuel runs a tight board, and tightness is why our on-time number holds, but there is room to build more scheduling flexibility for tenured drivers without giving up service.

### Compliance Findings and Customer Escalations Touching This Role

The September federal compliance review that produced our conditional safety rating cited hours-of-service records among its findings. Dispatch does not own the HOS record system — that sits with Safety — but dispatch assigns the work that generates the hours. The auditors flagged six instances in the sample period where dispatched route plans left drivers with less than 30 minutes of margin against their 14-hour window. None resulted in a violation, but the pattern indicates dispatch was planning to the edge of the clock. Emmanuel responded immediately and without defensiveness: within three weeks he added a 60-minute buffer rule to route planning and built a pre-dispatch hours check with Marisela Quintanilla's team. Since November, no dispatched route has planned a driver inside a one-hour margin. That corrective response is exactly what I expect from a senior supervisor, and it materially supports the terminal's path back to a satisfactory rating.

On the retail customer escalation: the 12 percent late rate on that account was driven primarily by dock congestion and appointment-window compression at their distribution center, not by dispatch failures, but Emmanuel treated it as his problem anyway. He assigned their freight to a dedicated pod of four veteran drivers, negotiated two revised delivery windows with the customer's transportation manager, and cut lates on that account from 12 percent to 6.8 percent by June. The customer's transportation manager wrote to me in May, unprompted: "Emmanuel is the reason we haven't pulled the freight yet." The account remains on notice — 6.8 percent is not good enough — but the trajectory exists because of him.

### Specific Incidents

- **February ice event:** During the three-day February freeze, Emmanuel slept at the terminal two nights, resequenced every route around road closures, and delivered 91 percent on-time through the event while two of our sister terminals fell below 70 percent.
- **March dispatch dispute:** A driver filed a complaint alleging Emmanuel spoke to him "dismissively" over the radio after a missed pickup. I reviewed the recording. Emmanuel's tone was curt but not abusive. He and the driver met with me, Emmanuel acknowledged the tone, and there has been no recurrence. I note it here for completeness, not as a pattern.

### Feedback From Drivers, Peers, and Customers

Driver feedback in the annual survey rated dispatch 4.1 out of 5, the highest supervisory score at the terminal. Representative comment: "The board is fair. You run your route right, Emmanuel takes care of you." Peer feedback from Jamal Whitfield credits Emmanuel with helping him sequence outbound loading during his first months as dock supervisor. Marisela Quintanilla noted that the pre-dispatch hours check "was Emmanuel's design more than mine." Customer feedback, per the retail account, is quoted above.

### Employee Self-Assessment (Emmanuel Owusu-Bempah, submitted July 28, 2026)

> "I held the number I was asked to hold in the worst driver market I've seen in six years. I rebuilt the routes because nobody else was going to. I'll own the HOS margin finding — I was planning too close to the clock and I fixed it. What I can't fix from this seat is why we lose drivers, and I'll be honest: I applied for the operations manager job because I'm ready for it, it was posted, and then it was frozen. I have an offer in New Braunfels. I'd rather stay. I need to know there's a path here."

### Manager Response to Self-Assessment

Emmanuel's account is accurate and his candor is characteristic. I owe him the same. The operations manager freeze was a corporate decision tied to the tonnage decline and the conditional rating; it was not a judgment on his readiness. In my assessment he is ready for the role today. I have submitted a written exception request to the regional vice president asking that the freeze be lifted for this position when the terminal's safety rating is restored, and I have named Emmanuel as my candidate. I cannot promise an outcome. I can promise advocacy, and this document is part of it.

### Compensation and Promotion Context

The wage pool this cycle is 2.5 percent and supervisor promotions are frozen. I am allocating Emmanuel 4.0 percent from the pool — the maximum differential I can justify — funded by holding other allocations below the pool average. It does not match what a competitor will pay an operations manager. I have said so to my leadership in writing.

### Overall Rating: 5 — Exceptional

### Goals for FY27

1. Hold terminal on-time performance at or above 96 percent; reduce the retail account's late rate below 4 percent by Q2.
2. Sustain the 60-minute HOS planning buffer with zero planned routes inside margin; co-own the HOS corrective action plan with Safety through the rating upgrade review.
3. Reduce empty miles a further 3 percent through backhaul matching with the New Braunfels and Laredo terminals.
4. Build a tenured-driver flexible scheduling pilot (minimum 8 drivers) by Q1 to address retention feedback.
5. Complete the corporate Operations Leadership Program (already nominated) to formalize promotion readiness.

## Performance Review 2 of 3

**Employee:** Marisela Quintanilla
**Title:** Safety Supervisor
**Review Period:** August 1, 2025 – July 31, 2026
**Reviewer:** Hala Nasrallah, Terminal Manager
**Date of Review:** August 9, 2026

### Summary of Role and Context

Marisela has served as safety supervisor for eleven years and owns driver qualification files, hours-of-service compliance records, accident prevention, new-driver road testing, and the terminal's compliance management system, on which she is the only trained user. This year is the most consequential of her tenure, for reasons both strong and serious. Her accident-prevention results were the best in her eleven years. At the same time, the September federal compliance review found 22 of 31 sampled driver qualification files incomplete — findings squarely within her ownership — and those findings were a direct contributor to the conditional safety rating now carried by this terminal. A conditional rating threatens customer contracts, insurance costs, and ultimately the terminal's viability. This review must hold both truths at once, and it will.

### Assessment Against FY26 Goals

**Goal 1: Reduce preventable accident rate below 2.5 per million miles.**
Result: **Exceeded.** The preventable accident rate fell from 3.1 to 1.9 per million miles, a 39 percent reduction and the best figure in terminal history. This happened during 68 percent driver turnover — meaning Marisela drove accidents down while the driver pool got greener, which is close to unheard of. Her methods were concrete: quarterly defensive-driving refreshers she taught herself, ride-along evaluations for every driver in their first 90 days, and a near-miss reporting program that logged 140 reports and produced 11 route or dock changes. Two insurance-premium reviews cited the improvement. This result likely prevented injuries, and I want that stated plainly before anything else in this review.

**Goal 2: Maintain audit-ready compliance records, including driver qualification files and hours-of-service documentation.**
Result: **Not achieved.** The September review sampled 31 driver qualification files and found 22 incomplete — missing or expired medical certificates, absent motor vehicle record annual reviews, unsigned road test certifications, and in four cases missing previous-employer safety verification. HOS record-keeping deficiencies were also cited. These findings produced the conditional rating. I have to be direct: this is a serious failure on a core responsibility, and the consequences reach every employee in this building. The retail customer on notice raised the conditional rating in their escalation letter. Two prospective accounts declined to onboard us pending the rating upgrade.

Context matters and I will state it fully. Marisela flagged the DQ file backlog to terminal management — including to me — in writing in March 2025 and again in June 2025, requesting either a part-time compliance clerk or backup training on the compliance system. Both requests were denied above my level for budget reasons. Turnover at 68 percent meant she was building new files at more than twice the historical rate while single-handedly running road tests, training, and accident response. The nine-day road test backlog is a symptom of the same single-point-of-failure staffing. None of that erases the outcome — files she owns were incomplete and the terminal is paying for it — but a fair review records that she asked for help, specifically and in advance, and the organization said no. I hold her accountable for the result. I hold the organization, including myself, accountable for the conditions.

**Goal 3: Onboard new drivers to road-test completion within 5 business days.**
Result: **Not achieved.** Average road-test wait is nine days against a five-day target. Every day of that wait costs us driver earnings, retention, and revenue capacity. The cause is structural — she is the only qualified road-test examiner — but the target was hers and it was missed.

### Corrective Action Progress Since September

Marisela's response to the findings has been rigorous and fast. She built a corrective action plan accepted by the reviewing agency in October, personally re-audited all 89 active driver files by December, and brought the terminal to 100 percent file completeness verified in a February self-audit and again in June. She created a file-completeness checklist now used at all eleven terminals, and she has begun training a backup user on the compliance system — a dock clerk, one hour per week, which is not enough and is addressed in her FY27 goals. The upgrade petition to restore a satisfactory rating is on track for filing in October 2026, and its evidentiary backbone is her work.

### Specific Incidents

- **September compliance review:** Detailed above. During the on-site review itself, the lead auditor commented that Marisela's accident-prevention documentation was "among the most thorough" he had seen at a carrier our size — a snapshot of this year in one sentence.
- **April rollover near-miss:** A second-month driver reported brake fade on a set of doubles. Marisela grounded the unit, traced the defect to a maintenance vendor lapse, and pushed a fleet-wide inspection that found two additional units with the same defect. Straightforwardly excellent safety work.

### Feedback From Drivers, Peers, and Customers

Drivers rated safety training 4.4 out of 5, with comments like "Marisela's ride-alongs are why I haven't had an accident." New hires, however, repeatedly cited the road-test wait: "Nine days sitting at half pay almost made me quit before I started." Emmanuel Owusu-Bempah credits her partnership on the pre-dispatch HOS check. The retail customer's escalation letter referenced the conditional rating as a "material concern," which is customer feedback of the hardest kind and it attaches to this function.

### Employee Self-Assessment (Marisela Quintanilla, submitted July 27, 2026)

> "The DQ file findings are mine. I own the files, so I own the finding, and I'm not going to hide behind the staffing situation. But I did ask for help twice in writing before the review, because I could see it coming. I was one person doing three people's compliance work in a 68 percent turnover year, and something was going to break. I chose to keep drivers alive first — training, ride-alongs, near-miss follow-up — and let paperwork slip. I'd make the same choice again, and I understand it still can't happen again. Give me a trained backup and a clerk, and I will hand you a satisfactory rating and a clean audit."

### Manager Response to Self-Assessment

I accept both halves of Marisela's statement: the ownership and the account of conditions. Her prioritization instinct — people over paperwork — reflects the values I want in a safety supervisor, but the DQ file requirements are not paperwork in the dismissive sense; they are the federal mechanism that verifies a driver is qualified to be on the road, and their failure endangered the terminal. The resolution is not to work harder — she cannot work harder — it is to eliminate the single point of failure, which is now funded: corporate approved a part-time compliance clerk in July as part of the corrective action plan. Her March and June 2025 memos are in the file and were part of the justification.

### Compensation and Promotion Context

The wage pool is 2.5 percent and promotions are frozen. Marisela receives 2.5 percent — the pool average. Given the split nature of this year, a maximum increase is not defensible and a below-average increase would punish her for a resourcing failure she predicted in writing. The freeze does not directly affect her, as she has not sought promotion, but I note that her eleven years and system-critical knowledge make her retention essential, and I have flagged her for a market-rate review when the freeze lifts.

### Overall Rating: 3 — Meets Expectations

*(Component ratings: Accident Prevention 5; Compliance Records 1; Corrective Action Response 5; Onboarding/Road Tests 2. The overall rating reflects exceptional life-safety results and exemplary remediation weighed against a serious compliance failure on an owned responsibility.)*

### Goals for FY27

1. Achieve restoration of a satisfactory safety rating: file the upgrade petition by October 31, 2026 with zero deficiencies in the pre-filing mock audit.
2. Maintain 100 percent DQ file completeness, verified by monthly self-audits and one third-party audit in Q3.
3. Fully train the compliance clerk and one backup on the compliance system by December 31, 2026, eliminating single-user dependency; document all procedures in a desk manual.
4. Reduce road-test wait to 4 business days by certifying a second examiner by Q2.
5. Hold preventable accident rate at or below 2.1 per million miles.
6. Sustain the near-miss program at 120+ reports with quarterly trend reviews shared with Dispatch and Dock.

## Performance Review 3 of 3

**Employee:** Jamal Whitfield
**Title:** Dock Supervisor
**Review Period:** October 6, 2025 – July 31, 2026 (partial year; promoted into role October 2025)
**Reviewer:** Hala Nasrallah, Terminal Manager
**Date of Review:** August 9, 2026

### Summary of Role and Context

Jamal was promoted to dock supervisor in October 2025 under difficult circumstances: his predecessor was terminated for falsifying a manifest, morale on the dock was poor, and trust in supervision was damaged. Jamal stepped up from a lead position into first-time supervision of a crew of 26 across two shifts, at a terminal handling 2,400 shipments a day, during a tonnage decline and a customer escalation in which dock performance was directly implicated. This review covers roughly ten months. First-year supervisors are assessed on trajectory as much as position, and Jamal's trajectory is strongly positive — with two developmental issues that must be named clearly and worked deliberately.

### Assessment Against FY26 Goals

**Goal 1: Restore dock integrity and throughput following the October leadership transition; target 17 bills per hour by year end.**
Result: **Exceeded.** Dock throughput rose from 14 bills per hour at his takeover to 19 by June, a 36 percent improvement against a target of 17. Jamal accomplished this by restriping the dock to shorten forklift travel, moving the heaviest inbound to doors nearest the strip zone, instituting a shift-start huddle with posted hourly targets, and cross-training eight workers on forklift certification to remove bottlenecks. The throughput gain directly supports the retail account recovery — freight that clears the dock faster makes its delivery window — and Emmanuel Owusu-Bempah has confirmed that outbound staging now consistently beats dispatch cut times, which it did not in September.

On integrity: after a predecessor terminated for manifest falsification, the first requirement of this job was that the numbers be real. Jamal instituted dual sign-off on exception manifests and an open-book policy on his shift metrics. Two spot audits of his manifests this year found zero discrepancies. The auditable honesty of his dock is, in the current climate, as important as its speed.

**Goal 2: Hold damage claims at or below prior-year levels while increasing throughput.**
Result: **Not achieved for the full period; recovered by year end.** Damage claims on Jamal's shift rose 40 percent in the first quarter of his tenure. The cause was foreseeable in hindsight: he pushed speed before the crew's handling habits could absorb it, and newer forklift operators moving at the new tempo clipped and dropped freight. The escalation letter from the retail customer cited damaged deliveries alongside lates, so this was not a cost-free learning curve — it touched our most at-risk account during its most sensitive period. To his credit, Jamal diagnosed and corrected it himself: he slowed the tempo targets for six weeks, ran handling retraining with Marisela Quintanilla's support, added corner protection requirements for the retail account's freight, and instituted a claims review at every Monday huddle. Claims returned to baseline by June and finished July 6 percent below the prior-year rate. The pattern — an aggressive change, a resulting problem, an honest correction — is a common first-year supervisor arc. The lesson to carry forward is sequencing: build capability before demanding speed.

**Goal 3: Establish fair, consistent crew management practices.**
Result: **Partially achieved.** Two senior dock workers filed complaints in March about how Jamal assigns overtime, alleging he favored the workers he had cross-trained and bypassed seniority expectations. I investigated. I found no favoritism in intent — Jamal was assigning overtime to the most flexible, most cross-trained workers because it made operational sense — but I found a real process failure: he never published overtime criteria, never explained the change from the prior seniority-based practice, and the senior workers learned the rules had changed by being passed over. In a crew still rebuilding trust after a supervisor was fired for dishonesty, opaque decision-making reads as unfairness even when it isn't. Jamal accepted this finding without argument, published a written overtime rotation in April that balances cross-training needs with seniority, and reviews it in the weekly huddle. There have been no complaints since. Both complainants told me in follow-up that the new system is "fair enough," and one added, "he listened, which is more than the last guy did." The issue is resolved; the developmental theme — communicate the why before changing the what — remains his primary growth area.

### Compliance Findings and Customer Escalations Touching This Role

The September compliance review predated Jamal's promotion by three weeks and its findings (HOS, DQ files) do not attach to dock operations. However, the terminal's conditional rating raises the stakes on everything, including dock accuracy, and Jamal's dual sign-off manifest controls are part of the terminal's broader integrity story that supports the rating upgrade petition. On the retail escalation, the dock's contribution — first-quarter damage and slow staging — has been corrected, and the customer's June scorecard showed damage claims on their freight at the lowest level in two years.

### Feedback From Drivers, Peers, and Customers

City drivers rated dock operations 3.9 out of 5, up from 3.1 the prior year, with comments crediting faster load-out and accurate staging: "My trailer is ready when I show up now. That's new." Peer feedback: Emmanuel Owusu-Bempah calls Jamal "the most improved operation in the building." Marisela Quintanilla praised his engagement in handling retraining but noted he initially "wanted the training done in half the time it takes to do right" — consistent with the sequencing theme above. Crew feedback is mixed-to-positive: strong marks for energy, honesty, and visibility on the floor; residual wariness among a few senior workers, improving since April.

### Employee Self-Assessment (Jamal Whitfield, submitted July 26, 2026)

> "I took over a dock that didn't trust supervisors and I was in a hurry to prove the promotion was right. The throughput number shows the hurry worked; the damage claims show what it cost. I own the Q1 claims spike completely — I pushed speed before I trained for it. On the overtime complaints, my logic was right and my communication was wrong, and I've fixed the communication. What I'm proudest of isn't 19 bills an hour. It's that two audits found my manifests clean, because after what happened before me, that's the number that had to be perfect."

### Manager Response to Self-Assessment

Jamal's self-assessment is accurate, proportionate, and self-aware — qualities that predict a strong supervisory career. His identification of manifest integrity as the year's most important result shows he understands the job he actually inherited, not just the job description. I concur with his diagnosis of both stumbles and consider both corrected, with communication discipline remaining the active development area.

### Compensation and Promotion Context

The wage pool is 2.5 percent and supervisor promotions are frozen. Jamal receives 3.0 percent, above pool average, reflecting a strong first-year trajectory tempered by the Q1 claims impact on an at-risk account. The promotion freeze has a specific consequence for him: his October promotion came with a partial pay adjustment, with the balance planned for a six-month review that the freeze then blocked. That deferred adjustment is documented, I have escalated it in writing, and it will be my first compensation action for this terminal when the freeze lifts. He should understand the delay as a corporate budget constraint, not a performance signal — his performance earned the role.

### Overall Rating: 4 — Exceeds Expectations

### Goals for FY27

1. Sustain throughput at 19+ bills per hour while holding damage claims at or below 0.8 percent of handled shipments for all four quarters — speed and care together, not sequentially.
2. Maintain zero manifest discrepancies across a minimum of four spot audits; extend dual sign-off procedures to the night shift by Q1.
3. Operate the published overtime rotation with quarterly crew feedback sessions; target crew survey score of 4.0 or higher on fairness questions.
4. Complete the corporate First-Line Leadership curriculum, with emphasis on the change-communication module, by Q2.
5. Cross-train four additional workers to forklift certification and develop one lead as a shift backup, so the dock is no longer dependent on Jamal's floor presence to hit targets.
6. Partner with Dispatch to cut retail-account staging exceptions to zero by Q2 in support of the sub-4-percent late-delivery target.
