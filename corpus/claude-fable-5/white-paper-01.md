## Abstract

The metalcasting sector across the Great Lakes region faces a workforce crisis that is no longer prospective. First-year separation on melt and molding lines among the Great Lakes Metalcasting Institute's 140 member foundries stands at 38 percent, average cost per separation at $11,800, and time to fill open hourly positions at 14 weeks. Thirty-four percent of the member hourly workforce — roughly 7,300 of 21,400 workers — is over age 55. Turnover is not merely an administrative expense: plants in the top turnover quartile run scrap at 9.4 percent against 5.1 percent in the bottom quartile and carry a recordable injury rate 60 percent higher. Two major agricultural equipment customers now require documented staffing commitments as a condition of long-term supply agreements, converting a human resources problem into a commercial one.

This paper presents the Institute's turnover census data, compares the five pipeline approaches members have deployed since 2019 — registered apprenticeship, high school youth apprenticeship and co-op, community college cohort partnerships, structured ninety-day onboarding, and temp-to-hire conversion — and works through a plant-level cost model and implementation sequence. The analysis concludes that registered apprenticeship, layered atop a structured onboarding program, delivers the strongest retention and quality outcomes, and that its net three-year cost of approximately $17,600 per apprentice is recovered within the first eighteen months through avoided separations alone. The paper addresses the four objections members raise most consistently — poaching, small-plant burden, collective bargaining seniority language, and supervisor resistance — and notes that a federal apprenticeship expansion grant closing November 14, 2026 materially improves the economics for plants that act this year.

## 1. Introduction: The Problem in the Sector

Metalcasting has always been demanding work. Melt deck and molding line jobs involve heat, noise, physical exertion, and shift schedules that compete poorly with warehouse and logistics employers offering comparable starting wages in climate-controlled buildings. For decades, foundries in the Great Lakes region compensated with wage premiums, strong benefits, and the implicit promise of a stable career. That bargain held as long as an experienced incumbent workforce carried institutional knowledge forward and a steady stream of applicants replaced natural attrition.

Both conditions have eroded. The applicant stream has thinned as regional manufacturing employment options have multiplied and as fewer young workers enter the trades. Meanwhile, the incumbent workforce is aging out: 34 percent of the hourly workforce across Institute member plants is over 55. At typical retirement rates, member foundries will need to replace roughly a third of their skilled hourly base within a decade — before accounting for turnover among newer hires, which is where the losses are now concentrated.

The Institute's turnover census, collected annually from members since 2019, shows first-year separation on melt and molding lines at 38 percent. In practical terms, a plant that hires ten workers onto the melt deck in January can expect to have lost four of them by the following January, most within the first ninety days. Each separation costs an average of $11,800 in recruiting, screening, personal protective equipment, training hours, supervisory time, overtime backfill, and lost throughput. Each vacancy takes an average of 14 weeks to fill, during which existing crews absorb the workload — accelerating fatigue, overtime cost, and, as Section 2 shows, scrap and injuries.

Two developments have raised the stakes beyond internal cost. First, two large agricultural equipment customers have begun requiring documented staffing commitments — evidence of workforce depth, training programs, and succession planning — as a condition of long-term supply agreements. Members without a credible pipeline story now risk losing contracts, not just production hours. Second, a federal apprenticeship expansion grant offering meaningful per-apprentice offsets closes its application window on November 14, 2026. Plants that establish or expand registered programs before that date can substantially reduce program cost; plants that wait will pay full freight.

This paper proceeds in six further sections. Section 2 presents the Institute's data. Section 3 compares the five pipeline approaches members have tried. Section 4 works through a cost model for a representative plant. Section 5 lays out an implementation sequence a single plant can follow over twelve months. Section 6 addresses member objections directly. Section 7 concludes.

## 2. What the Institute's Data Show

### 2.1 The Turnover Census

The Institute has collected standardized workforce data from members since 2019, covering separations by tenure band, cost per separation, requisition-to-start time, workforce age distribution, scrap rates, and OSHA recordable injury rates. Participation now covers 132 of 140 member plants, representing approximately 21,400 hourly workers across eight states. Table 1 summarizes the headline figures from the most recent census cycle.

**Table 1. Great Lakes Metalcasting Institute Turnover Census — Headline Metrics**

| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| First-year separation rate, melt and molding lines | 38% |
| Average fully loaded cost per separation | $11,800 |
| Average time to fill, hourly requisition | 14 weeks |
| Hourly workforce over age 55 | 34% (~7,300 workers) |
| Scrap rate, top turnover quartile plants | 9.4% |
| Scrap rate, bottom turnover quartile plants | 5.1% |
| Recordable injury rate differential, top vs. bottom quartile | +60% |

### 2.2 Turnover Is Concentrated Early

Census tenure-band data show that the large majority of first-year separations occur within the first ninety days, with a pronounced spike in weeks two through six — the period when new hires confront the physical reality of foundry work, often with minimal structured support. Workers who reach the one-year mark separate at a fraction of the first-year rate. The practical implication is that retention interventions yield the highest return when concentrated in the first ninety days, and that any pipeline approach should be judged in part on how well it prepares candidates for what the work actually is before they commit.

### 2.3 Turnover Is a Quality and Safety Problem

The most consequential finding in the census is the association between turnover and operational performance. Plants in the top turnover quartile run scrap at 9.4 percent; plants in the bottom quartile run 5.1 percent. For a mid-sized jobbing foundry pouring 12,000 tons annually, that 4.3-point differential represents hundreds of tons of remelt, rework, and lost margin every year — a cost that dwarfs the direct expense of separations.

The safety differential is equally stark: top-quartile turnover plants carry a recordable injury rate 60 percent higher than bottom-quartile plants. The mechanism is intuitive. Inexperienced workers are injured more often; experienced workers stretched by chronic vacancies and overtime make more errors; and supervisors consumed by training churn have less time for hazard observation and coaching. Correlation is not proof of causation, and some third factors — management quality, pay position, equipment condition — plausibly drive both turnover and performance. But the association is strong, consistent across census years, and aligned with the broader manufacturing literature. Members should treat turnover reduction as a scrap-reduction and injury-reduction initiative, not merely an HR metric.

### 2.4 The Demographic Cliff

With 34 percent of the hourly workforce over 55, the sector faces a structural replacement problem independent of turnover. Retirements will remove not headcount alone but the tacit knowledge — sand behavior, pattern quirks, furnace rhythm — that keeps scrap low and molds filling on schedule. A pipeline strategy must therefore do two things at once: reduce first-year losses and transfer knowledge from the retiring cohort before it walks out the door. This dual requirement is central to the comparison in Section 3, because the approaches differ sharply in how well they accomplish knowledge transfer.

## 3. Five Pipeline Approaches Compared

Members have deployed five distinct approaches since 2019. Each has produced results somewhere in the network, and each has failed somewhere. This section describes each approach, summarizes observed outcomes, and identifies the conditions under which it works. Table 2 provides a summary comparison.

### 3.1 Registered Apprenticeship

Registered apprenticeship pairs paid on-the-job learning under a journeyworker with related classroom instruction, structured over a defined term — typically three years for metalcasting occupations — and registered with the U.S. Department of Labor or a state apprenticeship agency. Cost across member programs runs roughly $27,000 per apprentice over three years, covering related instruction, mentor time, wage progression above entry scale, and administration. Available offsets — state tax credits, tuition support, and grant funds — total approximately $9,400 per apprentice, for a net cost near $17,600.

Retention outcomes are the strongest of the five approaches. Member plants with active registered programs report that the large majority of apprentices who complete the first year remain through completion and beyond; the structured wage progression, credential, and mentor relationship all give the apprentice concrete reasons to stay. Equally important, apprenticeship is the only approach that systematically transfers knowledge from senior journeyworkers to new entrants — directly addressing the demographic cliff. The credential also provides exactly the documentation that customers demanding staffing commitments want to see.

The drawbacks are real: up-front cost, administrative registration burden, a three-year horizon, and the mentor-capacity constraint at small plants. These are addressed in Sections 4 and 6.

### 3.2 High School Youth Apprenticeship and Co-op

Youth apprenticeship and co-op programs place high school juniors and seniors in part-time paid foundry roles combined with school-based technical instruction. Cost per participant is modest — typically $3,000 to $6,000 per year in wages, supervision, and coordination. The approach builds the long-term applicant pool, improves the industry's local image, and lets both parties evaluate fit before a full-time commitment.

Limitations are structural. Minors face restrictions on hazardous occupations, which keeps youth apprentices off melt decks and away from many core processes; placements concentrate in finishing, inspection, pattern shop, and maintenance-adjacent roles. Conversion to full-time employment is meaningful but slow: the pipeline yields workers two to four years after program entry, and some graduates leave for post-secondary education. Youth programs are a valuable feeder — several member plants report that youth apprentices who convert become their most stable hires — but they cannot close near-term vacancies.

### 3.3 Community College Cohort Partnerships

Under cohort partnerships, a plant (or a consortium of plants) sponsors a group of candidates through a short technical certificate — typically eight to sixteen weeks covering metallurgy fundamentals, safety, print reading, and hands-on lab work — with employment contingent on completion. Cost runs $4,000 to $8,000 per participant depending on tuition-sharing arrangements.

Cohort programs pre-screen effectively: candidates who complete a demanding certificate have demonstrated commitment, and first-90-day separation among cohort graduates is materially lower than among direct hires in member data. The consortium model also spreads cost and lets smaller plants participate. Weaknesses are scheduling rigidity — cohorts start when the college calendar allows, not when the vacancy opens — and dependence on the college's capacity and instructor availability. Cohort partnerships work best as a feeder into apprenticeship or structured onboarding rather than as a stand-alone solution.

### 3.4 Structured Ninety-Day In-House Onboarding

The structured onboarding model formalizes the first ninety days of employment: a defined training progression with checkpoints, an assigned peer mentor, scheduled supervisor check-ins at days 7, 30, 60, and 90, graduated production expectations, and a completion milestone (often with a wage bump). Cost is low — $1,500 to $3,000 per hire in mentor and administrative time — and implementation requires no external partner.

Member results are consistent: plants that implemented disciplined ninety-day programs cut first-90-day separations substantially, because the program targets exactly the tenure window where most losses occur. The approach's limitation is that it improves retention of whoever walks in the door but does nothing to enlarge or improve the applicant pool, and it provides no credential, no deep skill development, and no answer to the customer staffing-commitment requirement. It is, however, the essential foundation: every other approach performs better when it lands on top of structured onboarding, and apprenticeship programs launched without it show elevated first-year apprentice attrition.

### 3.5 Temp-to-Hire Conversion Through Staffing Agencies

Temp-to-hire places agency workers on the floor with conversion to direct employment after a probationary period, typically 90 days. It offers speed — agencies can supply candidates in days rather than the 14-week average requisition cycle — and shifts screening risk to the agency.

The census data are unfavorable on nearly every other dimension. Agency markups of 35 to 55 percent make temp labor expensive per productive hour. Conversion rates in member plants are low; many temporary workers treat the assignment as exactly that. Temporary workers show higher injury rates than direct hires in the same roles, consistent with national findings on temporary manufacturing labor, and the two-tier floor dynamic can corrode culture in both union and non-union plants. Temp-to-hire has a legitimate role smoothing seasonal demand and covering emergencies. As a primary pipeline strategy, it is the most expensive and least effective of the five.

**Table 2. Summary Comparison of Five Pipeline Approaches**

| Approach | Typical Cost per Participant | Time to Productive Worker | Retention Effect | Knowledge Transfer | Meets Customer Staffing Requirement |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Registered apprenticeship | ~$27,000 gross / ~$17,600 net over 3 yrs | Productive during term; fully skilled at 3 yrs | Strongest | Strong (journeyworker mentoring) | Yes — credentialed, documented |
| Youth apprenticeship / co-op | $3,000–$6,000/yr | 2–4 years to full-time | Strong among converts | Moderate | Partial |
| Community college cohort | $4,000–$8,000 | 2–4 months to hire-ready | Moderate–strong in first 90 days | Limited | Partial |
| Structured 90-day onboarding | $1,500–$3,000 | Immediate hire; 90 days to baseline | Strong in first 90 days | Limited | No |
| Temp-to-hire | 35–55% agency markup; low conversion | Days to placement | Weakest | Minimal | No |

### 3.6 The Emerging Consensus

No single approach suffices alone. The pattern across the best-performing member plants is a layered system: structured ninety-day onboarding as the universal foundation; community college cohorts and youth apprenticeship as feeders; registered apprenticeship as the retention and skill-development engine for core melt, molding, and maintenance roles; and temp-to-hire reserved for genuine demand spikes. Sections 4 and 5 model and sequence that layered system for a single representative plant.

## 4. The Cost Model: What Retention Investment Returns

### 4.1 The Baseline — What Turnover Costs a Representative Plant

Consider a representative member plant with 150 hourly employees, of whom 60 work melt and molding roles. At the census first-year separation rate of 38 percent applied to a typical annual hiring volume of 20 melt/molding hires, the plant loses roughly 8 first-year workers annually. At $11,800 per separation, direct turnover cost is approximately $94,400 per year — before counting the scrap and safety penalty documented in Section 2, and before counting the overtime and throughput cost of carrying vacancies for 14 weeks each.

If the plant sits in the upper half of the turnover distribution, the scrap differential alone plausibly costs several times the direct separation expense. For the cost model below we conservatively count only direct separation costs; readers should understand that the full return is larger.

### 4.2 The Investment — A Layered Program

The modeled program for the representative plant:

- **Structured ninety-day onboarding** for all 20 annual hires at $2,200 each: **$44,000 per year**.
- **Registered apprenticeship** cohort of 4 apprentices per year in melt, molding, and maintenance occupations. Gross cost $27,000 per apprentice over three years ($9,000 per apprentice per year); offsets of $9,400 per apprentice reduce net cost to $17,600, or roughly **$5,870 per apprentice per year — $23,500 per year at steady state** (three overlapping cohorts of 4).
- **Community college cohort participation**, 6 seats per year at $5,500: **$33,000 per year**.

Steady-state annual investment: approximately **$100,500**.

### 4.3 The Return

Member plants running comparable layered programs report first-year separation falling from the high-30s to the mid-teens within two years. Applying a conservative reduction from 38 percent to 19 percent for the representative plant:

- Separations avoided: ~4 per year × $11,800 = **$47,200** in direct separation savings.
- Vacancy weeks avoided: 4 vacancies × 14 weeks of overtime backfill and lost throughput, conservatively valued at $2,500 per vacancy-week = **$140,000**.
- Scrap improvement: if reduced churn moves the plant even one-third of the distance from top-quartile toward bottom-quartile scrap performance (roughly 1.4 points on a 12,000-ton plant), the recovered value plausibly exceeds **$150,000** annually, though this figure is the most assumption-dependent in the model.

**Table 3. Representative Plant — Annual Cost and Return Summary (Steady State)**

| Line Item | Annual Amount |
|---|---|
| Structured onboarding (20 hires) | ($44,000) |
| Registered apprenticeship, net of offsets (12 active apprentices) | ($23,500) |
| Community college cohort (6 seats) | ($33,000) |
| **Total investment** | **($100,500)** |
| Direct separation savings | $47,200 |
| Avoided vacancy carrying cost | $140,000 |
| Scrap improvement (conservative partial capture) | $150,000 |
| **Total conservative return** | **$337,200** |
| **Net annual benefit** | **~$236,700** |

Even discarding the scrap line entirely, the program returns approximately $187,000 against $100,500 invested. Under conservative assumptions, the plant recovers its investment within roughly eighteen months of program launch, with returns compounding as apprentice cohorts mature and the plant's reputation in the local labor market improves.

### 4.4 The Grant Window

The federal apprenticeship expansion grant closing **November 14, 2026** covers a substantial share of the $9,400 per-apprentice offset assumed above. Plants that miss the window will face net apprenticeship costs closer to the $27,000 gross figure, weakening — though not eliminating — the economics. The Institute's workforce program office will assist members with applications; plants intending to apply should begin registration paperwork no later than September 2026.

## 5. Implementation Sequence for a Single Plant

The following twelve-month sequence reflects the practices of the member plants with the strongest results. It assumes a plant starting with none of the five approaches formally in place.

**Months 1–2: Baseline and buy-in.**
Pull two years of separation data by tenure band, department, shift, and supervisor. Calculate the plant's own cost per separation rather than defaulting to the census average. Present the findings to the plant leadership team and, in union plants, to the local union leadership — early, before any program design. Identify the two or three supervisors whose crews retain best; they will anchor the mentor bench.

**Months 2–4: Launch structured ninety-day onboarding.**
This is first because it is cheapest, fastest, and requires no external partner. Define the ninety-day progression by job classification, select and train peer mentors (with a mentor premium — typically $0.75 to $1.50 per hour while mentoring), schedule the day-7/30/60/90 check-ins, and set a completion milestone. Every subsequent element lands on this foundation.

**Months 3–5: Begin apprenticeship registration and grant application.**
Contact the state apprenticeship office or the Institute's workforce program office to adapt existing metalcasting occupation frameworks — most of the standards work is already done and need not be built from scratch. In union plants, negotiate a letter of agreement covering apprentice wage progression and its relationship to seniority language (see Section 6.3). Submit the federal grant application well before the November 14, 2026 deadline.

**Months 4–6: Establish the community college partnership.**
Approach the nearest community or technical college with a specific proposal: seat commitments, tuition-share terms, employment-contingent-on-completion language, and input into the curriculum. Where a single plant cannot fill a cohort, the Institute will broker multi-plant consortia within a labor market.

**Months 6–9: Select and start the first apprentice cohort.**
Recruit from three sources in order of observed success: incumbent workers seeking advancement, community college cohort completers, and external applicants. Incumbent-first selection matters — it rewards existing employees, defuses union concerns, and produces apprentices who already know the environment.

**Months 9–12: Add the youth pipeline; review and adjust.**
With the core system running, engage the school district or regional youth apprenticeship consortium for the following academic year. At month twelve, review separation data against baseline, survey new hires and mentors, and adjust checkpoint content, mentor selection, and cohort sizing.

**Throughout: Restrict temp-to-hire to defined use cases** — seasonal peaks and emergency coverage — with a target ceiling (leading member plants use 5 percent of hourly headcount) and a standing rule that any agency worker on the floor past 90 days triggers a conversion-or-release decision.

**Table 4. Twelve-Month Implementation Timeline**

| Months | Milestone |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Baseline data analysis; leadership and union engagement; mentor identification |
| 2–4 | Structured 90-day onboarding launched for all new hires |
| 3–5 | Apprenticeship registration filed; federal grant application submitted (before Nov. 14, 2026) |
| 4–6 | Community college cohort partnership signed; first cohort scheduled |
| 6–9 | First apprentice cohort selected and started |
| 9–12 | Youth apprenticeship engagement for next school year; twelve-month program review |

## 6. Answering the Objections

Four objections arise in nearly every member conversation about pipeline investment. They deserve direct answers rather than dismissal, because each contains a legitimate concern.

### 6.1 "We'll train them and the plant down the road will hire them."

Poaching is real, and no answer eliminates it. But three points reframe the risk. First, the census data show the alternative is worse: plants that decline to train do not retain workers — they lose 38 percent of them in year one anyway, and pay $11,800 per loss for the privilege. The trained worker who stays three years and then leaves delivered three years of skilled production; the untrained worker who leaves in month two delivered a net loss. Second, retention data from member apprenticeship programs show that completers are the *least* likely workers to leave — the wage progression, credential investment, mentor relationship, and visible career path bind workers to the sponsoring plant far more effectively than the absence of training ever did. The plants losing workers to neighbors are overwhelmingly those with no development pathway to offer. Third, the Institute is prepared to broker regional non-solicitation understandings among members and, more constructively, cost-sharing arrangements under which a member that hires another member's recent apprenticeship completer reimburses a portion of documented training cost. Several regional trade groups in other sectors operate such arrangements successfully.

### 6.2 "We're too small. A plant under 75 employees can't carry this."

The fixed-cost burden of program administration, related instruction logistics, and mentor coverage genuinely falls harder on small plants. The answer is consortium structure, not exemption. Group apprenticeship sponsorship — in which the Institute or a multi-employer committee holds the registration, administers related instruction, and pools apprentices across plants for classroom cohorts — is an established Department of Labor model that removes nearly all administrative burden from the individual plant. A 60-person foundry sponsoring one or two apprentices through a group sponsor bears the wage and mentor-time costs only, roughly $6,000 to $7,000 net per apprentice per year after offsets. Small plants also gain proportionally more from cohort partnerships, where a two-seat commitment buys access to a screening pipeline the plant could never build alone. The Institute will stand up a group sponsorship for members before the grant window closes; small plants should contact the workforce program office rather than conclude the model excludes them.

### 6.3 "Our collective bargaining agreement's seniority language won't allow it."

Seniority provisions governing job bidding, shift preference, and layoff order can conflict with apprentice wage progressions and rotation requirements — if the program is designed without the union. The consistent experience of unionized member plants is that this objection dissolves when the union is brought in at the design stage rather than presented with a finished program. Apprenticeship is, historically, a labor institution; most internationals actively support registered programs, and a letter of agreement can establish apprentice classifications, progression rates, and rotation rights without opening the full agreement. Incumbent-first apprentice selection (Section 5) further aligns the program with seniority values by making it an advancement path for existing members rather than a side door around them. Plants should treat the CBA as a design parameter, not a barrier — and should expect the negotiation to take one to three months, which is why it appears early in the implementation sequence.

### 6.4 "My supervisors won't release trainees from production."

This is the objection most likely to quietly kill a program that leadership has formally approved. A supervisor measured solely on daily tons shipped is behaving rationally when he keeps the apprentice on the line instead of in rotation or related instruction. The fix is structural, not exhortative. First, adjust supervisor metrics: crews carrying apprentices should have production standards that formally account for training hours, and supervisor evaluations should include retention and training-completion measures alongside output. Second, schedule related instruction and rotations at the start of each quarter and treat them as fixed commitments, like maintenance windows — not as slack to be reclaimed when the schedule tightens. Third, pay the mentor premium and recognize mentoring publicly; supervisors release trainees far more readily to a program their best people are visibly proud to run. Plants that made these three changes report that supervisor resistance faded within two quarters; plants that relied on memos report that it did not.

## 7. Conclusion

The numbers in this paper describe a sector consuming its own future. A 38 percent first-year separation rate, a 14-week fill time, and a workforce one-third of which is within sight of retirement are not sustainable conditions — and the census data make clear that the cost is paid not only in recruiting expense but in scrap, in injuries, and now in commercial standing with customers who demand proof of workforce depth.

The five approaches members have tried are not competitors; they are components. Structured ninety-day onboarding is the cheap, fast, universal foundation. Community college cohorts and youth apprenticeship feed the pipeline. Temp-to-hire, tightly limited, absorbs demand spikes. And registered apprenticeship — the most expensive component at roughly $17,600 net per apprentice — is also the only one that simultaneously retains workers, transfers the knowledge of the retiring generation, and produces the credentialed documentation that supply agreements now require. Under conservative assumptions, a representative 150-person plant running the full layered system recovers its investment within eighteen months.

The objections members raise are legitimate design constraints, and each has a working answer visible somewhere in the member network: retention data and cost-sharing agreements against poaching; group sponsorship for small plants; early union partnership on seniority language; and metric changes that make supervisors owners of the program rather than obstacles to it.

The one element that cannot be redesigned is the calendar. The federal apprenticeship expansion grant closes November 14, 2026, and it covers a large share of the offsets on which the cost model rests. Plants that begin registration paperwork by September will make the window comfortably. The Institute's workforce program office stands ready to assist with registration, grant applications, consortium formation, and college partnerships. The plants that move this year will spend the next decade staffing from a pipeline. The plants that wait will spend it bidding against each other for a workforce that no one built.

## Appendix A. Turnover Census Methodology

The Institute's turnover census is administered annually each January, covering the prior calendar year. Participation is voluntary but incentivized through access to benchmarked reporting; 132 of 140 member plants submitted complete data in the most recent cycle, and no participating plant is identifiable in published aggregates.

**Definitions used throughout this paper:**

- **First-year separation rate.** Separations (voluntary and involuntary, excluding retirement and reduction in force) among employees with less than twelve months of tenure, divided by total hires in the trailing twelve months, calculated by department. Melt and molding figures include melt deck, pouring, molding line, and core room classifications.
- **Cost per separation.** A fully loaded figure comprising recruiting and advertising expense, screening and drug-testing cost, PPE issuance, orientation and training hours (trainee and trainer wages), supervisory administrative time, overtime backfill during the vacancy at the plant's actual premium rates, and estimated throughput loss where the plant tracks it. Plants unable to estimate throughput loss report the remaining components; the $11,800 average therefore understates true cost for most operations.
- **Time to fill.** Calendar weeks from requisition approval to the new hire's first shift, averaged across hourly requisitions.
- **Scrap rate.** Scrapped castings as a percentage of gross tons poured, as reported in the plant's own quality system. Quartile comparisons control for casting process (green sand, nobake, investment, permanent mold) so that the top-versus-bottom quartile differential reported in Section 2.3 compares like processes.
- **Recordable injury rate.** OSHA recordable cases per 200,000 hours worked, per the plant's OSHA 300A log.

Quartile assignments are recalculated annually. A plant's quartile position has proven persistent: 71 percent of plants in the top turnover quartile in 2021 remained in the top two quartiles in the most recent cycle, which is why the Institute treats turnover as a structural characteristic of a plant's management system rather than a transient labor-market condition.

## Appendix D. Glossary of Program Terms

**Group sponsorship.** A registered apprenticeship structure in which a trade association, joint committee, or intermediary holds the program registration and performs administration on behalf of multiple participating employers, each of which employs and pays its own apprentices.

**Journeyworker.** A worker who has completed a registered apprenticeship or attained equivalent documented competency in the occupation, qualified to supervise apprentices' on-the-job learning.

**Letter of agreement (LOA).** A negotiated supplement to a collective bargaining agreement addressing a defined subject — here, apprentice classifications, wage progression, and rotation rights — without reopening the full agreement.

**Related instruction (RI).** The classroom or lab component of a registered apprenticeship, typically 144 hours per year, delivered by a community college, online provider, or in-house instructors under the program standards.

**Time to fill.** See Appendix A.

**Youth apprenticeship.** A state-administered program combining part-time paid employment with school-based technical instruction for high school students, distinct from (and often a feeder into) adult registered apprenticeship.
