# Executive Summary: Northside Trade Collective — Workforce Development Initiative

**Prepared by Hana Kobayashi, Evaluation Director | October 24, 2025 | For the November 6 Donor Advisory Council Meeting**

**Finding and Recommendation:** The Northside Trade Collective workforce program is the strongest performer in the Opportunity Portfolio and the clearest candidate for renewal. I recommend the council approve a second three-year commitment at the current funding level of $1.8 million annually, with an option to add $200,000 in year two contingent on the Collective's plan to expand employer partnerships beyond its current base of eleven firms.

Three years ago, First Coast Community Trust committed $1.8 million annually to Northside Trade Collective to build a sector-based workforce training pipeline serving unemployed and underemployed adults in Jacksonville's Northside neighborhoods. The grant funded credentialing programs in logistics, healthcare support, and skilled trades, along with wraparound case management, transportation stipends, and employer-matching services. The theory of change was straightforward: short-cycle training tied directly to local employer demand would move participants from subsistence wages into family-sustaining jobs faster than traditional workforce development models.

The results support that theory. The program enrolled 480 adults over the grant period, placed 71 percent of them into jobs, and moved median hourly wages from $13.10 to $21.40 — a 63 percent increase that, annualized for a full-time worker, represents roughly $17,300 in additional yearly income per placed participant. Retention data, often the weak point in workforce programs, is also favorable: 64 percent of placed participants remained employed at the twelve-month mark, a figure that compares well to regional workforce board benchmarks, which typically show one-year retention in the 50 to 55 percent range for similar populations.

Program leadership attributes the retention rate to two design choices worth noting for the council's understanding of what the money bought. First, the Collective embedded job coaches who maintain contact with participants for a full year post-placement, intervening early when participants report conflicts with employers or barriers such as childcare or transportation. Second, the Collective built its curriculum in direct consultation with the eleven employer partners who ultimately hired most graduates, which reduced the mismatch between what was taught and what employers actually needed on day one. This is a program that has done what workforce development grants are supposed to do, at a cost per placement — roughly $5,300 per participant enrolled, or about $7,500 per participant placed — that compares favorably to peer programs in the region.

The case for renewal is not without caveats the council should weigh. Employer concentration is a real risk: three of the eleven partner firms accounted for 54 percent of all placements, meaning the program's outcomes are more exposed to individual employer hiring cycles than the aggregate numbers suggest. Program leadership has acknowledged this and has proposed using new-year funding specifically to diversify the employer base, targeting an additional six to eight partners in healthcare support and advanced manufacturing. The council may wish to condition any funding increase on evidence of progress toward that diversification rather than treating it as automatic.

It is also worth noting that the 29 percent of enrollees who were not placed, and the 36 percent of placed participants who did not remain employed at twelve months, together represent real people for whom the program did not fully deliver. Northside Trade Collective's own data suggests the most common reasons for non-placement were transportation barriers and childcare instability rather than lack of training completion, which argues for continued investment in wraparound supports rather than a pullback.

**What the Foundation Owes the Grantee and Its Participants:** Because this is a renewal recommendation rather than a wind-down, the foundation's principal obligation is timeliness and clarity. Northside Trade Collective has enrollment and hiring cycles tied to employer needs, and a funding gap between the current grant's expiration and a new commitment's start would force the Collective to pause intake, which in turn would cost participants access to jobs during the very hiring windows the program is designed around. I recommend that, if the council approves renewal, the foundation commit to executing the new grant agreement no later than January 15, 2026, so the Collective can maintain continuous enrollment. If the council instead wishes to renew at a reduced level or with different terms, the foundation owes the Collective at least 60 days' notice before any change takes effect, given that the organization has already made hiring and curriculum commitments premised on continuation at current funding. Finally, the foundation should be transparent with the Collective about the employer-diversification condition, if the council adopts it, so that the organization can begin that work immediately rather than learning of the expectation after the fact.

# Executive Summary: Legal Aid of Northeast Florida — Eviction Prevention Legal Program

**Prepared by Hana Kobayashi, Evaluation Director | October 24, 2025 | For the November 6 Donor Advisory Council Meeting**

**Finding and Recommendation:** The eviction prevention legal program has fallen well short of its service targets and has lost half its litigation capacity during the grant period. I recommend the council approve a one-year bridge grant of $1.1 million — half the current annual commitment — conditioned on a staffing recovery plan and a caseload reduction strategy, rather than either a full three-year renewal or an immediate termination.

First Coast Community Trust committed $2.2 million annually to fund a team of four staff attorneys, three paralegals, and a court-based navigator to represent low-income tenants facing eviction in Duval County courts, with a stated goal of reaching 1,500 households annually through direct representation, brief advice, and emergency rental assistance navigation. The program has served 610 households against that 1,500 target — a 41 percent achievement rate — and currently carries a backlog of 340 open matters, meaning that roughly 56 percent of the households it has managed to serve remain in active, unresolved cases rather than closed representations.

The proximate cause is staffing. The program has lost two of its four staff attorneys in the past nine months, one to a public defender's office and one to private practice, and has been unable to fill either position despite an active search. This is not a minor administrative hiccup: attorney capacity is the rate-limiting factor in a legal services program, and a 50 percent reduction in attorney staffing without a corresponding reduction in caseload assignment has produced exactly the backlog the data now shows. Program leadership reports that remaining attorneys are each carrying active caseloads 40 to 60 percent above the organization's own stated sustainable maximum, which raises both a quality-of-representation concern and a staff retention risk — the very risk that produced the initial departures.

It would be a mistake, however, to read these numbers as evidence that the program's model is unsound. Where representation has been completed, outcomes remain strong: 78 percent of fully represented tenants avoided eviction judgment, consistent with pre-grant baseline performance and with national data showing that legal representation is one of the most effective known interventions against eviction. The problem is capacity and throughput, not efficacy. The organization has also faced an external headwind the council should weigh: regional eviction filings rose an estimated 22 percent over the grant period amid rising rents, meaning the 1,500-household target was set against a demand baseline that has since shifted upward.

Given this, an outright non-renewal would end representation for hundreds of households currently in the pipeline and would eliminate the only dedicated eviction defense capacity of its kind in the county, while a full-strength three-year renewal without addressing the staffing and caseload problem would likely reproduce the same underperformance. A one-year bridge grant, sized to fund three attorneys rather than four and paired with an explicit hiring and caseload-cap requirement, allows the council to preserve capacity while insisting on a credible recovery plan before committing further multi-year funds. I recommend the bridge grant carry two conditions: that the organization fill its vacant attorney position within 120 days of the grant's start, and that it report caseload-per-attorney figures to the foundation quarterly rather than annually, so that Hana Kobayashi's office can flag capacity strain before it produces another backlog.

**What the Foundation Owes the Grantee and Its Participants:** The 340 households currently in the backlog did not choose to be caught in a funding transition, and the foundation has an obligation to ensure that a change in grant terms does not translate into abandoned representation mid-case. I recommend the foundation require, as a condition of any award, that the organization present a case-transition plan showing how existing open matters will be staffed through resolution regardless of the funding outcome. If the council declines even the bridge grant, the foundation owes the organization no less than 90 days' notice and should work with Legal Aid of Northeast Florida to identify referral partners — likely the statewide legal aid network or pro bono panels coordinated through the local bar — capable of absorbing active cases, since courts do not pause eviction dockets for grantee funding cycles. The foundation should also be candid with the anchor funders and court partners who have come to rely on this program's presence in Duval County housing court that its scale may be reduced in the near term, so that other legal and social service actors can plan accordingly.

# Executive Summary: Duval County School-Based Youth Mental Health Initiative

**Prepared by Hana Kobayashi, Evaluation Director | October 24, 2025 | For the November 6 Donor Advisory Council Meeting**

**Finding and Recommendation:** The school-based youth mental health initiative cannot yet demonstrate outcomes because the data-sharing agreement that would allow the foundation to track referral-to-treatment results was not executed until March 2025 — roughly two-thirds of the way through the grant period. I recommend the council approve a one-year, no-cost extension of the existing $1.5 million grant rather than a decision on renewal, so that a full year of data collected under the now-functioning data agreement can inform an evidence-based decision next November.

The grant funded a partnership among three Duval County school districts, First Coast Community Trust, and a consortium of community mental health providers to place licensed clinicians in schools, screen students for anxiety, depression, and trauma symptoms, and refer identified students to treatment. Over the grant period, the initiative completed 1,140 screenings across the three districts. Of students screened positive and referred for services, only 38 percent completed a first appointment — a conversion rate substantially below the 65 to 70 percent range that comparable school-based mental health referral programs typically report, and well below what the program's own logic model assumed when it was funded.

The council should understand why this figure exists before drawing conclusions from it. The initiative's design depended on a data-sharing agreement among the three districts, the foundation, and the treatment providers to allow referral tracking, appointment scheduling coordination, and eventual outcome measurement across systems that do not otherwise share student information. That agreement, which program leadership originally targeted for execution at grant launch, was not finalized until March 2025 due to differing district legal review processes and concerns raised by one district's general counsel about student data privacy under state law. For roughly two of the grant's three years, referrals were tracked manually and inconsistently across districts, and appointment-completion follow-up was, by the program's own admission, largely informal. The 38 percent conversion figure is almost certainly an undercount produced by incomplete tracking rather than a true measure of program failure, but it is also true that the foundation cannot currently distinguish between those two explanations with confidence, because the tool built to make that distinction has only been operating since spring.

This matters for the funding decision because a program that cannot yet demonstrate outcomes is different from a program that has demonstrated poor outcomes, and the council's usual renewal standard — sustained evidence of results — cannot be fairly applied here yet. The screenings themselves appear to have been conducted competently and at meaningful scale: 1,140 completed screenings across three districts, using a validated instrument, is a real accomplishment and reflects the money buying what it was intended to buy at the front end of the service pipeline. The open question is what happens after screening, and that is precisely the question the data-sharing agreement now allows the program to answer, going forward, in a way it could not before March.

I recommend against both an early full renewal, which would commit the foundation to three more years without evidence the referral pipeline works, and against non-renewal, which would end the program before the one tool capable of showing whether it works has had a chance to operate for a full cycle. A one-year extension at the current $1.5 million level, with a specific requirement that program leadership report referral-to-appointment conversion data quarterly beginning January 2026, gives the council a clean, comparable dataset by next fall's renewal cycle.

**What the Foundation Owes the Grantee and Its Participants:** The three partner districts and the treatment consortium invested significant institutional effort in finalizing the data-sharing agreement, and ending the grant now — before that investment can produce usable information — would waste both their effort and the foundation's own evaluation resources. The foundation owes the districts a clear, prompt communication that the extension is being granted specifically to allow the new data infrastructure to prove itself, so that district staff understand this is a measurement pause rather than a signal of dissatisfaction with their participation. More directly, the foundation owes the students currently in the referral pipeline continuity: pausing or ending school-based clinician placements mid-year would strand students mid-treatment or mid-referral in a way that is especially harmful for adolescents dealing with mental health concerns, for whom continuity of care is itself a clinical consideration. If the council opts not to extend, I recommend at minimum funding through the end of the current school year so that no student's care is interrupted mid-term.

# Executive Summary: Jacksonville Housing Partners — Housing Predevelopment Fund

**Prepared by Hana Kobayashi, Evaluation Director | October 24, 2025 | For the November 6 Donor Advisory Council Meeting**

**Finding and Recommendation:** The housing predevelopment fund has drawn 63 percent more capital than the foundation committed, has permitted only one of three planned sites, and has caused an anchor donor to place a $3 million pledge on hold pending this review. I recommend the council decline to renew the predevelopment fund in its current structure, instead converting the relationship to a single-site completion grant capped at the amount already drawn, and require Chief Program Officer Omar Zaidi's team to present a revised capital plan for the remaining two sites before any further foundation funds are committed.

The foundation committed $4 million to Jacksonville Housing Partners to cover predevelopment costs — site acquisition options, architectural and engineering design, environmental review, and permitting expenses — for three affordable housing sites intended to produce a combined 180 units of workforce and supportive housing. Predevelopment funding of this kind is inherently a bridge: it is meant to carry a project from concept to a permitted, financeable state, at which point construction financing and tax credit or bond financing typically take over. The program has instead drawn $6.5 million against the $4 million commitment, a $2.5 million overdraw that program staff attribute to unanticipated environmental remediation costs at two sites and to permitting delays that extended predevelopment carrying costs well beyond the original 18-month projection.

Only one of the three sites has received its permit. The other two remain in active predevelopment more than two years into a process the original grant assumed would take 18 to 24 months for all three sites combined. Program leadership, under Omar Zaidi's direction, has characterized the delays as typical for affordable housing development in the current regulatory and cost environment, and there is some truth to that: construction and permitting timelines across the sector have lengthened industry-wide amid rising material costs and municipal review backlogs. But the foundation is not simply a passive observer of sector-wide trends here; it is a funder that authorized a specific dollar commitment against a specific set of milestones, and the milestones have not been met while the dollars committed have already been exceeded.

The overdraw has direct external consequences the council must weigh. An anchor donor who had pledged $3 million toward the second phase of this housing work has placed that pledge on hold explicitly pending the outcome of this review, according to correspondence Hana Kobayashi's office has reviewed. That donor's stated concern is not the housing mission but the fund's financial discipline — specifically, whether a $4 million commitment that has already become a $6.5 million draw is evidence of a predevelopment process that will responsibly manage a much larger construction-phase commitment. This concern deserves to be taken seriously independent of the merits of the housing itself, because the foundation's credibility with other major donors is implicated by how it handles an overdraw of this magnitude.

I want to be clear that this recommendation is not a judgment that the housing itself is a bad investment. One permitted site with 60 planned units represents real progress toward addressing Jacksonville's affordable housing shortage, and abandoning that site at this stage, after most predevelopment costs have already been sunk, would be a poor use of the capital already committed. The recommendation is narrower: the open-ended, three-site predevelopment structure has not demonstrated the financial discipline the foundation needs before extending further capital, and the two unpermitted sites should not receive additional foundation predevelopment funds until Jacksonville Housing Partners presents a revised, site-specific capital plan with hard cost ceilings and a construction-financing pathway that does not depend on the foundation absorbing further overdraws.

**What the Foundation Owes the Grantee and Its Participants:** Jacksonville Housing Partners has already committed staff time, contractor relationships, and reputational capital with municipal permitting offices to all three sites, and the foundation's obligation is to be precise about what continues and what does not. I recommend the foundation commit, in writing, to funding the permitted site through to construction-ready status, since walking away from a site with a permit in hand after funding its predevelopment would waste the sunk investment and would harm the future housing residents who are the ultimate beneficiaries of this grant. For the two unpermitted sites, the foundation owes Jacksonville Housing Partners a clear and specific list of what a revised capital plan must contain in order to be considered for further funding, rather than a vague instruction to "do better," so that Omar Zaidi's team can respond concretely rather than guessing at the council's concerns. Finally, the foundation owes the anchor donor a direct, timely communication of the council's decision and reasoning, since that donor's $3 million pledge is contingent on confidence in this review; delaying that communication past the November meeting would extend uncertainty for a donor relationship the foundation cannot afford to mishandle.

The four programs, taken together, illustrate a pattern the council should weigh as it enters the November 6 meeting: financial discipline and program impact do not move in lockstep, and a strict up-or-down renewal vote applied uniformly across the portfolio would produce worse outcomes than a differentiated response calibrated to what each program's evidence actually shows. Northside Trade Collective has earned continuation on the strength of results. The eviction prevention program has earned continuation on the strength of mission and legal necessity, but not at full scale until its staffing crisis is addressed. The youth mental health initiative has earned patience, not yet a verdict, because the instrument needed to render a verdict has only recently begun working. The housing predevelopment fund has earned a hard stop on new commitments, though not abandonment of the one asset it has actually finished.

There is also a cross-cutting resource question the council will need to resolve at the same meeting, since the four recommendations do not draw equally on the $12 million Opportunity Portfolio envelope. A full renewal of Northside Trade Collective at $1.8 million plus the proposed $200,000 expansion option, a one-year bridge to Legal Aid of Northeast Florida at $1.1 million rather than $2.2 million, a one-year extension of the youth mental health initiative at the current $1.5 million, and a capped completion grant to Jacksonville Housing Partners sized to the single permitted site rather than the original $4 million three-site commitment together total roughly $6.6 million to $7.1 million in year-one obligations, depending on where the council sets the housing completion cap. That is a meaningful reduction from the current $12 million annual run rate of the Opportunity Portfolio, and Treasurer Etheridge may reasonably ask what happens to the difference.

I want to flag three ways the council could use that freed capacity, without recommending among them, since this is a policy choice for the council and President Delacroix-Baptiste rather than an evaluation finding. First, the foundation could hold the difference in reserve against the housing predevelopment fund's likely need for additional completion-stage capital once Jacksonville Housing Partners returns with a revised plan for its two unpermitted sites; given the pattern of cost overruns already observed, it would be prudent to assume the eventual ask will exceed the original $4 million baseline. Second, the council could use freed capacity to fund the quarterly monitoring the eviction prevention and youth mental health conditions now require; both conditions I have recommended above call for more frequent reporting than the portfolio has previously required, and Hana Kobayashi's office will need modest additional evaluation capacity to process that reporting on a quarterly rather than annual cycle. Third, the council could treat the reduction as an opportunity to open a competitive process for new Opportunity Portfolio entrants in 2027, which would be consistent with the original three-year, time-limited design of the portfolio rather than allowing it to calcify into four permanent grantee relationships.

None of these three options is mutually exclusive with the others, and the council may choose to split freed capacity among them. What matters for the November 6 vote itself is narrower: each of the four recommendations above can be adopted independently of how the council resolves the reserve question, since each is sized and conditioned on the merits of its own program rather than on the total portfolio envelope. I recommend the council take four separate votes rather than one omnibus vote on the Opportunity Portfolio as a whole, both because the evidentiary basis for each program differs so substantially and because a single combined vote would make it difficult for any council member to register support for, say, the workforce program's renewal while registering concern about the housing fund's overdraw.

Finally, a note on timing that affects all four recommendations equally. Because the current three-year commitments close December 31, 2025, and the council does not meet again before that date, any recommendation the council adopts on November 6 will need to be executed as a signed grant agreement, extension letter, or wind-down notice within a roughly seven-week window. I have asked Yvonne Delacroix-Baptiste's office to have draft agreements for all four scenarios — full renewal, bridge grant, no-cost extension, and capped completion grant — prepared in advance of the meeting so that whichever combination the council selects can move to execution immediately rather than requiring a second drafting cycle that would risk missing the December 31 closing date. Given the eviction prevention program's backlog and the youth mental health initiative's mid-school-year student caseload, a gap between the council's vote and executed paperwork carries real cost to participants in a way that a simple administrative delay would not for, say, a program between enrollment cycles. I would ask the council to treat the seven-week execution window as a binding constraint on its deliberations on November 6, not merely a scheduling preference.

A brief note on methodology, since several council members have asked in prior cycles how these figures were compiled and I want the basis for tonight's recommendations to be transparent rather than taken on faith. Hana Kobayashi's office conducted structured site visits at all four grantees between August and October 2025, each lasting one to two full days and including document review, staff interviews, and, where applicable, direct observation of service delivery — a screening session at one of the three mental health initiative's district sites, an intake session at Northside Trade Collective, a housing court observation with Legal Aid of Northeast Florida, and a walkthrough of the permitted housing site with Jacksonville Housing Partners' development team. Financial figures were reconciled against each grantee's own accounting records rather than taken solely from grant reports, which is how the $6.5 million draw against Jacksonville Housing Partners' $4 million commitment was identified; the organization's own quarterly reports to the foundation had not flagged the overdraw as clearly as the underlying ledgers showed it, a discrepancy I raised directly with Omar Zaidi's finance staff in September and which they have since acknowledged was a reporting gap rather than a concealment.

Outcome data for Northside Trade Collective — placement rates, wage figures, and twelve-month retention — were verified against unemployment insurance wage records where participants consented to data matching, covering 61 percent of the 480 enrolled participants; the remaining figures rely on grantee-reported employer verification, which is a somewhat weaker evidentiary standard and is noted as such in the full evaluation file available to any council member who requests it. The eviction prevention program's household-served and backlog figures come directly from the organization's case management system, to which Hana Kobayashi's office was given read access in September as part of this review; the 78 percent avoided-judgment rate for fully represented tenants is drawn from Duval County court disposition records matched to the organization's client list, and so carries higher confidence than the placement-verification figures from the workforce program. The youth mental health initiative's screening count of 1,140 is a hard figure drawn from the validated instrument's own administration log, while the 38 percent referral-to-appointment conversion figure is, as discussed above, a soft figure that predates the March 2025 data-sharing agreement and should be read with the caveats already stated.

I raise these distinctions not to hedge the recommendations above but to give the council an honest account of where the evidence is strong and where it is still developing, since the four programs sit at different points on that spectrum and the recommendations are calibrated accordingly. A full data appendix, including site visit notes, financial reconciliation worksheets, and the underlying wage and court-record matches, has been provided to Treasurer Etheridge's office and is available to any council member in advance of the November 6 meeting on request to Hana Kobayashi directly.

I am available to walk through any of the four programs in more detail before the meeting, and I will be present on November 6 to answer questions during deliberation. My recommendation, in summary form for the record: renew Northside Trade Collective at $1.8 million with a conditional $200,000 expansion option; approve a one-year, $1.1 million bridge grant to Legal Aid of Northeast Florida conditioned on attorney hiring and caseload reporting; extend the youth mental health initiative one year at the current $1.5 million with quarterly conversion-rate reporting beginning January 2026; and convert the Jacksonville Housing Partners relationship to a capped completion grant for the single permitted site, with no further predevelopment funding for the remaining two sites absent a revised capital plan meeting the standards described above.
