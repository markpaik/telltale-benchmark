# Delta Rise Academies: Redesigning a Literacy Initiative Under Pressure

## The Network and Its Situation

Delta Rise Academies operates five charter schools in Jackson, Mississippi, enrolling roughly 2,700 students across grades K through 8. The network serves a population that is overwhelmingly low-income: 88 percent of students qualify for free or reduced-price meals, a figure that places Delta Rise among the highest-poverty charter networks in the state. Jackson itself has struggled for more than a decade with population loss, disinvestment, and a public school system that has cycled through state takeovers and superintendent turnover. Delta Rise was founded, in part, as an answer to that instability — a promise to families that a different kind of school, with more autonomy and more accountability, could produce different results.

By the spring of 2023, the promise was not being kept, at least not in the subject that matters most for long-term trajectory: early reading. Third grade is the hinge point in Mississippi's education system because of the state's reading gate, a policy that requires students to demonstrate a minimum level of proficiency on the state reading assessment before advancing to fourth grade. In spring 2023, only 24 percent of Delta Rise third graders scored proficient in reading. Nineteen percent of third graders were retained under the gate — held back a full year because they had not cleared the bar. Both figures were symptoms of the same underlying problem, but the retention number was the one that showed up in every family's kitchen conversation, every school board meeting, and every local news segment about charter performance in Jackson.

Underneath the achievement numbers was a staffing crisis. Teacher turnover across the network's five schools reached 41 percent for the 2022–23 school year. In practice, this meant that fewer than six in ten teachers who started the year were still in their classrooms the following fall. For a literacy strategy in particular, this kind of turnover is corrosive: expertise in phonics instruction, small-group reading routines, and diagnostic assessment does not transfer automatically to a new hire walking in the door in August. Each wave of turnover reset the clock on classroom-level instructional capacity, even as network leaders tried to build systems that were supposed to be durable.

Executive Director Camille Bourdeaux had been in her role for four years by 2023, having come to Delta Rise from a regional nonprofit focused on school turnaround work in the Delta region of Mississippi. Chief Academic Officer Anand Krishnamurthy had joined eighteen months earlier, recruited specifically because of his background in structured literacy implementation at a charter network in Memphis. The two of them, along with the network's board, had concluded that incremental tweaks to curriculum and professional development were not going to close a gap of this size. They wanted something structural: a bet large enough to change the trajectory of the network's youngest readers before another cohort passed through third grade without the skills to read a passage independently.

## The Reasoning Behind the Original Design

The design that emerged in early 2023 rested on three pillars, and each one reflected a specific diagnosis of why children in Delta Rise classrooms were not learning to read.

The first pillar was curriculum. Krishnamurthy had reviewed the network's existing balanced literacy materials and concluded that they underserved students who needed explicit, systematic phonics instruction — which described the great majority of Delta Rise's population, given the low proficiency baseline. The network committed to adopting a structured literacy curriculum, aligned with the science of reading research that had gained traction nationally over the preceding several years. This was not a marginal materials swap; it meant retraining every K-3 teacher in the network on a new instructional sequence, new decodable texts, and new formative assessment routines tied to phonemic awareness and decoding skill rather than only comprehension.

The second pillar was time. Krishnamurthy and Bourdeaux believed — correctly, based on the broader research literature on early literacy — that inconsistent or interrupted reading instruction was part of what was holding proficiency down. They mandated a protected 90-minute literacy block at every grade K through 3, across all five schools, during which no assemblies, no pull-outs for other services, and no schedule interruptions would be permitted. The theory was straightforward: teachers needed enough uninterrupted time to run a full instructional cycle — whole group phonics instruction, guided reading in small groups, and independent practice — and if the block kept getting cannibalized by the ordinary chaos of school scheduling, no curriculum change would matter.

The third pillar was coaching capacity, and this is where the network decided it needed an outside partner rather than trying to build instructional coaching capacity from scratch. Delta Rise contracted with Meridian Literacy Partners, a literacy-focused consulting organization with a track record of supporting curriculum implementation in other Southern charter networks. Meridian's role was to provide coaches who would conduct classroom observations, model lessons, and run professional development sessions, with the explicit goal of driving fidelity to the new curriculum and protecting the integrity of the 90-minute block.

The full initiative was budgeted at $2.9 million over three years — curriculum materials and licenses, Meridian's contract, substitute coverage for training days, and assessment materials. To fund it, Bourdeaux and Krishnamurthy secured a $1.8 million grant from the Bienville Family Foundation, a Jackson-based funder with a long history of supporting local education initiatives. The foundation's program officer, Greta Lindholm, would be the primary point of contact for reporting and oversight, and the grant was structured in tranches tied to implementation benchmarks — a structure that would later prove consequential.

At the time, the design looked sound on paper. It addressed curriculum, time, and capacity simultaneously, which is what most of the literature on literacy reform recommends. Bourdeaux later described the planning process as "the most rigorous design work we'd done as a network," pointing to months of research trips to other Meridian-supported networks and consultation with reading researchers. What the design did not fully account for was the network's existing operational fragility — the very turnover and instability that had created the achievement gap in the first place — and how that fragility would interact with an ambitious, network-wide rollout.

## Year One: What the Rollout Revealed

The 2023–24 school year began with training sessions in August, curriculum rollout across all five schools simultaneously, and the new 90-minute block written into every K-3 master schedule. By December, it was clear that the implementation was not resembling the design.

The most immediate constraint was coaching capacity. Meridian had staffed the contract with a coach-to-teacher ratio of one coach for every 42 teachers. Given that effective literacy coaching cycles typically involve weekly or biweekly classroom visits, brief debriefs, and follow-up co-planning, a ratio of 1:42 made anything resembling sustained, individualized coaching mathematically impossible. Each coach was spread across multiple schools, and teachers reported going four to six weeks between coaching visits rather than receiving the frequent touchpoints the design assumed. Krishnamurthy would later acknowledge that the network had underestimated how many coaches an implementation of this scale required, and that Meridian's proposed staffing ratio — presented as standard for their model — had not been stress-tested against Delta Rise's specific school calendars, absence rates, and existing instructional coaching infrastructure, which was thin to begin with.

The consequence showed up directly in fidelity data. Classroom observations conducted during the fall and winter found only 38 percent of literacy blocks meeting fidelity criteria for the new curriculum — meaning that in nearly two out of every three observed classrooms, teachers were either not using the curriculum as designed, skipping components, or reverting to prior practices under the pressure of the school day. Without frequent coaching support, teachers who encountered friction with the new materials had nowhere to turn for real-time problem-solving, and many defaulted to familiar routines.

The second pillar — the protected 90-minute block — fared no better. Of the five schools in the network, only two actually protected the block as designed throughout the year. At the other three, the block was routinely interrupted by assemblies, pulled for intervention scheduling, shortened to accommodate testing windows, or simply not enforced by school leadership, who had other operational fires to manage and had not been given clear accountability structures to hold the line. This mattered enormously, because the curriculum was designed around the assumption of a full, uninterrupted 90 minutes; a block that was regularly cut to 60 or 70 minutes meant teachers were skipping components — usually the small-group guided reading rotations, which were the piece most directly tied to differentiated instruction for struggling readers.

The combined effect of thin coaching and inconsistent block protection showed up in the midyear assessment data: a gain of just three percentage points in proficiency from fall to winter benchmarks, far below what the network's theory of action had projected for a year with this level of investment. Given that the network had committed nearly a million dollars to year one implementation alone, a three-point midyear gain read, to board members and to the funder, as a program that was not working.

The human cost accumulated alongside the data. Teachers described the year as exhausting: new curriculum, new pacing guides, new assessment routines, minimal coaching support, and an accountability push around the 90-minute block that, at the three schools where it was not truly protected, created friction between teachers and administrators who felt caught between competing mandates. In December 2023, 22 teachers resigned from the network — a figure that, layered onto the network's already troubling 41 percent annual turnover rate, was read internally as a sign that the initiative, rather than stabilizing the network's instructional core, was accelerating its erosion. Exit interviews conducted by the network's HR team cited the volume of new initiatives, lack of coaching support, and a sense of being set up to fail on fidelity metrics they had little control over, given scheduling issues outside their authority.

Word of the implementation struggles reached the Bienville Family Foundation well before the year-end report was formally due. Greta Lindholm had been receiving quarterly updates from Krishnamurthy's team throughout the fall, and by January 2024 she requested an in-person meeting with Bourdeaux and Krishnamurthy to review the fidelity and assessment data directly. In February 2024, Lindholm informed the network that the foundation was placing the second tranche of funding — $600,000 — on hold pending evidence of a credible course correction. This was not a full withdrawal of support, but it was an unambiguous signal: the foundation had expected implementation benchmarks that had not been met, and continued funding was now conditional rather than assumed.

For Bourdeaux, the combination of weak fidelity data, minimal assessment gains, a wave of December resignations, and a funder publicly withholding money constituted a full crisis moment for the initiative — one that could not be managed with minor adjustments. She has since described February 2024 as "the month we had to decide whether to kill the whole thing or actually rebuild it," and the decision the network made was to rebuild.

## The Redesign

Bourdeaux and Krishnamurthy spent February and March 2024 in what they later called a diagnostic sprint — reviewing coaching logs, block-protection data by school, teacher exit interview themes, and assessment results school by school rather than only at the network level. What emerged was a clearer picture than the network had had at the outset: the initiative's failure was not a failure of curriculum choice, but a failure of implementation capacity and enforcement, concentrated unevenly across the five schools. Two schools had protected the block and shown modestly better fidelity; three had not, and were dragging down network-wide averages.

The redesign that followed involved four major changes, each targeted at a specific point of failure identified in year one.

First, Bourdeaux narrowed the initiative's scope from all five schools to three schools, choosing to concentrate resources rather than continuing to spread a thin coaching model across the entire network. This was a difficult decision, because it meant explicitly deprioritizing full implementation at two schools for a period of time — a decision that had to be communicated carefully to families and staff at those campuses, with the framing that the narrowing was a route back to full network implementation rather than an abandonment of those schools.

Second, the network changed its coaching staffing model entirely. Rather than continuing to contract coaching capacity through Meridian at a ratio the network could not sustain, Delta Rise hired four school-based literacy coaches directly onto its own payroll, embedded within the three focus schools rather than shared across the network. This shift addressed the coaching ratio problem at its root: school-based coaches could build relationships with teachers over time, be present daily rather than visiting on a rotating multi-school schedule, and respond to classroom needs in real time rather than during a scheduled visit six weeks out. Meridian's role shifted to a lighter-touch training and quality-assurance function rather than the primary delivery mechanism for coaching.

Third, the network cut the number of required formative assessments from six per year to three. This change responded directly to teacher feedback that assessment administration itself was consuming instructional time and adding to teacher burden without providing proportionate diagnostic value — six assessment windows meant significant time lost to test administration, scoring, and data entry, time that competed directly with the newly protected instructional block the network was trying to preserve. Reducing to three assessments (fall, winter, spring) freed up instructional time while still providing the network with meaningful trend data.

Fourth, and perhaps most consequentially, Bourdeaux made block protection an explicit, weighted condition in principal performance evaluations. In year one, protecting the 90-minute block had been a stated expectation without a clear enforcement mechanism, and the result was that three of five principals had allowed the block to erode under competing operational pressures. By tying block protection directly to principal evaluation criteria — with data pulled from schedule audits and coach observation logs — the network created an accountability structure that gave principals a direct incentive to defend the block against competing demands, rather than treating it as one more expectation among many that could be quietly deprioritized.

The redesigned model went into effect for the 2024–25 school year at three schools: Rosewood Elementary, led by Principal Shauna Delgadillo, became something of a flagship site for the new approach, with Literacy Coach Lead Ousmane Diallo coordinating the coaching model across the three focus schools. Delgadillo and Diallo worked closely together to establish weekly coaching cycles, protected planning time for teachers to debrief with coaches, and a school-level schedule-audit process to catch block interruptions before they became patterns. Their approach at Rosewood became something of a reference model that the other two focus schools adapted to their own contexts.

Krishnamurthy has since described the redesign's underlying logic simply: "We had tried to do everything everywhere at once, and we didn't have the capacity to do that well anywhere. Once we narrowed the footprint and put the coaching close to the classroom instead of spread across the network, the whole thing started to behave the way we'd originally designed it to behave." Bourdeaux, for her part, emphasized the accountability piece: "The curriculum was never the problem. The problem was that we asked people to protect ninety minutes and then didn't make it anyone's job to make sure that happened."

## Results at Both Sets of Schools

By spring 2025, the data showed a clear divergence between the three focus schools and the two schools that had been narrowed out of the intensive redesign.

At the three focus schools, third-grade reading proficiency reached 43 percent, up from the network-wide baseline of 24 percent in spring 2023 — nearly doubling proficiency in two years, with the bulk of the gain occurring during the redesigned second year rather than the original first year. This was a substantial improvement by any standard, particularly for a student population where 88 percent qualify for free or reduced-price meals and where the network had spent its first implementation year essentially treading water.

At the two schools that had not received the intensive coaching redesign, proficiency also improved, but far more modestly — moving from 24 percent to 29 percent over the same two-year period. This five-point gain likely reflected some spillover benefit from the curriculum adoption and general network-wide attention to literacy, but it was a fraction of the gain seen at the three focus schools, offering a rough natural comparison of what the redesigned coaching and accountability model contributed above and beyond the curriculum change alone.

Retention under the state reading gate, which had stood at 19 percent network-wide in spring 2023, fell to 8 percent by spring 2025 — more than halving the number of third graders held back for insufficient reading proficiency. This is arguably the most consequential number in the case, since gate retention carries direct, immediate consequences for individual students and families, unlike proficiency percentages, which are more abstract as a lived experience.

Teacher turnover, which had been 41 percent in the year preceding the initiative and had worsened acutely in December 2023 with the resignation wave, fell to 26 percent network-wide by the 2024–25 school year. This decline suggests that the redesign's changes — particularly the shift to embedded, supportive school-based coaching and the reduction in assessment burden — addressed some of the working-condition stressors that had been driving departures, even as the network maintained its instructional ambitions.

Total spending on the initiative reached $3.6 million by spring 2025, exceeding the original three-year budget of $2.9 million by $700,000, or roughly 24 percent over the initial plan. This overage reflected both the cost of hiring four school-based coaches as permanent staff rather than relying solely on the original contracted model, and the extended timeline the network needed to reach the results it was seeking. The $1.8 million Bienville Family Foundation grant, including the previously withheld $600,000 tranche, was ultimately released in full after Lindholm and the foundation reviewed the redesigned model's early fidelity and assessment data during the fall of 2024 and concluded that the network had demonstrated a credible course correction.

The gap between the three focus schools and the two non-focus schools raises a question the network is still actively working through: what to do about the two schools that lagged. Bourdeaux has indicated that expanding the school-based coaching model to the remaining two schools is the network's priority for the 2025–26 school year, using the now-proven Rosewood model as the template, though she has also acknowledged that doing so will require additional coach hires and additional budget beyond the $3.6 million already spent — meaning the true multi-year cost of bringing all five schools to the level achieved at the three focus schools will likely exceed the original $2.9 million budget by a considerably wider margin than the current $700,000 overage suggests.

## What the Network Would Do Differently

In retrospect, both Bourdeaux and Krishnamurthy point to a common thread across the specific mistakes of year one: the initial design confused ambition with readiness. The three-pillar strategy — curriculum, protected time, coaching — was not wrong in its content, but the network rolled it out across all five schools simultaneously without first establishing whether it had the coaching capacity or the accountability infrastructure to sustain that scale. A staffing ratio of one coach to 42 teachers should have been recognized during the planning phase as insufficient for the kind of frequent, relationship-based coaching the model depended on; instead, it was accepted as a starting point and only revealed as inadequate once fidelity data came in months later.

Krishnamurthy has said that if the network were designing the initiative again, it would pilot the full model — curriculum, block, and coaching — at two or three schools first, establish the coaching ratios and accountability mechanisms that actually produce fidelity, and only then scale to the remaining schools with a model that had already been tested under real conditions. "We treated the pilot and the scale-up as the same event," he said. "They should never have been the same event."

Bourdeaux has emphasized a second lesson, related to accountability design rather than staffing: commitments that are not tied to evaluation or consequence tend to erode under the ordinary pressure of school operations, no matter how much conviction leadership expresses at rollout. The 90-minute block failed at three of five schools in year one not because principals disagreed with its value, but because nothing in their day-to-day incentives made protecting it more urgent than the dozen other operational demands competing for the same time and attention. Embedding block protection into principal evaluation in year two did not introduce a new idea — principals already knew the block mattered — but it did introduce a mechanism that made the existing idea enforceable.

A third lesson concerns assessment burden and the tendency of ambitious reform efforts to over-instrument themselves. Six required assessments per year sounded, at the design stage, like rigorous data-driven management; in practice, it consumed instructional time and teacher goodwill without generating proportionate insight, particularly layered on top of a brand-new curriculum teachers were already struggling to implement. The network's decision to cut to three assessments was not a retreat from data-driven practice — the network still tracks fall, winter, and spring benchmarks closely — but a recognition that measurement systems compete with instructional time for the same finite hours in a school day, and that reform efforts need to budget for that competition explicitly rather than treating assessment as a costless addition.

A fourth and more institutional lesson concerns the relationship with the funder. Bourdeaux has noted that the quarterly reporting relationship with Lindholm, while initially experienced by network staff as an added compliance burden during an already difficult year, ultimately functioned as an early warning system that forced the network to confront fidelity and assessment data months before it might otherwise have done so with the same urgency. The tranche-based funding structure, and Lindholm's willingness to hold the second tranche rather than continue funding a model that was not working, is credited by both Bourdeaux and Krishnamurthy as having accelerated the redesign rather than merely punishing the network for year-one shortfalls. Had the full $1.8 million been disbursed upfront without benchmarks, the network suspects it might have continued the original five-school, thinly-staffed model for another full year before the data forced a reckoning — at a higher cost in both dollars and lost instructional time for another cohort of students.

Finally, both leaders point to a lesson about scope discipline that extends beyond this particular initiative: the decision to narrow from five schools to three, while difficult and initially read by some staff and families as a partial retreat, was the single change most responsible for the initiative's eventual success. Concentrating a fixed amount of coaching capacity on three schools rather than diluting it across five allowed the coaching ratio to reach a level where it could actually function as designed. The network's current thinking, as it plans the expansion back to all five schools, is that it should treat the current three-school model as a floor, not a template to compress — expansion should mean adding proportional coaching capacity for the new schools, not spreading the existing four school-based coaches more thinly across a larger footprint, which would risk recreating the exact capacity problem that caused the original breakdown.

## Takeaways for Similar Efforts

The Delta Rise case offers several transferable lessons for other school networks, funders, and reform-minded leaders considering a similarly ambitious literacy investment.

The first is that a well-designed instructional model can fail entirely for reasons that have nothing to do with the model's content. Structured literacy curricula, protected instructional blocks, and coaching-based professional development are all well-supported strategies in the broader research literature; none of them were the source of Delta Rise's year-one failure. The failure was operational — insufficient coaching capacity relative to the number of teachers, and insufficient enforcement mechanisms for a schedule commitment that depended on principal discretion. Leaders evaluating a stalled reform effort should audit implementation capacity and accountability structures before concluding that the underlying instructional strategy was flawed.

The second is that simultaneous, network-wide rollouts of ambitious multi-part initiatives carry a specific risk: they make it very difficult to distinguish between design failure and capacity failure while the initiative is underway, because every school is experiencing the same strain at once and there is no internal comparison group. Delta Rise's forced narrowing to three schools in year two inadvertently created something close to a natural experiment, comparing focus and non-focus schools, and the resulting five-point-versus-nineteen-point gap in proficiency growth provided far clearer evidence about what the coaching and accountability changes were actually contributing than the network-wide year-one data ever could have. Organizations planning similar initiatives should consider deliberately staging rollouts — not only for risk management, but because staged rollouts generate the internal comparisons needed to know what is actually working.

The third is that teacher turnover and instructional reform are tightly coupled in ways that can work in either direction. In year one, the demands of a new curriculum, thin coaching support, and unclear accountability contributed to a wave of resignations that further destabilized the very classrooms the initiative was trying to strengthen. In year two, embedded coaching support and a reduced assessment burden coincided with declining turnover, suggesting that literacy reforms in high-turnover environments should be evaluated partly on their working-condition effects, not solely on their student achievement effects, since the two are causally linked over any timeline longer than a single semester.

The fourth is that funder oversight structured around benchmarks and tranches, while uncomfortable for a grantee to experience in a difficult year, can function as a valuable forcing mechanism rather than merely a punitive one. Greta Lindholm's decision to hold the $600,000 tranche in February 2024 was, in retrospect, treated by Delta Rise's own leadership as the moment that catalyzed a redesign that might otherwise have been delayed. Funders considering how to structure grants for ambitious, multi-year instructional reforms might take from this that clear, data-tied tranche conditions — paired with a genuine willingness to work with a grantee toward a credible redesign, rather than simply withdrawing support — can improve outcomes more than either unconditional multi-year funding or an unforgiving pass/fail approach to renewal.

The fifth and final lesson concerns budget honesty. Delta Rise's original $2.9 million, three-year budget assumed a coaching model that turned out to be insufficient, and the network ultimately spent $3.6 million to reach its spring 2025 results at only three of its five schools — meaning the true cost of achieving these results at full network scale is still unknown and likely to exceed $3.6 million by a meaningful margin once the remaining two schools are brought into the model. Organizations designing similar initiatives should build contingency into initial budgets for the likely scenario that year-one implementation data will reveal capacity gaps requiring additional investment, rather than treating the original budget as a ceiling that discipline alone can enforce. The alternative — as Delta Rise discovered — is a choice in year two between abandoning a promising redesign for budgetary reasons or exceeding the original budget to fund the capacity the design actually requires. Delta Rise chose the latter, and the results at Rosewood Elementary and its two sister focus schools suggest that, at least in this case, it was the right call.
