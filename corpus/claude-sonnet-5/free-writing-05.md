# On the Weight of Small Doors

There is a door in my grandmother's kitchen that leads nowhere — sealed shut decades ago when the house next door was sold and the shared entryway bricked over from the other side. She never removed the door. It hung there for forty years, painted and repainted, hinges oiled out of habit, a doorknob polished by hands that knew it opened onto nothing.

I used to ask her why she kept it. She said a door doesn't stop being a door just because you can't walk through it anymore.

I think about this more than I should. About the things we keep functional long after their function has been sealed away by circumstance — a phone number we haven't dialed in years but haven't deleted, a language half-learned for a person no longer in the country, a recipe card in handwriting we're afraid to lose because losing the card feels like losing the hand that wrote it.

We call this sentimentality, usually with a slight curl of the lip, as if the sentimental is the unserious cousin of the practical. But I wonder if it's something closer to a private form of physics — an insistence that closed things retain their shape. That a door, even bricked over on the far side, still divides *here* from *somewhere*, even if the somewhere is only the past.

My grandmother died three years ago. The house was sold. I don't know if the new owners kept the door or tore it out for shelving, which is what I would have done, before I understood anything.

What I understood, eventually, was this: she wasn't confused about the door's uselessness. She knew precisely what it did and didn't do. She kept it oiled not because she expected to walk through it, but because a door that sticks is a small violence against the past, and she was not in the business of doing violence to her past, however sealed it had become.

I have my own doors now. A voicemail I haven't deleted. A boarding pass tucked in a book from a trip I took with someone I no longer speak to. A song I skip on shuffle not because I dislike it but because I like it too much to hear it casually.

None of these open onto anything. I keep them anyway, oiled, polished, hinged — because I have come to agree with her: a thing doesn't stop being a door just because you can't walk through it anymore. Some things you keep not for passage but for shape. For the specific, irreplaceable shape of the wall they used to interrupt.
