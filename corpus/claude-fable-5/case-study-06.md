# When the Model Broke: Delta Rise Academies and the Two-Year Struggle to Make Structured Literacy Work in Jackson, Mississippi

## A Case Study in Program Design, Failure, and Redesign

---

## Introduction

In the spring of 2023, the leadership team of Delta Rise Academies, a five-school charter network in Jackson, Mississippi, faced a set of numbers that could not be explained away. Twenty-four percent of the network's third graders had scored proficient on the state reading assessment. Nineteen percent of them — nearly one in five — had been retained under Mississippi's third-grade reading gate, held back a year because they could not demonstrate the literacy skills the state required for promotion. And the adults responsible for teaching those children were leaving: teacher turnover across the network had reached 41 percent, meaning that in a typical classroom, the odds were close to a coin flip that the teacher would not return the following August.

Delta Rise responded the way many networks in its position respond. It committed serious money — $2.9 million over three years — to a structured literacy initiative built on a research-aligned curriculum, a protected 90-minute daily reading block, and external coaching from a specialist provider. It secured a $1.8 million grant from a family foundation to anchor the budget. It announced the initiative with conviction and launched it across all five schools at once.

A year later, the initiative was failing on nearly every measure that mattered. Coaching ratios were unworkable, the protected block existed on paper but not in practice at three of five schools, midyear assessment gains were negligible, twenty-two teachers resigned in a single December, and the funder froze the second tranche of the grant.

What happened next is the reason this case is worth studying. Rather than abandoning the initiative or doubling down on the original design, Delta Rise's leadership conducted a candid diagnosis, narrowed the program's scope, brought coaching in-house, cut the assessment burden in half, and made instructional protection a matter of principal accountability rather than principal goodwill. By spring 2025, the three schools that received the redesigned program had nearly doubled their proficiency rate, retention under the reading gate had fallen from 19 percent to 8 percent, and teacher turnover had dropped fifteen points.

The case is not a story of a program that worked. It is a story of a program that failed, was honestly examined, and was rebuilt — at a real cost in money, staff, and credibility with a funder. For leaders of school networks, program officers at foundations, and providers of instructional coaching, the details of both the failure and the recovery carry lessons that a simple success story would not.

## Part One: The Network and Its Starting Conditions

### Jackson, and the students Delta Rise serves

Delta Rise Academies operates five charter schools in Jackson, Mississippi, serving approximately 2,700 students. The network's demographics reflect the city it serves: 88 percent of its students qualify for free or reduced-price meals, and most arrive at kindergarten already behind national norms in early literacy skills — letter recognition, phonemic awareness, and vocabulary. Jackson's public school landscape has been strained for years by declining enrollment, aging facilities, and the same teacher labor market pressures affecting districts across the Deep South. Delta Rise positioned itself as an alternative for families seeking structure and higher expectations, and it grew steadily through the late 2010s to its five-campus footprint.

Growth, however, outpaced instructional coherence. By 2022, the five schools operated with meaningfully different reading programs. Two schools used a balanced-literacy approach centered on leveled readers and guided reading groups. One used a phonics supplement layered over a basal reader. The other two used what their principals described, with some honesty, as "whatever the teachers built themselves." There was no shared scope and sequence, no common assessment calendar beyond state-mandated testing, and no networkwide expectation for how much daily time reading instruction would receive.

### The spring 2023 data

The consequences surfaced in the spring 2023 state assessment results:

- **24 percent of third graders scored proficient in reading**, well below the state average and far below what the network's leadership considered acceptable.
- **19 percent of third graders were retained** under Mississippi's Literacy-Based Promotion Act, the state's third-grade reading gate. Retention at that scale is not only an academic signal; it is an operational and emotional burden — larger third-grade cohorts the following year, discouraged families, and children repeating a grade in a system that had already failed to teach them to read.
- **Teacher turnover stood at 41 percent.** The instability compounded the instructional problem: every August, roughly two of every five classrooms were led by someone new to the school, often new to teaching, and certainly new to whatever reading approach the departed teacher had been using.

Executive Director Camille Bourdeaux, who had led the network since its early expansion, described the spring 2023 data internally as "a referendum on our core promise." Chief Academic Officer Anand Krishnamurthy, hired eighteen months earlier in part to bring academic coherence to the network, argued that the reading results, the retention rate, and the turnover rate were not three problems but one: teachers were being asked to succeed at the hardest instructional task in elementary education — teaching children to read — without a shared curriculum, adequate time, or expert support, and many were leaving rather than continuing to fail.

### The context Mississippi provided

It mattered that this crisis unfolded in Mississippi. The state had become, by 2023, an unlikely national reference point for literacy improvement. Its investments in the science of reading — teacher training in structured literacy, coaching, and the accountability pressure of the third-grade gate — had produced NAEP gains that education commentators labeled the "Mississippi miracle." Delta Rise's leaders were acutely aware that the state around them was improving while their network was not. The state's trajectory both raised the stakes and pointed toward a solution: structured literacy, implemented with fidelity, supported by coaching.

That framing shaped everything about what came next.

## Part Two: The Original Design and the Reasoning Behind It

### The three-part model

In the summer of 2023, Bourdeaux and Krishnamurthy committed $2.9 million over three years to a networkwide literacy initiative with three components:

1. **A structured literacy curriculum**, adopted networkwide, replacing the patchwork of existing approaches with a single research-aligned program grounded in systematic phonics, phonemic awareness, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension instruction.

2. **A protected 90-minute daily reading block** in every K–3 classroom, during which no pull-outs, assemblies, specials scheduling, or administrative interruptions would be permitted.

3. **External coaching from Meridian Literacy Partners**, a regional provider specializing in structured literacy implementation, contracted to train teachers, conduct fidelity observations, and provide ongoing job-embedded support.

The design also included a robust assessment regime: six assessment windows per year — a combination of universal screeners, curriculum-embedded unit assessments, and interim benchmarks — intended to give teachers and coaches frequent, actionable data.

### The funding

The Bienville Family Foundation, a philanthropy with a history of supporting literacy work in the Gulf South, awarded Delta Rise a $1.8 million grant covering roughly 60 percent of the three-year budget, disbursed in tranches tied to implementation milestones. Program officer Greta Lindholm was assigned to monitor the grant, with quarterly reporting requirements covering fidelity observation data, assessment results, and spending. The network committed the remaining $1.1 million from its operating budget and reserves.

The grant agreement's milestone structure would later prove consequential. The second tranche — $600,000 — was contingent on evidence of implementation fidelity and early assessment gains by the middle of year one.

### Why they designed it this way

In retrospect, every major design decision had a defensible rationale, and every one contained the seed of a year-one failure.

**Why all five schools at once?** Bourdeaux argued that a phased rollout would create a two-tier network — some children receiving the improved program, others waiting — which she considered ethically untenable given the urgency of the data. There was also a political logic: a networkwide launch signaled seriousness to the board, the foundation, and families. And there was a practical one: with 41 percent turnover, delaying any school's implementation meant training teachers who might not stay long enough to see it.

**Why external coaching?** The network had no internal coaching capacity and no track record of building it. Meridian Literacy Partners came with credentials, a training model, and references from other Mississippi districts. Contracting the expertise seemed faster and lower-risk than hiring coaches into a network that was struggling to retain even classroom teachers. Krishnamurthy also believed external coaches would carry more authority with skeptical veteran teachers than internally promoted peers would.

**Why six assessment windows?** The theory was straightforward: frequent data would let coaches and teachers catch students falling behind and adjust instruction quickly. In a network where 19 percent of third graders were being retained, early identification felt non-negotiable.

**Why rely on principals' cooperation for the protected block?** The block was announced as a networkwide policy, and Krishnamurthy assumed a policy announcement would suffice. There was no enforcement mechanism, no accountability link, and no systematic monitoring of whether the 90 minutes actually happened uninterrupted. This was less a decision than a non-decision — the design simply assumed compliance.

The initiative launched in August 2023 with a week of summer training led by Meridian, new curriculum materials in every K–3 classroom, and considerable public optimism.

## Part Three: Year One — What the Implementation Revealed

### The coaching ratio collapses first

The failure that surfaced earliest was arithmetic. Meridian Literacy Partners staffed the contract with coaches at a ratio of one coach for every 42 teachers across the five campuses. The contract had specified coaching "coverage" for all K–3 teachers, but the network had not scrutinized what that coverage would look like in practice. At a 1:42 ratio, a coach who spent every working hour in classrooms could observe each teacher roughly once every three to four weeks — and coaches did not spend every hour in classrooms. They ran training sessions, wrote fidelity reports, drove between five campuses, and managed the assessment logistics the six-window calendar demanded.

Teachers experienced the coaching not as job-embedded support but as episodic inspection. A coach would appear, observe a lesson, leave a fidelity rubric score, and vanish for a month. New teachers — and given the turnover rate, there were many — received no more attention than veterans. The debriefs that make coaching effective, the co-planning, the modeled lessons, the return visit to see whether feedback took hold: none of it happened at scale, because it could not.

### Fidelity at 38 percent

By midyear, Meridian's own observation data showed that only 38 percent of observed lessons met the fidelity criteria for the structured literacy curriculum. Teachers were skipping phonemic awareness routines they found awkward, substituting familiar guided-reading practices for the curriculum's decoding lessons, and compressing 90-minute lesson plans into whatever time they actually had.

The fidelity number was alarming, but its interpretation was contested. Meridian framed it as a teacher-will problem — resistance to change. Krishnamurthy increasingly saw it as a support problem: teachers who received a week of summer training and a monthly drive-by observation could not reasonably be expected to master an unfamiliar, technically demanding instructional approach. Both interpretations contained truth, but the disagreement itself corroded the partnership. Coaches came to be seen, in the words of one principal, as "auditors with rubrics."

### The block that wasn't

The protected 90-minute block, meanwhile, was quietly dissolving. By December 2023, network walkthroughs confirmed what teachers had been reporting: only two of the five schools were actually protecting the block. At the other three, the 90 minutes existed on the master schedule but was routinely invaded — students pulled for intervention services and special education minutes, classes interrupted for assemblies and picture days, specials rescheduled into the block when the art teacher was absent, intercom announcements fragmenting instruction.

The pattern was not random. The two schools that protected the block had principals who personally believed in the initiative and enforced the schedule against internal pressure. The three that did not had principals who faced the same pressures — service-minute compliance, staffing gaps, operational chaos — and resolved them at the reading block's expense, because nothing in their evaluation or in the network's management systems told them not to. The block had been announced as policy but designed as a suggestion.

The consequence was severe: structured literacy curricula are built as sequenced, cumulative daily lessons. A student who misses Tuesday's decoding lesson is less prepared for Wednesday's. In classrooms where the block was fragmented, the curriculum's internal logic broke down, which in turn made low fidelity nearly inevitable — teachers with 55 usable minutes triaged a 90-minute lesson however they could.

### Assessment overload

The six-window assessment calendar, intended to fuel responsive instruction, instead consumed it. Each window required days of administration in classrooms already short on time, followed by data entry and reporting. Teachers described a rhythm in which they finished one assessment cycle just in time to prepare for the next. Worse, the data went largely unused: with coaches stretched to 1:42, no one had capacity to sit with teachers and translate results into instructional adjustments. The network was generating data at six times the rate it could act on it.

### December 2023: twenty-two resignations

The human cost arrived in December. Twenty-two teachers resigned mid-year — an extraordinary number even for a network accustomed to 41 percent annual turnover, because mid-year departures are the most disruptive kind. Exit conversations, where they happened, repeated common themes: an unfamiliar curriculum with inadequate support, an assessment burden that felt punitive, observation scores that felt like surveillance, and — at the three non-protecting schools — the daily demoralization of being held to a program the building's own operations would not allow them to deliver.

The resignations forced emergency staffing measures: long-term substitutes, combined classes, and permit-holding hires with no literacy training at all. In those classrooms, the initiative effectively ceased to exist.

### Three points

Midyear benchmark assessments, administered in the winter window, showed an average gain of three points against fall baselines — statistically distinguishable from nothing, and far below the trajectory required to move third-grade proficiency meaningfully by spring. The number landed on Greta Lindholm's desk in the network's quarterly report alongside the 38 percent fidelity figure, the block-protection findings, and news of the December resignations.

## Part Four: The Funder Freezes — February 2024

In February 2024, Lindholm informed Bourdeaux that the Bienville Family Foundation was placing the $600,000 second tranche on hold. The milestone conditions had not been met, and the foundation's board was unwilling to release further funds against an implementation that its own reporting showed was failing.

The freeze was, in Bourdeaux's later description, "the worst week of my professional life and the most useful." The immediate effects were painful: a $600,000 hole in the year-two budget, an awkward conversation with the network's own board, and internal anxiety that the entire initiative would be cancelled. Meridian's contract, the coaching model, the curriculum investment — all of it was suddenly in question.

But the freeze also did something the network's internal governance had not: it forced a genuine reckoning. Lindholm did not simply withhold funds; she required a diagnostic review and a credible redesign as conditions for reconsidering the tranche. She was explicit that the foundation was not walking away — Bienville had funded literacy work long enough to know that first-year implementations fail more often than not — but that it would not fund a continuation of the current design.

Over six weeks in February and March 2024, Bourdeaux and Krishnamurthy led a diagnostic process that included teacher focus groups at all five schools, a review of Meridian's observation data against schedule and staffing records, structured interviews with all five principals, and an honest accounting of what the $1.4 million spent to date had actually purchased.

The diagnosis they presented to Lindholm and to their own board was blunt. The initiative had failed not because structured literacy was wrong for their students — the two schools that protected the block and happened to have somewhat better coaching access showed modestly better fidelity and gains — but because the implementation design had three fatal flaws: **coaching capacity was a fraction of what the model required; instructional time was governed by hope rather than accountability; and the assessment system generated more burden than insight.** Underneath all three was a scope error: the network had launched everywhere at once, spreading inadequate resources across five schools when it might have concentrated adequate resources on fewer.

## Part Five: The Redesign

The redesigned program, launched for the 2024–25 school year, changed four things.

### 1. Narrowing to three focus schools

Bourdeaux made the decision she had rejected a year earlier: the full program would concentrate on three schools, selected for a combination of need, principal commitment, and staffing stability. Rosewood Elementary, led by Principal Shauna Delgadillo — one of the two principals who had protected the block in year one — became the flagship site. The other two schools retained the curriculum and a lighter support package but were explicitly designated as second-phase sites rather than full participants.

The decision was ethically uncomfortable, and Bourdeaux did not pretend otherwise. In presentations to the board and to families, she framed it as a choice between two failures and one success: "We tried giving every school forty percent of what this work requires. This year, three schools get a hundred percent, and we build the capacity to bring the others in behind them."

### 2. Bringing coaching in-house

The Meridian contract was restructured and substantially reduced. In its place, the network hired four school-based literacy coaches as Delta Rise employees — full-time positions embedded at the three focus schools, with Ousmane Diallo, a former Rosewood teacher who had emerged in year one as the network's most credible internal literacy voice, promoted to Literacy Coach Lead.

The arithmetic transformed the model. Four coaches across three schools' K–3 teachers produced ratios in the range of one coach to twelve or fourteen teachers — a load at which real coaching becomes possible. Coaches now worked weekly cycles with each teacher: observation, debrief, co-planning, modeled lessons, and return visits. Because they were building-based staff rather than visiting contractors, they attended grade-level meetings, knew the students, and could distinguish a fidelity lapse caused by resistance from one caused by a schedule collision.

Meridian's remaining role shifted to what it did well: training the coaches themselves, providing summer institutes, and conducting periodic external fidelity audits — a check on the risk that internal coaches would grade their own homework.

### 3. Cutting assessments from six windows to three

The assessment calendar was cut in half: a fall universal screener, a winter benchmark, and the spring state-aligned assessment, supplemented by lightweight curriculum-embedded checks that teachers scored themselves without central reporting. The redesign's principle, articulated by Krishnamurthy, was that the network would administer no assessment it lacked the capacity to act on. The reclaimed instructional days — teachers estimated eight to ten per year — went back into the block. With coaches now available to sit with teachers over the data that remained, the three windows produced more instructional adjustment than the six had.

### 4. Making block protection a condition of principal evaluation

The most structurally significant change was the smallest to describe: protection of the 90-minute block became an explicit component of principal evaluations, with walkthrough-verified protection rates reported monthly to Krishnamurthy. Principals were given support to make protection feasible — help redesigning intervention schedules so service minutes wrapped around the block rather than puncturing it, authority to decline district-style operational interruptions, and a networkwide moratorium on scheduling anything into the K–3 morning block. But the accountability was unambiguous. What had been a request became a job requirement.

Delgadillo, who had protected the block without being told to, became the model's proof of concept and its informal trainer, walking the other two focus-school principals through the scheduling architecture she had built at Rosewood.

### The foundation's response

Lindholm and the Bienville board reviewed the redesign in late spring 2024 and released the $600,000 tranche against the new plan, with revised milestones: coaching ratios, block-protection rates verified by walkthrough, and winter benchmark gains at the three focus schools. Notably, the foundation accepted the narrowed scope — funding deeper implementation at three schools rather than shallow implementation at five — a decision Lindholm later described as the grant's most important, and one that required her own board to accept that the original theory of change had been wrong about scale.

The redesign also cost more than planned. Four coaching salaries, the restructured Meridian contract, retention stipends for teachers at the focus schools, and the extended summer training pushed total spending to $3.6 million by spring 2025 — $700,000 over the original three-year budget, covered by the released tranche, a supplemental foundation contribution, and network reserves.

## Part Six: The Results — Spring 2025

### The three focus schools

By the spring 2025 state assessments, the three focus schools reached **43 percent third-grade reading proficiency** — up from the 24 percent networkwide baseline two years earlier, and nearly double it. The gain was not uniform: Rosewood, with the longest run of protected instructional time and the most stable staff, led the three, but all three schools posted double-digit improvements.

The intermediate indicators behind the proficiency number moved first and explained it. Block-protection rates at the focus schools, verified by walkthrough, exceeded 90 percent by fall 2024. External fidelity audits conducted by Meridian showed fidelity climbing from the year-one 38 percent to above 75 percent by winter 2025 — still imperfect, but a level at which the curriculum's design could function. Winter benchmark gains, which had been three points in year one, ran into the double digits at the focus schools in year two.

### The reading gate

Retention under Mississippi's third-grade reading gate fell networkwide from **19 percent to 8 percent** — a change that translated into dozens of children promoted who, two years earlier, would have repeated third grade. For families, this was the most tangible result of the entire initiative; for the network's operations, it relieved the compounding burden of oversized repeat cohorts.

### Teacher turnover

Networkwide teacher turnover fell from **41 percent to 26 percent**, with the steepest declines at the focus schools. The causal story is necessarily entangled — retention stipends, a tightening regional labor market, and the coaching model all plausibly contributed — but exit and stay interviews pointed consistently to two factors: teachers felt supported rather than inspected, and they felt the network's demands were finally matched by the conditions to meet them. Several teachers who had considered leaving in year one cited their weekly coaching relationship as the reason they stayed. The mid-year exodus did not repeat; December 2024 saw three resignations, not twenty-two.

The turnover decline mattered instructionally as well as financially. Structured literacy rewards cumulative teacher expertise; a teacher in her second year with the curriculum is markedly more effective than one in her first. A network losing 41 percent of teachers annually can never accumulate that expertise. At 26 percent — still high by national standards, but transformed by local ones — accumulation became possible.

### The two non-focus schools

The two schools outside the full program moved from **24 percent to 29 percent proficiency**. They retained the curriculum, the three-window assessment calendar, and the networkwide block policy, and they received occasional support from Diallo's coaching team — but no embedded coaches and no principal-evaluation accountability comparable to the focus schools'.

The five-point gain at these schools is, analytically, one of the most useful numbers in the case. It suggests that the curriculum and reduced assessment burden alone produced modest improvement — but that the difference between five points and nineteen points was made by the embedded coaching and enforced instructional time. The network, in effect, ran an unintentional comparison, and the comparison isolated where the value actually lived. It was not primarily in the materials. It was in the adult support system and the protected time to use them.

Both non-focus schools were scheduled to enter the full model in 2025–26, with two additional coach hires budgeted and their principals spending release days at Rosewood during the spring.

### The funder relationship

The Bienville Family Foundation's confidence, badly damaged in February 2024, was substantially rebuilt — not because the results were perfect, but because the network's reporting had become honest. Lindholm noted to her board that the quarterly reports after the redesign included fidelity shortfalls, schools lagging behind targets, and unresolved problems alongside the gains, and that this candor was itself evidence the network had changed. The foundation entered discussions about supporting the phase-two expansion.

## Part Seven: What the Network Would Do Differently

Asked in spring 2025 to reflect on the two years, Bourdeaux, Krishnamurthy, Delgadillo, and Diallo converged on a consistent set of retrospective judgments.

**They would have started narrow.** The all-five-schools launch, defended in 2023 on equity grounds, delivered inequity anyway — an inadequate program for everyone rather than an adequate one for some. Bourdeaux's revised position: "Piloting isn't a betrayal of the kids who wait. Failing everywhere at once is."

**They would have interrogated the coaching ratio before signing.** The Meridian contract promised "coverage"; no one converted that word into minutes per teacher per month until the model was already failing. Krishnamurthy's rule going forward: any coaching contract must specify a ratio, and any ratio above roughly 1:15 for teachers new to a curriculum is a plan to observe, not a plan to coach.

**They would have built accountability for instructional time into the design from day one.** The protected block failed at exactly the schools where nothing made protection a leadership obligation. The lesson was not that principals were negligent but that schedules are contested territory, and instructional time loses every contest that leadership systems do not explicitly rig in its favor.

**They would have sized the assessment system to their capacity to act.** Six windows generated data no one could use and consumed time everyone needed. Three windows, paired with coaches who could translate data into practice, produced more instructional change with half the burden.

**They would have treated the funder as a thinking partner earlier.** The relationship with Lindholm before February 2024 consisted of compliance reporting that emphasized progress and buried problems. The tranche freeze, painful as it was, created the first genuinely honest exchange — and the redesign that followed was stronger for the foundation's pressure and, later, its flexibility on scope. Bourdeaux's conclusion: "We should have called Greta in October and said this isn't working. She would have helped. Instead we waited for the data to say it for us."

**They would have found their Diallo sooner.** The network's most effective coaching leader had been inside the organization all along, teaching at Rosewood. The assumption that expertise had to be imported delayed the discovery of expertise that could be developed — and that carried a credibility with peers no contractor could match.

## Part Eight: What a Reader in a Similar Position Should Take From This

The Delta Rise case will read as familiar to anyone who has led, funded, or coached a major instructional initiative. Its lessons generalize.

**1. Depth beats breadth when resources are fixed.** The single clearest finding in the case is the comparison the network accidentally ran on itself: schools with full implementation gained nineteen points; schools with materials but without embedded coaching and enforced time gained five. If your budget forces a choice between full implementation somewhere and partial implementation everywhere, the evidence here — consistent with the broader implementation-science literature — says choose depth. Partial implementation is not half the results; it is a small fraction of them.

**2. Coaching is a ratio, not a line item.** A coaching contract that does not specify how many teachers each coach serves is not a coaching contract; it is an observation contract. At 1:42, Meridian's coaches could not have succeeded regardless of skill. At 1:12, ordinary coaches transformed practice. Before signing, do the division.

**3. Instructional time must be governed, not announced.** Every school schedule is under constant siege from service minutes, operations, and emergencies. A protected block survives only where someone's job depends on protecting it. Delta Rise's block failed as a policy and succeeded as an evaluation criterion — the same words, backed by different consequences.

**4. Assessment capacity means capacity to respond, not capacity to administer.** Any assessment whose results no one has time to act on is pure cost. Size the assessment calendar to the coaching and planning capacity that will use it, then stop.

**5. Mid-year teacher resignations are a leading indicator, not an HR problem.** The twenty-two December departures preceded the funder freeze and confirmed the benchmark data. Networks should treat unusual mid-year attrition as an implementation alarm and investigate it as such, immediately.

**6. Funders can be part of the correction if the relationship permits honesty.** Lindholm's tranche freeze was the intervention Delta Rise's own governance had failed to make, and her willingness to fund a narrowed, redesigned program — rather than demanding the original plan be executed harder — was as important as the freeze itself. Grantees should surface problems before milestones force them; funders should structure milestones so that honest failure triggers redesign, not just penalty.

**7. Failure in year one is information, not verdict.** The structured literacy model that failed in 2023–24 and the one that succeeded in 2024–25 used the same curriculum, served the same children, and pursued the same goal. What changed was the implementation architecture around it. The most consequential decision Delta Rise made was not the 2023 decision to launch the initiative. It was the 2024 decision to diagnose honestly, keep the sound core, and rebuild everything around it — at a cost of $700,000 over budget, a bruised funder relationship, and a hard public admission. Two years on, 43 percent proficiency, an 8 percent retention rate, and a stabilizing teaching force suggest it was the best money and humility the network ever spent.
