# Scrap Rate Escalation at Keystone Precision Forge, January 2025 – February 2026: Sources of the Increase and Implications for the June Die Capital Decision

**Prepared by:** Devendra Ramaswamy, Senior Quality Engineer
**Prepared for:** Marcus Ellery, Chief Operating Officer
**Reviewed by:** Rosalind Fairweather, Director of Continuous Improvement
**Requested by:** Yolanda Brzezinski, Plant Manager, Butler Operations
**Date:** 24 April 2026

---

## 1. The Question and What Was Found

Between January 2025 and February 2026, the combined scrap rate across the Butler plants rose from 3.1 percent of units produced to 5.8 percent, while monthly production volume remained essentially flat. At current volumes and an average fully burdened cost of roughly $41 per scrapped forging, the increase represents an annualized loss of approximately $1.8 million. In March, plant manager Yolanda Brzezinski asked the quality engineering group to determine what is driving the increase and, specifically, whether the evidence supports the $2.4 million full die-set replacement that maintenance director Gerald Tomczak has placed on the June capital agenda on the theory that worn dies are the primary cause.

This brief answers that question using the plant's manufacturing execution system (MES) records — a working sample of 1.94 million parts logged over the fourteen-month window, each tagged by press line, die set, operator badge, shift, and steel heat lot. The analysis cross-tabulates scrap outcomes across shift, operator tenure, steel source, and die condition; fits a logistic regression to separate the overlapping effects; and decomposes the 2.7-point rise into its contributing sources.

The headline findings are as follows. First, the single largest contributor to the scrap increase is not die condition but incoming steel. Two heat lots from a newly qualified vendor, Allegheny Bar and Billet, entered production in October 2025 with certified sulfur content at 0.048 and 0.049 weight percent — at the very top of the 0.050 specification ceiling and roughly double the 0.022–0.028 range typical of the legacy supplier. Parts forged from these lots scrap at markedly elevated rates on both shifts, and the timing of their introduction coincides precisely with the steepest segment of the scrap trend. We attribute approximately 1.5 percentage points of the 2.7-point rise to this material.

Second, die wear is a genuine but secondary contributor, worth roughly 0.6 points, and most of that effect is concentrated in two die sets — D-12 and D-17 — that are well past their nominal 400,000-cycle service life. Critically, die wear and the high-sulfur steel interact: worn dies running Allegheny material scrap at 9.1 percent, far above what either factor produces alone. The die problem is real, but it is a two-die problem amplified by bad steel, not a fleet-wide problem.

Third, the striking raw gap between night-shift scrap (7.4 percent) and day-shift scrap (4.2 percent) is largely an artifact of material allocation, not shift performance. The night shift draws 61 percent of its work from the two Allegheny heat lots; days draw only 18 percent. Once steel source and die assignment are controlled, the residual night effect shrinks to roughly 1.4 points — still meaningful, and plausibly connected to the turnover wave that left 38 percent of night headcount with under six months' tenure, but far smaller than the raw comparison suggests. The tenure effect itself is the least secure finding in this brief, because the failed night-shift badge scanner left roughly 14 percent of night records without operator attribution between May and September 2025.

The implication for the June decision is direct: the full $2.4 million die replacement, evaluated against the roughly 0.6 points of scrap it can plausibly recover, has a payback approaching six years and should not be approved as scoped. A targeted replacement of D-12 and D-17, at an estimated $780,000, recovers most of the die-related scrap at a payback near three years. The larger and cheaper win — worth approximately $1.0 million annually — is quarantining the remaining Allegheny material, tightening the purchasing sulfur specification to 0.035 maximum, and re-qualifying the vendor or reverting volume to the legacy supplier.

## 2. Data and Methods

### 2.1 Source records and assembly

The analysis rests on part-level records extracted from the plant MES, which captures every forging as it exits the press with a timestamped record carrying the press line, the die set mounted at the time of the stroke, the operator badge scanned at shift start on that press, the shift designation, and the steel heat lot loaded into the induction furnace feeding that line. Scrap disposition is entered at the in-line inspection station and at final inspection, coded to one of eleven defect categories; for this analysis the codes were consolidated into four families: surface defects (laps, hot tears, seams), underfill and short-fill, dimensional and flash defects, and other (handling damage, heat-treat rejects, miscellaneous).

The extraction covers 1 January 2025 through 28 February 2026 for the primary analysis window, with a longer historical pull back to January 2021 used only to establish baseline seasonality and die-life norms. Press lines 2 through 7 have complete MES coverage across the full history. Line 1 was instrumented only in August 2025; its records enter the cross-sectional comparisons from that date forward but are excluded from all trend calculations, for reasons discussed in Section 4. After removal of test strokes, setup pieces, and records with corrupted lot identifiers (together under 0.4 percent of raw records), the working sample is 1.94 million parts.

Operator badge capture is the weakest field. The badge scanner on the night-shift entry point at Plant 2 failed intermittently from May through September 2025 before replacement, and during that window roughly 14 percent of night-shift part records carry no operator attribution. These records retain valid press, die, shift, and heat-lot fields; only the operator — and therefore the tenure classification — is missing. The handling of these records is described in Section 2.3.

Supporting data were joined from three additional sources: the maintenance department's die-cycle counters, which log cumulative strokes per die set and were reconciled against MES stroke counts (agreement within 1.2 percent); vendor material certificates for all heat lots consumed in the window, keyed to the MES lot identifiers; and human resources records providing hire dates for tenure classification, with tenure banded at under six months, six to twenty-four months, and over twenty-four months.

### 2.2 Analytical approach

The analysis proceeds in four stages. First, the monthly scrap trend is charted and annotated against known events: the badge scanner outage, the July–September 2025 turnover wave, the October 2025 arrival of the first Allegheny heat lots, and the dates at which individual die sets crossed 350,000 and 400,000 cumulative cycles. Second, scrap rates are cross-tabulated across the four candidate factors — shift, tenure band, steel source, and die-age quartile — both marginally and jointly, because the central analytical problem here is confounding: the night shift disproportionately runs Allegheny steel, new operators are disproportionately assigned to nights, and scheduling happened to route a large share of Allegheny material across the oldest die sets.

Third, a part-level logistic regression was fit on the October 2025 – February 2026 subperiod (approximately 695,000 parts), when all four factors were simultaneously in play. The model specifies scrap as the outcome, with indicators for Allegheny heat lot, night shift, and tenure band; cumulative die cycles (per 100,000) as a continuous term; an interaction between Allegheny material and dies beyond 400,000 cycles; and fixed effects for part family to absorb differences in forging complexity. Because scrap events are correlated within a heat lot — a lot with an inclusion problem produces clustered failures — standard errors are clustered on heat lot rather than treated as independent across parts. This is a consequential choice: naive standard errors would overstate precision roughly threefold.

Fourth, the 2.7-point rise from January 2025 to February 2026 is decomposed using a shift-share method: the February 2026 factor mix (share of production on Allegheny steel, on worn dies, staffed by new-tenure operators at night) is applied to January 2025 baseline rates, and each factor's contribution is computed as the change attributable to its mix shift plus its rate effect, with the interaction term allocated to the die category. The decomposition is approximate by construction, and the residual is reported rather than forced to zero.

### 2.3 Treatment of missing badge data

Records missing operator attribution cannot be assigned a tenure band. Three treatments were run in parallel: (a) exclusion of unattributed records from tenure comparisons only, retaining them for all other analyses; (b) a lower-bound scenario assigning all unattributed night records to experienced operators; and (c) an upper-bound scenario assigning them all to under-six-month operators. All tenure results in this brief are reported as the exclusion-case estimate with the bounding range attached. Because the scanner outage (May–September 2025) largely predates the arrival of Allegheny steel (October 2025), the missing-badge problem does not contaminate the material analysis, which is the load-bearing finding.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 The trend and its timing

The monthly scrap rate was stable in the first three quarters of 2025, moving between 3.1 and 3.5 percent — within the plant's historical seasonal band established from the 2021–2024 pull. The break occurs in October 2025: 4.1 percent that month, 4.9 in November, 5.2 in December, 5.5 in January 2026, and 5.8 in February 2026.

*Figure 1 (described):* A line chart of monthly scrap rate, January 2025 through February 2026, with a flat segment near 3.3 percent through September, then a steep, nearly linear climb to 5.8 percent. Four annotations mark the badge scanner outage window (May–September, during the flat segment), the completion of the turnover wave (September), the first Allegheny heat lot consumed in production (first week of October, immediately preceding the inflection), and the months in which die sets D-12 and D-17 crossed 400,000 and then 480,000 cycles (November and January respectively, during the climb). The visual point is that the inflection coincides with the steel change, not with the workforce change, which was complete before the trend broke, and not with die aging, which is gradual rather than stepped.

The defect-code composition sharpens the timing story. Between January 2025 and February 2026, surface defects — laps, hot tears, seams — rose from 0.8 to 2.6 percent of production, accounting for 1.8 points of the total 2.7-point increase. Dimensional and flash defects rose from 0.9 to 1.3 percent; underfill from 0.7 to 1.0; other codes from 0.7 to 0.9. Hot tearing and lapping are the classic signatures of sulfide inclusions and hot shortness in high-sulfur steel; dimensional drift and flash thickness variation are the classic signatures of die wear. The defect mix says the problem is roughly two-thirds metallurgical and one-third mechanical, before any statistical modeling is done.

*Figure 2 (described):* A stacked area chart of monthly scrap by consolidated defect family. The surface-defect band is thin and stable through September, then widens sharply from October onward and accounts for most of the total area growth. The dimensional/flash band widens gradually across the whole period. The underfill and other bands are nearly constant.

### 3.2 Shift and steel: unbraiding the central confound

The raw shift comparison — 7.4 percent scrap on nights against 4.2 percent on days — invites the conclusion that the night shift is the problem. The material allocation data say otherwise. Over October 2025 – February 2026, the night shift drew 61 percent of its work from the two Allegheny heat lots (internal identifiers H-24117 and H-24162); the day shift drew 18 percent. This is a scheduling artifact: the Allegheny bar arrived in two large deliveries staged at Plant 2, whose night-heavy loading pattern meant the material was drawn down disproportionately after 6 p.m.

Table 1 presents the joint cross-tabulation, which is the clearest single container for the core result.

| | Allegheny lots (H-24117, H-24162) | Legacy supplier lots | Shift overall |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Day shift** | 6.1% | 3.8% | 4.2% |
| **Night shift** | 8.6% | 5.5% | 7.4% |
| **Steel overall** | 8.1% | 4.4% | — |
| **Share of shift volume, night** | 61% | 39% | |
| **Share of shift volume, day** | 18% | 82% | |

Read across the rows: on both shifts, Allegheny material carries a scrap penalty of 2.3 to 3.1 percentage points over legacy steel run on the same presses in the same period. Read down the columns: within each steel source, the night shift carries a penalty of 1.7 to 2.5 points over days. Both effects are real, but their magnitudes are unequal in consequence, because the material effect applies to a large and growing share of throughput and because — as Section 3.4 shows — the material effect compounds with die condition in a way the shift effect does not.

The Allegheny certificates themselves are consistent with the observed failure mode. Both lots certify sulfur at 0.048 and 0.049 weight percent against a specification maximum of 0.050 — technically conforming, but at a level roughly double the 0.022–0.028 typical of the legacy supplier's certs over the prior three years. Elevated sulfur produces manganese sulfide stringers that open into laps and hot tears under the strain rates and die chilling conditions of hammer and press forging, precisely the defect family that dominates the increase. The material passed receiving inspection because receiving inspection checks conformance to specification, and the specification permits it. The specification, not the inspection, is the gap.

### 3.3 Tenure: a real but smaller and less secure effect

Operators with under six months' tenure constitute 38 percent of night-shift headcount following the July–September 2025 turnover wave, in which 47 operators departed and 52 were hired. Their raw scrap rate on nights is 6.9 percent; the same operators working day assignments scrap at 5.1 percent.

Two features of these numbers warrant attention before any causal reading. First, 6.9 percent is *below* the night-shift average of 7.4 percent — new operators on nights scrap less than their experienced counterparts, whose implied rate is approximately 7.7 percent. This inversion is explained by assignment practice: supervisors route new hires to lower-complexity part families and, disproportionately, to the newer die sets, while experienced operators absorb the difficult jobs, including most of the Allegheny-heavy runs on the older dies. On days, where assignment is less stratified, the expected pattern reappears: new operators scrap at 5.1 percent against roughly 4.0 percent for experienced day operators. The raw tenure comparisons, in other words, are contaminated in opposite directions on the two shifts, and neither can be read at face value.

Second, this is the analysis most damaged by the badge scanner failure. Approximately 14 percent of night-shift records from May through September 2025 carry no operator attribution and cannot be tenure-classified. Under the exclusion treatment, the complexity- and material-adjusted tenure effect (from the regression in Section 3.5) is about 1.6 points on comparable work. Under the bounding scenarios, in which all unattributed records are assigned first to experienced and then to new operators, the adjusted effect ranges from 0.9 to 2.1 points. The direction is stable — new operators scrap more on like-for-like work — but the magnitude is genuinely uncertain, and this brief treats it accordingly.

### 3.4 Die condition: two die sets, one interaction

The plant runs 22 active die sets against a nominal service life of 400,000 cycles. As of February 2026, six sets exceed 350,000 cycles and two — D-12 and D-17 — exceed 480,000, having been extended past nominal life during the 2025 capital deferral. Sorting production by die-age quartile in the February 2026 cross-section gives scrap rates of 3.9 percent in the newest quartile, 4.6 in the second, 5.3 in the third, and 6.8 in the oldest — a gradient that, taken alone, appears to support the maintenance department's position.

The gradient does not survive stratification by steel source intact. Within legacy-supplier steel, the die-age gradient runs from 3.3 percent (newest quartile) to 4.5 percent (oldest) — a real but modest 1.2-point spread. Within Allegheny material, the same gradient runs from 5.6 to 9.1 percent — a 3.5-point spread. The oldest dies happened to run a disproportionate share of the Allegheny lots (again a scheduling artifact: D-12 and D-17 serve the hydraulic cylinder-gland family that dominated the Allegheny-fed runs), so the raw die-age gradient captures a substantial amount of steel effect wearing a die costume.

*Figure 3 (described):* A scatter of scrap rate against cumulative die cycles, one point per die-set-month, with two fitted lines: one for months in which the die ran predominantly legacy steel, one for months running predominantly Allegheny material. The legacy line rises gently across the cycle range. The Allegheny line starts higher and rises steeply, with the divergence widening sharply beyond 400,000 cycles. The visual point is an interaction, not two parallel penalties: worn dies tolerate good steel reasonably well and bad steel poorly.

The mechanism is plausible and consistent with the tooling literature and with what die maintenance observes at teardown: sulfide-laden material tears and galls at the flash lands and in high-strain die features, and worn dies — with degraded surface finish and opened radii — provide exactly the initiation sites that make marginal material fail. The steel accelerates die degradation, and the degraded dies punish the steel. Teardown inspection of D-12 in January found flash-land wear roughly 40 percent beyond the condemnation limit, corroborating the counter data.

### 3.5 The regression and the decomposition

The logistic model on the October 2025 – February 2026 subperiod, with part-family fixed effects and standard errors clustered on heat lot, yields the following adjusted odds ratios. Allegheny heat lot: 1.92 (95 percent interval 1.51–2.44). Night shift: 1.38 (1.19–1.60). Cumulative die cycles, per 100,000: 1.07 (1.03–1.11). Under-six-month tenure: 1.11 (0.94–1.31), not statistically distinguishable from no effect after controls, though the interval is consistent with the modest positive effect seen in the stratified day-shift comparison. The interaction of Allegheny material with dies beyond 400,000 cycles: 1.44 (1.16–1.79). In plain terms: the steel roughly doubles the odds of scrap on its own; running that steel across an end-of-life die multiplies the elevated odds by a further 44 percent; the night shift retains a moderate residual effect after material and die assignment are stripped out; and tenure, once assignment patterns are controlled, contributes little that can be separated from noise given the badge-data damage.

The shift-share decomposition of the 2.7-point rise (January 2025 baseline 3.1 percent, February 2026 rate 5.8 percent) allocates approximately: 1.5 points to the Allegheny material (its rate penalty times its share of February throughput), 0.6 points to die condition inclusive of the interaction term, 0.3 points to workforce and shift-mix effects, and 0.3 points residual and unexplained. The residual likely contains some combination of measurement noise, minor process drift, and the portion of the interaction that the allocation convention assigns imperfectly. The ordering of the first two contributions is robust to every alternative allocation convention tested; under no defensible convention does die condition account for more than 0.9 points of the 2.7.

## 4. Limitations

Four limitations bound the confidence that should attach to these findings, in descending order of consequence.

**The missing badge scans degrade the tenure analysis and only the tenure analysis.** With 14 percent of night-shift records unattributed from May through September 2025, the tenure effect cannot be pinned more tightly than the 0.9-to-2.1-point bounding range, and the regression's null-consistent tenure coefficient should be read as "not separately identifiable," not "demonstrated absent." Two saving features limit the damage. The outage window largely precedes the October arrival of Allegheny steel, so the material findings rest on months with near-complete attribution of the fields they require — and those findings do not depend on the operator field at all. And the missing records retain valid shift, press, die, and heat-lot data, so they participate fully in every non-tenure analysis. The practical consequence is that this brief can say confidently what the steel and the dies are doing and only approximately what the new workforce is doing.

**Line 1's short history constrains the trend but not the cross-section.** Because line 1 was instrumented only in August 2025, it contributes no pre-period baseline, and including it in the trend would mechanically distort the January 2025 starting point. It is therefore excluded from all trend and decomposition figures. Cross-sectionally, its post-August behavior is unremarkable: 5.2 percent scrap over August 2025 – February 2026, within the fleet range for its die ages and material mix. A sensitivity check including line 1 moves the February 2026 fleet rate from 5.8 to 5.9 percent and changes no coefficient in the regression by more than its interval width. The exclusion costs generality — if line 1 behaved differently before August 2025, we cannot know it — but does not threaten any stated conclusion.

**Two heat lots is a thin base for a vendor-level judgment.** The Allegheny effect is estimated from two lots, and clustering the standard errors on heat lot honestly reflects that the effective sample for the material comparison is far smaller than 695,000 parts. The 1.92 odds ratio is well estimated for *these lots*; whether it generalizes to Allegheny's production at large, or reflects two unlucky heats, cannot be settled from plant data. The certificates' sulfur values, sitting at the specification ceiling on both lots, suggest a vendor practice rather than an accident, but that is inference, not observation. The recommended designed trial and the tightened purchasing specification in Section 5 are the appropriate responses to this uncertainty; a permanent vendor disqualification on two lots would outrun the evidence.

**Observational data cannot fully retire the die hypothesis.** Steel, dies, operators, and shifts were not randomly assigned to one another; they were scheduled, and scheduling responds to exactly the factors under study. The stratified comparisons and the fixed-effects model control for the confounding we can see, but a residual pattern — for instance, if supervisors quietly routed the most difficult geometries to the oldest dies *and* the Allegheny bar — could inflate the material estimate at the dies' expense. The defect-code composition (surface defects, the metallurgical signature, driving two-thirds of the increase) argues against a large hidden die effect, but the clean separation requires the controlled trial proposed below: legacy steel run deliberately across both new and end-of-life dies on matched part numbers. That trial is cheap, fast, and should precede any capital release beyond the targeted two-die replacement.

## 5. Implications for the June Capital Decision

### 5.1 The economics of the options

Annualized volume over the analysis window is approximately 1.66 million units. At $41 average fully burdened cost per scrapped forging, each percentage point of scrap costs roughly $680,000 per year. The 2.7-point deterioration therefore costs approximately $1.8 million annually, distributed per the decomposition: roughly $1.0 million attributable to the Allegheny material, $410,000 to die condition, and the balance to workforce effects and residual.

Against this arithmetic, the proposed $2.4 million fleet-wide die replacement addresses, at the outside, the 0.6-point die contribution — approximately $410,000 per year — for a simple payback near 5.9 years, well outside the plant's three-year capital hurdle. Approving it as scoped would spend the largest available capital sum on the second-largest problem while leaving the largest problem untouched.

### 5.2 Recommended actions

**First, act on the steel, immediately and before June.** Quarantine the remaining inventory from heat lots H-24117 and H-24162 pending disposition; the unconsumed balance is worth far less than the scrap it will generate. Amend the purchasing specification to a sulfur maximum of 0.035 weight percent — comfortably inside the forging process window and consistent with the legacy supplier's demonstrated practice — so that conforming-but-marginal material can no longer pass receiving. Require Allegheny to re-qualify under the tightened specification with a monitored trial lot, or revert the volume to the legacy supplier. Expected recovery: on the order of 1.5 scrap points, roughly $1.0 million annually, at a cost limited to any per-ton price premium for the tighter chemistry and the writedown on quarantined bar. This action requires no capital committee approval and should not wait for one.

**Second, re-scope the June die request from fleet replacement to targeted replacement.** Replace D-12 and D-17 — both beyond 480,000 cycles, both condemned-limit at teardown, and both carrying the interaction effect that makes them the most expensive dies in the fleet to keep running — at an estimated $780,000. Expected recovery is on the order of 0.35 points, roughly $240,000 annually, for a payback near 3.3 years on scrap alone, improving further when avoided unplanned die-change downtime is counted. Gerald Tomczak's underlying concern is vindicated in part: two of his dies are genuinely finished, and the deferral that pushed them past nominal life should not be repeated. But the evidence does not support condemning the other twenty sets, several of which will not approach end-of-life until 2027–2028 under normal wear — and their wear rate itself will slow once the high-sulfur material is out of the process.

**Third, run the discriminating trial before releasing any further die capital.** A two-week designed run — matched part numbers, legacy steel only, split across a new die set and the two remaining oldest survivors — will isolate the pure die effect that the observational data cannot fully pin down. If the trial shows die-attributable scrap materially above the 1.2-point gradient estimated here, the balance of the fleet replacement can return to the Q4 capital agenda with evidence behind it. If it confirms this brief's estimate, the plant will have avoided committing $1.6 million against a problem that two die sets and a purchasing amendment largely solve.

**Fourth, close the two operational gaps the analysis exposed.** The night-shift badge scanner has been replaced, but attribution completeness should become a monitored MES metric so that the next analysis of this kind is not fought with one hand tied. And the night-shift residual effect — the 1.38 adjusted odds ratio that survives all material and die controls — deserves its own follow-up: a structured review of night supervision ratios, the pairing of new hires with experienced operators on complex families, and first-piece inspection discipline after 6 p.m. The turnover wave is a fact; whether it must cost the plant a point of scrap is a management choice.

### 5.3 The bottom line

The scrap increase is real, expensive, and — the central finding of this brief — predominantly a materials problem wearing the disguise of a shift problem and partially misdiagnosed as a die problem. The confounds ran in a direction that flattered the die hypothesis: the worst steel happened to run at night, on the oldest dies, during a period of workforce churn, so every simple comparison pointed somewhere plausible and every simple comparison was partly wrong. Unbraided, the evidence supports spending roughly $780,000 in June rather than $2.4 million, acting on steel sourcing this quarter at near-zero capital cost, and reserving judgment on the remaining dies until a two-week trial can say what fourteen months of production records, for all their volume, cannot quite prove alone. Taken together, the recommended actions address an estimated 2.1 to 2.4 of the 2.7 lost points — on the order of $1.5 million a year — for less than a third of the capital currently on the table.
