**Prepared for the System Executive Council and the Quality Committee of the Board**
**Presented June 12, 2025 | Aminata Sow, MD, MPH, Chief Quality Officer**

## Executive Summary No. 1: Sepsis Early-Warning Bundle

**Program reviewed:** System-wide sepsis early-warning and three-hour bundle program, all four campuses
**Review window:** January 2024 through March 2025

### Finding and Recommendation

The sepsis early-warning bundle is the strongest-performing clinical program this office has evaluated in the current cycle, and one of the strongest in the history of the evaluation function at Vulcan Valley. Over the fifteen-month review window, three-hour bundle compliance rose from 61 percent to 88 percent across all four campuses, and in-hospital sepsis mortality fell from 18.4 percent to 12.9 percent. The program achieved these results on a total investment of $1.9 million. The finding of this review is that the program has met or exceeded every clinical target set at launch, has done so at every campus rather than only at the flagship, and has done so at a cost that compares favorably with any intervention of comparable mortality effect available to this system.

The recommendation to the Executive Council is unambiguous: **fund the program as a permanent operating expense beginning in the coming fiscal year, remove it from pilot status, and extend its methodology to the two adjacent clinical domains — hospital-acquired pneumonia and post-surgical deterioration — where the same early-warning architecture is most likely to transfer.** This office further recommends that the sepsis program's governance model, which paired a physician champion and a nursing champion at each campus with a single system-level dashboard, be treated as the template for future multi-campus clinical initiatives.

### Clinical Evidence

At the start of the review window, sepsis bundle compliance at Vulcan Valley sat at 61 percent, a figure that placed the system below the median for comparable regional health systems and that translated, in human terms, into roughly four of every ten septic patients not receiving blood cultures, broad-spectrum antibiotics, lactate measurement, and appropriate fluid resuscitation within the three-hour window that the evidence base identifies as decisive. In-hospital sepsis mortality stood at 18.4 percent.

The program made three interventions. First, it embedded an early-warning algorithm in the electronic health record that flags physiologic deterioration consistent with sepsis and routes the alert simultaneously to the bedside nurse and the rapid-response team, rather than relying on the traditional sequential escalation that had been costing the system, by our chart-review estimate, a median of ninety minutes per case. Second, it standardized the bundle order set across all four campuses, eliminating the campus-level variation in antibiotic selection and fluid protocols that had made compliance auditing nearly impossible before 2024. Third, it instituted weekly compliance review at the unit level, with every fallout case reviewed within seven days and the findings returned to the treating team.

The results were rapid and durable. Compliance crossed 75 percent within the first two quarters and reached 88 percent by the close of the review window. Critically, the improvement was not concentrated at a single campus: the lowest-performing campus in the system closed the window at 84 percent, and the spread between the best and worst campus narrowed from nineteen points to six. Mortality followed compliance with the lag the literature predicts. In-hospital sepsis mortality declined from 18.4 percent to 12.9 percent, a 5.5-point absolute reduction. Applied to the system's sepsis volume over the review window, that reduction corresponds to an estimated 130 to 145 lives — patients who left our hospitals alive who, at the baseline mortality rate, would not have. This office does not present that estimate lightly, and we have subjected it to sensitivity analysis against case-mix drift and coding change; the finding survives.

We also examined the program for unintended consequences. Alert fatigue was the principal risk identified at launch, and the review found it manageable: the algorithm's positive predictive value held above the threshold set by the clinical steering group, and nursing survey data showed alert-related dissatisfaction declining, not rising, over the window as the workflow matured. Antibiotic stewardship metrics were monitored throughout; broad-spectrum antibiotic days per thousand patient days rose modestly in the first two quarters and then returned to baseline as de-escalation protocols took hold, and no increase in *C. difficile* incidence was observed.

### Financial Evidence

Total program spending over the review window was $1.9 million, covering the EHR build, the campus champion stipends, the compliance-review staffing, and education. Against that outlay, the program generated measurable savings in three categories. Sepsis-related intensive care days declined as earlier intervention reduced progression to septic shock; average sepsis length of stay fell by 1.3 days; and the system's exposure under value-based purchasing and readmission penalty programs improved. Our conservative estimate of direct cost avoidance over the window is $4.6 million to $5.4 million, yielding a return of roughly $2.40 to $2.80 per dollar invested before any accounting for the mortality benefit itself. The cost per estimated life saved falls between $13,000 and $15,000 — a figure that stands on its own.

### Commitments for the Coming Fiscal Year

For the coming fiscal year, the system commits to the following. First, sepsis bundle funding of $1.4 million will move into the base operating budget of each campus, with the reduction from pilot-year spending reflecting the completion of one-time build costs. Second, the system commits to sustaining compliance at or above 85 percent at every campus, with campus-level performance reported quarterly to this Committee; any campus falling below 80 percent for two consecutive quarters will trigger a formal remediation review by this office. Third, the system commits to a design study, due to the Executive Council by the end of the second quarter, on extending the early-warning architecture to hospital-acquired pneumonia and post-surgical deterioration, with a target of piloting at least one extension before the fiscal year closes. Fourth, the system commits to publishing the program's methods and results, because the improvement here is of a magnitude that the broader Alabama hospital community should be able to learn from.

This program is what a clinical investment is supposed to look like. The Council should fund it permanently and ask the rest of the portfolio to meet its standard.

## Executive Summary No. 2: Hospital-at-Home Program

**Program reviewed:** Hospital-at-home acute care program, sponsored by Julio Ferrante, MD, Chief Medical Officer
**Review window:** January 2024 through March 2025

### Finding and Recommendation

The hospital-at-home program has not met its enrollment targets, has not met its clinical targets, and has consumed its budget without producing the census that would allow either problem to be diagnosed with confidence. Over the review window, the program enrolled 96 patients against a plan of 400, spent $4.2 million, and posted a 30-day readmission rate of 14.6 percent against 11.2 percent for a matched inpatient comparison group. The regulatory waiver under which the program bills its principal payer expires September 30, 2025, and its renewal is not assured. The finding of this review is that the program, as currently constituted, is underperforming on every dimension by which it asked to be judged at launch.

The recommendation to the Executive Council is **conditional wind-down**: the program should stop new enrollment at the end of the current quarter unless, before that date, the sponsor presents a restructuring plan that credibly resolves the referral pipeline failure described below, secures written confirmation of the waiver's renewal, and brings the projected cost per episode within 115 percent of the comparable inpatient cost. Absent all three conditions, the program should complete care for currently enrolled patients, close in an orderly fashion before the waiver lapses, and preserve its protocols and staff competencies for possible relaunch under more favorable regulatory and referral conditions. This office wants to be clear that this recommendation is not a judgment against the hospital-at-home model, which has a genuine evidence base nationally. It is a judgment about this implementation at this time.

### Clinical Evidence

The program was designed to deliver inpatient-level acute care in patients' homes for a defined set of conditions — principally heart failure, cellulitis, community-acquired pneumonia, and COPD exacerbation — using twice-daily in-person visits, continuous remote monitoring, and physician oversight by telehealth. The clinical case at launch rested on the national literature showing equivalent or better outcomes at lower cost, with the readmission rate as the headline clinical metric and a target of matching or beating the inpatient comparator.

The program did not meet that target. The 30-day readmission rate for hospital-at-home patients was 14.6 percent, against 11.2 percent for a matched inpatient cohort constructed on diagnosis, age, comorbidity burden, and payer. A 3.4-point unfavorable gap on 96 patients does not reach conventional statistical significance, and the sponsor has argued, fairly, that the sample is too small to condemn the model. This office accepts that argument as far as it goes — but it cuts both ways. The program cannot demonstrate clinical equivalence, and the reason it cannot is the enrollment failure, which is itself a finding. A program that cannot fill its census cannot prove its safety, and a program that cannot prove its safety after fifteen months and $4.2 million cannot ask for indefinite patience.

The enrollment failure has an identifiable anatomy, and the review traced it carefully because it bears directly on whether restructuring is realistic. The program planned for 400 patients on an assumption that hospitalists and emergency physicians would refer eligible patients at the point of the admission decision. In practice, referral required the treating physician to interrupt a familiar admission workflow, complete a separate eligibility screen, and obtain patient consent for an unfamiliar model of care — all during the most compressed minutes of the clinical day. Referral volumes ran at roughly a third of projection from the first quarter and never recovered despite two rounds of physician education. Of patients actually referred, a substantial share declined enrollment, most commonly citing caregiver burden or discomfort with home-based acute care. The pipeline problem, in short, was structural, not promotional, and the review found no evidence that further education alone would fix it. Any credible restructuring must embed eligibility screening into the EHR admission pathway itself so that qualifying patients are identified automatically rather than by physician recall.

On the care actually delivered, the review found genuine strengths that the Council should weigh. There were no serious safety events, no escalation failures, and patient-experience scores among the enrolled — while drawn from a small and self-selected sample — were the highest of any program in this review cycle. The clinical teams performed well. The model around them did not.

### Financial Evidence

The program spent $4.2 million over the review window to serve 96 patients — a cost of approximately $43,700 per enrolled patient, against a planned per-patient cost, at full census, of roughly $10,500. The overwhelming driver of the gap is fixed cost spread over a fractional census: the program's staffing, monitoring platform, and logistics infrastructure were built for 400 patients and carried at that scale throughout. At full census, the model's unit economics may well work; at one-quarter census, they cannot, and the review found no scenario under current referral performance in which the program reaches break-even census within the coming fiscal year.

The waiver deadline compounds the financial risk. The program's payment model depends on a payer waiver permitting inpatient-equivalent reimbursement for home-based acute care, and that waiver expires September 30, 2025. If it lapses, the program's revenue model collapses regardless of census. Continuing to enroll patients into a program whose payment authority may terminate in under four months, without written assurance of renewal, would be imprudent.

### Commitments for the Coming Fiscal Year

For the coming fiscal year, the system commits to the following. First, no new enrollment after the end of the current quarter unless the three conditions above — a credible referral restructuring embedded in the admission workflow, written waiver renewal, and a cost-per-episode projection within 115 percent of inpatient cost — are met and verified by this office. Second, if wind-down proceeds, the system commits to completing care for all enrolled patients to full episode closure, to a documented preservation of the program's clinical protocols, monitoring vendor terms, and staff competencies, and to a formal after-action report to this Committee within ninety days of closure. Third, whether the program continues or closes, the system commits to a decision memorandum from the Chief Medical Officer to the Executive Council no later than August 15, 2025, so that the waiver deadline is met by a decision rather than by default. Fourth, the system commits that any future relaunch proposal will come back through this office with enrollment assumptions grounded in the referral performance actually observed here, not in the national literature's best cases.

Dr. Ferrante has been a candid and cooperative sponsor throughout this review, and his conviction that the model can work at Vulcan Valley may yet be vindicated. But conviction is not census, and the Council should hold this program to its own numbers.

## Executive Summary No. 3: Community Paramedic Program

**Program reviewed:** Community paramedic program for high-utilizing emergency department patients
**Review window:** January 2024 through March 2025

### Finding and Recommendation

The community paramedic program presents the most genuinely mixed picture in this review cycle, and this office has resisted the temptation to resolve the ambiguity in either direction by rhetorical force. The program enrolled 212 high-utilizing emergency department patients over the review window and reduced their emergency visits by 34 percent — a clinical result that is real, clinically meaningful, and consistent with the better programs in the national literature. At the same time, the program enrolled only 40 percent of the patients referred to it, and it has produced no measurable cost offset: the emergency visits avoided have not translated into savings the finance function can identify on any ledger. The finding of this review is that the program works clinically for the patients it reaches, reaches too few of them, and has not yet demonstrated that its clinical effect carries an economic one.

The recommendation to the Executive Council is **conditional continuation for one further fiscal year**, on three explicit conditions: that enrollment conversion rise from 40 percent to at least 60 percent of referred patients; that total enrollment reach at least 350 patients; and that the program, working with the finance function under a measurement framework this office will specify, either demonstrate a measurable cost offset or demonstrate rigorously why the offset is structural rather than remediable. If those conditions are not met by the third quarter of the coming fiscal year, the program should come back to the Council for a termination decision with the evidence in hand.

### Clinical Evidence

The program targets patients with six or more emergency department visits in the trailing twelve months — a population that at Vulcan Valley, as everywhere, is small in number and enormous in resource consumption, and whose visits are driven as much by unmanaged chronic disease, behavioral health need, medication failure, and social instability as by acute illness. Enrolled patients receive scheduled home visits from specially trained paramedics who perform clinical assessment, medication reconciliation, chronic disease monitoring, and connection to primary care and social services, with escalation pathways to the patient's physician rather than to the emergency department.

For the 212 patients enrolled, the results are good. Emergency department visits among enrollees fell 34 percent against each patient's own prior-year baseline, and the review tested that figure against the obvious methodological threat — regression to the mean, since high utilizers tend to fall toward average utilization over time regardless of intervention. Using a comparison group of eligible-but-unenrolled patients, the review found that enrollees' visit reduction substantially exceeded the background decline in the comparison group; the program effect survives the correction, though at a somewhat smaller magnitude than the headline figure. Chart review found credible individual mechanisms behind the aggregate number: medication regimens corrected in the home, decompensating heart failure caught before crisis, patients connected to primary care after years without it. Patient-level testimony gathered during the review was, in several cases, moving, and the Committee should not discount it merely because it is anecdotal; these are among the most medically and socially fragile patients the system serves.

The enrollment problem, however, is serious and is where the clinical promise leaks away. Of patients referred to the program — by emergency physicians, case managers, and the utilization data itself — only 40 percent enrolled. The review examined the fallout and found three drivers of roughly equal weight: patients who could not be contacted, most often because of unstable housing or disconnected phones; patients contacted who declined, frequently expressing distrust of home visits; and referrals lost to administrative delay, where the interval between referral and first outreach attempt stretched beyond two weeks and the moment of patient receptivity passed. The first driver is partly structural, but the second and third are remediable: programs elsewhere have raised conversion materially through warm handoffs at the emergency department bedside — enrolling the patient during the visit rather than by phone afterward — and through outreach within 72 hours of referral. The program has done neither systematically, and this office regards that as the central operational failure of the review window.

### Financial Evidence

The economic case is where the program has not delivered, and the Council deserves a candid account of why. A 34 percent reduction in emergency visits among 212 patients is a real reduction in utilization, but it has produced no measurable cost offset for three reasons the review was able to identify. First, the avoided visits are marginal volume in departments whose staffing and fixed costs do not flex at this scale; the system's costs do not fall because 212 patients came less often. Second, a meaningful share of the target population is covered under fee-for-service arrangements in which avoided visits are, bluntly, avoided revenue as well as avoided cost — a payment-model problem, not a program failure, but a real constraint on the business case. Third, and most correctably, the program launched without a cost-measurement framework, so offsets that may exist — in avoided admissions, in reduced observation stays, in value-based contract performance — were never instrumented for capture.

The program's operating cost over the window was modest relative to the other programs in this review, and this office notes that its cost per enrolled patient would fall substantially at the enrollment volumes the conditions above require. But a program cannot be sustained indefinitely on clinical goodwill and an unmeasured business case. The coming fiscal year must either produce the measurement or produce the decision.

### Commitments for the Coming Fiscal Year

For the coming fiscal year, the system commits to the following. First, the program will implement bedside enrollment in all four emergency departments and a 72-hour outreach standard for all other referrals, with conversion reported monthly. Second, the system commits to the enrollment targets stated above — 60 percent conversion and 350 total enrolled — as conditions of continued funding, reported quarterly to this Committee. Third, the finance function and this office will jointly build and operate a cost-offset measurement framework, capturing avoided admissions and observation stays, value-based contract effects, and total cost of care for enrolled patients under capitated arrangements, with a first report in the second quarter. Fourth, the system commits to pursuing, through its payer contracting function, at least one value-based arrangement under which the program's utilization reductions accrue to the system rather than against it. Fifth, the system commits to a go/no-go review before this Committee in the third quarter of the coming fiscal year, at which the program will be continued, restructured, or closed on the evidence.

The program is doing right by its patients. The coming year must determine whether it can also do right by the system that funds it, and the conditions above are designed to make that determination unavoidable.

## Executive Summary No. 4: Scheduling Center Consolidation

**Program reviewed:** Centralized patient scheduling center consolidation, led by Bridget Nkemelu, RN, MSN, Nurse Executive, and Meredith Tanaka-Bell, Chief Financial Officer
**Review window:** January 2024 through March 2025

### Finding and Recommendation

The scheduling center consolidation is 59 percent over budget and is missing its central performance target by a factor of more than three. The project was budgeted at $6.1 million and has spent $9.7 million. The consolidated center now handles 380,000 calls per quarter — the full intended volume, which the review confirms as a genuine operational accomplishment — but abandons 17 percent of those calls against a 5 percent target. At current volume, that gap represents roughly 45,000 abandoned calls every quarter: patients who tried to schedule care with this system and gave up before anyone answered. The finding of this review is that the consolidation succeeded as a migration and has failed, to date, as a service, and that its cost overrun was driven by identifiable planning failures rather than by external shock.

The recommendation to the Executive Council is **corrective action under enhanced oversight, not reversal**. The review examined the alternative of de-consolidating back to campus-level scheduling and found it would cost an estimated $3 million to $4 million more, take twelve to eighteen months, and return the system to a fragmented model whose deficiencies motivated this project in the first place. The consolidated center is the right structure operating badly, and the remedy is to fix its operation. Specifically, this office recommends that the Council: place the center under a formal performance improvement plan with monthly reporting to the Executive Council and quarterly reporting to this Committee; approve a defined and capped remediation investment tied to the staffing and technology corrections described below; and set an abandonment-rate glide path of 12 percent by the end of the second quarter of the coming fiscal year, 8 percent by the end of the third, and the 5 percent target by fiscal year end, with a mandatory return to the Council if any milestone is missed.

### Operational and Clinical Evidence

The consolidation replaced eleven separate campus- and clinic-level scheduling operations with a single center intended to give patients one number, standardize scheduling protocols, and improve schedule utilization across the system. The migration itself is complete: all four campuses and their associated ambulatory sites route scheduling through the center, and the 380,000 calls per quarter it handles confirm that the intended volume arrived. The review credits the project team, and Ms. Nkemelu's operational leadership in particular, for executing a complex migration without a service interruption at any site.

But volume arrived faster than capability. The 17 percent abandonment rate is the headline failure, and the review traced it to three root causes. First, the staffing model was built on a consultant's call-volume forecast that proved low by roughly a quarter, because it was derived from the legacy centers' recorded volumes — which never captured calls that failed at the old, worse phone systems, calls handled informally at front desks, and the surge of repeat calls that abandonment itself generates. The center has been understaffed against true demand from the day it opened. Second, agent training was compressed to meet the migration timeline; agents were brought live on specialty scheduling protocols they had not mastered, which lengthened handle times, which lengthened queues, which raised abandonment in a self-reinforcing loop. Average handle time remains well above the design assumption, and the review found agent turnover in the center running high — an entirely predictable consequence of staffing a call center at a level that guarantees its agents spend every shift losing. Third, the telephony platform's intelligent routing and callback features, which were designed to absorb exactly this kind of demand surge, were descoped during the budget crisis described below and have not been fully deployed.

The Committee should be clear about why abandonment is a clinical issue and not merely a service metric. The review sampled abandoned-call patients and found measurable downstream effects: delayed specialty appointments, patients diverting to the emergency department for schedulable needs, and referral leakage to competing systems. Every abandoned call is a patient the system invited to seek care elsewhere. On the positive side of the ledger, the review confirms real gains that argue for repair rather than retreat: no-show rates at consolidated clinics have declined, schedule utilization in several high-demand specialties has improved as the center fills cancellations from a shared queue, and patients who do reach an agent report satisfaction comparable to the legacy baseline. The structure is generating exactly the benefits it promised — for the 83 percent of callers who get through.

### Financial Evidence

The $3.6 million overrun decomposes, in the review's accounting, into three parts. Roughly $1.4 million reflects telephony and EHR-integration costs that were underscoped at approval: the integration work needed to give a centralized agent visibility into eleven legacy scheduling templates was materially more complex than budgeted, and the review found that this complexity was flagged by the IT function during planning and not carried into the budget. Roughly $1.2 million reflects staffing above plan, as the center hired against actual rather than forecast volume. The remaining approximately $1.0 million reflects extended dual operations, as legacy sites ran longer than planned during a migration that slipped two quarters. The review finds these overruns were foreseeable and, in the case of the integration costs, foreseen; the project's governance did not have a mechanism that forced re-baselining when the early signals appeared, and spending continued against a budget everyone involved knew to be obsolete by mid-2024. This is a governance finding as much as a financial one, and this office recommends the Council adopt a standing re-baselining trigger — mandatory Council review at 15 percent variance — for all future capital projects of this scale.

The forward economics nonetheless favor completion. The center's steady-state operating cost, even at corrected staffing, remains below the combined cost of the eleven legacy operations it replaced, and the revenue now leaking through abandoned calls — which the finance function conservatively estimates in the millions annually across diverted visits and lost referrals — is recoverable through the remediation investment, which is a fraction of that leakage. The worst available outcome, financially, is the current one sustained: full consolidation cost incurred, full consolidation benefit unrealized.

### Commitments for the Coming Fiscal Year

For the coming fiscal year, the system commits to the following. First, a capped remediation investment, jointly certified by Ms. Nkemelu and Ms. Tanaka-Bell, funding the corrected staffing model, a rebuilt training program with specialty-protocol certification before agents take live calls, and full deployment of the callback and intelligent-routing capabilities descoped in 2024. Second, the abandonment glide path stated above — 12 percent, 8 percent, and 5 percent by fiscal year end — with monthly reporting to the Executive Council and an automatic return to the Council for a structural decision if any milestone is missed. Third, publication of a patient-facing service standard, including a maximum-wait commitment and guaranteed callback, so that the center is accountable to the people it serves and not only to its dashboards. Fourth, no further scope additions to the center — no new service lines, no new sites — until the 8 percent milestone is achieved. Fifth, adoption of the 15 percent re-baselining trigger for future capital projects, so that the governance failure documented here is not repeated elsewhere in the portfolio.

The consolidation was the right decision executed on wrong assumptions. The coming fiscal year is the window in which its leaders have committed to prove the distinction matters, and this office will report on their progress at every meeting of this Committee until the target is met.
