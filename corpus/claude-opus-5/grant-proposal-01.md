# Rural Readers Initiative

## Application Narrative

**Applicant:** Kings Basin Unified School District, Hanford, California
**Project Title:** Kings Basin Reads: Coaching for Early Literacy in a Rural Agricultural Community
**Request:** $2,400,000 over four years ($600,000 annually)
**Required Match:** $600,000 cash (25 percent), committed
**Primary Contact:** DeAndre Booker, Grants Manager
**Submission Deadline:** December 12

## Statement of Need

Kings Basin Unified School District serves 6,420 students across eleven schools in and around Hanford, California, in the agricultural center of Kings County. The district's enrollment reflects the communities that sustain the region's dairy, tree nut, and row crop economy. Seventy-four percent of students are Hispanic or Latino. Nine percent speak Punjabi at home, the legacy of a Sikh farming community established here across three generations. Eighty-two percent qualify for free or reduced-price meals. Thirty-six percent are classified as English learners. Twelve percent are eligible for migrant education services, meaning their families move for agricultural work and their children's schooling is interrupted by that movement.

Districtwide third-grade English language arts proficiency stands at 24 percent. Three of every four children in Kings Basin finish third grade unable to read at grade level.

That number is the center of this application, and we want to be precise about what it means rather than let it function as a rhetorical device. Third grade is the year instruction stops teaching children to read and begins requiring them to read in order to learn. A child who arrives in fourth grade without fluent decoding and adequate vocabulary does not simply fall behind in reading. That child falls behind in science, in social studies, in the word problems that carry mathematics, and increasingly in the disposition to try. The research on this transition is unambiguous, and so is our local experience. Our own longitudinal data show that students who score below standard in third-grade ELA are four times as likely to be chronically absent by seventh grade and less than half as likely to complete A–G requirements by graduation.

At 24 percent proficiency, Kings Basin is producing roughly 340 fourth graders each year who are not ready for what fourth grade asks of them. Over the four years of the grant period, that is approximately 1,360 children.

### Why proficiency is where it is

We do not attribute this outcome to any single cause, and we are skeptical of applications that do. Several conditions compound one another here.

**Instructional coherence has been uneven.** Kings Basin adopted a new ELA core program in 2021, but adoption is not implementation. Classroom observation data collected by Director of Curriculum Elena Vasquez-Ortiz across the 2024–25 school year found substantial variation in how the program's foundational skills block was delivered. In a sample of 84 K–3 classroom observations, 41 percent showed a foundational skills block that lasted fewer than 20 minutes against a recommended 45, and 29 percent showed no explicit phonemic awareness instruction at all in the observed window. This is not a story about teacher unwillingness. It is a story about what happens when a complex program is handed to teachers without sustained, job-embedded support for the specific instructional moves it requires.

**Language development and literacy instruction are frequently treated as separate projects.** With 36 percent of students designated English learners and a substantial number of others who are fluent English proficient but still developing academic language, integrated and designated English language development must be woven into literacy instruction rather than appended to it. Our observations found designated ELD most often scheduled at the end of the day, delivered by whichever adult was available, and disconnected from the morning's literacy content.

**Mobility interrupts instruction for our migrant-eligible students.** Twelve percent of our students — approximately 770 children — are eligible for migrant education services. For a first grader who leaves in October and returns in March, the phonics scope and sequence is not a sequence at all. It is a set of fragments. Our current systems for diagnosing and closing those gaps on re-entry are inconsistent across schools.

**Multilingual assets are underused.** Nine percent of our students speak Punjabi at home. We have exactly two credentialed staff members who speak Punjabi. Our Spanish-language family engagement materials are strong; our Punjabi-language materials are minimal. Families who could be powerful partners in early literacy are receiving less from us than they should.

**Chronic absenteeism is elevated.** Districtwide chronic absenteeism was 24.1 percent in 2024–25, above the state average and concentrated in the K–3 grades at our highest-poverty sites. A child who misses eighteen days of first grade misses roughly a tenth of the phonics instruction that year.

### Why rural matters here

Hanford is 35 miles from Fresno and roughly three hours from either Sacramento or Los Angeles. This geography has practical consequences for school improvement that funders located in metropolitan areas sometimes underestimate.

Professional development that requires travel costs us more per participant and takes teachers out of classrooms longer. The regional labor market for credentialed teachers is thin, and thinner still for teachers with reading specialist credentials or bilingual authorization. When we post a K–3 opening, we often receive fewer than five qualified applicants. In 2024–25 we filled four positions with interns still completing credential programs. Our teacher retention rate districtwide was 84 percent, but at our three highest-need elementary sites it was 76 percent, meaning roughly one in four teachers at the schools that most need instructional continuity left within a year.

External vendors charge travel premiums to reach us or decline to serve us at all. The consultant model that works in a district with 60,000 students and a dense provider market does not work in Kings Basin. What works here is building capacity inside the organization and inside the region — which is precisely the design logic of this proposal.

### What we already know works, because we did it

Between 2019 and 2023, Kings Basin operated an early literacy coaching model at three elementary schools, funded through a combination of Title I set-aside and a state early literacy grant. Over that four-year period, third-grade ELA proficiency at those three sites rose from 21 percent to 34 percent — an increase of 13 percentage points, or 62 percent in relative terms. Across the same period, districtwide proficiency was essentially flat, moving from 23 percent to 24 percent.

We want to characterize this evidence honestly. This was not a randomized trial. The three sites were selected in part because their principals volunteered, which introduces selection effects we cannot fully rule out. The comparison is to the district as a whole rather than to matched schools. Staff turnover at the three sites during the period was somewhat lower than district average, which may be an effect of the coaching or may be a confound.

What we can say is this: three schools serving demographically comparable populations, working within the same district systems and the same fiscal constraints, moved a metric that the rest of the district did not move, over a sustained period, using a defined and documented model. That is a stronger evidentiary basis than most rural districts can offer for most of what they propose, and it is our own evidence about our own children.

The model ended in 2023 when the state grant concluded. Proficiency at the three sites has since declined to 31 percent — still well above district average, but drifting. We understand that drift as the predictable consequence of removing a support structure rather than institutionalizing it. This application is our attempt to do it correctly the second time.

## Project Description

**Kings Basin Reads** extends the 2019–2023 coaching model to eight elementary schools serving approximately 2,900 students in transitional kindergarten through third grade, while restoring and deepening the model at the three original sites. The project's central claim is simple: sustained, job-embedded instructional coaching, delivered by well-supervised coaches with protected time and clear instructional focus, changes what happens in classrooms, and what happens in classrooms changes what children can read.

### The coaching model

The model rests on five components, all carried forward from the 2019–2023 implementation with refinements based on what we learned.

**One, coaches are full-time and dedicated.** Each coach serves two schools and carries no classroom teaching assignment, no testing coordination, and no substitute coverage duty. The single most common failure mode in school coaching is the coach who is also everything else. In our prior implementation, we protected coach time in year one, allowed it to erode in year two under site-level staffing pressure, and had to formally re-protect it in year three through a memorandum signed by each principal. We will begin with that memorandum rather than arrive at it.

Four coaches will be hired, serving eight schools. Coaches will be recruited first from within the district, with preference for teachers who have demonstrated strong K–3 literacy outcomes and who hold or are pursuing a reading certificate. At least one coach position will be designated bilingual Spanish, and we will actively recruit a Punjabi-speaking candidate.

**Two, coaching follows a defined cycle.** Each coach works with each assigned teacher in three-week cycles: a planning conversation grounded in student work and assessment data, at least two in-classroom sessions (modeling, co-teaching, or observation with immediate feedback), and a debrief that sets the focus for the next cycle. Coaches log every cycle in a shared system. Target dosage is a minimum of 18 cycles per teacher per year for K–3 teachers.

**Three, the instructional focus is narrow and explicit.** Coaching addresses foundational skills instruction — phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency — and the integration of oral language and vocabulary development for multilingual learners. Coaches do not coach on classroom management, technology integration, or general pedagogy. Narrowness is what makes coaching effective; the temptation to broaden it is what makes it ineffective.

**Four, coaches work with grade-level teams, not only individuals.** Each coach facilitates a bi-weekly grade-level data meeting at each site, using DIBELS progress monitoring and curriculum-embedded assessment to identify students needing intervention and to surface instructional patterns.

**Five, coaches are supervised by someone who is not the principal.** This is a structural point we consider essential. Coaches supervised by site principals become quasi-administrators, and teachers stop being candid with them. In our prior implementation, coach supervision sat with a single district coaching lead — a design whose fragility we discuss below. In this proposal, coach supervision is contracted to the Kings County Office of Education.

### Partner roles

**Kings County Office of Education — coach supervision and professional learning.** KCOE will provide a 0.5 FTE literacy coordinator who supervises the four coaches, facilitates a monthly coach professional learning community, conducts quarterly co-observation with each coach, and serves as the escalation point when coaching relationships stall. KCOE's involvement means coach supervision does not depend on a single district employee, and it embeds the model in a regional structure that outlasts our grant. KCOE has committed in writing and will be compensated from grant funds at $78,000 annually.

**West Hills Community College — paraeducator-to-teacher pathway.** West Hills operates campuses in Coalinga and Lemoore, the latter twelve miles from Hanford. The pathway supports up to twelve Kings Basin paraeducators per cohort in completing an associate degree and transferring into a bachelor's and credential program, with coursework scheduled around the school day and delivered partly at Kings Basin sites. Grant funds cover tuition assistance, books, and a stipend for paraeducators enrolled and in good standing.

This partnership addresses our teacher pipeline problem directly. Our paraeducators are overwhelmingly local, overwhelmingly bilingual, and overwhelmingly people who intend to stay in Kings County. They are the most promising source of the stable, linguistically matched teaching workforce our children need. We project eight to ten pathway participants entering credential programs over four years.

**Kings County Library — summer literacy program.** Summer learning loss is a documented driver of the achievement gap and is amplified for migrant-eligible students whose families travel. The library will operate a six-week summer program at three branches and two school sites, serving approximately 400 K–3 students annually, staffed by library personnel and Kings Basin teachers on summer contracts. Programming includes small-group reading, book distribution (ten books per child annually, to be kept), and family literacy sessions in English, Spanish, and Punjabi. Grant funds support teacher summer contracts and book purchases at $52,000 annually; the library contributes staff time and facilities.

### Scale and phasing

**Year 1:** Hire and train four coaches; restore full model at the three original sites; launch at three additional sites (six schools total). Establish KCOE supervision. First pathway cohort of six paraeducators. First summer program.

**Year 2:** Expand to eight schools. Second pathway cohort. Refine intervention protocols for mobile students based on year-one data.

**Year 3:** Full implementation at eight schools. Third pathway cohort. Begin transition planning, including identification of positions to be absorbed into ongoing funding.

**Year 4:** Full implementation. Fourth pathway cohort. Complete transition of coaching positions to sustaining funds per the schedule in the Sustainability section. Final evaluation.

### Serving mobile and multilingual students

Two design elements specifically address populations our current systems serve least well.

For migrant-eligible students, coaches will implement a re-entry protocol: any K–3 student enrolling or re-enrolling after the first four weeks of school receives a DIBELS assessment within five instructional days, and results generate a specific intervention plan reviewed at the next grade-level data meeting. We will also establish records-transfer coordination with districts in the Salinas Valley, the Imperial Valley, and Yuma County, Arizona, the three destinations our families most commonly report.

For Punjabi-speaking families, we will develop family literacy materials in Punjabi, contract interpretation for family literacy sessions and parent-teacher conferences at the three sites with the highest Punjabi enrollment, and prioritize Punjabi-speaking candidates in both coach recruitment and the paraeducator pathway. We state plainly that we are starting from a low baseline here and that progress will be incremental.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

We present three goals with objectives tied to the five metrics the foundation requires for annual reporting, plus implementation measures that let us detect problems before outcome data would reveal them.

### Goal 1: Increase third-grade English language arts proficiency at participating schools.

**Objective 1.1.** Third-grade ELA proficiency at the eight participating schools will rise from a baseline of 24 percent (2024–25) to 36 percent by spring of Year 4, as measured by CAASPP.

*Interim targets: 26 percent (Y1), 29 percent (Y2), 33 percent (Y3), 36 percent (Y4).*

This target is calibrated to our prior results — 13 points over four years at three sites — adjusted downward slightly for the greater difficulty of scaling to eight schools and adjusted for the fact that three sites begin at 31 percent rather than 21 percent. We could have proposed a more impressive number. We would rather propose one we believe.

**Objective 1.2.** Third-grade ELA proficiency among English learners and reclassified fluent English proficient students at participating schools will rise from 16 percent to 27 percent by Year 4.

**Objective 1.3.** Third-grade ELA proficiency among migrant-eligible students will rise from 14 percent to 24 percent by Year 4.

### Goal 2: Improve foundational reading skill development in grades K–3.

**Objective 2.1.** The percentage of K–3 students at or above benchmark on the DIBELS composite score will increase from 38 percent to 55 percent by end of Year 4, measured at spring administration.

*Interim targets: 42 percent (Y1), 46 percent (Y2), 51 percent (Y3), 55 percent (Y4).*

**Objective 2.2.** The percentage of K–3 students in the DIBELS "well below benchmark" category will decrease from 31 percent to 18 percent by Year 4.

**Objective 2.3.** Median within-year DIBELS composite growth for students beginning the year below benchmark will meet or exceed the 60th percentile of national growth norms in each of Years 2 through 4.

Objective 2.3 matters because it isolates the question of whether we are moving the children who most need moving, independent of enrollment composition changes.

### Goal 3: Strengthen conditions that sustain literacy instruction — attendance, teacher stability, and instructional capacity.

**Objective 3.1.** Chronic absenteeism in grades K–3 at participating schools will decrease from 24.1 percent to 16 percent by Year 4.

*Interim targets: 22 percent (Y1), 20 percent (Y2), 18 percent (Y3), 16 percent (Y4).*

We are not proposing coaching as an attendance intervention and would not claim that causal chain. Coaches are one element of a broader attendance strategy that includes existing site attendance teams and community liaison staffing funded outside this grant. We report the metric because the foundation requires it and because it is a genuine condition of literacy growth.

**Objective 3.2.** Year-over-year teacher retention at participating schools will increase from 76 percent to 87 percent by Year 4, and will exceed district average in each year of the grant.

**Objective 3.3.** At least twelve Kings Basin paraeducators will be enrolled in the West Hills pathway by end of Year 2, and at least eight will have entered a credential program by end of Year 4.

**Objective 3.4.** Cost per student served will not exceed $210 in any grant year, calculated as total grant expenditure plus match divided by unduplicated K–3 students at participating schools.

*Projected: $750,000 annual project cost ÷ approximately 2,900 K–3 students = $259 in Year 1, declining to approximately $195 by Year 4 as enrollment served expands and startup costs retire.*

We report this figure with a caveat we think the foundation will appreciate: cost per student served is a useful budget discipline and a poor measure of value. A dollar spent on a coach reaches every child that coach's teachers teach, this year and in subsequent years. We will report the metric as defined and will also report cost per student moved from below to at benchmark on DIBELS, which we consider more informative.

### Implementation objectives

**Objective 4.1.** Each K–3 teacher at participating schools will receive a minimum of 18 documented coaching cycles annually beginning in Year 2 (14 in Year 1, accounting for hiring and onboarding).

**Objective 4.2.** Classroom observation data will show at least 85 percent of K–3 classrooms delivering a foundational skills block of 40 minutes or more by end of Year 2, up from 59 percent at baseline.

**Objective 4.3.** One hundred percent of K–3 students enrolling after the first four weeks will receive a DIBELS assessment within five instructional days beginning in Year 2.

**Objective 4.4.** At least 400 K–3 students will participate in the summer literacy program annually, with at least 60 percent attending four or more of six weeks.

## Evaluation Plan

### Design

Kings Basin Reads will be evaluated through a **quasi-experimental comparative interrupted time series** design supplemented by implementation fidelity measurement and qualitative inquiry.

The outcome analysis compares the eight participating schools against a comparison group constructed from the three non-participating Kings Basin elementary schools and, to increase statistical power, a set of matched schools drawn from neighboring Kings and Tulare county districts. Matching will use percentage free or reduced-price meal eligible, percentage English learner, percentage migrant-eligible, enrollment size, and three prior years of third-grade ELA proficiency. We anticipate a comparison pool of twelve to sixteen schools.

We selected this design because random assignment is not feasible — we cannot ethically or practically withhold coaching from schools within our own district — and because the interrupted time series structure allows us to use the pre-intervention trend at participating schools as part of the counterfactual, which strengthens causal inference relative to simple pre-post comparison.

We acknowledge the design's limits. Schools were not randomly selected. Comparison schools operate under different district leadership and may adopt their own initiatives. We will document known comparison-school initiatives annually and report them as threats to validity rather than discovering them at the end.

### External evaluator

Kings Basin will contract an independent external evaluator through a competitive RFP process in the first quarter of Year 1, budgeted at $58,000 annually. Required qualifications include prior experience evaluating literacy interventions, familiarity with California assessment systems, and demonstrated capacity to work with rural districts. The evaluator will report to a project steering committee, not to the superintendent alone, and the contract will specify that the evaluator retains authority over the content of evaluation reports.

We include that last provision deliberately. An evaluator who can be edited is not independent.

### Data sources and collection

| Measure | Source | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Third-grade ELA proficiency | CAASPP | Annual (spring) |
| DIBELS composite and subtests | Amplify mCLASS | 3× annually + progress monitoring |
| Chronic absenteeism | Aeries SIS | Monthly; annual for reporting |
| Teacher retention | HR records | Annual (October 1 snapshot) |
| Cost per student served | Escape financial system | Annual |
| Coaching cycles delivered | Coaching log | Continuous |
| Classroom instructional practice | Structured observation protocol | 2× annually, stratified sample |
| Teacher perception of coaching | Anonymous survey | Annual (spring) |
| Family engagement | Sign-in and survey | Per event |
| Summer program participation | Library and district records | Annual |

### Implementation fidelity

Outcome evaluation without fidelity measurement produces uninterpretable results. If proficiency does not move, we need to know whether the model failed or whether the model was never delivered. Fidelity will be assessed against five indicators: coaching cycle dosage, coach time protection (percentage of coach time in coaching activity, from time logs, target ≥70 percent), grade-level data meeting frequency, instructional focus adherence (from coaching logs coded by the evaluator), and supervision contact.

Each participating school receives an annual fidelity rating. Outcome analysis will be reported both for all participating schools and disaggregated by fidelity level.

### Qualitative component

The evaluator will conduct annual focus groups with teachers (four groups, stratified by grade band and site), interviews with all coaches and principals, and interviews with a sample of twenty families including Spanish-speaking and Punjabi-speaking families with interpretation provided. Qualitative work addresses questions the quantitative data cannot: why coaching works better at some sites, what teachers experience as useful versus performative, what families understand about their children's reading.

### Reporting

Annual reports will be submitted to the Aldous Renwick Foundation within 90 days of the close of each grant year, covering all five required metrics — third-grade proficiency, DIBELS composite growth, chronic absenteeism, teacher retention at participating schools, and cost per student served — plus implementation fidelity, partner activity, and budget-to-actual. Reports will include disaggregation by English learner status, migrant eligibility, and home language.

A summative report at the close of Year 4 will address the central evaluation question: did Kings Basin Reads produce third-grade reading gains at participating schools beyond what would have occurred otherwise, and under what implementation conditions?

We commit in advance to reporting null or negative findings with the same clarity as positive ones. A funder who receives only good news from a grantee is not receiving information.

### Continuous improvement

Evaluation data will be reviewed quarterly by the steering committee — the superintendent, director of curriculum, assistant superintendent of business, grants manager, KCOE literacy coordinator, two principals on rotation, and two teacher representatives. The committee has authority to adjust implementation mid-year. In our prior implementation, we learned in year two that coaches were spending disproportionate time with teachers who sought them out and less with teachers who did not, precisely inverting the need distribution. We corrected it with an assignment protocol. That kind of correction requires data reviewed on a cycle short enough to act on.

### Two matters in our record

We describe both fully because we would rather the foundation hear them from us.

**The lapsed Delaney Trust award.** In 2022, Kings Basin received a $600,000 award from the Delaney Trust to support early literacy intervention. The award lapsed in 2024 with $410,000 drawn — $190,000, or 32 percent, unexpended.

The proximate cause was the resignation of the district's coaching lead in March 2023. That individual held sole responsibility for the coaching component, supervised the coaches, approved coaching-related expenditures, and maintained the relationship with the funder. When she left for a county office position, the district did not backfill for seven months. During that period coaching activity contracted, associated expenditures did not occur, and no one held the responsibility of noticing that drawdown had fallen behind schedule.

The underlying cause was structural. We had built a single-point-of-failure design and had no monitoring system that would surface the failure before it became irreversible. Grants management at the time was distributed across program directors without a central function.

Four things changed as a result. First, the district created the grants manager position, filled by DeAndre Booker in January 2024, with explicit responsibility for monthly drawdown monitoring across all awards and a standing report to the superintendent's cabinet on any award more than 15 percent behind its expenditure schedule. Second, we adopted a policy that no grant-funded position central to a project's operation may be the sole holder of project knowledge; every key position now has a documented backup and a transition file. Third, this project's design places coach supervision with KCOE rather than with a single district employee, which is a direct architectural response to what failed. Fourth, the project's staffing plan includes the project director role at 0.4 FTE held by Dr. Vasquez-Ortiz, with the superintendent as designated backup, so that project continuity does not depend on any one person's continued employment.

We also note that the Delaney Trust award was not mismanaged in the sense of funds misspent. Every dollar drawn was drawn appropriately and documented. The failure was one of execution capacity, not integrity. We believe that distinction matters, and we believe the corrections we made address the actual failure rather than gesturing at it.

**The FY2024 single audit finding.** The district's FY2024 single audit under Uniform Guidance included a finding related to time-and-effort certification for federally funded staff. Specifically, the auditors found that semi-annual certifications for employees working solely on a single federal award were not consistently obtained within the required period, and that for a sample of employees splitting time across multiple funding sources, personnel activity reports did not consistently reflect after-the-fact distribution of actual activity. The finding was classified as significant deficiency, not material weakness. There was no questioned cost finding and no finding of unallowable expenditure.

Assistant Superintendent Tanabe assumed responsibility for corrective action upon receipt of the finding. The corrective action plan, accepted by the California Department of Education, includes: implementation of an electronic time-and-effort certification module within Escape, deployed August 2025, which generates automated certification prompts and blocks payroll certification until outstanding attestations clear; a revised written procedure specifying certification frequency, approval authority, and retention; training for all site administrators and department heads, completed October 2025; and quarterly internal review by the business office with results reported to the superintendent.

The FY2025 audit fieldwork was completed in October 2025. The preliminary management letter indicates the prior finding has been resolved and no repeat finding is anticipated. Final FY2025 audit issuance is expected in January 2026, and we will provide it to the foundation upon receipt whether or not the outcome is favorable.

We recognize that a lapsed award and an audit finding in adjacent years present a pattern a careful funder will notice. Our position is that both stemmed from thin administrative infrastructure in a district that had grown its grant portfolio faster than its capacity to manage it, that both have been addressed with structural rather than cosmetic changes, and that the resulting infrastructure — a dedicated grants manager, an electronic certification system, distributed project accountability — is materially stronger than what existed when either problem arose. We would rather present a district that has fixed identifiable problems than one that has never been tested.

## Sustainability

Sustainability is the section where grant applications most often become unserious. We will try not to.

The honest problem is this. Kings Basin's supplemental and concentration funding under California's Local Control Funding Formula is already committed through 2030 to obligations adopted in the current Local Control and Accountability Plan: community liaison positions, expanded transitional kindergarten staffing, the multi-tiered system of supports team, and instructional aide ratios at high-need sites. There is no unallocated pool waiting to absorb four coaching positions in 2030. Any application that claims otherwise about a district in our position is either uninformed or dissembling.

So the question DeAndre Booker must answer — how does coaching continue after year four — has to be answered with a specific plan rather than an assurance.

### The plan

**One: staged absorption into the general fund through position conversion, beginning in Year 3.**

The district's five-year fiscal projection, reviewed by the governing board in September 2025, anticipates seven certificated retirements at participating schools between 2028 and 2031, four of them in K–3. Rather than replace each retirement one-for-one, the district will convert two of those vacancies into coaching positions, absorbing them into base general fund salary expenditure. This is possible because coaching positions are certificated positions at comparable salary — we are not adding cost so much as redirecting it.

The governing board adopted a resolution on November 4, 2025, stating its intent to sustain no fewer than two coaching positions through general fund absorption beginning in fiscal year 2029–30, contingent on evidence of student outcome gains as documented by the project evaluation. That resolution is attached. We draw the foundation's attention to the contingency clause: the board committed to sustaining a program that works, not to sustaining a program regardless. We consider that the more credible commitment.

**Two: transition of coach supervision to KCOE's ongoing regional structure.**

The KCOE literacy coordinator role is grant-funded during the project period. KCOE has indicated its intent to incorporate the function into its regional service model at the close of the grant, supported by a fee-for-service arrangement across participating districts. Kings Basin's share is projected at approximately $18,000 annually, a figure the district can absorb within existing county service purchases. KCOE's letter addresses this directly. We characterize this as an intent rather than a guarantee, because that is what it is.

**Three: capacity that persists without the position.**

Some of what this project builds does not require ongoing funding to survive. Teachers coached for four years retain the instructional practice. Grade-level data meeting structures, once established as the way work gets done, persist in master schedules. The re-entry assessment protocol for mobile students becomes a procedure, not a program. Curriculum-embedded assessment routines outlive the coach who introduced them.

We want to be careful here, because "building capacity" is the most common way applicants avoid the sustainability question. Our prior implementation gives us actual evidence: after the 2023 funding ended and coaching stopped, the three sites declined from 34 percent to 31 percent — a real loss, but they did not return to 21 percent. Roughly three-quarters of the gain persisted through two years without coaching. That is what embedded capacity looks like, and it is neither nothing nor sufficient.

**Four: the paraeducator pathway as a self-reinforcing structure.**

Pathway participants who become Kings Basin teachers arrive with several years of practice in a district where the coaching model has been the norm. They are also, on the evidence of similar programs, substantially more likely to remain in the district long-term than externally recruited teachers. West Hills has committed to continuing the pathway beyond the grant period through its own institutional resources and state Teacher Residency Grant funds for which it is eligible. Kings Basin's ongoing contribution — release time and mentor teacher stipends — is projected at $24,000 annually and is includable in the LCAP as a certificated staff recruitment and retention action, a permissible use of supplemental funds under state guidance.

**Five: the summer program as a library function.**

Kings County Library has indicated intent to continue summer literacy programming at reduced scale using its own resources and Library Services and Technology Act funds, with Kings Basin contributing teacher summer contracts for two of the five sites at approximately $16,000 annually.

### What this adds up to, and what it does not

At the close of the grant period, the projected sustained annual commitment is approximately $268,000 — two coaching positions, KCOE supervision share, pathway support, and summer contribution — against a project cost of $750,000 annually during the grant. That is roughly 36 percent of full project scale, sustained indefinitely from identified sources.

We are not going to claim we will sustain 100 percent. We would be claiming something we cannot deliver, and the foundation would have no way to hold us to it after the grant closed.

What we will claim is this: two coaches serving four schools with KCOE supervision, a functioning teacher pipeline, embedded instructional practice at eight schools, and persisting data routines constitute a materially different district than the one submitting this application. The alternative to a partial sustainability plan is not a complete one. It is a district that never builds the capacity at all.

We also commit to pursuing additional funding during the grant period — specifically the California Early Literacy Support Block Grant, for which we anticipate eligibility in the 2028 cycle, and federal Comprehensive Literacy State Development subgrants. We list these as pursued opportunities, not as sustainability commitments, because they are not yet secured.

### Personnel — $412,000 annually

**Literacy coaches (4.0 FTE) — $364,000.** Four full-time certificated coaches at an average salary of $76,000 plus benefits at 20 percent. Salaries are placed at the district's certificated schedule step 12, reflecting the experience level required. Coaches are grant-funded in full through Year 2; beginning in Year 3, one position shifts to district match, and in Year 4 a second shifts, phasing absorption ahead of grant close rather than at it.

**Project director (0.4 FTE) — $48,000.** Dr. Vasquez-Ortiz at 0.4 FTE including benefits. Grant funds 0.2 FTE ($24,000); district match funds 0.2 FTE ($24,000).

Note on time-and-effort: all grant-funded personnel will be certified through the electronic module described in Organizational Capacity. Coaches working 100 percent on this award will complete semi-annual certifications; the project director, splitting across funding sources, will complete monthly personnel activity reports reflecting after-the-fact actual distribution.

### Contracted services — $188,000 annually

**KCOE coach supervision — $78,000.** Contract for 0.5 FTE literacy coordinator including salary, benefits, and travel within the county. Deliverables specified in the contract include monthly PLC facilitation, quarterly co-observation with each coach, and quarterly written supervision summaries.

**External evaluation — $58,000.** Independent evaluator selected through competitive RFP. Scope includes design and analysis, annual data collection, fidelity measurement, qualitative work, annual reports, and the summative report.

**West Hills pathway support — $52,000.** Tuition assistance, textbooks, and stipends for pathway participants at approximately $4,300 per participant for twelve participants. West Hills contributes advising, cohort coordination, and campus-based instruction at no charge to the grant.

### Summer literacy program — $52,000 annually

Teacher summer contracts for eight teachers across five sites for six weeks ($38,000), book purchases at ten books per child for 400 children ($10,000), and materials and family session interpretation ($4,000). Coach training in the LETRS sequence and coaching methodology in Years 1 and 2 ($18,000, reducing to $10,000 in Years 3 and 4 as training shifts to internal capacity); substitute release for teacher participation in coaching-related professional learning, 240 days annually at $185 ($44,400 in Years 1–2, reducing thereafter); classroom foundational skills materials, decodable text libraries, and progress monitoring materials ($16,000 in Year 1, $8,000 thereafter). ### Family engagement — $22,000 annually

Punjabi and Spanish translation of family literacy materials, interpretation for family sessions and conferences at three sites, and family literacy event costs. Year 1 includes higher one-time translation development costs; later years weight toward interpretation services.

### Travel — $14,000 annually

Coach mileage between assigned sites and to KCOE (Kings County distances make this a real rather than nominal cost), project director travel for state literacy convenings, and one annual out-of-region conference for two staff.

### Fiscal controls specific to this award

Given our history, we are attaching controls beyond standard practice. Drawdowns will be requested quarterly against actual expenditure, with the grants manager producing a monthly budget-to-actual report. Any variance exceeding 12 percent against the annual expenditure schedule triggers written notice to the superintendent and, if unresolved within 60 days, written notice to the foundation. We propose this notification voluntarily. The Delaney Trust learned of our shortfall at the end. We would rather the Aldous Renwick Foundation learn of any shortfall while it can still be corrected.

### Cost per student served

Total annual project cost of $750,000 against approximately 2,900 K–3 students at participating schools yields $259 per student in Year 1. As the project reaches full eight-school scale and startup costs retire, the figure declines to approximately $195 by Year 4. Across the four-year period, the project will serve an estimated 4,600 unduplicated K–3 students at a cost of approximately $652 per student over the full period of their participation.

For context: a single year of retention costs the district approximately $13,400 per student. If Kings Basin Reads prevents forty retentions across four years — a conservative estimate given projected proficiency gains — the fiscal offset alone approaches $536,000, before any consideration of what it is worth to a child to read.

## Closing

Kings Basin Unified is asking the Aldous Renwick Foundation to fund something we have already done once, at smaller scale, with measured results, in a district where three-quarters of third graders cannot read at grade level.

We have told you where our prior implementation was fragile and how we have redesigned around that fragility. We have told you about a lapsed award and an audit finding and what specifically changed as a result. We have told you what portion of this work we can sustain and what portion we cannot.

We would rather be evaluated on an accurate account than selected on a flattering one. The children in Hanford need the coaching model restored and extended, and we believe we can now execute it in a way we could not four years ago.
