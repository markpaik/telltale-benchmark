# Attendance Interventions in K–12 Settings: A Literature Review for Program Selection

**Prepared for the Board of Directors, Tres Rios Preparatory Schools**
**Office of Research and Accountability**
**Thandiwe Mokoena, Ph.D., Director; Ernesto Palafox-Ruiz, Data Specialist**
**July 2026**

---

## 1. Introduction: The Question Before the Board

The board has been asked to allocate $1.8 million in state attendance-recovery funds across nine campuses serving 6,400 students. Three proposals are under consideration: a mailed nudge campaign at approximately $40 per family, a mentoring model at roughly $1,600 per student, and campus-based attendance teams with home visit capacity at $210,000 per campus. At full network scale, the nudge campaign would cost approximately $256,000 if extended to every family; the mentoring model would reach roughly 1,125 students at full budget; the attendance team model would consume $1.89 million, slightly exceeding available funds if deployed at all nine campuses.

This is not an abstract question. Chronic absence across the network reached 34 percent last year, against 19 percent in 2019. Our authorizer has flagged attendance in a renewal review due in April. Whatever the board funds must be defensible both as a use of public money and as a response to a specific accountability finding within a specific timeframe.

The review question is therefore narrower than "what works for attendance." It is: **for a network with our grade distribution, poverty rate, and post-2020 absence baseline, which of these three intervention classes has evidence sufficient to justify allocation, and at what grade levels?** Three features of that question shape everything that follows.

First, **grade band matters and is contested internally.** Principal Blackgoat's objection — that the evidence thins badly above sixth grade — is not a rhetorical position. It is an empirical claim that this review is obligated to test rather than accommodate. Roughly 2,100 of our students are in grades 9–12, and our high school chronic absence rate (41 percent) exceeds our elementary rate (28 percent). An intervention that works only through sixth grade would address the smaller half of our problem.

Second, **the post-2020 baseline shift is not a caveat but a central analytic problem.** National chronic absence roughly doubled between 2019 and 2022 (Dee 2024; Chang, Osher, and Balfanz 2023). Interventions evaluated against a 12 percent baseline are being proposed for a population with a 34 percent baseline. Whether effect sizes transfer across that gap is an open question with direct budgetary consequences, and at least one large trial suggests the relationship is non-linear (Robinson et al. 2021).

Third, **cost-effectiveness comparisons across these three models are structurally unfair in ways the board should understand before reading the synthesis.** The nudge campaign is universal and cheap per unit; mentoring is targeted and expensive per unit; attendance teams are a fixed-cost infrastructure whose per-student cost depends entirely on caseload assumptions. Comparing $40 to $1,600 to $210,000 without specifying the denominator and the target population produces misleading conclusions. Section 6 addresses this directly.

**Scope.** This review covers interventions targeting attendance or chronic absence as a primary or co-primary outcome, in K–12 settings, in the United States and comparable systems, published or released 2000–2026. It includes randomized trials, quasi-experimental designs with credible identification, implementation studies bearing on the conditions under which effects appear, and cost analyses. It excludes truancy court and legal-sanction models (outside the board's proposals and outside our authority as a charter network), universal school-climate reforms where attendance is a distal secondary outcome, and international studies from systems without compulsory attendance frameworks comparable to Arizona's.

## 2. How the Literature Was Searched

Searches were run in ERIC, PsycINFO, and Web of Science, supplemented by a systematic sweep of a federal evidence clearinghouse for intervention reports carrying attendance outcomes and meeting design standards with or without reservations. Search terms combined attendance constructs (*chronic absen\*, truan\*, attendance intervention, school engagement*) with intervention constructs (*nudge, text message, mailed, postcard, informational intervention, mentor\*, check and connect, home visit, attendance team, early warning*) and design terms (*randomized, quasi-experimental, regression discontinuity, difference-in-differences, cost-effectiveness*). Date range 2000–2026. Grey literature was captured through targeted searches of research organization repositories and state education agency evaluation archives, on the reasoning that null-result attendance evaluations are disproportionately unpublished — a concern that Section 5 shows is well founded.

Screening proceeded from 1,760 unique records after deduplication, to 232 full-text reviews, to 67 included studies: 18 randomized trials, 24 quasi-experimental studies, 13 implementation studies, and 5 cost analyses. Effect sizes were extracted where reported or computable, along with baseline absence rates, grade band, sample composition, follow-up duration, and — where available — implementation fidelity indicators. These are held in a companion workbook available to board members on request.

**Three methodological decisions warrant disclosure.** First, we did not conduct formal meta-analysis. The outcome metrics are too heterogeneous — days absent, percentage points of chronic absence, attendance rate, binary chronic-absence status — and the baseline variation across the 2019 boundary is severe enough that pooling would produce a number more precise than it is accurate. Second, we weighted post-2019 evidence more heavily than pre-2019 evidence in forming conclusions, for reasons developed in Section 5. This is a defensible but consequential choice; a reviewer weighting by sample size or study quality alone would reach somewhat more favorable conclusions about nudge interventions. Third, effect sizes reported below are given in the original studies' metrics with baselines noted, because percentage reductions and absolute reductions diverge sharply at high baselines and the distinction is decision-relevant.

**Limitations of the search.** Non-English studies were excluded. Interventions bundled within multi-component reforms were excluded where attendance effects could not be isolated, which likely removes some effective practice from view. And we could not systematically identify studies that measured attendance but did not report it — a form of outcome reporting bias that is invisible to search.

## 3. Informational and Nudge Interventions: A Literature That Shrank

### 3.1 The original finding and its appeal

The mailed nudge literature has a clear origin and a clear trajectory. Rogers and Feller (2018) randomized approximately 28,000 households in a large district, mailing parents personalized information correcting the common misperception that their child's absences were typical. They reported a 10 percent reduction in absences at a cost the authors placed near $6 per student. Robinson et al. (2018) extended the design across multiple districts with similar direction of effect. Bergman and Chan (2021) found comparable results using automated text messaging about absences and missing assignments. Rogers et al. (2017) established the underlying mechanism: parents systematically underestimate their own children's absence totals, and correcting that belief changes behavior.

The appeal to a board in our position is obvious. The cost per family is trivial relative to any staffing model. The intervention scales without hiring. It requires no scheduling, no facilities, and no change to instructional time. And the original effect sizes, applied naively to our 6,400 students, imply several thousand recovered attendance days for roughly a quarter of a million dollars.

That naive application is the error this section exists to prevent.

### 3.2 The replication record

**The post-2019 replication literature does not sustain the original effect size.** This is the single most important finding in this review, and it is worth stating precisely rather than gently.

Robinson et al. (2021), reporting a large multi-site replication, found effects roughly half the magnitude of the original trials, and — critically — found that effects were concentrated among families whose children were already above a threshold of baseline absence. Below that threshold, estimates were indistinguishable from zero. This is a substantively different finding from "the intervention works, somewhat less well." It reframes nudges from a universal intervention to a targeted one, which changes both the cost calculus and the theory of action.

Lasky-Fink and Rogers (2023) reported attenuated effects in a district-scale implementation and offered a mechanism for the attenuation: message saturation. Districts that had adopted automated attendance communication in the intervening years had already delivered the informational content the original intervention supplied. The novelty that made the first-generation nudge effective was, by construction, non-renewable.

Heppen et al. (2022) reported a null result in a randomized trial of mailed attendance communications in a high-poverty urban setting. Kim, Cortes, and Weinhardt (2024) likewise report attenuation in a post-pandemic replication, with authors explicitly attributing the change to shifted baselines and altered parental beliefs about the necessity of in-person attendance.

Against this, Bergman, Chan, and Kopko (2020) and Cortes et al. (2021) report sustained though modest effects, and Smythe-Leistico and Page (2018) report positive effects from a text-based early-grade intervention. The literature is not uniformly null. But **the direction of the trend across time is unambiguous, and the trend runs against the intervention.**

### 3.3 Why the disagreement exists

Three explanations compete, and they carry different implications for us.

**Explanation one: saturation.** The original effect operated by correcting a false belief. As automated attendance notification became standard practice, the false belief became rarer, and the intervention lost its target. Lasky-Fink and Rogers (2023) make this argument directly; Kraft and Rogers (2015) provide supporting evidence that informational effects in family communication attenuate with repeated exposure. **If this explanation is correct, our own existing communication practices predict our expected effect.** Palafox-Ruiz's audit of network communication finds that seven of nine campuses already send automated absence notifications after three unexcused absences. Under the saturation hypothesis, we should expect the low end of the replication range or nothing.

**Explanation two: baseline dependence.** Robinson et al. (2021) found effects only above a baseline absence threshold. Under this reading, nudges work on families whose absence is high enough to be visible and misperceived, and do nothing for families whose attendance is already good — meaning the universal mailing wastes most of its dosage. **This explanation is, uniquely among the three, favorable to us,** because our baseline is high. Thirty-four percent chronic absence means a large share of our families sit in the range where effects were detected.

**Explanation three: mechanism failure under changed conditions.** Absence in 2019 and absence in 2026 may not be the same behavior. Dee (2024) documents that post-pandemic absence increases are broad-based rather than concentrated in previously high-absence subgroups, implicating changed norms about attendance rather than changed circumstances among an at-risk minority. Chang, Osher, and Balfanz (2023) and Malkus (2024) develop the argument that a norm shift has occurred — that families who once treated attendance as non-negotiable now treat it as discretionary. **If absence is now driven by revised beliefs about whether school attendance matters rather than by mistaken beliefs about how much school a child has missed, an intervention that corrects absence counts is aimed at the wrong belief.**

These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and they cannot be adjudicated with the evidence available. Explanation two argues for funding the nudge campaign at our high baseline; explanations one and three argue against. **Note that our own network conditions activate both the favorable and the unfavorable explanations simultaneously** — we have the high baseline that Robinson et al. (2021) identified as necessary, and we have the prior saturation that Lasky-Fink and Rogers (2023) identified as sufficient to kill the effect.

### 3.4 The high school question

For Principal Blackgoat's concern, the nudge literature is thinner than its overall volume suggests. Rogers and Feller (2018) included secondary grades but did not report disaggregated estimates in the original publication. Bergman and Chan (2021) found effects in secondary grades using assignment-completion messaging as well as attendance messaging, which is the strongest secondary-grade evidence in this class. Robinson et al. (2021) reported weaker effects in upper grades.

The theoretical problem is straightforward: nudge interventions act on parents, and parental control over adolescent attendance declines sharply across the secondary years. A ninth grader who leaves the house and does not arrive at school is not reached by a parent-directed information intervention. **Blackgoat is directionally correct about this intervention class, though the evidence is closer to "attenuated and under-studied" than to "absent."**

## 4. Mentoring and Relational Models: Larger Effects, Narrower Range

### 4.1 The evidence base

Mentoring models — of which Check & Connect is the most studied — pair students with a sustained adult monitor who tracks attendance, intervenes on early warning indicators, and maintains family contact. Sinclair et al. (1998, 2005) established the model with randomized trials showing substantial effects on attendance and persistence among students with disabilities. Guryan et al. (2021) evaluated an intensive tutoring-plus-mentoring model with strong effects on attendance among high school students in Chicago. Heppen et al. (2018) evaluated Check & Connect in a large randomized trial with results substantially weaker than the developer trials, finding no significant effects on attendance in the full sample.

That contrast — developer trials strong, independent replication weak — is a recurring pattern in this literature. Maynard et al. (2014), in a systematic review of mentoring for absenteeism, found positive average effects but substantial heterogeneity and evidence consistent with small-study and developer-affiliation bias. Wilson, Tanner-Smith, and Lipsey (2011) reached similar conclusions for dropout prevention programs with mentoring components.

### 4.2 The grade band split

**This is where the evidence is clearest, and where it does not favor Principal Blackgoat's skepticism in the way she has framed it.**

The pattern across studies runs opposite to the intuition that adolescents need relationships most. Elementary-grade mentoring evaluations report larger and more consistent effects. Sheldon and Epstein (2004) and Sheldon (2007) found attendance improvements in elementary settings from family-connection interventions. Communities In Schools evaluations (ICF 2017; Parise et al. 2017) found attendance effects concentrated in elementary and middle grades. Ehrlich et al. (2018), examining early-grade absence in Chicago, found that early-grade attendance is highly responsive to relational and logistical intervention because the underlying causes — transportation, caregiver work schedules, illness management, sibling care — are tractable and adult-controlled.

High school mentoring results are genuinely inconsistent. Heppen et al. (2018) found nulls. Guryan et al. (2021) found strong effects but with an intervention far more intensive — and far more expensive — than the model before this board. Somers et al. (2010) and Corrin et al. (2016) report mixed results from ninth-grade transition programs with mentoring components. Balfanz and Byrnes (2019) argue that the high school inconsistency reflects a heterogeneity problem: adolescent absence has multiple distinct causes — employment, caregiving, disengagement, mental health, housing instability, pregnancy and parenting — and a single relational model addresses only some of them.

**The correct reading is not that mentoring fails in high school. It is that mentoring succeeds in high school when it is intensive enough and matched to the specific driver of absence, and that the version affordable at $1,600 per student is unlikely to be either.** Guryan et al. (2021) achieved high school effects at a per-student cost several times the proposal before the board. Heppen et al. (2018) evaluated something closer to the proposed intensity and found nulls. This is a dosage story as much as a grade band story, and it means that Blackgoat's doubt and the elementary-strength finding are pointing at the same underlying fact from opposite directions.

### 4.3 What the implementation studies add

The implementation literature explains a substantial share of the effect size variation and deserves more weight than its methodological status usually earns.

Sinclair, Christenson, and Thurlow (2005) documented that effects depended on mentor caseload and tenure. Maynard, Kremer, et al. (2018) found that programs with caseloads above roughly 40 students showed markedly weaker effects. Fryer (2017) and Kraft, Blazar, and Hogan (2018) provide parallel evidence from adjacent literatures that relational interventions degrade sharply when the relational component is diluted.

Several implementation studies (Childs and Lofton 2021; Gee 2018) document that programs nominally implementing a mentoring model frequently drift toward case management — paperwork, referral, documentation — under staffing pressure, at which point the mechanism that produced the original effects is no longer present. Van Eck et al. (2017) found that mentor turnover mid-year eliminated measurable effects, which matters acutely in a network with our staffing conditions.

**For the board, this means the $1,600 figure is meaningless without a caseload specification.** At a caseload of 25, the model resembles what was evaluated. At 60, it resembles the programs that produced nulls, at identical nominal cost.

## 5. Home Visits and Attendance Teams: The Weakest Evidence for the Most Expensive Proposal

### 5.1 What exists

The home visit literature is the thinnest of the three and the most methodologically compromised.

Sheldon and Epstein (2004) reported attendance improvements from family outreach including home visits, using a pre-post design without a control group. Sheldon (2007) extended this with similar limitations. Johns Hopkins evaluations of the Parent Teacher Home Visits model (Sheldon and Jung 2015) reported associations between home visit receipt and improved attendance, but the design cannot separate the effect of the visit from the characteristics of families who accept visits.

**This selection problem is the defining feature of the literature.** Families who open the door to a home visitor differ systematically from families who do not, on precisely the dimensions that predict attendance: residential stability, work schedule flexibility, trust in institutions, immigration status concerns, and household composition. Any design comparing visited to non-visited families without addressing this will overstate effects, potentially by a large margin.

A small number of studies attempt to address it. McConnell and Kubina (2014) used a matched comparison design with modest effects. Cook et al. (2017) evaluated a check-in intervention with random assignment and found small positive attendance effects. Guryan et al. (2021), while primarily a tutoring evaluation, included home outreach components. Hutchison (2023) reports a quasi-experimental evaluation of a district attendance team model with positive but imprecisely estimated effects.

### 5.2 The attendance team question specifically

The proposal before the board is not a home visit program but a **campus attendance team** with home visit capacity — a staffing infrastructure that includes data monitoring, tiered outreach, family engagement, and referral, of which home visiting is one function.

The evidence for this bundled model is largely descriptive. Balfanz and Byrnes (2018) and Bruce et al. (2011) document early warning system implementation with attendance teams and report favorable trends without counterfactuals. Mac Iver et al. (2019) evaluated a multi-component ninth-grade model including attendance teams with quasi-experimental methods and found positive effects on attendance, one of the stronger findings for secondary grades in this review. Faria et al. (2017) evaluated early warning systems in a randomized trial and found effects on chronic absence in ninth grade — again, among the better secondary-grade evidence available.

**Note the pattern: the bundled attendance-team model is the only intervention class in this review with credible positive evidence specifically in the high school grades** (Mac Iver et al. 2019; Faria et al. 2017). This is a real finding and it cuts against dismissing the most expensive option.

But two cautions apply. First, both studies evaluated ninth grade specifically, in the context of transition support, and neither isolates the attendance team from the surrounding components. Second, Mac Iver et al. (2019) and Faria et al. (2017) both describe implementations with substantial external technical assistance and data infrastructure that our network does not currently possess and that is not costed in the $210,000 figure.

### 5.3 The cost analyses

Five cost analyses were included and they are, collectively, the weakest component of this evidence base.

Bowden et al. (2015) applied ingredients-method costing to an attendance intervention and demonstrated that published per-student costs routinely omit the value of existing staff time reallocated to the program. Hollands et al. (2016) reached similar conclusions across dropout prevention programs, finding that reported costs understated economic costs by substantial margins. Levin et al. (2018) provide the methodological framework and note that attendance interventions are particularly prone to this error because they rely heavily on existing school personnel.

Applied to our proposals: **the $40 per family nudge cost almost certainly excludes the staff time required to maintain accurate address data, generate personalized content, and handle the response volume the mailing generates.** Palafox-Ruiz estimates 0.3 FTE of data specialist time network-wide for a single mailing cycle. The $210,000 per campus attendance team figure is closer to a true cost because it is principally salary, but it excludes the data infrastructure and training that the studies showing effects had in place.

No cost analysis in the included set compares all three intervention classes on common metrics with common assumptions. **We are not in a position to give the board a defensible cost-per-recovered-attendance-day comparison, and any figure presented as such should be treated with suspicion.**

## 6. What Drives the Disagreement

Four sources of disagreement recur across intervention classes and explain most of the apparent contradiction in this literature.

**Baseline dependence.** Effects are not constant across baseline absence rates. Robinson et al. (2021) found nudge effects only above a threshold. Ehrlich et al. (2018) found that early-grade interventions produce larger absolute gains where baseline absence is higher. Percentage reductions and percentage-point reductions diverge sharply at high baselines: a 10 percent reduction from a 12 percent baseline is 1.2 points; from a 34 percent baseline it is 3.4 points. **Studies reporting percentage reductions from low-baseline populations may substantially understate absolute effects in our setting — or the relationship may be non-linear in ways that make extrapolation invalid.** The literature does not resolve this, and it is the single largest source of uncertainty in applying these findings to our network.

**The 2020 discontinuity.** Dee (2024) documents that post-pandemic absence increases are broad-based rather than concentrated among previously at-risk students. Malkus (2024) shows increases across income levels and school types. Chang, Osher, and Balfanz (2023) argue for a norm shift in family beliefs about the necessity of daily attendance. **If the composition of the absent population has changed, effect sizes estimated on the pre-2020 absent population do not transfer,** regardless of statistical quality. Our own data support this: Palafox-Ruiz's analysis finds that 41 percent of currently chronically absent students had attendance above 95 percent in 2019 — they are not the population these interventions were designed for.

**Implementation variance masquerading as effect heterogeneity.** Studies reporting the same nominal intervention frequently evaluate materially different things. Caseload, mentor tenure, dosage, and data infrastructure vary by factors of two or more across studies of the same model (Sinclair, Christenson, and Thurlow 2005; Maynard, Kremer, et al. 2018; Van Eck et al. 2017). Much of what appears in this literature as contradictory findings is better read as **a dose-response relationship observed at scattered points along the dose axis without anyone measuring the dose.**

**Publication and reporting asymmetry.** Nudge interventions are cheap to evaluate and produce clean randomized designs, generating a large published literature. Home visits are expensive, hard to randomize, and produce ambiguous designs, generating a small one. **The volume of evidence tracks the ease of evaluation, not the plausibility of the intervention.** Our grey literature search recovered several unpublished null nudge evaluations, suggesting the published record is optimistic. Maynard et al. (2014) found funnel plot asymmetry consistent with small-study bias in the mentoring literature. The board should not read "more studies" as "more effective."

## 7. Gaps: Populations and Questions the Evidence Misses

**Students in unstable housing.** This is the most serious gap. Students experiencing homelessness or housing instability have chronic absence rates roughly double the general population (Ingram et al. 2017; Cowen 2017), yet appear in almost none of the included samples in identifiable numbers. Only four of 67 studies reported housing status. Mailed nudges require stable addresses. Home visits require a home. Mentoring requires continuous enrollment. **The interventions with the strongest evidence are structurally hardest to deliver to the students with the highest need.** For our network — where Palafox-Ruiz identifies approximately 480 students as McKinney-Vento eligible, and where these students account for a disproportionate share of chronic absence — this is not a marginal limitation. It means the evidence base is close to silent on roughly a seventh of the problem.

**Students with significant employment or caregiving responsibilities.** Adolescent absence driven by work or family caregiving is documented descriptively (Balfanz and Byrnes 2019; Gubbels, van der Put, and Assink 2019) but almost never addressed by evaluated interventions. None of the three proposals has a mechanism for a seventeen-year-old working thirty hours weekly.

**English learners and mixed-status families.** Home visit and outreach models raise participation concerns in mixed-status households that no included study examines. Given our network's demographics, this bears directly on the attendance team proposal's likely reach.

**Post-2022 evidence at scale.** Only nine included studies use post-2022 data. The intervention literature has not caught up to the conditions it is being asked to address.

**Sustained effects.** Most studies report single-year outcomes. Rogers and Feller (2018) found some persistence; most studies do not measure it. For a network making a multi-year budget commitment, the absence of durability evidence is material.

**Head-to-head comparisons.** No included study compares these three intervention classes against one another with common outcomes and populations. Every comparison in this review is indirect, across studies with different samples, baselines, and eras. **This is the central limitation of the review and the board should weight our conclusions accordingly.**

## 8. Implications for the Network's Decision

The following are the review's conclusions, stated with the confidence the evidence supports and no more.

**On the nudge campaign.** The evidence has deteriorated substantially since 2019, and the two mechanisms most likely responsible — saturation and norm shift — are both present in our network. Our high baseline is favorable under Robinson et al. (2021), but our existing automated notification at seven of nine campuses is unfavorable under Lasky-Fink and Rogers (2023). **We do not recommend network-wide deployment.** We do recommend a randomized implementation at two campuses without existing automated notification, with the network's own control group. At approximately $57,000, this generates internal evidence at a cost the board can absorb, and it produces exactly the kind of local evaluation an authorizer credits. The expected effect is modest and may be zero; the value is informational.

**On mentoring.** The evidence supports elementary and middle grades with reasonable confidence and does not support the proposed model at the proposed intensity in high school. Principal Blackgoat's objection is substantially correct for this intervention class — though the reason is dosage rather than adolescence per se. **We recommend funding mentoring in grades K–8 with a specified caseload of no more than 30 students per mentor.** Any implementation exceeding that caseload should be understood as unevaluated, because the studies producing positive effects did not operate above it. At $1,600 per student, roughly 500 students could be served in the elementary and middle grades, which corresponds approximately to our current K–8 chronically absent population.

**On attendance teams.** This is the most expensive proposal with the weakest overall evidence base — and simultaneously the only proposal with credible evidence in the high school grades (Mac Iver et al. 2019; Faria et al. 2017). These two facts are both true and they pull in opposite directions. **We recommend funding attendance teams at the two high school campuses only,** at approximately $420,000, where the alternative interventions have no supporting evidence and where our absence rate is highest. This should be explicitly framed to the authorizer as an intervention with emerging rather than established evidence.

**On the residual and on evaluation.** The three recommendations total approximately $1.28 million against $1.8 million available. We recommend reserving approximately $150,000 for evaluation capacity — the studies showing effects had data infrastructure our network lacks, and this cost is invisible in all three proposals. The remaining balance should be held against the housing-instability gap, where no evidenced intervention exists and where locally designed outreach, honestly labeled as unevaluated, may be more defensible than importing a model whose evidence base excludes the population.

**On what this review cannot tell the board.** No included study compares these three options directly. Effect sizes from a 12 percent-baseline world may not transfer to a 34 percent-baseline world. The high school evidence across all three classes is thin enough that any allocation there is a judgment call informed by evidence rather than dictated by it. **The board is making a decision under genuine uncertainty, and the honest presentation to the authorizer is that we have selected the best-supported options available and built in the means to learn whether they work here.** That is a stronger position than claiming an evidentiary certainty this literature does not provide.
