# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 1 OF 4

**Small-Format Urban Stores — Pilot Review, Fiscal 2023 Openings Through Fiscal 2025 (period ended February 1, 2025)**

**To:** Bettina Halloran, Chief Merchandising Officer; Dimitri Andrakis, Chief Financial Officer; Talmadge Reeve, Operating Partner, Hollis Creek Partners
**From:** Sun-Hee Park, Director, Strategy and Performance Office
**Date:** March 4, 2025
**For:** March capital meeting, decision item

---

## Finding and Recommendation

The small-format urban pilot is the only one of the four fiscal 2025 programs that outperformed its underwriting on every dimension we set at approval. Four stores opened in calendar 2023 produced $912 in sales per selling square foot against a chain average of $640, and all four reached store-level breakeven in 14 months against a 22-month plan. The productivity is not an artifact of small denominators; the format is generating a genuinely different trip, at a higher gross margin rate, in trade areas where our conventional box cannot be sited economically.

**We recommend the capital committee approve six additional small-format sites across fiscal 2026 and fiscal 2027 at a program authorization of $29.4 million, released in two tranches of three stores each, with the second tranche conditioned on the first three stores clearing $780 in sales per square foot in their first full year.** We further recommend the committee adopt the site qualification screen set out below as a binding gate rather than a guideline, because the two weakest performers in the pilot both failed one of its criteria and were approved on judgment.

## The Evidence

The four pilot stores — Glenwood South in Raleigh, Ninth Street in Durham, Upper King in Charleston, and Augusta Road in Greenville — average 12,400 selling square feet against roughly 36,000 for a conventional Palmetto store. Combined fiscal 2025 sales were $45.2 million, or 3.2 percent of chain volume, on 4.6 percent of the company's store count and 1.6 percent of its selling area. Average store volume of $11.3 million is less than half a conventional store's $23.4 million, which is the point that has caused some confusion in prior discussions: these stores are not meant to replace volume, they are meant to convert capital efficiently. On that measure they are the best assets we own.

Build-out averaged $4.6 million per store including leasehold improvements, refrigeration, and equipment, plus $310,000 of opening inventory. Four-wall EBITDA in fiscal 2025 averaged $830,000 per store, or 7.3 percent of sales, against 6.4 percent for the conventional fleet. That produces an unlevered cash-on-cash return of 18.0 percent at the store level, comfortably above the 11 percent hurdle the committee applies to new-store capital and roughly triple what the remodel program has delivered. The 14-month breakeven matters as much as the return itself, because it shortens the period during which a new site is a drag on district results and reduces the political cost of opening stores in markets where operations leadership is skeptical.

The margin structure explains most of the outperformance. Gross margin in the pilot stores ran 31.4 percent against 27.1 percent chain-wide, driven by mix rather than by pricing — we hold price parity with the nearest conventional store on all 4,100 items the formats share. Prepared foods, coffee, and food-service account for 17 percent of small-format sales against 6 percent chain-wide, and center-store grocery falls to 31 percent from 46 percent. The customer is buying today's meal and tomorrow's breakfast, not a two-week replenishment. Average basket is $28.40 against $48.10 chain-wide, but trip frequency runs 2.4 visits per week per active household against 1.3, so annual spend per household is 6 percent *higher* in the small format than in a conventional store.

Two costs run against us and both were correctly forecast. Occupancy averages $38.10 per square foot against $11.40 in our suburban leases, or $471,000 per store per year — 4.2 percent of sales versus 1.8 percent. Shrink runs 3.4 percent against 2.6 percent, a function of the fresh and prepared mix and of the compressed backroom, which forces daily rather than weekly receiving. Neither cost is deteriorating. Shrink improved 40 basis points between the first and second full year as the prepared-foods production plans matured.

Cannibalization was the risk the committee flagged at approval, and it is real but small. Two of the four stores sit within 3.1 miles of an existing Palmetto. Using loyalty-card transfer analysis against a matched control set, we measure 4.2 percent of the affected stores' volume moving to the new units, or $1.9 million annually. Net new sales to the chain are therefore $43.3 million of the $45.2 million reported. Both cannibalized stores have since returned to positive comparable sales.

Second-year performance is the most encouraging figure in the review. The three stores with a full second year comped positive 8.4 percent, against a chain comp of 1.9 percent. Small formats in the industry frequently open hot on novelty and settle back below plan; ours have not. New-mover data and residential permitting in the four trade areas suggest the demand base is still building.

Where the pilot showed weakness, the cause was site selection rather than format. Augusta Road, the lowest performer at $744 per square foot, sits in a trade area with a daytime population of 19,000 within a half mile — well below the others — and was approved because favorable lease terms became available on short notice. Its returns are acceptable but unremarkable. That experience produced the screen we ask the committee to make binding:

- Selling area between 11,000 and 14,000 square feet, with at least 40 feet of continuous street frontage
- Daytime population of 28,000 or more within a half-mile radius
- Median household income of $72,000 or more within a half-mile radius
- At least 3,400 residential units built or permitted within a half mile since 2019
- No existing Palmetto store within 2.0 miles
- Base rent not exceeding 4.5 percent of underwritten year-two sales

Every proposed site should clear all six. Sites clearing five should come to the committee as exceptions with the failed criterion named in the request.

## Fiscal 2026 Commitments Already in Place

The chain has committed $9.4 million of fiscal 2026 capital to two small-format stores already in the approved plan. The South End store in Charlotte has an executed 15-year lease with rent commencing August 1, 2025 and is scheduled to open in October 2025; $1.2 million of design, permitting, and long-lead refrigeration is spent or under purchase order. The Ghent store in Norfolk has an executed lease with an outside opening date of March 2026 and $640,000 committed. Neither is affected by the recommendation above; both are inside the first tranche and count against the $29.4 million authorization, which therefore requests $20.0 million of incremental capital.

Separately, $2.1 million is committed in fiscal 2026 to expand the Raleigh commissary from 11,000 to 18,000 square feet. That project was justified on the existing four stores plus Charlotte and Norfolk and does not require the six additional sites to earn its return; it does, however, cap the format at roughly nine stores. A seventh through tenth store would require a second production site in the Carolinas, which we estimate at $3.8 million and which is not in any current plan. The committee should understand that the six-store recommendation carries that follow-on obligation in fiscal 2028.

*Approximately 1,290 words.*

# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 2 OF 4

**Third-Party Delivery Marketplace Partnership — Pilot Review, Fiscal 2025 (period ended February 1, 2025)**

**To:** Bettina Halloran, Chief Merchandising Officer; Dimitri Andrakis, Chief Financial Officer; Talmadge Reeve, Operating Partner, Hollis Creek Partners
**From:** Sun-Hee Park, Director, Strategy and Performance Office
**Date:** March 4, 2025
**For:** March capital meeting, decision item

---

## Finding and Recommendation

The third-party delivery partnership lost money on every order it filled, and it is not a volume problem that scale will solve. Across 41 stores in fiscal 2025 the platform handled 380,000 orders producing $14.2 million in sales and a negative contribution margin of $1.9 million — a loss of $5.00 per order before any allocation of overhead. The structural cause is a contract that permits the platform to set retail price at our shelf price while charging us 18 percent commission on that same price. There is no order volume at which that arithmetic turns positive.

**We recommend the committee authorize the Strategy and Performance Office and the General Counsel to deliver notice of non-renewal on or before June 2, 2026, the last date permitted under the agreement's 120-day notice provision, unless the partner agrees in writing before that date to the three terms set out below. We further recommend immediate execution of a loss-mitigation package that we estimate will reduce the fiscal 2026 loss from $1.9 million to approximately $700,000 without breaching the agreement, and reallocation of the resulting attention and labor to first-party curbside pickup, which earns $3.10 per order.**

## The Evidence

The per-order economics are the whole argument, and they are not close. The average delivery basket in fiscal 2025 was $37.44, which is 22 percent below the $48.10 comparable in-store basket. At a 26.4 percent gross margin rate — slightly below the chain rate, because the delivery catalog skews to national brands — each order generates $9.88 of gross profit. Against that we pay 18 percent commission to the platform, or $6.74. We spend an average of 17.5 minutes of store labor picking and staging each order at a loaded rate of $18.60, or $5.43. Packaging, insulated bags, and cold-chain materials cost $0.82. Refunds, item credits, and shrink on orders never collected cost $0.95. Platform integration fees, the contractual marketing minimum, and our two-person support desk cost $0.94. Total cost per order is $14.88 against $9.88 of gross profit. The gap is $5.00, and 380,000 orders makes it $1.9 million.

Two of those cost lines deserve emphasis because they are where the committee's questions have historically landed. First, the commission is charged on our shelf price and the agreement's price-parity clause forbids us from marking up the delivery catalog. Every competitor of scale that operates profitably on these platforms applies a 9 to 14 percent menu uplift. We negotiated that right away in exchange for a lower headline commission rate, and it was the wrong trade. Second, the labor figure is real and auditable. We time-studied 640 pick sessions across nine stores in the third quarter. The 17.5-minute average reflects a 31.2-item order picked through a full selling floor during trading hours, with no dedicated pick space in 30 of the 41 participating stores.

The substitution rate is the operational failure underneath the financial one. Thirty-one percent of ordered items were substituted or short-shipped. That figure is roughly triple what a well-run first-party operation produces and it is not principally an in-stock problem — our shelf in-stock across the same stores averaged 94.6 percent. It is a picker-behavior problem created by piece-rate pressure and by a platform interface that makes substitution faster than a customer callback. The consequences compound. We logged 1,100 formal refund claims at an average of $63, or $69,000 direct. Far larger is the 47,000 automatic item-level credits averaging $6.20, or $291,000. Largest of all is the retention damage: households that experienced a substitution-driven refund reordered within 90 days at a 22 percent rate against a 41 percent baseline for delivery customers generally. We are paying $5.00 an order to teach 47,000 households that Palmetto sends the wrong thing.

Incrementality is the last defense usually offered for these arrangements, and it does not hold either. Matching the delivery file against our loyalty database, 34 percent of delivery households were already active Palmetto shoppers in the prior 12 months, and their total annual spend with us declined 4.1 percent after they began ordering through the platform — they moved trips, at a smaller basket and a negative margin, from a channel that made money to one that does not. The genuinely new households, 66 percent of the file, spent an average of $211 with us over the year. At a 26.4 percent margin that is $56 of gross profit against acquisition economics that never turn.

The contrast with our own curbside pickup operation sharpens the case. Curbside handled 214,000 orders in fiscal 2025 at an average basket of $61.20 — 27 percent *above* the in-store basket, because customers stock up when there is no delivery fee anchoring them to a small order. Substitution runs 9 percent. Contribution is positive $3.10 per order, or $663,000 for the year. Every operational instinct the delivery pilot produced points toward putting labor, space, and merchandising attention there.

If the partner wishes to continue, three terms would make the arrangement viable and we recommend the committee authorize no lesser package:

1. **Price authority.** Removal of the price-parity clause, permitting a menu uplift of up to 12 percent on the delivery catalog.
2. **Catalog control.** Our unilateral right to set and restrict the assortment offered, including exclusion of low-margin and high-substitution categories.
3. **Substitution accountability.** A contractual substitution ceiling of 12 percent with platform-funded credits for every point above it, and our right to picker-training standards and to remove individual pickers from our stores.

Absent all three, the agreement should lapse.

The interim mitigation package requires no consent. Trimming the catalog from 12,400 to roughly 6,800 items removes the categories where substitution and margin are worst — fresh meat cut to order, produce sold by weight, bakery, and floral — and we model that at $410,000 of loss avoided. Withdrawing Palmetto-funded promotional participation, which we currently subsidize at $0.44 per order with no measured lift, saves $167,000. Consolidating picking into 14 hub stores rather than 41, with dedicated pick stations and trained staff, reduces pick time to a modeled 11 minutes and saves roughly $620,000. Together these bring the fiscal 2026 loss to approximately $700,000, which is the price of an orderly exit rather than a disorderly one.

## Fiscal 2026 Commitments Already in Place

The agreement runs through September 30, 2026 and cannot be terminated for convenience before then, so the chain is committed to 19 more months of participation regardless of the committee's decision. Inside that term we are contractually obligated to a $340,000 annual marketing and integration minimum, of which $85,000 has been invoiced for the first quarter of fiscal 2026.

The fiscal 2026 capital plan also carries $610,000 for dedicated pick-station build-outs at 11 stores, of which $190,000 is spent or under purchase order. We recommend the committee retain this spend but redirect the eight unstarted stations to the hub-store model described above. Those assets serve first-party curbside identically and will hold their value after the marketplace agreement ends; cancelling them would forfeit the mitigation savings and the pickup growth at once.

*Approximately 1,320 words.*

# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 3 OF 4

**Private Label Expansion Program — Pilot Review, Fiscal 2023 Through Fiscal 2025 (period ended February 1, 2025)**

**To:** Bettina Halloran, Chief Merchandising Officer; Dimitri Andrakis, Chief Financial Officer; Talmadge Reeve, Operating Partner, Hollis Creek Partners
**From:** Sun-Hee Park, Director, Strategy and Performance Office
**Date:** March 4, 2025
**For:** March capital meeting, decision item with governance recommendation

---

## Finding and Recommendation

The private label expansion led by SVP of Operations Jamal Whitfield has delivered its financial objective and should continue. Penetration rose from 18 to 24 percent of sales across three fiscal years and chain gross margin improved 140 basis points, from 26.9 to 28.3 percent, worth approximately $19.6 million annually at current volume. That is the largest margin improvement any single initiative has produced in the period under review, and the program's core thesis — that our customers will accept a well-executed store brand at a 22 to 28 percent price gap — is confirmed.

The finding that requires the committee's attention is that the *method* is now destroying value at the margin. In dairy and frozen, where the program pursued penetration by delisting the number-two national brand rather than by winning switching on merit, unit velocity has fallen for three consecutive quarters and nine items launched in the period were discontinued inside twelve months at a write-off of $840,000.

**We recommend the committee approve continued expansion with three changes: reset the fiscal 2027 penetration target from 30 percent to 26.5 percent; prohibit delisting of the number-one or number-two national brand in any category as a mechanism for building private label share; and impose a 26-week velocity gate on every new item, with automatic review at 60 percent of forecast rather than the current discretionary process. We further recommend the program's governance move from Operations alone to a standing committee co-chaired by the Chief Merchandising Officer and the SVP of Operations.**

## The Evidence

The program added 412 items across nine categories between fiscal 2023 and fiscal 2025 under two tiers — Palmetto Kitchen at national-brand quality and roughly a 22 percent price gap, and Palmetto Value at opening price point and roughly a 38 percent gap. Fiscal 2025 alone added 168 items. Six points of penetration on $1.4 billion represents approximately $84 million of sales converted from national brands to our own, and because our private label items carry a blended gross margin of 34.1 percent against 24.6 percent on the national brands they displaced, the arithmetic of the margin gain is straightforward and we have verified it against the general ledger rather than accepting the category reports.

Where the program earned its results honestly — center store dry grocery, paper and household, health and beauty care, and the Palmetto Kitchen premium tier across all categories — the pattern is clean. Category dollars held or grew, unit velocity held, and the share shift shows up as genuine substitution inside the same shopping trip. Household penetration of the Palmetto Kitchen tier reached 61 percent of loyalty households, and repeat rate on those items runs 3.4 purchases per buying household per quarter. Those are the numbers of a brand customers have chosen.

Dairy and frozen tell a different story and it is the reason this review is a decision item rather than a status report. Dairy units per store per week are down 7.4 percent against the prior year while dairy dollars are up only 0.4 percent — the category is holding revenue on price, not on demand. Frozen is worse: units down 9.1 percent and dollars down 2.3 percent, which on a category representing roughly $98 million of chain sales is $2.25 million of lost revenue. Together the two categories have given back approximately $2.4 million of the $19.6 million margin gain, and the trend line is still moving the wrong way.

The cause is identifiable and it is not the quality of the private label items themselves, which test acceptably in blind panels. In eleven dairy subcategories and eight frozen subcategories, the program delisted the number-two national brand to free facings. Customers whose preferred brand disappeared did not trade into our item at the rate the model assumed. Loyalty analysis of the 41,000 households whose top dairy brand was delisted shows a 3.2 percent decline in total trips and a 5.8 percent decline in dairy spend with us over the following two quarters. Panel data indicates roughly 11 percent of that cohort's dairy dollars now transact at a competitor. We removed the second-best reason to shop the aisle and captured only part of it.

The nine discontinued items make the same point in miniature. Five of the nine were line extensions launched into a hole created by a delisting rather than into demonstrated demand:

- Palmetto Kitchen Greek yogurt, four-pack, three flavors
- Palmetto Kitchen frozen skillet meals, two varieties
- Palmetto Value shredded cheese blend, 32-ounce
- Palmetto Kitchen frozen breakfast sandwiches, two varieties
- Palmetto Value frozen vegetable steam bags, one variety

The direct cost was $840,000: $416,000 in inventory write-down, $273,000 in destroyed packaging across 41,000 units, and $151,000 in tooling and plate charges not recoverable. Beyond that, two supplier agreements carried minimum annual volumes we did not meet, triggering $215,000 in shortfall payments. Quality is a secondary contributor and should not be ignored — complaint rate on frozen entrées runs 2.4 per 10,000 units against 1.1 for the national-brand equivalents we displaced — but four of the nine failures were pure demand failures, not quality failures.

The recommended 26.5 percent target is not a retreat. It is the level our modeling supports without further delistings in protected categories, and it still adds roughly 60 basis points of chain gross margin over two years, or $8.4 million. The 30 percent target in the original plan is achievable only by continuing to remove national brands from dairy, frozen, and beverages, and our evidence is that the last three points of penetration bought that way cost more in traffic than they return in margin.

On governance, we make the recommendation with care and without criticism of Mr. Whitfield, whose execution of sourcing, quality control, and cost has been excellent and is the reason the margin result exists at all. The failures in this review are assortment and demand-forecasting failures, which sit naturally with Merchandising. A program that decides which national brands leave the shelf is making a merchandising decision, and it should be made where the category and consumer data live.

## Fiscal 2026 Commitments Already in Place

The chain has 190 items in the development pipeline for fiscal 2026, against which $2.4 million is committed in tooling, packaging design, plate charges, and first-production runs. Fourteen supplier agreements carry minimum annual volume commitments totaling $31 million at cost. Of the 190 items, 22 are dairy or frozen line extensions of the type this review identifies as the failure mode, carrying $310,000 of committed spend.

We recommend those 22 items be re-gated against the new velocity standard before any further spend, and that the committee authorize up to $410,000 to renegotiate or exit the two supplier agreements that carry their volume minimums. Cancelling the items outright without addressing the contracts would convert $310,000 of committed spend into $525,000 of committed spend plus shortfall payments. The remaining 168 items in the pipeline are unaffected and should proceed.

*Approximately 1,300 words.*

# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 4 OF 4

**Store Remodel Program — Pilot Review, Fiscal 2024 Authorization Through Fiscal 2025 (period ended February 1, 2025)**

**To:** Bettina Halloran, Chief Merchandising Officer; Dimitri Andrakis, Chief Financial Officer; Talmadge Reeve, Operating Partner, Hollis Creek Partners
**From:** Sun-Hee Park, Director, Strategy and Performance Office
**Date:** March 4, 2025
**For:** March capital meeting, decision item

---

## Finding and Recommendation

The remodel program is the clearest capital failure in the fiscal 2025 portfolio and it failed on both sides of the return equation simultaneously. The committee approved 11 remodels at $2.3 million each, or $25.3 million, underwritten to an 11 percent comparable sales lift. Seven stores are complete at an average cost of $3.4 million — 48 percent over budget — and have delivered a weighted comparable sales lift of 6.1 percent, 55 percent of the underwritten figure. The resulting unlevered cash return on the completed set is 6.2 percent against an 11 percent hurdle and against the 18.4 percent the committee was shown at approval. Four remodels have been paused since January 2025 at the direction of the Chief Financial Officer, which was the correct call.

**We recommend the committee decline to release the four paused remodels under their current scope and cost, and instead approve a restructured two-tier program for re-underwriting in the fiscal 2027 cycle. We recommend releasing $2.9 million to complete Store 118 in Hickory, which is demolished and cannot be left in its present condition, under a re-scoped package. We recommend returning $6.3 million of the fiscal 2026 remodel authorization to the general capital pool, where the small-format program described in Summary 1 offers a materially better return on the same dollars.**

## The Evidence

The cost overrun is not a single failure of estimating. It is six failures, each individually modest and collectively fatal, and the composition matters because it tells the committee which are fixable. Against the $1.1 million average overrun per completed store:

- Refrigeration, principally the conversion to transcritical CO2 racks that was assumed at approval but priced from a three-year-old benchmark: **$355,000**
- Electrical service upgrades required by the new refrigeration and prepared-foods load, discovered after design in five of seven stores: **$220,000**
- Mechanical and HVAC, largely rooftop replacement triggered by code once the electrical scope opened: **$150,000**
- Structural remediation and asbestos abatement in buildings averaging 31 years old: **$120,000**
- Scope added after capital approval, principally merchandising department additions requested during construction: **$175,000**
- General contractor escalation and full contingency draw: **$80,000**

Four of these six were foreseeable with better pre-approval diligence. The refrigeration figure was stale, and a current quote would have caught it. The electrical, mechanical, and structural conditions would have been identified by a full existing-conditions survey, which we did not commission because it costs approximately $34,000 per store and the program was approved on a portfolio basis. Spending $374,000 across 11 stores would have prevented roughly $5.4 million of surprise. The post-approval scope additions are a governance failure and are the easiest of the six to eliminate.

The sales side underperformed for a different and more concerning reason. The 6.1 percent weighted lift, measured on trailing 52 weeks post-reopening against a control set of 14 non-remodeled stores, conceals an extreme range: individual store results run from 1.4 percent to 12.8 percent. Two stores carried the program. Both of those two received a full prepared-foods department — a service deli, a hot bar, and a made-to-order counter — and both sit in trade areas with measurable residential growth. The five underperformers received what the program documents call a "refresh-plus-refrigeration" package: new décor, flooring, lighting, and cases, with no change to what the store sells or how customers use it. Those five averaged a 3.2 percent lift. The conclusion is uncomfortable but unambiguous. Cosmetic remodels do not move sales at a level that justifies $3.4 million. Departmental additions in growing trade areas do.

Lift decay compounds the problem. Across the seven completed stores the comparable lift ran 9.2 percent in the first quarter after reopening, 7.4 percent in the second, 5.9 percent in the third, and 4.8 percent in the fourth. Our underwriting assumed an 11 percent lift held flat for five years. If the observed decay curve continues at even half its current rate, the five-year return falls well below the figure cited above.

Translating this into the return the committee actually cares about: the remodel set averages $16.8 million in annual sales, below the chain average because older stores were prioritized. A 6.1 percent lift produces $1.02 million of incremental sales, which at a 27.5 percent margin rate produces $282,000 of incremental gross profit. Net of $70,000 in incremental store operating expense, the annual contribution is $212,000 against $3.4 million of capital — 6.2 percent. The approval case showed an 11 percent lift on $2.3 million producing $423,000 against $2.3 million, or 18.4 percent. We lost roughly six points of return to cost and six to sales.

One cost was omitted from the underwriting entirely and should be corrected in any future model. Phased construction averaged 11.4 weeks per store, during which sales ran 13.8 percent below the control set. That is approximately $390,000 of gross profit forgone per store, or $2.7 million across the seven completed remodels, none of which appeared in the approval case.

The restructured program we recommend for fiscal 2027 has two tiers. A refresh tier at approximately $1.1 million covers lighting, flooring, décor, and case doors, targeted at stores where the physical plant is tired but the offer is right; it is underwritten to a 2.5 percent lift, which the data supports, and returns roughly 9 percent — marginal but defensible in stores with long lease terms. A full tier at approximately $3.2 million adds a genuine departmental change and is limited to stores with at least 12 years of remaining lease term and demonstrated trade-area household growth of 4 percent or better over five years; it is underwritten to a 9 percent lift. Both tiers should require a guaranteed maximum price contract, a design freeze 90 days before permit application with no post-approval scope additions below the committee level, and a completed existing-conditions survey before the capital request is written.

## Fiscal 2026 Commitments Already in Place

The fiscal 2026 capital plan carries $9.2 million for the four paused remodels — Store 118 in Hickory, Store 043 in Florence, Store 061 in Rocky Mount, and Store 127 in Danville. Against that, $2.1 million is already committed: $1.4 million in refrigeration and case equipment ordered for Hickory and Florence, $310,000 in completed demolition and permitting at Hickory, and $390,000 in architectural and engineering fees across all four.

Hickory is the only genuine constraint. The store is demolished to the slab and closed, and every week it remains closed costs approximately $61,000 in gross profit and erodes a trade area we will have to win back. We recommend a re-scoped $2.9 million completion using the equipment already purchased, without the departmental additions in the original design, and a target reopening in the third quarter of fiscal 2026.

The remaining $1.4 million of ordered equipment is not stranded. Refrigeration cases specified for Florence are compatible with the refresh tier and with two of the small-format stores in Summary 1, and we recommend they be warehoused for redeployment rather than cancelled; cancellation carries a 15 percent restocking charge of approximately $210,000 and forfeits eleven months of lead time. Florence, Rocky Mount, and Danville should remain paused pending re-underwriting, and the $6.3 million of unspent authorization attached to them should be returned to the general pool at this meeting.

*Approximately 1,310 words.*
