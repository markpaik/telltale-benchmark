## 1. Executive Summary

This review presents plant-level operating performance for the four manufacturing facilities of Vandalia Industrial Group — Huntington, West Virginia; Springfield, Ohio; Muncie, Indiana; and Chambersburg, Pennsylvania — for the first half of fiscal year 2026. It is prepared at the request of Chief Executive Officer Doreen Whitaker for the Executive Committee's regular semiannual review, and, because of the serious safety citations issued at Springfield in March, an expanded safety section is included for the Audit Committee's review of compliance and liability exposure. That section was contributed directly by Malik Ferguson, Director of Environmental Health and Safety.

The first half was a difficult period operationally. None of the four plants met its overall equipment effectiveness or first-pass yield targets, and three of the four carry specific, material problems that will affect results into the second half and, in two cases, into fiscal 2027. Huntington absorbed an eleven-week press rebuild delay and 340 hours of unplanned downtime that pulled overall equipment effectiveness to 61 percent against a 72 percent target. Springfield's March safety inspection produced three serious citations and an $84,600 proposed penalty, and the plant's recordable injury rate of 4.8 stands at more than double the company average of 2.1. Muncie learned in May that a $28 million-a-year appliance customer will exit in January 2027, a decision that leaves the plant at 63 percent utilization today and will strand substantially more capacity once the departure takes effect. Chambersburg's new $22 million coating line opened five months late and continues to run at a 78 percent first-pass yield against a 94 percent design target.

Layered on top of these plant-specific issues are three company-wide pressures: a 19 percent increase in resin costs, overtime running at 14 percent of direct labor hours, and 121 open production positions across the four sites. Each of these compounds the plant-level problems — unfilled positions push overtime higher, overtime fatigue contributes to both quality and safety exposure, and resin inflation erodes the margin cushion the company would otherwise use to absorb the Huntington and Chambersburg inefficiencies.

The purpose of this review is to give the Executive Committee a clear, plant-by-plant account of what happened, what it is costing the company, and what management is doing about it, and to give the Audit Committee a complete accounting of the safety findings, their financial exposure, and their implications for the control environment. Recommendations and near-term milestones are noted at the close of each section and summarized in Section 9.

## 3. Plant-by-Plant Review

### 3.1 Huntington, West Virginia — 620 Employees

Huntington is the company's largest single site and its headquarters plant, producing coated metal components and engineered polymer housings for both heating equipment and appliance customers. The plant's first-half performance was dominated by a capital project that did not go according to plan.

The plant undertook a scheduled rebuild of Press Line 3, a stamping asset central to the metal-components product family, during the second quarter of the fiscal year. The rebuild was budgeted at six weeks. It ran eleven weeks — a five-week overrun — because of two compounding issues: a delay in delivery of a rebuilt hydraulic servo assembly from the original equipment manufacturer, and the discovery, once the press was partially disassembled, of frame cracking that had not been visible during the pre-rebuild inspection and required additional welding and stress-relief work not in the original scope. The combined effect was 340 hours of unplanned downtime beyond the planned rebuild window, spread across the balance of the quarter as the line was brought back into full production and as changeover procedures were re-validated.

The downtime, together with softer-than-planned output on two other lines while volume was redirected to cover the Press Line 3 gap, drove overall equipment effectiveness to 61 percent against the plant's 72 percent target — an eleven-point shortfall. Scrap for the half stood at 4.9 percent, nearly two points above the 3.0 percent company target, reflecting both the instability of the rebuilt press during requalification and elevated scrap on the substitute production routing used while Press Line 3 was down.

The financial effect of the Huntington shortfall is material. At the plant's approximate throughput rate, 340 hours of unplanned downtime represents an estimated $2.1 million in lost contribution during the half, before considering the incremental overtime and expedited freight costs incurred to protect customer delivery commitments during the outage. Huntington did not miss a customer ship date during the rebuild, which is to the credit of the plant's scheduling and logistics team, but that outcome was achieved through extensive overtime — Huntington's overtime rate for the half ran above the company average, a point addressed further in Section 6.

Management has taken the following corrective steps. The press rebuild is now complete and Press Line 3 has run at target rate for the four weeks preceding this report. A root-cause review of the frame cracking has been commissioned with the original equipment manufacturer, and a revised preventive-maintenance and inspection protocol for the remaining stamping assets at Huntington — several of which are of similar age and duty cycle — is being developed to prevent a recurrence on the next scheduled rebuild, planned for Press Line 5 in the fourth quarter. Scrap reduction is being addressed through a renewed changeover-standardization effort led by the plant's continuous-improvement team, with a target of returning to 3.0 percent scrap by the end of the third quarter.

**Second-half outlook:** With Press Line 3 restored, Huntington is expected to recover toward its 72 percent OEE target by the fourth quarter, though full-year OEE will not reach target because of the first-half shortfall. The plant remains the company's most important single site for the heating-equipment product line and carries no customer-retention risk comparable to Muncie's.

### 3.2 Springfield, Ohio — 430 Employees

Springfield, under plant manager Hae-Won Jeong, produces coated metal components primarily for appliance manufacturers. Springfield's operating metrics for the half were closer to target than Huntington's or Chambersburg's — OEE of 68 percent against 72 percent, and scrap of 3.1 percent, only slightly above target — but the plant's safety performance during the period is the most serious issue addressed in this review and is treated in full in Section 4.

Briefly, in operational terms: Springfield's March inspection by state occupational safety authorities resulted in three serious citations and a proposed penalty of $84,600. The plant's recordable injury rate for the trailing twelve months stands at 4.8 per 200,000 hours worked, against a company average of 2.1 — more than double. Ms. Jeong and Mr. Ferguson's team have been engaged in an abatement and remediation program since the inspection, described in Section 4, and Springfield's production schedule has not been materially disrupted by the citations to date, though management should be aware that further enforcement action, including potential referral for repeat or willful classification if abatement deadlines are missed, remains a live risk.

Springfield's underlying production performance, absent the safety findings, is broadly acceptable: the plant is running at 89 percent utilization, the highest of the four sites, reflecting strong order flow from its appliance-manufacturer customer base. Management does not want the relative strength of Springfield's throughput numbers to obscure the seriousness of the safety findings, and the two are discussed together with that caution in mind in Section 4.

### 3.3 Muncie, Indiana — 510 Employees

Muncie, under plant manager Ruben Alcantara, produces engineered polymer components primarily for appliance manufacturers, and its first-half operating metrics were the strongest in the network on a pure efficiency basis: OEE of 70 percent, the closest of any plant to the 72 percent target, and scrap of 2.8 percent, below the 3.0 percent company target. Recordable injury rate at Muncie, 1.6, is the best in the network.

The plant's central issue for this review is not a production or safety problem but a commercial one, disclosed to management in May of this fiscal year: Muncie's largest single customer, an appliance manufacturer representing approximately $28 million in annual revenue — roughly 27 percent of the plant's total output on a revenue basis — notified the company that it will move this business to an alternative supplier effective January 2027. This decision followed a multi-year sourcing review by the customer that Vandalia's account team participated in but did not win; the customer's stated rationale centered on a competitor's lower delivered cost from a facility closer to the customer's own assembly operations, rather than on any quality or delivery failure at Muncie.

The immediate effect of the announcement, well ahead of the actual transition date, has been on plant utilization and morale rather than on current output: Muncie is running at 63 percent utilization today, the lowest of the four plants, as the customer has already begun phasing down order volume ahead of the formal January 2027 transition to give its new supplier time to ramp. This is addressed in detail, including financial exposure and mitigation options, in Section 7.

Aside from the customer loss, Muncie's operating fundamentals remain the strongest in the network, and Mr. Alcantara's team should be recognized for sustaining OEE and quality performance during a period in which morale has been affected by uncertainty about the plant's future workload.

### 3.4 Chambersburg, Pennsylvania — 290 Employees

Chambersburg is the company's smallest plant and the site of its most significant recent capital investment: a $22 million coating line intended to expand the plant's capacity in high-durability coated components for heating-equipment customers, and to reduce the company's reliance on Huntington and Springfield for that product family. The line was originally scheduled to begin commercial production in October 2025; it did not reach commercial start-up until March 2026, a five-month delay.

The delay resulted from a combination of factors: extended commissioning time for the line's new curing-oven control system, which required three rounds of software revision by the equipment vendor before temperature uniformity met specification; a six-week delay in delivery of a replacement blower assembly after the original unit failed acceptance testing; and additional operator training time needed because the line's control interface differs substantially from Chambersburg's legacy coating equipment.

Now in commercial operation, the line is running at a first-pass yield of 78 percent against a 94 percent design target — a sixteen-point shortfall that reflects a process still being tuned rather than a fundamental equipment or design flaw, according to the joint assessment of Chambersburg engineering and the equipment vendor. The gap between actual and target yield is costing the plant an estimated $180,000 per month in rework and scrap-related cost at current run rates, a figure that should decline steadily as the process stabilizes but that will affect Chambersburg's contribution for at least the next two quarters. Detail on the launch, corrective actions, and revised timeline to design yield is provided in Section 8.

Chambersburg's overall plant OEE for the half, at 64 percent against a 72 percent target, reflects both the new line's startup inefficiency and the disruption to the plant's existing coating operations caused by diverting maintenance and engineering resources to the new-line commissioning effort during the fourth quarter.

## 4. Safety Review

*This section is contributed by Malik Ferguson, Director of Environmental Health and Safety, for the Audit Committee's review of the March inspection findings at Springfield and their company-wide implications.*

### 4.1 Springfield Inspection Findings

On March 4–6, 2026, inspectors from the state occupational safety authority conducted an unannounced inspection of the Springfield facility, prompted by an employee complaint filed in late February concerning machine guarding on the plant's coating-line conveyor system. The inspection resulted in three serious citations:

| Citation | Standard Cited | Description | Proposed Penalty |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Machine Guarding | Inadequate point-of-operation guarding on two conveyor transfer points on Coating Line 2 | $38,700 |
| 2 | Lockout/Tagout | Incomplete energy-control procedures for maintenance work on the same conveyor system | $29,400 |
| 3 | Hazard Communication | Outdated safety data sheets and incomplete employee training records for two coating chemicals introduced in the prior twelve months | $16,500 |
| **Total** | | | **$84,600** |

None of the citations arose from an injury during the inspection itself, but the guarding and lockout findings are directly relevant to Springfield's elevated injury history, discussed below. Abatement periods range from 15 to 30 days from the citation date; the machine-guarding and lockout items, as the more serious findings, carry the shorter abatement windows. Mr. Ferguson's team, working with Ms. Jeong and Springfield's maintenance department, has completed physical guarding modifications on both cited transfer points and has revised the lockout/tagout procedure for the conveyor system; both corrective actions were verified and documented ahead of their respective abatement deadlines. The hazard communication citation is in process, with updated safety data sheets obtained from the chemical suppliers and a refresher training session for affected employees scheduled to be completed within the abatement window.

The company has 15 working days from receipt of the citations to contest the findings or penalty amount with the state review commission. Management is not recommending a contest of the underlying violations, which reflect genuine conditions that have been corrected, but is reviewing the penalty calculation with outside counsel to determine whether the proposed amount reflects appropriate credit for the plant's abatement good faith and prior inspection history. A recommendation on whether to contest the penalty amount will be brought to the Audit Committee separately within thirty days.

### 4.2 Springfield Injury Rate

Independent of the March inspection, Springfield's trailing-twelve-month recordable injury rate of 4.8 per 200,000 hours worked is more than double the company average of 2.1 and is the highest of the four plants. A review of Springfield's OSHA 300 log for the period shows the injuries are concentrated in two categories: strain and overexertion injuries associated with manual material handling on the coating-line finishing stations (accounting for roughly half of recordables), and minor lacerations and pinch-point injuries consistent with the guarding deficiencies identified in the March inspection.

This pattern suggests the injury rate and the citations are not coincidental but reflect a common underlying condition: production line design and workstation ergonomics at Springfield that have not kept pace with the plant's growth in volume over the past several years. Mr. Ferguson's team has begun an ergonomic assessment of the finishing stations, with recommendations expected by the end of the third quarter, and has increased the frequency of safety walk-throughs at Springfield from monthly to biweekly for the remainder of fiscal 2026.

### 4.3 Company-Wide Implications

The Springfield findings should be read by the Executive Committee and Audit Committee as a signal to review safety program consistency across all four plants, not as an isolated Springfield issue. Mr. Ferguson's department conducts annual safety audits at each plant, but the Springfield findings arose from conditions — inadequate guarding on a conveyor system that had been in service for several years, and incomplete lockout procedures — that a routine audit could reasonably have been expected to identify. Accordingly, Mr. Ferguson is recommending, and the Executive Committee should consider authorizing, an accelerated cross-plant guarding and lockout/tagout audit at Huntington, Muncie, and Chambersburg, to be completed within the second quarter of the second half of fiscal 2026, ahead of the normal annual audit cycle. The estimated cost of the accelerated audit, using a combination of internal staff and a third-party safety consultant, is $95,000.

Beyond the direct financial exposure of the Springfield penalty, the Audit Committee should be aware that a documented pattern of serious citations, particularly if repeated at another site, could affect the company's eligibility for certain customer supplier-qualification programs — several of Vandalia's appliance-manufacturer customers, including the customer discussed in Section 7, include safety performance as a factor in supplier scorecards. Springfield's citations and injury rate should therefore be treated as both a compliance matter and a commercial risk factor.

## 6. Cost and Staffing Pressures

Three company-wide pressures compound the plant-specific issues described above and warrant Executive Committee attention as a set, because each interacts with the others.

**Resin costs.** Engineering resin, the primary polymer input for the Huntington, Muncie, and Chambersburg polymer-molding operations, rose 19 percent year over year, driven by feedstock volatility in the domestic petrochemical market and the consolidation of two mid-sized resin suppliers into a larger competitor during the second quarter, which reduced the number of qualified alternative sources available to procurement. Purchasing has partially mitigated the increase through a fourth-quarter contract renegotiation that locks pricing for 60 percent of projected second-half volume, but the unhedged portion remains exposed to further increases. The 19 percent increase, applied against the company's resin spend base, represents an estimated $9.4 million in incremental first-half cost across the network, concentrated most heavily at Muncie and Chambersburg given their polymer-intensive product mix.

**Overtime.** Company-wide overtime reached 14 percent of direct labor hours for the half, against a level management has historically treated as sustainable at 8 percent. Overtime is not evenly distributed: Huntington's rate is highest, driven by the Press Line 3 rebuild coverage described in Section 3.1, followed by Chambersburg, where overtime has been used to staff both the new coating line commissioning and ongoing legacy production simultaneously. Sustained overtime at this level carries three risks the Executive Committee should weigh together: direct cost (overtime hours cost the company an estimated premium of $6.8 million for the half above straight-time equivalent), fatigue-related quality and safety risk of the kind discussed in Section 4, and retention risk, as sustained mandatory overtime has been cited in exit interviews at both Huntington and Chambersburg as a factor in voluntary turnover.

**Open positions.** 121 production positions remain open across the network against budgeted headcount, roughly 6.5 percent of total production headcount. The openings are concentrated in skilled trades — maintenance technicians, coating-line operators, and press setters — rather than general production labor, reflecting a tight regional labor market for skilled manufacturing trades in each of the four plant communities. Human resources has expanded recruiting partnerships with two additional technical colleges near Huntington and Chambersburg and has raised starting pay for maintenance technician roles by 8 percent company-wide, effective the start of the third quarter, but management does not expect the position gap to close meaningfully before the fourth quarter at the earliest.

The interaction among these three pressures is the point management wants to emphasize to the Executive Committee: open positions drive overtime, overtime drives fatigue and turnover, turnover reopens positions, and resin inflation reduces the margin available to absorb overtime premium cost without price action. Procurement and human resources are coordinating a joint mitigation plan, summarized in Section 9, but the Executive Committee should expect these pressures to persist through at least the third quarter.

## 7. Muncie Customer Loss — Detail and Mitigation

The loss of Muncie's $28 million appliance-manufacturer account, effective January 2027, is the most significant medium-term commercial issue in this review, and this section provides the detail the Executive Committee will need to evaluate mitigation options.

**Financial exposure.** The $28 million in annual revenue represents approximately 5.4 percent of the company's total prior-year revenue and, on the plant's own cost structure, an estimated $6.5 million in annual contribution margin. The transition is structured as a phased volume reduction rather than a single cutoff: the customer has already reduced order volume by approximately 15 percent as of this report, with further step-downs expected through the remainder of calendar 2026 before the account fully exits in January 2027. This phased approach is reflected in Muncie's current 63 percent utilization figure and will worsen before it stabilizes, absent replacement business.

**Root cause.** The account team's debrief with the customer indicates the decision centered on landed cost, specifically the customer's preference for a supplier with a facility closer to its own final-assembly plant, reducing freight cost and inventory carrying time. Quality and delivery performance were affirmatively cited by the customer as not being factors in the decision — Muncie's scorecard with this customer was in good standing at the time of the decision — which management views as relevant context: this is a structural cost and logistics loss, not a performance failure, and the same dynamic could affect other accounts with similar geographic profiles if not addressed.

**Mitigation options under evaluation.** Three workstreams are underway. First, Muncie's business development team, supported by the corporate sales organization, has identified four prospective replacement accounts within the appliance and adjacent small-equipment sectors that could utilize the freed capacity, representing a combined pipeline of approximately $19 million in potential annual revenue; two of the four are in active quoting, with decisions expected in the third quarter. Second, management is evaluating whether a portion of Muncie's freed capacity can be used to reshore production currently run at Huntington on an overflow basis, which would partially offset Huntington's own capacity constraints during the Press Line 3 recovery period discussed in Section 3.1. Third, and least developed at this stage, is a review of whether Muncie's cost structure could be adjusted — through selective automation investment or headcount right-sizing timed to the actual volume step-downs — to improve the plant's competitiveness for future geographically-sensitive accounts of this kind.

**Headcount implications.** If the pipeline replacement business does not materialize at a level sufficient to offset the departing account, Muncie's headcount, currently 510, would need to be evaluated for right-sizing beginning in the fourth calendar quarter of 2026, ahead of the January 2027 transition. No headcount action is recommended at this time; management's preference is to preserve the workforce through the third quarter while the replacement-business pipeline develops, and to bring a specific staffing recommendation to the Executive Committee no later than the next quarterly review if the pipeline has not converted to firm orders by that point.

## 8. Chambersburg Launch — Detail and Path to Design Yield

The Chambersburg coating line represents the company's largest single capital project of the past three years, and its five-month delay and continuing yield shortfall warrant a fuller account than the summary in Section 3.4.

**Timeline.** The project was approved by the Executive Committee in the prior fiscal year with a planned commercial start-up of October 2025. Equipment installation was substantially complete on schedule in September 2025. The delay occurred entirely in the commissioning phase: curing-oven control-system software required three revision cycles between October and December 2025 to achieve specified temperature uniformity across the coating zone; a replacement blower assembly, needed after the original unit failed acceptance vibration testing in November, was not delivered by the vendor until early January 2026 because of a supply-chain backlog at the vendor's own subcomponent supplier; and operator training and process qualification, originally scheduled to run concurrently with the final weeks of equipment commissioning, instead had to follow it sequentially, adding approximately six additional weeks before commercial production began in March 2026.

**Current yield performance.** First-pass yield since commercial start-up has averaged 78 percent against the 94 percent design target. Engineering's assessment, shared jointly with the equipment vendor, attributes the gap to three specific and, in management's assessment, addressable causes: line speed is currently set 12 percent below design rate to maintain coating thickness consistency while the curing-zone temperature profile is fine-tuned, a restriction expected to be lifted incrementally over the next two months as the profile stabilizes; substrate pre-treatment parameters, carried over from the legacy line, have not yet been fully re-optimized for the new line's different coating chemistry and are producing a higher rate of adhesion-related rejects than expected; and operator proficiency, while improving, has not yet reached the steady-state level assumed in the 94 percent design target, which was itself based on the vendor's experienced-operator benchmark rather than a ramp-adjusted figure.

**Cost impact.** At current yield levels, Chambersburg is incurring an estimated $180,000 per month in incremental rework, scrap, and reprocessing cost attributable to the yield gap, a figure engineering expects to decline by roughly a third by the end of the third quarter as the line-speed and pre-treatment corrections take effect, with a further decline expected in the fourth quarter as operator proficiency matures. Management's current estimate is that the line will reach 90 percent first-pass yield by the end of the fourth quarter and approach the full 94 percent design target early in fiscal 2027, roughly two quarters behind the original project plan.

**Vendor accountability.** Because a meaningful share of the delay is attributable to the equipment vendor — the control-system revisions and the blower-assembly delay together account for roughly three of the five months lost — the company's contracts team is pursuing a liquidated-damages claim under the equipment purchase agreement. The claim is in early stages; management will report the outcome to the Executive Committee separately once the vendor has responded to the formal claim, expected within the next quarter.

**Site leadership.** As noted in Section 2, Chambersburg's plant leadership is in transition, with the plant engineering manager serving in an interim operational capacity since the coating-line commissioning period placed unusual demands on the prior structure. A permanent plant manager search is underway, with an internal and external slate both under consideration; management expects to bring a recommendation to Ms. Whitaker within the current quarter.

## 9. Summary of Recommendations and Near-Term Milestones

The following actions are recommended for Executive Committee approval or awareness, and, where noted, for Audit Committee review:

1. **Huntington** — Complete root-cause review of Press Line 3 frame cracking with the equipment vendor and revise the stamping-asset preventive-maintenance protocol before the Press Line 5 rebuild scheduled for the fourth quarter. Continue scrap-reduction effort with a target of 3.0 percent by end of third quarter.

2. **Springfield** — Complete all outstanding citation abatement items ahead of deadlines; bring a recommendation on whether to contest the $84,600 proposed penalty to the Audit Committee within thirty days. Complete the finishing-station ergonomic assessment by end of third quarter.

3. **Muncie** — Continue active pursuit of the $19 million replacement-business pipeline; bring a specific staffing recommendation to the Executive Committee at the next quarterly review if pipeline orders have not converted by that point. Evaluate feasibility of using freed Muncie capacity to relieve Huntington overflow.

4. **Chambersburg** — Continue line-speed and pre-treatment optimization toward the 90 percent yield milestone by end of fourth quarter; report outcome of the vendor liquidated-damages claim; finalize permanent plant manager appointment within the current quarter.

5. **Safety (Audit Committee)** — Authorize the accelerated cross-plant guarding and lockout/tagout audit at Huntington, Muncie, and Chambersburg, estimated at $95,000, to be completed within the second quarter of the second half of fiscal 2026.

6. **Cost and staffing** — Continue the joint procurement/human resources mitigation plan addressing resin cost exposure, overtime reduction, and skilled-trades recruiting; management will report progress against the 8 percent sustainable overtime target at the next quarterly review.

Management believes each of these issues is addressable within the corrective timelines described above, but flags for the Executive Committee that the combined effect of the Huntington downtime, Springfield's compliance exposure, the Muncie transition, and the Chambersburg ramp will continue to weigh on second-half consolidated operating results, with the most significant single risk being whether the Muncie replacement-business pipeline converts in time to offset the January 2027 customer transition.

Ms. Charbonneau and Mr. Ferguson are available to discuss any section of this review in greater detail at the Executive Committee and Audit Committee meetings.
