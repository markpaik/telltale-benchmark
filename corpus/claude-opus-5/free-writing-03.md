**Still**

---

**1. *adj.* Free from sound or movement; calm.**

The heron in the shallows has made a decision about time. It has decided to spend it. Twenty minutes it will stand in six inches of water with one foot lifted, not out of patience — patience implies an interior weather of restlessness being suppressed — but because stillness is what it has instead of speed. It cannot outswim a fish. It can only outwait one.

There is a kind of stillness that is not the absence of motion but its concentration. The archer at full draw. The held breath before the name is read. A rope, pulled from both ends, is doing more work than a rope in a heap on the floor, and looks like it is doing less.

In the 1840s, to be photographed was to be clamped. The daguerreotype needed minutes of light, and so the sitter's head was fixed in an iron brace behind the neck, invisible in the final plate, and the arms were arranged on props, and the eyes were asked to hold. This is why the people in those portraits look the way they do — not solemn, exactly, and not sad, but *braced*. They have the faces of people being asked to survive an interval. We read their expressions as the temperament of the nineteenth century. It is only the effort of not moving.

---

**2. *n.* An apparatus for distilling.**

This one is a stranger. The others in this entry share a bloodline — Old English *stille*, quiet, at rest — but the copper still is a foundling that wandered in from Latin, from *destillare*, to drip down, and shortened itself at the door until it looked like family. The vessel that drips has nothing to do with the heron. They are homonyms who have lived together so long they have started to resemble each other.

And yet. What a still does is a kind of stillness, too: it takes a large muddled volume and refuses most of it. Heat, and then the patience of the coil, and what comes out the other end is smaller and truer and will burn. Reduction as a form of attention. You cannot hurry it. Run the fire too hot and you carry over everything you were trying to leave behind.

The Arabic *al-anbīq*, the alembic, from the Greek *ambix*, a cup — the word arrived in Europe already full. Distillation came to us the way most useful things do: sideways, through translation, carrying its container with it.

I like that the two meanings converged by accident. It suggests that language, given enough centuries, will hallucinate coherence. It will find a rhyme between a bird and a boiler and simply decide the rhyme was always the point.

---

**3. *adv.* Up to this time; even now. *conj.* Nevertheless.**

Here is the drift, and it is the most beautiful thing I know about the word.

It began as motionlessness. Then, because a thing that does not move is a thing that continues, it came to mean *continuously* — he stood still weeping, meaning he kept on. Then, because a thing that continues arrives at the present, it came to mean *even now*. *She is still alive.* And then, because to arrive at the present against expectation is to have argued with something, it became a conjunction of defiance. *Still, I went.* *Still, we tried.*

Watch what happened. The word for not moving became the word for not stopping. Stillness turned itself inside out and came up as persistence.

I don't think this is a paradox. I think the language noticed something we keep having to relearn: that endurance and quiet are the same gesture seen from two ends. The heron is not doing nothing. The rope is not slack. What we call giving up and what we call holding on can look identical from across the water, and the difference is entirely a matter of which direction the pull is coming from.

*Still, I went.* Two words, and inside them a whole unnarrated history of objection — the people who said don't, the weather, the cost, the good reasons. The word swallows the argument and reports only that the argument was had and lost. English has no more efficient container for a life.

In German the verb *stillen* means to nurse a child. It also means to quench a thirst, and to staunch a wound — to stop blood. One word for feeding and for stopping the loss. To still a baby is to fill it; to still a bleeding is to close it; and in English, to still a crying child is only to make it quiet. Somewhere between the two languages there is a question about whether we soothe by giving or by sealing, and neither language will answer it.

And there is the other word, the one that comes when the two meanings — motionless, and *even now* — collide inside a single birth. The word is cruel because it is exact. It names the thing by what it withholds. There was no cry, and there will be no *even now.* I include it because a word does not get to be lovely in three of its rooms and be excused from the fourth. This is the room where the word does its damage, and it does it with the same syllable that describes the heron.

---

**4. *n.* A single photographic frame extracted from a moving picture.**

For a long time it was taught that we see film as motion because of persistence of vision — the retina holds each frame a moment too long, the images overlap, the gap is papered over. This turns out to be roughly wrong. The better account involves the brain's active insistence on continuity: shown two similar things in two nearby places, it does not perceive two things. It perceives one thing that *went*. The motion is not a smear left on the eye. It is a story told by the visual cortex, at twenty-four assertions per second, about causation.

Which means the still is the fact and the film is the argument. The strip is a fence of separate pickets and we see a running horse.

The Dutch named the genre *stilleven* in the 1650s — still life, the lemon half-peeled, the rind in its long spiral, the glass with its inch of wine and its fly. The French call it *nature morte*, dead nature, which is more honest and much worse. The Dutch were right. It is not that the lemon is dead. It is that the lemon has been detained. The whole subject of those paintings is the sixty seconds before the fly lands, held open with a crowbar, and every one of them is a photograph taken four hundred years too early by a man who had to hold the shutter open with his hand for six weeks.

---

**Coda**

And there is the still small voice — the King James translators reaching for the Hebrew *qol demamah daqqah*, which is closer to *a sound of thin silence*, a phrase that should not work and does. Elijah in the cave: the wind that broke the mountains, and God was not in the wind; the earthquake, and not in the earthquake; the fire, and not in the fire. And after the fire, a sound of thin silence. The point of the passage is not that God is quiet. It is that after everything enormous has finished happening, something is still going on, and you have to have stopped moving to hear it.

Four rooms, then, and a stranger in one of them. Motionless. Reduced to what will burn. Even now, nevertheless, against the argument. And the single frame that the eye refuses to accept as single.

The word is doing something no one designed. It is holding *stopped* and *continuing* in the same three letters and declining to say which one it means, so that every time we use it we are quietly asserting that these are not opposites — that to persist is to remain, that to remain is to be, in some sense, unmoved.

Still here.

Both meanings. That's the whole trick of it.
