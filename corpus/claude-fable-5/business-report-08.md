## 1. Executive Summary

Vandalia Industrial Group closed the first half of fiscal 2026 in a weaker operating position than the plan contemplated, and the causes are neither uniform nor confined to a single site. Each of our four plants carries a distinct and serious problem: Huntington absorbed an eleven-week slip on a critical press rebuild that dragged overall equipment effectiveness eleven points below target; Springfield drew three serious citations and an $84,600 proposed penalty from a March compliance inspection while running a recordable injury rate more than double the company standard; Muncie learned in May that a $28 million appliance account will leave in January 2027, a loss that will push an already underutilized plant to a level that is difficult to sustain; and Chambersburg brought its $22 million coating line online five months late and is holding first-pass yield sixteen points below the design target.

Layered over the plant-level issues are three company-wide pressures. Resin costs rose 19 percent against the prior year, a movement we have only partially recovered through pricing and substitution. Overtime reached 14 percent of direct labor hours, well above the 8 percent level we consider sustainable, and it is being driven simultaneously by the 121 open production positions we have been unable to fill and by the schedule disruption at Huntington and Chambersburg. These three forces — material inflation, labor scarcity, and overtime dependence — feed each other, and the second half will require deliberate action on all three rather than incremental correction on any one.

This report was prepared at the request of Chief Executive Doreen Whitaker with plant-level detail throughout. Because of the March safety inspection at Springfield, the safety section and the compliance discussion are addressed to the audit committee as well as the executive committee. Malik Ferguson, our director of environmental health and safety, authored Section 7 and reviewed the safety content elsewhere in the document.

The summary judgment is this: the company's fundamentals — our customer relationships, our engineering capability, our four-plant footprint — remain sound, but first-half execution fell short at every site, and the Muncie customer decision tells us that at least one major customer has concluded we are not indispensable. The second-half agenda set out in Section 10 is built to change that conclusion before it spreads.

## 2. Company Overview and Report Scope

Vandalia Industrial Group manufactures engineered polymer components and coated metal components for heating equipment and appliance original equipment manufacturers. We operate four plants — Huntington, West Virginia (headquarters and largest site); Springfield, Ohio; Muncie, Indiana; and Chambersburg, Pennsylvania — employ 1,850 people, and posted $520 million in revenue last fiscal year.

This review covers manufacturing operations for the first half of fiscal 2026. Where events after the close of the period materially change the outlook — the March inspection findings at Springfield and the May customer notification at Muncie are the two significant cases — they are included, because a first-half review that omitted them would misstate the position the company actually occupies.

The report is organized as follows: Section 3 presents the consolidated operating picture; Sections 4 through 6 and Section 8 cover the four plants individually, each with its performance data and its principal problem; Section 7 presents the safety review and the company-wide implications of the Springfield findings; Section 9 addresses cost and staffing pressures across the network; and Section 10 sets the second-half priorities and the specific commitments I am prepared to be measured against.

## 3. Consolidated Operating Performance

Taken as a whole, the first half was a period in which volume held roughly to plan but the cost and quality of producing that volume deteriorated. Consolidated overall equipment effectiveness came in at 66 percent against a 74 percent weighted target, with the shortfall concentrated at Huntington and Chambersburg. Scrap ran at 4.1 percent of production value against a 3.0 percent target, with Huntington's 4.9 percent and Chambersburg's launch-related losses the principal contributors. On-time delivery slipped to 89 percent against a 96 percent standard, and the misses cluster around the same two events: the Huntington press rebuild and the Chambersburg coating line delay.

The financial consequence of these operating misses is a margin story rather than a revenue story. We shipped close to plan by expediting, running overtime, and in several weeks air-freighting product that should have moved by truck. Those recovery costs are real money — overtime at 14 percent of direct hours alone represents a premium labor cost well above budget — and they explain most of the gap between the volume we produced and the margin we earned on it.

Two consolidated numbers deserve particular attention from the committee. First, the recordable injury rate of 2.9 against our 2.1 standard is not evenly distributed: three plants are at or near standard, and Springfield at 4.8 pulls the company figure up. That concentration is discussed at length in Sections 5 and 7. Second, the 121 open production positions represent roughly 8 percent of the production workforce, and vacancy at that level is not a hiring inconvenience — it is an operating constraint that shows up in the overtime figure, in training compression, and, as Section 7 argues, in the safety data.

## 4. Huntington Plant Review

### 4.1 Performance Data

Huntington is our largest and oldest plant, with 620 employees, and it carries the large-press molding work that anchors several of our heating-equipment programs. Its first-half numbers are dominated by a single event, but the numbers themselves should be stated plainly first.

| Metric | Target | H1 Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Overall equipment effectiveness | 72% | 61% |
| Scrap rate | 3.0% | 4.9% |
| Unplanned downtime (press area) | — | 340 hours |
| Recordable injury rate | 2.1 | 2.0 |
| On-time delivery | 96% | 87% |
| Headcount / open positions | 620 budgeted | 620 on roll; 38 open production positions within that count |

### 4.2 The Press Rebuild Slip

The plant's principal problem in the first half was the rebuild of its primary large-tonnage press, which slipped eleven weeks past its planned completion and cost 340 hours of unplanned downtime beyond the outage window we had scheduled and buffered for. The consequences ran through every other number on the plant's scorecard: OEE finished at 61 percent against the 72 percent target, an eleven-point miss that is almost entirely attributable to the availability loss from the extended outage; scrap rose to 4.9 percent as work was shifted onto secondary presses running tooling they were not optimized for; and on-time delivery fell to 87 percent despite heavy overtime and expedited freight.

The rebuild slipped for three reasons, in roughly equal measure. First, the platen castings arrived from the supplier six weeks late, and our contract carried no meaningful delay remedy — a procurement gap we have since corrected in the purchase terms for capital rebuilds. Second, when the press was opened, the frame wear was worse than the pre-outage inspection had predicted, adding scope that could not have been deferred. Third, the rebuild contractor's crew was pulled to another customer for nine days mid-project, a breach of the schedule commitment that we are pursuing commercially but that cost us time we could not recover.

I want to be direct about accountability. The pre-outage inspection should have been more invasive — a borescope and ultrasonic survey of the frame would likely have found the additional wear and let us plan the scope rather than discover it. That was a planning decision made in my organization, and the lesson is now embedded in the capital rebuild standard: no major press rebuild proceeds without a full non-destructive survey during the planning phase.

### 4.3 Recovery and Outlook

The press returned to production in the final week of the period and has run at rated availability since. The plant's recovery plan has three elements: burning down the delivery backlog by the end of the first quarter of the second half, which will keep overtime elevated at Huntington through that quarter; returning scrap to the 3.0 percent standard as work migrates back off the secondary presses, which is already visible in the weekly data; and completing the deferred preventive maintenance that was postponed during the recovery push, which must not be sacrificed again to schedule pressure. Huntington's safety performance, it should be noted, held at 2.0 through the disruption — evidence that schedule pressure and safety discipline can coexist when supervision insists on it, a point that matters for the Springfield discussion that follows.

## 5. Springfield Plant Review

### 5.1 Performance Data

Springfield, under plant manager Hae-Won Jeong, employs 430 people in polymer molding and assembly. Its production metrics for the half were unremarkable — OEE near target, scrap slightly favorable, delivery on standard. Its safety and compliance position is the worst in the company and is the reason this report goes to the audit committee.

### 5.2 The March Inspection and Citations

A regulatory compliance inspection conducted in March resulted in three serious citations and a proposed penalty of $84,600. The citations, in summary: first, machine guarding deficiencies on a bank of older molding presses, where interlocked guards had been modified in a manner that defeated their protective function; second, lockout/tagout program failures, specifically the absence of machine-specific energy control procedures for several pieces of equipment and documented instances of maintenance work performed without full isolation; and third, deficiencies in the powered industrial truck program, including expired operator certifications and a pattern of pre-shift inspections recorded without being performed.

The company is contesting elements of the penalty calculation through counsel, but I want the committee to hear the operational judgment without the legal overlay: the underlying conditions were real, they were findable by our own audit program and were not found, and two of the three citation areas — guarding modification and lockout discipline — describe conditions that could plausibly have produced a fatality. The $84,600 penalty is not the material exposure. The material exposure was to our people.

### 5.3 The Injury Rate

Springfield's recordable injury rate of 4.8 is more than double the company standard of 2.1 and has been trending upward for three consecutive quarters. The injury log shows a pattern consistent with the citation findings: laceration and caught-in incidents at the older presses, a strain profile associated with manual material handling that mechanization was budgeted to address two years ago and was deferred, and two forklift-related near-misses that in retrospect were warnings.

Section 7 addresses causation and the company-wide response in detail. Here I will say only that Hae-Won Jeong inherited a plant with an aging asset base and a thin maintenance organization, has been candid with me throughout, and has committed fully to the abatement plan. The accountability question is not whether Springfield's leadership responded once the findings were made — they have — but why our own governance did not surface these conditions first. That question belongs to my organization and to the corporate EHS function, and Section 7 answers it.

### 5.4 Abatement Status

All three citation areas are under formal abatement. Guarding on the affected presses has been restored to design configuration and third-party verified; machine-specific lockout procedures are complete for 84 percent of Springfield's equipment with the balance scheduled within sixty days; and the forklift program has been rebuilt with recertification of all operators completed in June. Abatement documentation is available to the audit committee on request.

## 6. Muncie Plant Review

### 6.1 Performance Data

Muncie, under plant manager Ruben Alcantara, employs 510 people producing appliance components. The plant's first-half execution was the steadiest in the network. Its problem is not operational; it is commercial, and it is large.

| Metric | Target | H1 Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Overall equipment effectiveness | 72% | 71% |
| Scrap rate | 3.0% | 3.1% |
| On-time delivery | 96% | 97% |
| Recordable injury rate | 2.1 | 1.8 |
| Capacity utilization | 78% planned | 71% actual, falling to 63% post-transition |
| Open production positions | — | 22 |

### 6.2 The Customer Loss

In May, an appliance customer representing approximately $28 million in annual revenue notified us that it will transition its business away from Vandalia effective January 2027. The account is Muncie's second-largest and the transition will take the plant from an already soft 71 percent utilization to approximately 63 percent — a level at which the plant's fixed cost absorption becomes seriously problematic and its per-unit costs on remaining programs become uncompetitive, risking a spiral in which cost pressure drives further losses.

The customer's stated reasons, delivered in a candid exit discussion that Ruben Alcantara and our commercial team conducted in June, were threefold: price, where a competitor's proposal came in roughly 9 percent below our renewal quote; responsiveness on engineering change requests, where the customer cited average turnaround times that were double the competitor's commitment; and a general assessment that Vandalia had not brought them cost-reduction ideas in three years. The first reason is partly a resin-inflation story — our quote carried material cost recovery the competitor may be choosing not to price — but the second and third reasons are ours entirely, and they should concern us beyond Muncie, because the same customer-facing engineering processes serve every account in the company.

I want to be precise about what this loss is and is not. It is not a quality or delivery failure; Muncie's scorecard with this customer was strong, and the customer said so. It is a loss on value — on price, speed, and ideas — and that is in some ways more troubling, because it means good execution alone did not retain the business.

### 6.3 Response Plan

The response has four tracks running in parallel. First, a retention effort: the transition is announced but not complete, and our commercial team is pursuing a partial retention of two product families where our tooling position gives the customer a switching cost; the realistic probability is modest and we are planning the plant as though the full loss occurs. Second, backfill: Muncie's open capacity is now the featured asset in every new-business quote, and three active quotations totaling roughly $16 million in annual revenue could land production in the plant during calendar 2027, though none is committed. Third, work transfer: we are evaluating moving two product lines from Huntington to Muncie, which would relieve Huntington's post-rebuild congestion and lift Muncie utilization by an estimated four points; a decision is due in October. Fourth, cost structure: if backfill and transfer together do not bring utilization above 72 percent by mid-2027, we will bring the committee a restructuring option for Muncie, because carrying a 510-person plant at 63 percent indefinitely is not a plan.

The workforce dimension deserves explicit mention. Muncie's employees will learn of the customer loss through the transition activity if not through communication, and the only responsible course is candor: Ruben Alcantara will hold plant-wide meetings in September laying out the loss, the backfill effort, and the honest range of outcomes. A skilled and stable workforce is Muncie's principal asset in winning replacement work, and we will not keep it by managing information.

## 7. Safety Review and Company-Wide Implications

*Contributed by Malik Ferguson, Director, Environmental Health and Safety*

### 7.1 Company Safety Performance

The company's recordable injury rate for the first half was 2.9 against our standard of 2.1. Three plants performed at or better than standard — Huntington at 2.0, Muncie at 1.8, and Chambersburg at 2.3 during a launch period that historically elevates risk. Springfield at 4.8 accounts for the entire company variance.

| Plant | Recordable Rate | vs. Standard (2.1) | Trend (3 quarters) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Huntington | 2.0 | Favorable | Stable |
| Springfield | 4.8 | +2.7 unfavorable | Rising |
| Muncie | 1.8 | Favorable | Improving |
| Chambersburg | 2.3 | +0.2 unfavorable | Stable |
| **Company** | **2.9** | **+0.8 unfavorable** | **Rising** |

### 7.2 What the Springfield Findings Actually Mean

It would be convenient to read the March inspection as a Springfield problem, and the abatement work described in Section 5.4 is genuinely on track. But the audit committee should understand why I do not accept the localized reading, and my reasoning rests on three facts.

First, our internal audit program visited Springfield eleven months before the inspection and did not identify the guarding modifications or the lockout gaps. That is not a Springfield failure; that is a failure of the corporate audit protocol I own. Our audits were sampling documentation rather than verifying physical conditions, and a documentation audit will pass a plant whose paperwork is complete and whose presses are unguarded. The protocol has been rewritten: audits now require physical verification of guarding on a 100 percent basis for high-energy equipment and observed — not documented — lockout execution.

Second, the conditions cited at Springfield were adaptations to production pressure, not acts of recklessness. Guards were modified to reduce cycle interruptions; lockout was shortcut to compress maintenance windows; forklift inspections were pencil-whipped by operators running behind. Every plant in this company is under the same production pressure, amplified by 121 open positions and 14 percent overtime. The question is not whether other plants have Springfield's citations; it is whether they have Springfield's temptations, and the answer is plainly yes. Fatigue from sustained overtime and the dilution of experience from vacancy-driven churn are recognized precursors to exactly the incident types Springfield logged.

Third, the connection between staffing and safety is visible in our own data. Across the four plants, the sites with the highest overtime percentages and the highest proportion of employees with under two years' tenure are the sites with the worst injury trends. Springfield leads on all three measures. This is why Section 9's staffing discussion is a safety discussion, not merely a cost discussion.

### 7.3 Company-Wide Actions

Five actions are underway, all with completion dates in the second half:

1. **Full-scope physical safety audits at all four plants** using the rewritten protocol, sequenced Springfield (complete), Huntington (September), Muncie (October), Chambersburg (November), each with findings reported to me and to the audit committee without plant-level filtering.
2. **Machine guarding capital review** across the network, with a prioritized remediation list and funding request to the executive committee by October; preliminary walkdowns suggest exposure at Huntington's secondary press bank comparable in kind, if not degree, to Springfield's.
3. **Lockout/tagout program rebuild** to the machine-specific standard at all plants, not only Springfield, with completion by December.
4. **Overtime fatigue rule**: no employee exceeds sixty hours in any week or works more than twelve consecutive days, enforced through the timekeeping system rather than supervisory discretion, effective September 1.
5. **Leading-indicator reporting** — near-misses, stop-work events, audit findings — added to the monthly executive scorecard alongside the lagging injury rate, because a company that manages safety by injury count is managing by autopsy.

I will report abatement and audit status to the audit committee quarterly until the program is complete.

## 8. Chambersburg Plant Review

### 8.1 Performance Data

Chambersburg is our newest and smallest plant at 290 employees, and its first half was defined by the launch of the $22 million coating line — the largest single capital investment in the company's recent history and the foundation of our coated-metal growth strategy with heating-equipment customers.

| Metric | Target | H1 Actual |
|---|---|---|
| Coating line start of production | Plan date | 5 months late |
| First-pass yield (coating line) | 94% design target | 78% |
| Overall equipment effectiveness (plant) | 68% | 58% |
| Recordable injury rate | 2.1 | 2.3 |
| On-time delivery | 96% | 84% |
| Open production positions | — | 32 |

### 8.2 The Launch: Schedule and Yield

The coating line entered production five months behind plan. The delay decomposed into equipment delivery slippage from the primary line integrator (roughly two months), installation and utility scope growth discovered during construction (roughly one month), and a commissioning period that ran two months long because the line could not hold coating thickness tolerance across the full conveyor width. Customer commitments made against the original date were covered through a combination of outside processing at a toll coater — at a significant cost premium and with quality escapes that generated two customer complaints — and rescheduling negotiated program by program. The commercial damage was contained but not zero; one heating-equipment customer has told us plainly that its next program award depends on this line demonstrating stability.

The line is now running, and the current problem is yield. First-pass yield stands at 78 percent against a 94 percent design target, a sixteen-point gap that translates directly into rework cost, capacity loss, and margin erosion on every part across the line. The defect Pareto is dominated by three modes: coating thickness variation at the conveyor edges (roughly half the losses), surface contamination traceable to the pretreatment washer chemistry (roughly a quarter), and cure-oven temperature nonuniformity (most of the balance). Each mode has an assigned engineering owner, a containment measure in place, and a corrective action in flight; the integrator remains on site under warranty for the thickness and oven issues.

### 8.3 Honest Assessment and Ramp Plan

The candid assessment is that we launched this line with an inexperienced local team supporting a technology new to the company, and we under-invested in launch support. Chambersburg's workforce is capable but young in the process; 32 open positions and the general labor scarcity discussed in Section 9 made it impossible to hire the coating-experienced technicians the ramp plan assumed, and we substituted training for experience on a compressed schedule. That was a known risk we accepted, and it materialized.

The ramp plan targets first-pass yield of 85 percent by end of October and 90 percent by end of December, with the 94 percent design target held for mid-fiscal-2027. Those dates are engineering-based commitments tied to the corrective actions above, not aspirations, and I will report against them monthly. Two experienced process engineers have been transferred from Huntington to Chambersburg through the ramp, and we have retained the coating technology consultant who supported the original process design for two days per week on site. If the October milestone is missed, the escalation path is a full process re-qualification with the integrator at their cost under the performance guarantee — a contractual remedy we preserved and will use.

## 9. Company-Wide Cost and Staffing Pressures

### 9.1 Resin Inflation

Resin costs rose 19 percent against the prior year, against a planning assumption of 4 percent. For a company whose polymer products carry material content in the range of 40 to 50 percent of cost, a movement of that magnitude is the single largest financial pressure on the business. Our recovery to date has come through three channels: contractual raw-material pass-through clauses, which exist on roughly 55 percent of our polymer revenue and lag the market by one to two quarters; negotiated price adjustments on non-indexed business, where recovery has been partial and hard-won; and material substitution and regrind-content increases on programs where customer specifications permit, worth roughly two points of the nineteen.

The uncovered exposure — the portion of the increase we are absorbing — is the principal driver of the margin compression the executive committee has seen in the financial reporting. Three second-half actions follow: extending index-based pass-through language to renewal contracts as they come up, with a target of 75 percent of polymer revenue covered within eighteen months; a formal material-substitution engineering program with dedicated resources, because the two points we captured opportunistically suggest more is available systematically; and consolidated resin purchasing across the four plants, which today buy on three separate agreements — an obvious inefficiency that a network our size should not carry.

The Muncie pricing loss described in Section 6.2 is a warning about the limits of pass-through as a strategy. If competitors absorb material inflation to buy share, recovery pricing costs us business. The sustainable answer is cost reduction that funds competitive pricing, which is why the substitution program and the yield recovery at Chambersburg matter beyond their direct savings.

### 9.2 Staffing: 121 Open Positions

The company carries 121 open production positions against a production workforce of roughly 1,500 — an 8 percent vacancy rate distributed as 38 at Huntington, 29 at Springfield, 22 at Muncie, and 32 at Chambersburg. The vacancies are concentrated in skilled classifications: press operators, coating technicians, maintenance mechanics, and quality inspectors. Time-to-fill for skilled roles now averages fourteen weeks, and first-year turnover among new production hires runs 34 percent, meaning a substantial fraction of our recruiting effort refills the same positions.

The root causes are partly market and partly us. All four plant communities have tight industrial labor markets, but wage benchmarking completed in June shows our production rates trailing local market medians by 6 to 11 percent depending on site and classification — a gap that explains both the fill difficulty and the turnover. A market wage adjustment proposal is being prepared with human resources for executive committee review in September; its cost is significant, but Section 9.3 makes the case that we are already paying much of it in overtime premium, and Section 7 makes the case that we are paying part of it in injuries.

### 9.3 Overtime at 14 Percent

Overtime reached 14 percent of direct labor hours against a sustainable planning level of 8 percent. The arithmetic connecting this to the vacancy problem is straightforward: 121 vacant positions represent roughly 5 percent of scheduled production hours, and covering those hours at time-and-a-half — plus the schedule-recovery overtime at Huntington and Chambersburg — accounts for essentially the entire excess. We are, in effect, paying a 50 percent premium to buy back the hours we cannot hire, and receiving fatigued labor in exchange.

The costs of sustained overtime at this level go beyond the premium. Fatigue degrades quality and safety, as Section 7 documents. Voluntary overtime willingness is declining — several departments have moved to mandatory scheduling, which is corroding morale and feeding the turnover that feeds the vacancies. This is a loop, and the only durable exit is filling the positions, which returns the discussion to wages. The interim protection is the sixty-hour fatigue cap described in Section 7.3, which will constrain schedule flexibility in the short term and is the right constraint to accept.

### 9.4 The Combined Picture

| Pressure | Magnitude | Primary Second-Half Action | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resin inflation | +19% vs. +4% plan | Pass-through extension; substitution program; consolidated buying | VP Manufacturing / Procurement |
| Open positions | 121 (8% of production workforce) | Market wage adjustment proposal, September | VP Manufacturing / HR |
| Overtime | 14% of direct hours | Vacancy fill; 60-hour fatigue cap; Huntington backlog burn-down | Plant managers |

These three pressures should be understood as one system. Material inflation compresses margin, which restrains wage action, which sustains vacancies, which drives overtime, which drives fatigue, cost, and turnover. Breaking the loop requires spending money on wages that the margin picture makes uncomfortable, and my recommendation to the committee is that we spend it, because every alternative in the loop is more expensive.

## 10. Second-Half Priorities and Commitments

The second-half agenda concentrates on six commitments, each with an owner and a date, against which I ask the committee to measure this organization at the fiscal year-end review.

1. **Complete Springfield abatement and the four-plant physical safety audit program**, with quarterly reporting to the audit committee. Springfield's recordable rate target for the second half is 3.0, on a path to standard by mid-fiscal 2027. Owner: Malik Ferguson with Hae-Won Jeong. Audit completion: November.

2. **Restore Huntington to target performance**: OEE at 72 percent, scrap at 3.0 percent, and delivery backlog eliminated. Owner: Huntington plant leadership under my direct review. Date: end of Q3 FY2026.

3. **Hit the Chambersburg yield ramp**: 85 percent first-pass yield by end of October, 90 percent by end of December, with monthly reporting and the integrator warranty remedy invoked if October is missed. Owner: Chambersburg launch leadership with transferred engineering support.

4. **Execute the Muncie response**: partial-retention pursuit concluded by October, the Huntington-to-Muncie work transfer decision made in October, and backfill quotations pressed to decision, with a restructuring option prepared as the contingency if utilization cannot reach 72 percent by mid-2027. Owner: Ruben Alcantara with the commercial organization.

5. **Bring the market wage adjustment to the executive committee in September** and, if approved, reduce open positions from 121 to below 60 by fiscal year-end, with overtime falling toward 10 percent as vacancies close. Owner: myself with human resources.

6. **Recover resin economics**: pass-through coverage extended at every second-half renewal, the substitution engineering program staffed by October, and consolidated resin purchasing agreements in place by fiscal year-end. Owner: myself with procurement.

The first half exposed weaknesses in execution, in governance, and in our value proposition to at least one major customer. None of those weaknesses is structural, and all of them are named in this report with owners and dates attached. The second half is where we demonstrate that naming them was the beginning of fixing them.
