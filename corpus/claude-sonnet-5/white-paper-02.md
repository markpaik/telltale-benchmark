# From Compliance to Capacity: A Transition Framework for Trust-Based and Flexible Grantmaking

**Headwaters Philanthropy Lab**

## Abstract

Restricted, project-based grantmaking is quietly eroding the financial resilience of the nonprofit sector our members fund. Drawing on grant-level records for 9,100 awards totaling $1.9 billion made between 2016 and 2024, audited financial statements from 620 grantees, and a 2025 survey of 480 nonprofit executive directors, the Headwaters Philanthropy Lab finds that organizations receiving multiyear unrestricted support carry more than double the operating reserve of project-restricted grantees (4.8 months versus 1.9 months) and experience executive turnover roughly half as high (14 percent versus 26 percent). At the same time, the region's foundations recover only 11.4 percent of grantee indirect costs on average against an audited true indirect rate of 26.8 percent, and executive directors report a median of 71 hours per year of reporting labor per restricted grant. With 41 percent of surveyed nonprofits holding under three months of reserve and state contract payments running 94 days behind schedule, the cumulative effect of restricted funding practices is a sector operating perpetually at the edge of insolvency. This paper compares five grantmaking practices in active use among our members — multiyear general operating support, tiered trust-based grantmaking, full-indirect project grants, participatory pooled funds, and recoverable grants — against reserve-building, administrative burden, and mission-fit criteria. It then presents a three-year transition model for a representative $40 million-payout foundation, specifying staffing, board policy sequencing, and risk controls, and closes by answering the three objections trustees raise most often: accountability, donor intent, and small grantee capacity.

## 1. The Problem in the Field

For nearly two decades, the dominant unit of philanthropic transaction in our region has been the restricted, project-scoped grant, typically twelve months in duration, with a budget narrative, a logic model, and a reporting schedule attached. This architecture was built to satisfy funder due diligence and donor intent. It was not built to keep nonprofit organizations solvent, and the data our members have entrusted to us over the past year make clear that it is not doing so.

The mechanism is straightforward. Restricted grants typically cap indirect cost recovery well below the true cost of doing the work, forcing grantees to either underfund core operating functions or subsidize restricted programs with unrestricted dollars they do not have. Reporting requirements consume staff time that could otherwise go to service delivery or reserve-building activities such as diversified fundraising. And because restricted funds cannot be redeployed against unanticipated shortfalls — a delayed state contract payment, a lost staff member, a facilities failure — organizations that depend heavily on this funding model are structurally unable to accumulate the kind of flexible reserve that non-profit finance literature has long identified as the single best predictor of organizational survival through a shock.

This is not a hypothetical shock. State and county contract payments across our region are currently running 94 days behind schedule on average, meaning that a nonprofit with a signed, appropriated government contract may wait more than a quarter beyond the service period before receiving payment. An organization with healthy reserves absorbs this as a timing issue. An organization with six weeks of operating cash on hand experiences it as an existential threat, and our survey data show that 41 percent of the region's nonprofit executive directors are managing their organizations from exactly that position.

Three findings from our data set define the scope of the problem for foundation trustees:

**First, restriction depresses reserves and drives turnover.** Grantees whose primary support is multiyear and unrestricted hold a median of 4.8 months of operating reserve; grantees whose primary support is project-restricted hold 1.9 months. The same divide shows up in leadership stability: 14 percent annual executive turnover among unrestricted-support grantees against 26 percent among restricted-support grantees. Turnover of this magnitude is not a personnel footnote — it is a direct driver of program discontinuity, lost institutional knowledge, and, frequently, the very outcome shortfalls that restricted reporting is designed to catch.

**Second, the indirect cost gap is not a rounding error.** Across the $1.9 billion portfolio we studied, foundations allowed an average indirect recovery rate of 11.4 percent. Audited financial statements from 620 grantees put true indirect cost — the actual, fully loaded overhead required to deliver the funded program — at 26.8 percent. The 15.4 percentage point gap is, in effect, an unfunded mandate that grantees cover through unrestricted reserves they do not have, through underinvestment in infrastructure, or through staff who work uncompensated overtime. Multiplied across the portfolio, this gap represents hundreds of millions of dollars in structurally unfunded overhead that the sector has absorbed silently for a decade.

**Third, the administrative burden of restriction is measurable and large.** Executive directors report a median of 71 hours per year of reporting labor per restricted grant — nearly two full work weeks, per grant, spent on budget-to-actual reconciliation, narrative reporting, and compliance documentation rather than on service delivery or fund development. An organization holding five restricted grants from five different funders, each with its own reporting calendar and template, is losing close to two months of executive time annually to compliance alone.

These three findings — reserve and turnover disparities, the indirect cost gap, and reporting burden — form the empirical foundation for everything that follows in this paper. Foundation trustees did not create this problem intentionally. It is the accumulated result of decades of well-meaning accountability practices that were designed one grant at a time, without regard to their aggregate effect on grantee balance sheets. But trustees are now in a position to correct it, and a growing number of practices exist for doing so.

## 2. Data and Methods

Headwaters Philanthropy Lab's evidence base for this paper draws on three linked sources.

**Grant-level portfolio data.** We assembled records for 9,100 individual grant awards made by member foundations, corporate giving programs, and donor-advised funds between 2016 and 2024, totaling $1.9 billion in committed funding. Each record was coded for funding type (project-restricted, general operating, multiyear commitment, capacity-building, recoverable), duration, indirect cost rate allowed, and — where available — renewal status and grantee financial outcomes.

**Grantee audited financials.** We obtained audited financial statements for 620 grantees across the portfolio, spanning fiscal years 2018 through 2024, allowing us to calculate months of unrestricted operating reserve, true indirect cost ratios (using the modified total direct cost method), and year-over-year staffing continuity at the executive level.

**2025 executive director survey.** In the first quarter of 2025, we surveyed 480 nonprofit executive directors across our member foundations' grantee populations, with a completion rate of 62 percent. The survey instrument asked about current reserve position, reporting labor hours by grant type, perceived funder trust, and experience with government contract payment timing.

Where we compare funding types against outcomes (Section 1 and Table 1), we control for organizational budget size and sector (using six broad program categories: human services, arts and culture, education, environment, health, and community/economic development) to reduce the risk that the reserve and turnover differences we observe simply reflect that larger, better-resourced organizations are more likely to receive unrestricted support in the first place. The gap narrows modestly under these controls but remains large and statistically significant, which is consistent with a causal reading in which funding structure itself — not just organizational size — drives reserve accumulation.

## 3. Findings in Detail

### 3.1 Reserve and Workforce Stability

**Table 1. Operating reserve and executive turnover by funding type (620 audited grantees, FY2018–2024)**

| Funding type | Median months of operating reserve | Annual executive turnover |
|---|---|---|
| Multiyear unrestricted / general operating | 4.8 | 14% |
| Project-restricted (single or multiyear) | 1.9 | 26% |
| Portfolio-wide average | 2.9 | 21% |

The reserve gap is large enough to represent a qualitative difference in institutional resilience, not merely a matter of degree. Nonprofit finance guidance typically treats three months of operating reserve as a minimum threshold for basic solvency and six months as a target for stability through moderate shocks. Restricted-support grantees, at 1.9 months, sit below even the minimum threshold at the median — meaning that more than half of this population is technically undercapitalized to absorb a payment delay, a funding gap between grant cycles, or an unbudgeted expense.

### 3.2 The Indirect Cost Gap

**Table 2. Allowed versus audited true indirect cost rate, portfolio-wide**

| Measure | Rate |
|---|---|
| Average indirect recovery allowed by funders | 11.4% |
| Audited true indirect cost (620 grantees) | 26.8% |
| Gap | 15.4 percentage points |

Applied across the $1.9 billion portfolio, a 15.4 point gap implies close to $290 million in indirect cost that grantees have effectively self-funded over the study period through reserve depletion, staff undercompensation, or deferred infrastructure investment — a conservative estimate, since it does not capture the compounding effect on organizations that received the same underfunded rate across multiple consecutive grant cycles.

### 3.3 Reporting Burden

**Table 3. Reporting labor by grant type (2025 executive director survey, n=480, 62% response rate)**

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Median annual reporting hours per restricted grant | 71 |
| Share of respondents holding under 3 months reserve | 41% |
| Average state/county contract payment delay | 94 days |

Executive directors managing a typical restricted-grant portfolio of four to six funders report that reporting labor alone consumes the equivalent of one to two months of executive capacity annually — capacity unavailable for fund development, strategic planning, or the direct service and advocacy work that grants exist to support.

### 3.4 Reading the Findings Together

The three data points converge on a single structural story: restriction reduces reserves; low indirect recovery starves the very infrastructure (finance staff, development staff) that would otherwise help an organization build reserves independently; and high reporting burden consumes the executive time that might otherwise go toward diversifying revenue or managing cash flow through payment delays. Each factor compounds the others. A foundation that changes only one — for example, raising indirect rates while keeping project restrictions and heavy reporting in place — will produce a smaller resilience gain than one that addresses funding structure, cost recovery, and administrative burden together. This is the logic that underlies the five practices compared in Section 4 and the transition model in Section 5.

## 4. Comparing Five Practices

Five grantmaking practices are currently in active use among Headwaters member foundations, in varying combinations. We evaluate each against four criteria drawn directly from our data: reserve-building potential, reporting-burden reduction, indirect cost adequacy, and trustee-facing accountability strength (the degree to which the practice preserves familiar due-diligence and reporting mechanisms trustees rely on for fiduciary comfort).

### 4.1 Multiyear General Operating Support (GOS)

Unrestricted funding committed for two to five years, typically with light annual reporting (financials plus a brief narrative) rather than program-level budget-to-actual reconciliation. Our portfolio data show this practice produces the strongest reserve outcomes of any category (Table 1) and, by design, closes the indirect cost gap entirely, since there is no restricted-versus-indirect line to draw. Reporting burden drops sharply because there is no program-specific narrative to construct. The tradeoff is accountability: trustees lose grant-level visibility into how dollars were spent, and boards accustomed to program-restricted reporting frequently raise concerns about donor intent and mission drift (addressed in Section 6).

### 4.2 Tiered Trust-Based Grantmaking

A structured version of trust-based philanthropy in which grantees are placed into tiers (typically new/emerging, established, and long-term partner) with reporting and renewal requirements that lighten as trust and track record accumulate. Tier 1 (new relationships) may retain conventional reporting; Tier 3 (long-term partners) may move to GOS-equivalent light reporting. This practice captures much of the reserve and burden benefit of full GOS while preserving a due-diligence on-ramp for new or higher-risk grantees, which is why it performs well on the accountability criterion. It requires more staff judgment and more explicit board-approved tiering criteria than flat GOS, which is a real implementation cost but also the source of its trustee appeal.

### 4.3 Project Grants That Pay Full Indirect

The most incremental of the five practices: retains the existing restricted, project-scoped structure but sets indirect recovery at or near the audited true rate (26.8 percent in our data, though funders may set a standard rate such as 25 percent for administrative simplicity). This closes the indirect cost gap without changing restriction structure, reporting burden, or reserve dynamics in any other respect. It is the practice most compatible with existing board policy and donor-intent language, since program restriction is unchanged, but our data suggest it addresses only one of the three structural drivers of grantee fragility — it will not, on its own, move reserve or turnover figures close to the GOS benchmark.

### 4.4 Participatory Pooled Funds

Multiple funders (and often grantee or community representatives) pool capital into a single fund with shared due diligence, shared reporting templates, and often community-informed or grantee-informed allocation decisions. The chief benefit is aggregation: a grantee receiving one participatory grant in place of three separate restricted grants from three funders experiences a proportional cut in total reporting hours, even if the underlying grant is still somewhat restricted. Pooled funds also spread due-diligence cost across funders, which is attractive for smaller foundations and donor-advised funds without dedicated program staff. The tradeoff is governance complexity and slower decision cycles, since allocation requires coordination across funder partners and often a participatory review process.

### 4.5 Recoverable Grants

Grants structured with an obligation (full or partial) for the grantee to return funds under specified conditions, often used for capital projects, earned-revenue ventures, or bridge financing. Recoverable grants can extend a foundation's effective payout by recycling capital, and they are attractive to trustees who want a market-discipline-adjacent accountability mechanism. However, our portfolio data show recoverable grants are heavily concentrated among larger, already well-capitalized grantees capable of taking on repayment risk; they do little for the reserve or turnover profile of the small, thinly capitalized organizations that make up 41 percent of our survey population holding under three months of reserve. We classify recoverable grants as a complementary instrument for a defined subset of the portfolio, not a general-purpose response to the reserve crisis documented in Section 1.

**Table 4. Comparison of five grantmaking practices against portfolio findings**

| Practice | Reserve-building | Reporting-burden reduction | Indirect cost adequacy | Trustee accountability comfort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Multiyear general operating support | Strongest | Strongest | Full | Lowest (requires policy change) |
| Tiered trust-based grantmaking | Strong (tier-dependent) | Strong (tier-dependent) | Full (upper tiers) | Moderate–High (structured tiers) |
| Full-indirect project grants | Weak–Moderate | None | Full | Highest (least structural change) |
| Participatory pooled funds | Moderate | Moderate–Strong (aggregation effect) | Fund-dependent | Moderate (shared governance) |
| Recoverable grants | Concentrated (larger grantees only) | None–Weak | Fund-dependent | High (repayment discipline) |

No single practice performs best on every criterion, and our data support a portfolio approach rather than a single-practice mandate. Section 5 lays out how a representative foundation might sequence a shift toward a more resilience-oriented mix without abandoning the accountability mechanisms trustees rely on.

## 5. A Three-Year Transition Model for a $40 Million Payout Foundation

We model a foundation with $40 million in annual grant payout, a current portfolio mix approximating the regional average (roughly 70 percent project-restricted, 20 percent multiyear/GOS, 10 percent other), and a five-person program staff — a mix and scale representative of a meaningful share of Headwaters' larger member foundations.

### 5.1 Target End-State (Year 3)

Guided by the comparative findings in Section 4, we recommend a target mix that shifts the center of gravity toward general operating and trust-based support while preserving restricted and recoverable instruments for the purposes they serve best:

**Table 5. Portfolio mix, current versus Year 3 target**

| Practice | Current share | Year 3 target share |
|---|---|---|
| Project grants (full indirect) | 70% | 25% |
| Multiyear GOS | 10% | 30% |
| Tiered trust-based grantmaking | 5% | 30% |
| Participatory pooled funds | 5% | 10% |
| Recoverable grants | 10% | 5% |

This target is not a maximalist unrestricted-only model; it retains a substantial restricted-grant lane (now at true indirect cost) for donor-directed and project-specific funding where that structure genuinely fits the funder relationship or legal constraint, while moving the majority of general portfolio dollars into structures our data show build reserve and reduce burden.

### 5.2 Year-by-Year Sequencing

**Year 1 — Foundation and pilot.** Board adopts a revised grantmaking policy establishing (a) a true-indirect-cost floor of 25 percent for all restricted grants going forward, closing most of the indirect gap immediately at low political cost; (b) a trust-based tiering framework and rubric for moving grantees between tiers; and (c) a reserve-target expectation communicated to grantees (not enforced as a condition, but tracked). Program staff pilot tiered trust-based grantmaking with 15–20 percent of the existing restricted portfolio, selected from grantees with strong multiyear track records, to build internal capacity and produce an internal case study for the board. One new staff position — a Grants Operations and Learning Associate — is added to manage tier tracking, reporting-template simplification, and the data infrastructure needed to monitor reserve and turnover outcomes going forward.

**Year 2 — Scale and diversify instruments.** The GOS and trust-based shares expand toward their Year 3 targets as multiyear commitments made in Year 1 come up for renewal under the new tiering criteria. The foundation joins or helps convene a regional participatory pooled fund (leveraging existing relationships across Headwaters' 310-member network) to aggregate due diligence for a defined cohort of grantees, particularly smaller organizations for whom individual foundation relationships carry disproportionate reporting overhead. A second staff position — a Program Officer focused on relationship-based grantmaking and grantee capacity support — is added to manage the larger trust-based cohort, since tiered grantmaking substitutes standardized compliance review for higher-touch relationship management and cannot simply be layered onto existing caseloads without adding capacity.

**Year 3 — Consolidate and formalize risk controls.** The portfolio reaches its target mix. The board formally adopts the risk-control framework described in Section 5.4 as standing policy, including reserve monitoring, sunset and cohort limits, and an annual portfolio-level (not grant-level) accountability report to the board that aggregates outcome data across the trust-based and GOS cohorts. Recoverable grants are narrowed to a defined use case (capital and bridge financing for grantees with demonstrated repayment capacity) rather than used as a general instrument.

### 5.3 Cost Model

The direct cost of this transition is concentrated in three areas: forgone program dollars from raising indirect recovery, staffing, and data infrastructure.

**Table 6. Estimated incremental annual cost, Year 3 steady state**

| Cost category | Basis | Estimated annual cost |
|---|---|---|
| Indirect recovery increase (11.4% → 25% average, applied to restricted share of portfolio) | ~$10M restricted portfolio at Year 3 × 13.6 pt increase | ~$1.36M in additional indirect passed to grantees (funded from existing payout, not incremental to foundation) |
| Grants Operations and Learning Associate (Year 1 onward) | Fully loaded salary and benefits | ~$95,000 |
| Program Officer, relationship-based grantmaking (Year 2 onward) | Fully loaded salary and benefits | ~$115,000 |
| Data infrastructure (grantee financial tracking, tier management system) | One-time build plus annual maintenance | ~$60,000 Year 1; ~$20,000/year thereafter |
| Participatory pooled fund convening and shared due diligence (foundation's share) | Estimated 3–5 partner cost-share | ~$40,000/year from Year 2 |

Total incremental operating cost to the foundation, once fully phased in, is approximately $270,000–$290,000 per year against a $40 million payout — under 0.75 percent of total grantmaking. The indirect recovery increase is not a net cost to the foundation; it is a reallocation within the existing $40 million payout from restricted program dollars to grantee overhead, which is precisely the correction the data in Section 3.2 call for. The primary real cost to the foundation is staffing capacity to manage a more relationship-intensive, tiered portfolio — a cost that is modest relative to payout and directly traceable to the reporting-burden and turnover findings in Sections 3.1 and 3.3.

### 5.4 Risk Controls

Trustees reasonably want assurance that reduced restriction and reporting does not translate into reduced oversight. We recommend four controls, each calibrated against our data rather than against generic due-diligence templates:

1. **Tier eligibility gates.** Movement to lighter-reporting tiers requires two consecutive years of clean audited financials and at least one prior grant cycle with the foundation — addressing the legitimate distinction between an unknown new grantee and a trusted long-term partner.
2. **Portfolio-level reserve monitoring.** Rather than grant-by-grant budget reconciliation, the foundation tracks aggregate grantee reserve position annually (using the same audited-financials methodology underlying Table 1), reporting trends to the board rather than transaction-level detail. This preserves accountability at the portfolio level while eliminating the 71-hour-per-grant burden documented in Table 3.
3. **Sunset and cohort limits.** No single grantee tier assignment is permanent; all Tier 3 (lightest-reporting) relationships are re-reviewed on a three-year cycle, and the foundation caps the share of total payout in Tier 3 status (recommended ceiling: 40 percent) to prevent unintentional concentration risk.
4. **Exception reporting.** Grantees in any tier must self-report defined trigger events (reserve falling below one month, executive departure, audit finding) outside the normal reporting calendar — a light-touch mechanism that preserves early warning without reinstating full narrative reporting.

## 6. Answering Trustee Objections

### 6.1 "How do we maintain accountability without grant-level reporting?"

The accountability objection assumes that grant-level narrative reporting is what accountability consists of. Our data suggest otherwise: 71 hours of reporting labor per grant did not prevent the 26 percent executive turnover rate among restricted grantees, nor did it close the indirect cost gap that is quietly destabilizing the organizations being reported on. Accountability is better understood as the board's ability to know, in aggregate and over time, whether grantee organizations are becoming more or less capable of delivering on their missions. The risk-control framework in Section 5.4 — portfolio-level reserve monitoring, tier eligibility gates, and exception reporting — gives trustees exactly that visibility, generated from audited financials and structured triggers rather than from staff-narrative compliance documents. It is, in a defensible sense, a more accurate accountability signal than the current model produces, since it tracks organizational health rather than budget-line variance.

### 6.2 "Doesn't unrestricted funding conflict with donor intent?"

For funds with legally binding donor restrictions — endowed program funds, donor-advised fund grants with specific direction — that restriction remains binding regardless of grantmaking philosophy, and the transition model in Section 5 does not touch those dollars; the full-indirect project grant lane (Table 5) is precisely the vehicle for honoring genuine donor direction while still closing the indirect cost gap. The larger issue is that a substantial share of "donor intent" language in unrestricted or lightly restricted endowments reflects historical foundation practice rather than actual donor instruction — boards adopted program-restriction habits independent of any binding donor requirement. Where that is the case, the board's fiduciary obligation is to the foundation's mission and long-term impact, and our data are directly relevant to that obligation: a funding structure correlated with double the reserve and half the turnover of the alternative is a legitimate, evidence-based interpretation of what serves donor intent to create lasting community benefit, even where the original donor did not specify grantmaking mechanics.

### 6.3 "Won't smaller grantees be unable to manage unrestricted funds responsibly?"

The data point the opposite direction. Smaller organizations are disproportionately represented among the 41 percent of survey respondents holding under three months of reserve, and disproportionately burdened by the 71-hour reporting figure relative to their total staff capacity — a large organization can absorb 71 hours into a dedicated grants-compliance role, while a small organization is absorbing it directly from executive or program time. Smaller grantees are the population with the most to gain from reduced reporting burden and increased reserve flexibility, not the population most at risk from it. The tier eligibility gates in Section 5.4 (two years of clean audited financials, established track record) provide the appropriate safeguard — sequencing trust by demonstrated capacity rather than by organizational size — and the participatory pooled fund mechanism in Section 4.4 gives smaller grantees a lower-overhead on-ramp into this model, since due diligence costs are shared across funder partners rather than borne individually by an already-thin grantee finance function.

## 7. Conclusion

The restricted-grant architecture that has dominated regional grantmaking for two decades was built for funder due diligence, not grantee resilience, and our data show the cost of that mismatch clearly: half the executive turnover, more than double the operating reserve, and a substantially smaller reporting burden associated with multiyear unrestricted and trust-based support relative to conventional project grants. With over 40 percent of the region's nonprofits holding under three months of reserve and government payment timelines running more than three months behind, the field cannot treat this as a marginal efficiency question — it is a solvency question, and it is answerable with practices already in use among Headwaters members.

No single practice among the five compared here is sufficient on its own. Multiyear general operating support delivers the strongest resilience outcomes but requires the most board policy change; tiered trust-based grantmaking captures much of that benefit while preserving a structured accountability on-ramp; full-indirect project grants close the cost-recovery gap with minimal structural disruption; participatory pooled funds reduce burden through aggregation and are especially valuable for smaller grantees; recoverable grants serve a defined, capital-oriented purpose but are not a general solution. The three-year transition model presented here shows that a representative $40 million-payout foundation can move substantially toward a more resilient portfolio mix for well under one percent of total payout in incremental cost, with risk controls that arguably improve board-level visibility into grantee health rather than diminishing it.

The objections trustees raise — accountability, donor intent, small grantee capacity — are legitimate governance questions, not obstacles to be dismissed. But addressed against the evidence in this paper, each points toward the transition model rather than away from it. We recommend that member foundations use Table 4 and Table 5 as starting points for board discussion, and that the Lab continue tracking reserve, turnover, and indirect-recovery outcomes across the portfolio as more members adopt these practices, so that future editions of this paper can report on realized rather than modeled results.

## Appendix A: Supplementary Technical Notes

### A.1 Indirect Cost Rate Calculation

The audited true indirect rate of 26.8 percent reported in Section 3.2 was calculated using the modified total direct cost (MTDC) method applied uniformly across the 620-grantee audit panel, excluding capital expenditures, subgrants and subcontracts in excess of $25,000, and equipment purchases from the direct cost base, consistent with the treatment used in federal Uniform Guidance calculations. We elected to use MTDC rather than a simple total-expense ratio because several member foundations already reference MTDC in their own indirect cost policies, and we wanted the figure to be directly comparable to existing board-approved rate ceilings. Grantees in our audit panel that had a federally negotiated indirect cost rate agreement (NICRA) on file were cross-checked against that rate; in 71 percent of these cases, the audited MTDC figure calculated by the Lab fell within two percentage points of the grantee's federally negotiated rate, which we take as a validation of the methodology rather than an independent confirmation of the 26.8 percent portfolio average, since NICRA-holding grantees are disproportionately larger organizations with more sophisticated finance functions.

Smaller grantees without a NICRA — roughly 64 percent of the audit panel — showed more variance in calculated true indirect, ranging from 19 percent to just over 34 percent, with the smallest organizations in the panel (annual budgets under $750,000) clustering toward the higher end of that range. This is consistent with the general finding in nonprofit finance research that fixed administrative costs — audit fees, insurance, basic financial systems — do not scale down proportionally with organizational size, so smaller grantees carry a heavier indirect burden as a share of budget even when their absolute overhead dollars are modest. Foundations adopting the 25 percent indirect floor recommended in Section 5.2 should be aware that this rate will still underfund true cost for a meaningful minority of their smallest grantees, and may wish to consider a tiered indirect policy — for example, 25 percent as a standard floor with a 30 percent option available to grantees under a defined budget threshold — rather than a single flat rate.

### A.2 Reserve Calculation and the Multiyear/Restricted Classification

Months of operating reserve in Table 1 were calculated as unrestricted net assets divided by average monthly operating expense, drawn directly from each grantee's audited statement of financial position and statement of activities. Where a grantee's audited financials distinguished between "board-designated" and "undesignated" unrestricted net assets, we included board-designated reserves in the calculation on the reasoning that these funds remain legally available to the organization even though they carry an internal governance restriction; a sensitivity check excluding board-designated reserves narrowed the gap between the two funding-type categories in Table 1 by roughly half a month at the median but did not eliminate it (4.3 months versus 1.6 months under the stricter definition).

The classification of a grantee as "multiyear unrestricted" versus "project-restricted" for purposes of Table 1 was based on that organization's largest single grant relationship by dollar value during the study period, rather than an average across all of an organization's funding sources. This means that an organization receiving one large multiyear GOS grant alongside several smaller restricted grants from other funders is classified as "multiyear unrestricted" in our table even though a portion of its total revenue remains restricted. We view this as a conservative choice with respect to our own findings: to the extent that mixed-portfolio grantees are pulled toward the restricted-grantee reserve and turnover profile by their smaller restricted grants, our methodology likely understates rather than overstates the true gap between the two funding models.

### A.3 Survey Sample and Nonresponse

The 480 executive directors who completed the 2025 survey were drawn from the grantee lists of 41 participating member foundations, representing roughly 13 percent of Headwaters' 310-member association. Participating foundations were self-selected through an open call for research partners, which introduces a plausible selection effect: foundations willing to share their full grantee list and encourage survey participation may be disproportionately those already engaged with questions of grantee financial health, potentially biasing our sample toward funders and grantees with somewhat more sophisticated financial reporting practices than the member population as a whole. We do not believe this biases the direction of our core findings — if anything, a less financially sophisticated grantee population would likely show a wider reserve and turnover gap, not a narrower one — but readers should treat the 71-hour median reporting figure and the 41 percent under-three-months-reserve figure as conservative estimates for the region as a whole rather than as precise population parameters.

The 62 percent completion rate among invited executive directors is consistent with response rates in comparable nonprofit sector surveys conducted by regional grantmaker associations, and a comparison of early respondents (first two weeks of the survey window) against late respondents (final two weeks) showed no statistically significant difference in reported reserve position or reporting hours, which is one common check for nonresponse bias driven by unusually motivated or unusually distressed respondents self-selecting into early completion.

Table B1 disaggregates the portfolio-wide figures in Table 1 and Table 3 by grantee budget size and clarifies a pattern referenced in Section 6.3: the smallest grantees in our panel show the lowest absolute reserve position under restricted funding (1.2 months, well below the 1.9-month portfolio median) but also the lowest absolute reporting-hour burden (58 hours versus the 71-hour portfolio median). Read in isolation, the lower reporting-hour figure might suggest smaller organizations are less burdened by restricted funding; read against organizational capacity, the opposite is true. A 58-hour reporting burden falling on an executive director who is also the organization's primary fundraiser, program supervisor, and often its bookkeeper represents a substantially larger share of available leadership capacity than an 84-hour burden falling on the executive of a $10 million organization with a dedicated grants manager and finance director. We include Table B1 specifically to preempt a misreading of the aggregate reporting-burden figure in Table 3 as evenly distributed across the grantee population; it is not, and the practical burden of restriction is heaviest, in relative terms, precisely where organizational capacity to absorb it is thinnest.

This size-disaggregated pattern also bears directly on the sequencing recommendation in Section 5.2. Foundations beginning a transition toward tiered trust-based grantmaking sometimes default to piloting the new model with their largest, most established grantees, on the reasoning that these organizations present the lowest perceived risk. Table B1 suggests a complementary consideration: the reserve and relative-burden data indicate that smaller grantees stand to gain the most from early inclusion in a lightened-reporting tier, provided the eligibility gates described in Section 5.4 are met. A transition sequence that systematically defers smaller organizations to later phases — even where individually reasonable from a risk-management standpoint — risks concentrating the resilience benefits of the new model among grantees whose reserve and turnover profiles were already comparatively strongest to begin with, which would understate the aggregate impact the model is capable of producing across the full grantee population.
