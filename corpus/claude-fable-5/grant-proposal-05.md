# City of Sorrel Creek, Oklahoma

## Application to the Tallgrass Civic Trust — Main Street Resilience Program

**Project Title:** Sorrel Creek Downtown Flood Resilience and Economic Recovery Initiative

**Applicant:** City of Sorrel Creek, Oklahoma
**Requested Amount:** $4,000,000 over four years
**Local Match:** $800,000 (20 percent)
**Submission Date:** August 22, 2026
**Primary Contact:** Renata Duchamp, Grants Coordinator, City of Sorrel Creek

---

## Statement of Need

The City of Sorrel Creek, a north-central Oklahoma community of 31,240 residents, stands at the intersection of two compounding crises: repeated, worsening flooding along Sorrel Creek and its downtown tributaries, and a downtown economy that has been hollowed out in significant part because of that flooding. Neither problem can be solved in isolation. This application asks the Tallgrass Civic Trust to invest in a single, integrated response to both.

### Flood History and Physical Vulnerability

Sorrel Creek has experienced two major flood events in the past five years. The May 2019 flood inundated the downtown core and adjacent residential neighborhoods for three days, and the September 2023 flood — driven by a stalled frontal system that dropped more than nine inches of rain in thirty-six hours — exceeded the 2019 high-water marks in most of the affected area. Together, the two events damaged 214 structures and generated $18.6 million in insurance claims. The National Flood Insurance Program has identified a concentrated cluster of repetitive-loss properties in the lowest-lying blocks between the creek channel and the historic downtown, where the same homes and commercial buildings have flooded, been repaired, and flooded again.

The city's own damage assessments confirm what residents already know: the floodplain mapped in the 1980s no longer describes the creek's actual behavior. Upstream development, channel sedimentation, and more intense rainfall events have pushed floodwaters into blocks that were never expected to flood. Thirty-four properties in the target area have been identified as repetitive-loss or substantial-damage structures. Their owners face an impossible cycle: rising flood insurance premiums, declining property values, and the certainty — not the possibility — that the water will return. Several owners have simply walked away, leaving vacant, deteriorating structures that depress surrounding values and complicate emergency response.

### Economic Consequences

The economic damage tracks the physical damage almost exactly. Downtown commercial vacancy now stands at 29 percent, concentrated in the flood-affected blocks. Citywide sales tax receipts are down 8 percent from 2019 — a decline that began with the first flood and deepened after the second, as businesses that had rebuilt once declined to rebuild twice. For a city that depends on sales tax for the majority of its general fund, this decline directly constrains the municipal services, including drainage maintenance and code enforcement, that would otherwise help address the underlying problem. The flood and the fiscal decline feed each other.

The businesses that remain downtown operate under a persistent shadow. Lenders are reluctant to finance improvements in the floodplain. Insurance costs have risen sharply for those who can obtain coverage at all. Prospective tenants tour vacant storefronts, ask about the high-water lines still visible on interior walls, and choose highway-corridor locations instead. Without intervention, the city projects downtown vacancy will exceed 35 percent within three years, with corresponding further erosion of the tax base.

### Who Bears the Burden

The burden of this dual crisis does not fall evenly. Twenty-four percent of Sorrel Creek's residents are Native American, and tribal families are disproportionately represented in the flood-affected residential blocks, where housing has historically been more affordable precisely because of its flood exposure. Nineteen percent of city residents live below the federal poverty line — well above the state average — and low-income homeowners in the repetitive-loss area frequently lack flood insurance or carry only structure coverage, meaning each flood consumes savings, retirement funds, or borrowed money that will never be recovered. Thirty-two percent of downtown businesses are minority-owned, many of them family enterprises operating on thin margins in the lowest-rent — and lowest-elevation — commercial spaces. When these businesses close after a flood, their owners lose not just income but accumulated equity and, often, the only commercial foothold their families have ever held.

Two of the parcels within the project area carry cultural and environmental significance requiring formal consultation with the tribal environmental office, a partner in this application. The city regards this consultation not as a regulatory hurdle but as a substantive component of project design, and the tribal environmental office has been engaged since the concept stage.

### Why Now, and Why This Funder

Sorrel Creek cannot buy its way out of this problem with local revenue alone; the same floods that created the need have shrunk the resources available to meet it. Federal mitigation programs address structures but not economic recovery; economic development programs address storefronts but not the water that keeps filling them. The Main Street Resilience Program is, to the city's knowledge, the only funding opportunity that treats flood mitigation and downtown economic vitality as a single problem — which is exactly what they are in Sorrel Creek. The Trust's stated outcome measures — structures removed from the 100-year floodplain, reduction in flood insurance claims, downtown occupancy, sales tax receipts, and jobs retained — read as a precise description of what this community needs to change.

## Project Description

The Sorrel Creek Downtown Flood Resilience and Economic Recovery Initiative is a four-year, three-component program that permanently removes the most flood-damaged structures from harm's way, converts the vacated land into functional flood storage and public open space, and hardens the downtown blocks that will remain so that businesses can invest, insure, and operate with confidence.

### Component One: Voluntary Acquisition and Demolition of 34 Repetitive-Loss Properties (Years 1–3)

The city will acquire, through voluntary purchase at pre-flood fair market value, 34 repetitive-loss properties in the mapped target area between the creek channel and the downtown commercial district. Twenty-six of these are residential structures; eight are commercial or mixed-use. Acquired structures will be demolished, utilities capped, and the parcels deed-restricted as permanent open space, removing them from the 100-year floodplain inventory in perpetuity.

Acquisition will proceed in three annual phases, prioritized by (a) severity and frequency of past losses, (b) owner readiness, and (c) contribution to the contiguous footprint needed for Component Two. Every acquisition is voluntary; no property will be taken by eminent domain under this program. The city has conducted preliminary outreach to affected owners: as of July 2026, owners of 27 of the 34 properties have signed non-binding letters of interest, a participation rate that reflects both flood fatigue and careful, sustained engagement by city staff and the Main Street association.

Relocation support is integral to this component. Residential owners and tenants will receive relocation assistance consistent with Uniform Relocation Act standards. For the eight commercial properties, the Sorrel Creek Main Street association will operate a business relocation program — described further under partner roles — that matches displaced businesses with vacant downtown storefronts outside the acquisition footprint, pairing each relocation with fit-out assistance so that displacement becomes reoccupancy of currently vacant space rather than business closure.

Critically, and in direct response to lessons the city learned from a prior award (disclosed fully in the Organizational Capacity section), **no demolition or ground disturbance will occur on any parcel until all applicable environmental and cultural reviews are complete and documented**, including formal consultation with the tribal environmental office on the two parcels requiring it. The project schedule builds a full review-and-clearance period into each acquisition phase before any demolition contract is issued.

### Component Two: Eleven-Acre Detention Basin and Floodable Greenway (Years 2–4)

The contiguous land assembled through Component One, together with two city-owned parcels, will be regraded and constructed as an eleven-acre stormwater detention facility designed as a floodable public greenway. In dry conditions — the overwhelming majority of the time — the site functions as public open space: a walking and cycling trail connecting the residential neighborhoods to downtown, native tallgrass prairie plantings, and gathering areas designed in consultation with the tribal environmental office to reflect the cultural landscape of the site. During flood events, the basin captures and detains creek overflow that currently spreads across downtown streets and into buildings.

Preliminary hydraulic modeling by the university engineering extension indicates the detention facility, in combination with Component Three, will reduce the 100-year flood elevation in the downtown core by 1.8 to 2.4 feet — enough to remove an estimated 40 additional structures from the effective 100-year floodplain beyond the 34 acquired, and to reduce projected flood depths at the remaining downtown structures from destructive to manageable levels. Final design will be completed under a professional engineering services contract in Year 1, with the university extension providing independent design review.

### Component Three: Utility Elevation and Hardening on Three Downtown Blocks (Years 2–4)

On the three downtown commercial blocks that will remain in service adjacent to the greenway, the city will elevate and flood-harden critical utilities: electrical transformers and service connections raised above the revised base flood elevation, floodproofed sewer backflow prevention, elevated HVAC and mechanical placements for participating building owners, and hardened street-level infrastructure including flood-resistant streetscape elements at the greenway edge. This component directly addresses the reason businesses could not reopen quickly after 2019 and 2023: even structures with modest interior damage were closed for weeks awaiting utility restoration. Elevating utilities converts a future flood from a months-long business interruption into a days-long cleanup.

### Integration of Components

The three components are sequenced deliberately. Acquisition (Component One) creates the land for detention (Component Two); detention lowers flood elevations, which makes utility hardening (Component Three) effective and makes reinvestment in the surviving blocks rational; and the greenway itself becomes a downtown amenity — the kind of public space that draws foot traffic, supports events, and gives prospective tenants a reason beyond low rent to locate in Sorrel Creek. The project converts the city's greatest liability, its floodplain, into its most distinctive public asset.

### Partner Roles

**County Emergency Management** will integrate the project into the county hazard mitigation plan, provide flood documentation and damage-history records supporting acquisition prioritization, and coordinate flood-warning protocols for the greenway (automated gates, signage, and closure procedures during high-water events).

**Tribal Environmental Office** will conduct required formal consultation on the two culturally significant parcels, participate in environmental review across the full project footprint, and co-design the cultural and interpretive elements of the greenway. A consultation protocol and timeline have been jointly drafted and are reflected in the project schedule; consultation begins in month one of the award, well before any parcel-specific action.

**University Engineering Extension** will provide hydraulic and hydrologic modeling, independent review of the detention basin design, construction-phase technical assistance, and post-construction performance monitoring — including the flood-elevation modeling that underpins the project's outcome measures. The extension's involvement also directly mitigates the city's current engineering staffing gap, addressed in the Organizational Capacity section.

**Sorrel Creek Main Street Association** will operate the business relocation program for the eight displaced commercial occupants, recruit tenants for vacant downtown storefronts as flood risk declines, administer a modest storefront fit-out match fund within the project budget, and lead marketing of the greenway-adjacent district. The association will also track the business-level data — occupancy, relocations completed, jobs retained — required for the evaluation plan.

**City of Sorrel Creek** serves as applicant, fiscal agent, and lead implementer, responsible for acquisition, demolition, construction contracting, compliance, and reporting, under the leadership of City Manager Ingrid Sundberg with day-to-day direction by Emergency Manager Hector Villalpando and grants administration by Grants Coordinator Renata Duchamp.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

The initiative pursues two goals, corresponding to the two halves of the community's crisis and to the Trust's five outcome measures.

### Goal 1: Permanently reduce flood risk to Sorrel Creek's downtown and adjacent neighborhoods.

**Objective 1.1 — Structures removed from the 100-year floodplain.** Acquire and demolish 34 repetitive-loss structures by the end of Year 3, removing all 34 from the floodplain permanently through deed restriction. Through the detention facility's reduction of the base flood elevation, remove an estimated 40 additional standing structures from the effective 100-year floodplain by the end of Year 4, as documented by post-construction hydraulic modeling and a Letter of Map Revision submission. **Target: 74 total structures removed from the 100-year floodplain.**

**Objective 1.2 — Reduction in flood insurance claims.** Reduce flood insurance claims originating in the project area by at least 80 percent against the 2019–2023 baseline ($18.6 million across two events; annualized baseline of approximately $3.7 million). Because claims are event-driven, this objective will be measured against modeled avoided losses annually and against actual claims following any flood event during or after the project period. **Target: at least 80 percent reduction in claims from the project area for events of 100-year magnitude or less.**

**Objective 1.3 — Detention capacity constructed.** Complete construction of the eleven-acre detention basin and floodable greenway by the end of Year 4, achieving a verified 1.8-foot minimum reduction in the downtown 100-year flood elevation.

**Objective 1.4 — Utility resilience.** Elevate or floodproof critical utilities serving 100 percent of occupied structures on the three target downtown blocks by the end of Year 4, with a post-flood utility restoration target of 72 hours or less for any future event at or below the 100-year magnitude.

### Goal 2: Restore downtown Sorrel Creek's economic vitality.

**Objective 2.1 — Downtown occupancy.** Reduce downtown commercial vacancy from 29 percent to 18 percent or lower by the end of Year 4, through (a) relocation of all eight displaced businesses into currently vacant downtown storefronts, (b) recruitment of at least ten net new downtown business tenancies as flood risk and insurance costs decline, and (c) removal of the eight worst flood-damaged commercial structures from the vacancy denominator through acquisition. **Target: downtown occupancy of 82 percent or higher.**

**Objective 2.2 — Sales tax receipts.** Restore citywide sales tax receipts to at least 100 percent of 2019 levels (inflation-adjusted) by the end of Year 4, reversing the current 8 percent decline, with downtown-district receipts tracked separately as the primary indicator of project effect.

**Objective 2.3 — Jobs retained.** Retain 100 percent of the estimated 61 jobs at the eight relocating businesses, and retain at least 90 percent of the approximately 420 existing jobs in the three hardened downtown blocks through the end of the project period, verified through the Main Street association's annual business census.

**Objective 2.4 — Equitable participation.** Ensure that minority-owned businesses participate in relocation assistance, fit-out funds, and utility hardening at rates at least proportional to their 32 percent share of downtown businesses, and that outreach to Native American property owners in the acquisition area is conducted in coordination with tribal partners. Participation rates will be tracked and reported annually.

All objectives specify a baseline, a target, a data source, and a responsible party, as detailed in the Evaluation Plan.

## Evaluation Plan

The city proposes an evaluation framework built directly on the Trust's five program measures, supplemented by process measures that allow early course correction. Evaluation is designed into the project rather than appended to it: the same partners who implement each component are responsible for generating its performance data, and an independent party — the university engineering extension — verifies the technical outcomes.

### Outcome Measures, Data Sources, and Methods

**Structures removed from the 100-year floodplain.** Measured by (a) count of acquired, demolished, and deed-restricted parcels, verified through recorded deeds and demolition completion certificates maintained by the Grants Coordinator; and (b) count of standing structures removed from the effective floodplain by the revised base flood elevation, verified through the university extension's post-construction hydraulic model and the city's Letter of Map Revision submission to FEMA. Reported annually; final verification in Year 4.

**Reduction in flood insurance claims.** Measured against the documented 2019–2023 baseline using (a) NFIP and private-carrier claims data for the project area, obtained through the county emergency management office and the state NFIP coordinator, and (b) modeled avoided-loss estimates produced annually by the university extension using the calibrated hydraulic model, so that progress can be demonstrated even in years without a flood event. If a flood occurs during the project period, actual claims will be compared against modeled expectations for pre-project conditions — a natural experiment the city hopes to avoid but is prepared to measure.

**Downtown occupancy.** Measured by a quarterly storefront-level occupancy census conducted by the Main Street association using a consistent building inventory established at baseline (29 percent vacancy as of Q2 2026). The inventory will separately track relocated businesses, new tenancies, closures, and structures removed by acquisition, so that occupancy gains attributable to the project can be distinguished from denominator effects.

**Sales tax receipts.** Measured from Oklahoma Tax Commission monthly remittance data, tracked citywide and — using the city's downtown reporting district — for the downtown core specifically, adjusted for inflation and for any statutory rate changes. Baseline: 2019 receipts. Reported quarterly by the City Finance Department.

**Jobs retained.** Measured through the Main Street association's annual downtown business census, which collects employment counts by establishment, supplemented by relocation-agreement reporting from each of the eight relocated businesses at six, twelve, and twenty-four months post-relocation.

### Process Measures

To allow the city and the Trust to identify problems early, the city will also track and report: acquisition offers extended, accepted, and closed against the phased schedule; environmental and cultural reviews initiated and cleared, including tribal consultation milestones on the two designated parcels; relocations completed for residential and commercial occupants; design, permitting, and construction milestones for the detention facility and utility work; and minority-business and Native American homeowner participation rates under Objective 2.4.

### Reporting, Governance, and Use of Findings

A Project Steering Committee — the City Manager, Emergency Manager, Grants Coordinator, Public Works Superintendent, and one designated representative each from the county emergency management office, tribal environmental office, university extension, and Main Street association — will meet monthly, review the process dashboard, and resolve implementation issues. The Grants Coordinator will submit semi-annual performance reports to the Trust combining outcome and process data, with variances from targets explained and corrective actions identified. The university extension will produce an independent technical evaluation at project midpoint (end of Year 2) and closeout, including the final hydraulic verification of flood-elevation reduction. Annual public presentations to the City Council, held in the evening and publicized in the acquisition-area neighborhoods, will keep affected residents informed and give them a standing forum to raise concerns.

Evaluation findings will not sit on a shelf. Phase 2 and Phase 3 acquisition priorities may be adjusted based on Phase 1 closing rates; storefront recruitment strategies will be adjusted quarterly based on occupancy data; and the midpoint technical evaluation will inform final construction sequencing.

## Organizational Capacity

### Leadership and Staffing

The City of Sorrel Creek will implement this project through an experienced core team. **City Manager Ingrid Sundberg**, who has led the city administration for nine years, will hold ultimate administrative responsibility and chairs the Project Steering Committee. **Emergency Manager Hector Villalpando** will serve as project director; he managed the city's response and recovery operations for both the 2019 and 2023 floods, maintains the repetitive-loss property inventory that defines the acquisition list, and holds standing working relationships with county emergency management and the state hazard mitigation office. **Grants Coordinator Renata Duchamp** will manage compliance, procurement documentation, financial reporting, and Trust communications; she administers the city's current portfolio of state and federal awards. **Public Works Superintendent Otis Brumley** will oversee demolition, construction, and utility work.

### Disclosure: 2021 FEMA Deobligation

The city discloses, fully and without qualification, that in 2022 FEMA deobligated $1.1 million of a 2021 hazard mitigation award to the City of Sorrel Creek. The deobligation occurred because the city began demolition on award-funded parcels before the required environmental and historic preservation review was complete. The error was procedural, not financial — funds were expended on eligible activities — but the sequencing violation was unambiguous, and FEMA's action was warranted. The city reported the deobligation to its council publicly, repaid the disallowed amount, and commissioned an internal review of what went wrong: in substance, demolition contracting and environmental review were managed on separate tracks by separate staff, with no control preventing one from outrunning the other.

The city has since implemented corrective measures that are now standard practice and are built into this application's design. First, a written pre-construction compliance checklist, administered by the Grants Coordinator, must be signed off before any demolition or construction contract is issued on any grant-funded project; the checklist includes documented completion of all environmental, historic, and cultural reviews. Second, environmental review is now initiated at the acquisition-offer stage rather than after closing, so clearance and closing proceed in parallel and clearance never becomes an afterthought. Third, the project schedule for this initiative includes explicit review-and-clearance milestones for each acquisition phase, visible to the Steering Committee and reportable to the Trust, and the tribal environmental office's consultation on the two designated parcels is scheduled to begin in month one. The city believes the most credible evidence of institutional learning is a project designed so the same failure cannot recur, and it invites the Trust to examine these controls in detail during application review.

### Staffing Gap and Mitigation: Vacant City Engineer Position

The city also discloses that the staff engineer position in the Public Works Department has been vacant for fourteen months, a consequence of a regional shortage of licensed civil engineers and the city's constrained salary schedule. The city is not asking the Trust to fund a project that depends on a position it has been unable to fill. Instead, the project design routes all engineering-intensive work through two channels with demonstrated capacity: a competitively procured professional engineering services contract, budgeted in Year 1, for detention basin design, construction documents, and construction administration; and the university engineering extension, which provides independent design review, modeling, and construction-phase technical assistance under a signed memorandum of agreement. Superintendent Brumley, a thirty-year public works professional, will manage these contracts and coordinate field operations. The city continues active recruitment for the engineer position, has increased the advertised salary range, and will assign the position to this project if filled — but the project schedule and budget assume it is not.

### Fiscal and Administrative Systems

The city operates on an annually audited budget with an unmodified opinion in each of the past six audits. Grant funds are tracked in dedicated accounts with expenditure-level coding; procurement follows Oklahoma competitive bidding statutes; and the city carries the insurance and bonding required for construction contracting. The Grants Coordinator maintains the compliance calendar and document repository for all active awards.

### Partner Capacity

Each partner brings demonstrated capacity: the county emergency management office maintains the FEMA-approved multi-jurisdictional hazard mitigation plan in which this project is a listed action; the tribal environmental office conducts consultations of this kind routinely and has assigned a lead reviewer; the university extension has completed hydraulic studies for eleven Oklahoma communities in the past five years; and the Main Street association is a nationally accredited Main Street America program with a full-time director. Letters of commitment from all four partners accompany this application.

## Sustainability

The Trust rightly asks not only whether a project can be built, but whether its benefits will endure. This initiative is designed so that its core benefits are permanent by construction, its ongoing costs are modest and funded, and its economic effects are self-reinforcing.

### Permanence of Flood-Risk Reduction

The acquisition component's benefits require no ongoing funding to sustain. Acquired parcels will be deed-restricted as permanent open space, an irrevocable removal of structures from the floodplain. The detention basin is an earthen facility with an engineered outlet structure — infrastructure with a multi-decade design life and low mechanical complexity, deliberately chosen over pump-dependent alternatives precisely to minimize long-term operating burden.

### Greenway and Detention Facility Maintenance

The city commits to maintaining the greenway and detention facility as a permanent responsibility of the Public Works Department, and this application budgets for the transition. Routine maintenance comprises mowing and native-prairie management, trail and amenity upkeep, outlet-structure inspection and debris removal, and post-event sediment survey and removal as needed. The university extension will prepare an operations and maintenance manual as a deliverable of the design contract, including inspection schedules and sediment-management triggers.

Annual maintenance cost is estimated at $85,000. The city will fund this through three sources: (1) a dedicated line item in the Public Works operating budget beginning in Year 3, adopted by council resolution concurrent with construction contracting; (2) reallocation of current expenditures — the city presently spends approximately $40,000 annually on emergency drainage clearing, post-flood debris removal, and mowing of the vacant flood-damaged parcels, costs the project largely eliminates; and (3) the recovered sales tax revenue projected under Objective 2.2, a fraction of which exceeds the maintenance obligation. The native prairie planting palette, developed with the tribal environmental office, was selected in part because established tallgrass prairie requires substantially less maintenance than turf. A volunteer stewardship program organized through the Main Street association and tribal partners will supplement — not substitute for — city maintenance, supporting plantings, cleanups, and programming.

The county emergency management office will maintain the flood-warning and greenway-closure protocol as part of its standing operations, at no incremental cost to the project.

### Economic Sustainability

The economic components are structured to become self-sustaining. Relocated and newly recruited businesses pay rent to private building owners, generating the private reinvestment incentive that currently does not exist; declining flood insurance premiums, documented through the Letter of Map Revision, permanently reduce operating costs for every downtown business; and restored sales tax receipts rebuild the city's general fund capacity, including for maintenance. The Main Street association's tenant-recruitment and district-marketing functions continue after the grant period under its existing accredited program.

### Local Match: Commitment and Contingency

The city's 20 percent match of $800,000 is proposed from a general obligation bond issue that will go before Sorrel Creek voters in September 2026 — one month after this application deadline. The city discloses this timing plainly and has prepared a firm contingency rather than an assumption.

If the bond passes, $800,000 in bond proceeds will be committed to the project by council resolution within sixty days of the election. Mayor Franklin Standing Bear is leading the public campaign, and pre-election survey work indicates majority support, driven by the same flood fatigue that produced the 88 percent letter-of-interest rate among acquisition-area owners.

If the bond fails, the city will meet the full match through a combination already identified and quantified: (1) $350,000 from the city's capital improvement fund balance, which the council has reviewed and confirmed as available without displacing committed projects; (2) $250,000 in documented in-kind match from city-performed demolition site work, right-of-way, and the two city-owned parcels contributed to the detention footprint, valued by independent appraisal; and (3) $200,000 phased from general fund capital allocations across fiscal years 2027–2029, consistent with the project's four-year drawdown schedule. City Manager Sundberg and the City Council have reviewed this contingency, and a council resolution committing the city to the $800,000 match **regardless of the bond outcome**, specifying the contingency sources, accompanies this application. The Trust's investment does not depend on the September election; the election determines only which local dollars fulfill a commitment the city has already made.

## Budget Narrative

**Total Project Cost: $4,800,000 — Tallgrass Civic Trust: $4,000,000 (83.3%) — City of Sorrel Creek match: $800,000 (16.7% of total; 20% of Trust request)**

### Component One: Acquisition, Relocation, and Demolition — $2,310,000

*Property acquisition — $1,700,000 (Trust: $1,450,000; Match: $250,000).* Voluntary purchase of 34 repetitive-loss properties at pre-flood fair market value, averaging $50,000 per property (residential average $42,000; commercial average $76,000), based on the county assessor's roll adjusted by a preliminary appraisal review. Each acquisition will be supported by an independent appraisal. Match includes the appraised value of the two city-owned parcels contributed to the project footprint ($110,000) and city right-of-way contributions ($140,000), documented by independent appraisal.

*Relocation assistance — $280,000 (Trust: $280,000).* URA-consistent residential relocation for an estimated 26 households ($6,500 average, $169,000) and commercial relocation and moving assistance for eight businesses ($111,000), administered by the city with the Main Street association coordinating commercial placements.

*Demolition, utility capping, and site clearance — $240,000 (Trust: $140,000; Match: $100,000).* Demolition of 34 structures at an average of $7,000, including asbestos survey and abatement where required, utility disconnection and capping, and rough grading. Match reflects in-kind city crew labor and equipment for site clearance and grading, valued at established municipal equipment rates. No demolition contract will be issued for any parcel until the pre-construction compliance checklist, including all environmental and cultural clearances, is complete.

*Environmental review, cultural consultation, and title/closing costs — $90,000 (Trust: $90,000).* Phase I environmental site assessments across the acquisition area, parcel-specific reviews, support costs for formal tribal consultation on the two designated parcels (including compensation for the tribal environmental office's technical review time), title work, and closing costs at approximately $1,500 per parcel.

### Component Two: Detention Basin and Floodable Greenway — $1,610,000

*Engineering design and construction administration — $310,000 (Trust: $310,000).* Competitively procured professional engineering services contract covering hydraulic design, construction documents, permitting (including Section 404 coordination), bid support, construction administration, and the operations and maintenance manual. The university extension's independent design review and modeling ($60,000 of this line) is contracted separately to preserve its independence.

*Basin construction — $980,000 (Trust: $830,000; Match: $150,000).* Excavation and grading of approximately 11 acres, engineered outlet structure, creek-bank tie-ins, and erosion control. Estimate is based on the university extension's preliminary quantities at current regional unit costs plus a 15 percent contingency embedded in the line. Match reflects city crew hauling and site work at municipal equipment rates.

*Greenway improvements — $320,000 (Trust: $320,000).* Trail construction (approximately 1.1 miles of accessible surfaced trail), native tallgrass prairie establishment, cultural and interpretive elements co-designed with the tribal environmental office, seating, lighting at trail entrances, flood-warning signage, and automated closure gates coordinated with county emergency management protocols.

### Component Three: Downtown Utility Elevation and Hardening — $610,000

*Utility elevation and floodproofing — $530,000 (Trust: $530,000).* Elevation of electrical transformers and service connections above the revised base flood elevation on three downtown blocks (coordinated with the electric utility), sewer backflow prevention devices for all occupied structures, elevated mechanical platforms for participating building owners, and flood-resistant streetscape restoration at the greenway edge.

*Storefront fit-out match fund — $80,000 (Trust: $80,000).* Matching grants of up to $10,000 per business, administered by the Main Street association under city-approved guidelines, prioritizing the eight relocated businesses and minority-owned enterprises consistent with Objective 2.4. Owner match of at least 1:1 is required, leveraging a minimum of $80,000 in private investment.

### Project Administration and Evaluation — $270,000

*Project management and grants administration — $170,000 (Trust: $70,000; Match: $100,000).* Partial salary support across four years for the Grants Coordinator's compliance, reporting, and financial management functions (Trust share) and the documented, time-tracked project effort of the Emergency Manager and Public Works Superintendent (city cash match, from the contingency sources identified in the Sustainability section or bond proceeds).

*Evaluation and performance monitoring — $70,000 (Trust: $70,000).* University extension contract for annual modeled avoided-loss analysis, midpoint and closeout technical evaluations, and final hydraulic verification supporting the Letter of Map Revision; Main Street association data collection for the quarterly occupancy census and annual business/jobs census.

*Public engagement and outreach — $30,000 (Trust: $30,000).* Acquisition-area owner outreach materials and translation as needed, coordination of tribal community engagement with the tribal environmental office, annual public meetings, and project communications.

### Match Summary — $800,000

Cash: $450,000 (capital improvement fund and/or bond proceeds: $350,000; staff project effort: $100,000). In-kind: $350,000 (contributed real property and right-of-way: $250,000; city crew labor and equipment: $100,000). All in-kind values are supported by independent appraisal or municipal equipment/labor rate schedules and will be documented to Trust standards. As detailed in the Sustainability section, the council resolution accompanying this application commits the full match independent of the September 2026 bond election outcome.

### Cost Reasonableness and Fiscal Controls

Unit costs are drawn from the county assessor's roll, the university extension's preliminary engineering quantities, current regional construction cost data, and the city's actual demolition and site-work costs from the 2023 recovery. All contracts will be competitively procured under Oklahoma statute. No Trust funds will be drawn for any demolition or construction activity on any parcel until the pre-construction compliance checklist for that parcel is complete — the control adopted after the 2021 deobligation, now the city's standing safeguard and the Trust's assurance that this investment will be spent in the right order as well as on the right things.
