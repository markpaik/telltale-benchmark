## 1. Executive Summary

This proposal asks the Board of Directors to authorize the acquisition of the Palm Grove Inn, a 96-room motel in San Bernardino, and its conversion into 88 units of permanent supportive housing (PSH) for chronically homeless adults. The total development cost is $19.4 million, funded by an $8.6 million San Bernardino County housing award, $4.2 million in low-income housing tax credit equity, a $3.5 million bank construction loan, $1.9 million in federal Community Development Block Grant funds, and $1.2 million from Vista Alta's operating reserve.

The decision cannot be deferred. The purchase agreement negotiated by Real Estate Director Trevor Lindgren lapses if the Board does not act on October 9, and the county's $8.6 million award is reserved only through March 31, 2027. If we do not sign, deposit $600,000, and close by January 15, 2027, both the site and the county funding are lost — the county has stated plainly that the award will be redirected to another sponsor.

The project is the largest single undertaking in Vista Alta's history, and it carries real risk. It commits $1.2 million of a reserve that currently stands at 41 days of cash, requires a nonrefundable $600,000 deposit at signing, and depends on a services funding plan that is fully committed only through year two. The Board is also entitled to weigh our record: the 2023 Loma Verde rehabilitation finished eleven months late and $1.8 million over budget, and Treasurer Wan-Chen Liu is right to insist that we demonstrate what has changed. Neighborhood opposition, voiced at two town halls convened by the council district representative, adds political risk to the entitlement path.

This memorandum lays out the site and the people it would house, the full budget and financing structure including our reserve exposure, the fourteen-month construction schedule and lease-up plan, the services model and its eleven-position staffing plan, the alternatives we considered and rejected, the principal risks with specific mitigations, and the precise authorizations requested. Management's recommendation is to proceed. The conditions we have attached to that recommendation — cost contingencies, third-party construction oversight, a services reserve, and defined off-ramps — are described in Sections 8 and 9.

## 2. Why Now: The Case for Acting on October 9

Three deadlines converge on this meeting.

**First, the purchase agreement expires.** The seller has given us a negotiated price of $7.9 million — approximately $82,000 per room, below recent comparable motel sales in the Inland Empire — in exchange for certainty: a $600,000 nonrefundable deposit at signing and a hard closing date of January 15, 2027. The seller has other interested parties, including at least one operator who would keep the property running as a nightly-rate motel. If the Board does not authorize signing on October 9, the agreement lapses and the price and terms are gone.

**Second, the county award expires.** San Bernardino County's $8.6 million award — 44 percent of the total development cost — is reserved for this project only through March 31, 2027. The county requires an executed purchase agreement, a closed acquisition, and evidence of full financing before disbursement. County staff have told us directly that if we cannot demonstrate site control and a closing by mid-January, the award will be redirected to the next project on the county's list. This is not soft money we can chase again next cycle; it is the product of a competitive application we won on the strength of this specific site.

**Third, the need is not waiting.** The most recent point-in-time count identified more than 4,100 people experiencing homelessness in San Bernardino County, roughly a third of them chronically homeless with a disabling condition. The county's coordinated entry system has a by-name list of chronically homeless adults who qualify for permanent supportive housing today and for whom no units exist. Eighty-eight units would represent a meaningful expansion of the county's PSH inventory and would be the first PSH development Vista Alta has ever delivered.

We recognize the discomfort of a forced timeline. Management did not choose these deadlines; they are the terms on which the site and the money are available. What we can control is the quality of our underwriting, the discipline of our execution, and the honesty with which we present the risks. That is what this document attempts to do.

## 3. The Site and the Population It Would House

### 3.1 The Property

The Palm Grove Inn is a 96-room, two-story motel on an approximately 2.1-acre parcel on a commercial corridor in San Bernardino, within the boundaries of the council district whose representative has convened the recent town halls. The property was built in the mid-1980s and operated continuously as a motel until the current owner reduced operations during the pandemic. It is currently about 40 percent occupied on nightly and weekly rates.

The physical configuration is well suited to conversion. Rooms average approximately 320 square feet, each with a private bathroom — a critical feature, because it means every unit can meet permanent housing standards without gut reconstruction of the plumbing core. The conversion scope combines pairs of rooms in some wings and reconfigures others to produce 88 studio units averaging 340 to 400 square feet, each with a kitchenette (two-burner cooktop, refrigerator, sink, microwave), new flooring, new HVAC through-wall units, and full ADA conversion of nine units (10 percent). The former lobby, office, and pool building will be converted into a 24-hour staffed front desk, case management offices, a community room with communal kitchen, laundry facilities, a nurse's exam room, and secure package and medication storage. The pool will be filled and the deck converted to a landscaped courtyard. Site work includes new fencing, controlled-access gating, lighting, cameras, and parking-lot restriping.

Our pre-offer due diligence included a Phase I environmental site assessment (no recognized environmental conditions; a Phase II was recommended and is budgeted for the diligence period), a property condition assessment identifying roof replacement, repiping of two wings, electrical panel upgrades, and full HVAC replacement — all carried in the budget — and a preliminary title review showing no encumbrances that would impede the project.

The site is within a half mile of a bus rapid transit stop, a quarter mile of a grocery store and pharmacy, and two miles of a federally qualified health center with which Marcus Delahoussaye's team has an existing referral relationship. These proximities matter operationally: PSH residents overwhelmingly do not own cars, and transit and pharmacy access are among the strongest predictors of housing retention.

### 3.2 The Population

The project will serve chronically homeless single adults referred exclusively through the county's coordinated entry system, prioritized by vulnerability and length of homelessness. Under the county award's regulatory agreement, all 88 units will be restricted to households at or below 30 percent of area median income for a term of 55 years. Residents will hold standard leases and pay 30 percent of their income toward rent, with the balance covered by project-based rental subsidies committed by the county housing authority for an initial fifteen-year term with renewal expectancy.

Based on the county's by-name list, we anticipate a resident population in which roughly two-thirds have a behavioral health condition, about half have a chronic physical health condition, and a substantial share are over age 55 — the fastest-growing segment of the homeless population statewide. These characteristics drive the services design described in Section 6.

It is worth stating what permanent supportive housing is and is not, because the town hall commentary reflected genuine confusion. PSH is not a shelter. It is not transitional. Residents are tenants with leases, rights, and obligations, screened through coordinated entry, living in their own apartments with on-site support. National and California evidence consistently shows housing retention rates of 85 percent or better in well-run PSH, and shows that supportive housing reduces — not increases — emergency calls, encampments, and street homelessness in surrounding areas relative to a distressed nightly-rate motel, which is what the Palm Grove Inn is today.

### 4.3 Reserve Exposure — Stated Plainly

Vista Alta's operating reserve currently stands at 41 days of cash against an $11.4 million annual budget — approximately $1.28 million. This project asks the Board to commit $1.2 million of it, in two tranches:

- **$600,000 nonrefundable deposit at signing** (within days of Board approval). This money is at risk from the moment it is wired. If the project fails to close for any reason — including a failure of any other funding source — the deposit is lost.
- **$600,000 at construction closing**, disbursed alongside other sources.

During the construction period, the reserve would fall to approximately 22 days of cash at its lowest point before recovery through three mechanisms: (1) repayment of $400,000 of the reserve contribution from tax credit equity installments at construction completion, per the investor letter of intent; (2) receipt of the earned (non-deferred) developer fee of $400,000 across the development period; and (3) ordinary operating surpluses from the existing 340-unit portfolio, which have averaged roughly $350,000 annually over the past three years. Under the base-case pro forma, the reserve returns to 40+ days within nine months of construction completion and exceeds its current level within eighteen months of stabilization, after which the $250,000 deferred developer fee is paid from project cash flow over the early operating years.

Treasurer Liu has asked management to state the downside without varnish, and it is this: **if the project fails after signing but before closing, Vista Alta loses $600,000 plus incurred diligence costs, reducing the reserve to roughly 20 days of cash with no project to show for it.** The probability-weighted analysis in Section 8 addresses how we have constrained that scenario, but the Board should approve this project only with clear eyes about that exposure. Management's judgment is that the risk is acceptable because every funding source other than final CDBG environmental clearance is committed or under executed term sheet before signing, and because the off-ramp structure in Section 9 requires management to return to the Board before the second tranche if any source falls away.

Three features of this schedule deserve the Board's attention.

**The conversion scope is inherently more predictable than Loma Verde.** The 2023 Loma Verde rehabilitation was an occupied rehab of a 1960s building with residents in place, which forced phased work, tenant relocations, and repeated discovery of concealed conditions. Palm Grove will be vacant at closing (the seller is responsible for winding down motel operations and delivering the property empty — a condition of the purchase agreement), the building envelope and unit layouts are uniform, and the property condition assessment plus the budgeted Phase II investigation are designed to surface concealed conditions before we price the construction contract, not after.

**Lease-up is fast because referrals are queued.** Unlike market-rate lease-up, PSH lease-up draws from the county's coordinated entry by-name list. The county housing authority's project-based subsidy commitment obligates it to process referrals at a pace supporting 20–25 move-ins per month. Marcus Delahoussaye's team will begin document-readiness work with prospective residents during month 12 of construction so that income certifications and subsidy paperwork are substantially complete before certificates of occupancy issue.

**The schedule carries slack where it matters.** The tax credit equity pay-in schedule and construction loan term tolerate up to four months of construction delay before triggering extension fees; the pro forma carries interest reserves sized to sixteen months rather than fourteen. The one date with no slack is January 15: the closing. Everything between October 9 and January 15 — Phase II, design, permit submittal, loan closing — is on a critical path that Trevor Lindgren's team has mapped week by week, and the entitlement risk discussion in Section 8.1 addresses the principal threat to it.

## 6. Services Model and Staffing

### 6.1 The Model

Permanent supportive housing works when housing and services are integrated but distinct: residents hold leases like any tenant, and services are offered persistently but are voluntary, following the Housing First evidence base that California statute now requires of state-funded PSH. The Palm Grove services design has four elements:

**24-hour front desk.** The building will be staffed around the clock, every day. The front desk controls building access, responds to incidents, coordinates with case managers and after-hours on-call clinical support, and is the single most important factor in both resident safety and neighborhood confidence. It is also the element neighbors at the town halls most consistently said they wanted assurance about, and it is a firm design commitment, not an aspiration to be trimmed if budgets tighten.

**Case management at 1:20.** Five case managers, each carrying a caseload of no more than twenty residents, will provide individualized service plans, benefits enrollment and advocacy (SSI/SSDI, CalFresh, Medi-Cal), linkage to behavioral health and substance use treatment, eviction prevention, and daily-living support. The 1:20 ratio is the standard for high-acuity PSH and is a condition of the state Medi-Cal services funding described below.

**Part-time on-site nursing.** A part-time registered nurse (0.5 FTE) will run an on-site clinic two to three days per week, providing health assessments, medication support, chronic disease monitoring, and warm hand-offs to the nearby federally qualified health center. Given the anticipated age and health profile of residents, on-site nursing is among the highest-value dollars in the budget.

**Community programming and property coordination.** Group programming — meals, peer support, life-skills workshops — plus tight coordination between services staff and property management so that lease compliance problems are addressed as service opportunities before they become eviction filings. Our target, consistent with well-performing PSH statewide, is a twelve-month housing retention rate of 85 percent or better.

Property management (one site manager and one maintenance technician) is carried in the property operating budget funded by rental subsidy income, not in the services budget, consistent with standard PSH underwriting.

### 6.3 Services Budget and the Funding Gap

The annual services cost at stabilization is **$1.4 million**. The state's Medi-Cal pathway — through which enhanced care management and community supports for this population are now billable — is projected to cover **$780,000** annually, based on the acuity profile of the coordinated-entry referral pool and billing rates our team has validated with two managed care plans operating in the county. That leaves an annual gap of **$620,000**.

The gap is covered for years one and two: the county award carries a capitalized services allowance and the county behavioral health department has committed a two-year services contract that together bridge the difference. **Beyond year two, the $620,000 has no committed source.** This is the single largest long-term risk in the proposal, and Section 8.4 sets out the mitigation plan in full, including a project-funded transition services reserve, a pipeline of identified renewal sources, and — critically — a Board-approved floor below which the services model will not be allowed to fall regardless of funding weather.

## 7. Alternatives Considered

Management evaluated three alternatives seriously before recommending this project.

### 7.1 New Construction on the City-Owned Parcel

The City of San Bernardino has indicated willingness to negotiate disposition of a 1.6-acre parcel for affordable housing. We modeled a 75-unit new-construction PSH project on that site. The analysis was not close:

- **Cost:** Estimated total development cost of $36–41 million ($480,000–$545,000 per unit) versus $19.4 million ($220,455 per unit) for the conversion — roughly $260,000 to $325,000 more per unit.
- **Funding:** The county's $8.6 million award is committed to the Palm Grove site and is not transferable; a new-construction project would require an entirely new capital stack, including a competitive 9 percent tax credit award for which the statewide success rate is under 40 percent per round.
- **Timeline:** Disposition negotiation, entitlement, design, and competitive financing would put occupancy in 2030–2031 at the earliest, versus 2028 for the conversion — three additional years during which 88 households remain unhoused.
- **Risk:** New construction carries greater cost escalation exposure, and the city parcel would require the same or greater community engagement effort with no incumbent nuisance use to replace.

The city parcel remains a legitimate future opportunity — potentially for family affordable housing rather than PSH — and pursuing this project does not foreclose it. But as an alternative to Palm Grove for this funding and this population, it fails on cost, speed, and feasibility.

### 7.2 Passing on the Site

Declining the project preserves the reserve at 41 days, avoids all execution risk, and spares the organization a contested entitlement process. Management weighed this seriously; Vista Alta has never done PSH, and Loma Verde is a fresh scar. But passing has real costs of its own:

- The $8.6 million county award — the largest single award in Vista Alta's history — is redirected, and our credibility for future county awards is diminished. Funders remember sponsors who win awards and cannot execute.
- The site is likely acquired by another motel operator, and the neighborhood's stated grievances about the property — nightly-rate turnover, calls for service, blight — continue indefinitely.
- The mission cost: 88 units of housing for the county's most vulnerable residents, at a per-unit cost we are unlikely to see again, do not get built.
- Strategically, motel conversion is the most cost-effective PSH production model available in California, and the state and county funding architecture increasingly favors sponsors who can execute it. Passing now likely means Vista Alta exits this line of work before entering it.

### 7.3 A Smaller or Phased Version

We examined acquiring the property and converting it in two phases, or partnering with a larger PSH developer as co-sponsor. Phasing fails financially: the county award and tax credit equity both require the full 88-unit scope, and operating a half-converted property alongside active construction recreates the exact occupied-rehab conditions that damaged Loma Verde. Co-sponsorship was explored with two regional PSH developers; both would require the developer fee and effective control, converting Vista Alta into a passive local partner. Given that the county award was won on Vista Alta's application and local standing, management concluded that a strengthened owner's-side team (Section 8.2) achieves the risk-management benefit of partnership without surrendering the project.

## 8. Risks and Mitigations

### 8.1 Entitlement and Community Opposition Risk

**The risk.** The council district representative has held two town halls at which neighbors spoke against the project, citing concerns about safety, property values, and concentration of services. Although state law (including AB 2011 and related statutes governing supportive housing and motel conversions) substantially limits discretionary denial of this use, and the project's by-right pathway means the city's role is largely ministerial, opposition can still slow permit processing, generate CEQA-adjacent challenges, and sour the political environment for future work.

**Mitigations.**
- **Legal posture.** Our land use counsel has confirmed the project qualifies for streamlined, by-right approval under state supportive housing statutes, meaning the city cannot lawfully deny or condition it on discretionary grounds. This does not eliminate delay risk, but it caps it.
- **A community benefits commitment.** Management proposes to formalize, in writing and before closing, commitments that respond directly to what neighbors actually asked for at the town halls: the 24-hour staffed front desk; a good-neighbor policy with a published community phone line answered around the clock; exterior lighting, fencing, and camera coverage; a community advisory committee meeting quarterly with neighborhood representatives; and landscaping and façade improvements that visibly upgrade the corridor.
- **The counterfactual argument.** The most persuasive point at any future meeting is the status quo: a 40-percent-occupied nightly-rate motel generates far more police calls per room than well-run PSH. We will present the property's current calls-for-service data alongside call data from comparable converted PSH properties in the region.
- **Elected engagement.** I have requested meetings with the council representative and county supervisor before closing, and will report to the Board on those conversations.

### 8.2 Construction Cost and Schedule Risk — and What Changed After Loma Verde

**The risk.** Treasurer Liu's reminder is fair and important: Loma Verde finished eleven months late and $1.8 million over budget. If Palm Grove overran by a comparable proportion, the reserve could not absorb it.

**What went wrong at Loma Verde.** The post-project review the Board received in early 2024 identified four causes: an occupied-building phasing plan that collapsed under tenant relocation delays; concealed conditions (structural and plumbing) discovered mid-construction because pre-construction investigation was underfunded; a construction contract without a guaranteed maximum price, leaving Vista Alta holding escalation risk; and an owner's-side team too thin to catch problems early.

**What is different here.**
- **Vacant building.** The property is delivered empty at closing. The single largest Loma Verde failure mode — occupied phasing — does not exist on this project.
- **Front-loaded investigation.** The budget funds Phase II environmental work, destructive testing of representative units, roof cores, and repipe scoping *before* the construction contract is priced, during the October–December diligence window and before the second reserve tranche is committed.
- **Guaranteed maximum price contract.** The construction contract will be a GMP with a pre-qualified general contractor experienced in motel conversions (three regional firms have been shortlisted), with liquidated damages for delay beyond a defined grace period.
- **Real contingency, Board-controlled.** The budget carries a 10 percent hard cost contingency ($745,000) plus soft cost contingency. Contingency draws above 5 percent of hard costs require notification to the Board's finance committee before release.
- **Third-party owner's representative.** We will engage an independent construction management firm — a role that did not exist at Loma Verde — reporting to Trevor Lindgren and, quarterly, directly to the Board, with authority to review all pay applications and change orders.
- **Simple, repetitive scope.** Eighty-eight nearly identical units in a uniform 1980s building is a fundamentally more predictable scope than a 1960s occupied rehab.

Management will not claim the overrun risk is zero. We claim it is bounded: with a GMP contract, a vacant building, front-loaded investigation, and $895,000 of total contingency, the realistic downside is measured in weeks and low six figures, not months and millions.

### 8.3 Financing and Closing Risk

**The risk.** The $600,000 deposit is nonrefundable at signing, but three sources — tax credit equity, the construction loan, and CDBG — are not fully closed until January. A failure of any one between October and January strands the deposit.

**Mitigations.** The equity letter of intent and bank term sheet are executed, with underwriting substantially complete; both institutions have closed comparable conversions in the region. The CDBG environmental (NEPA) review is the least certain item and is on a critical path; the city's administering department projects clearance by mid-December, and counsel has structured the transaction so no "choice-limiting action" impairs the federal funds. As a backstop, management has identified a $1.9 million contingency plan combining a modest increase in the permanent loan and a state motel-conversion program application should CDBG fail. Finally, the off-ramp in Section 9 requires management to return to the Board before wiring the second $600,000 tranche if any source has deteriorated materially.

### 8.4 Services Funding Risk Beyond Year Two

**The risk.** The $620,000 annual gap between the $1.4 million services cost and the $780,000 Medi-Cal pathway has committed sources only through year two of operations. An unfunded gap in year three would force service cuts precisely when the building's population is fully housed and acuity is highest — the failure pattern that has damaged PSH projects elsewhere in the state.

**Mitigations.**
- **Capitalized transition services reserve.** The development budget and first-year operating surpluses will capitalize a services reserve of $500,000 by the end of year one — sufficient to cover roughly 80 percent of one year's gap — giving the organization a full funding cycle of runway before any cut would be contemplated.
- **Maximize Medi-Cal capture.** The $780,000 projection is deliberately conservative; comparable projects with disciplined billing capture $900,000–$1,000,000 at this acuity level. Marcus Delahoussaye will hire the Program Manager with Medi-Cal billing supervision as an explicit qualification, and we will contract with a billing-support intermediary for the first eighteen months. Every incremental Medi-Cal dollar shrinks the structural gap.
- **Identified renewal pipeline.** Candidate sources for years three forward include the county behavioral health department's ongoing PSH services contracts (our year-one/two contract creates renewal standing), state homeless housing program allocations that flow to the county on a recurring basis, managed care plan community reinvestment funds, and philanthropic support for which PSH services are a stated regional priority. None is guaranteed; the mitigation is that there are several, they renew on different cycles, and we begin cultivating all of them in year one, not year three.
- **A Board-approved floor.** Management proposes that the Board adopt, as part of this authorization, a services floor: the 24-hour front desk and a case management ratio no worse than 1:28 are protected commitments that Vista Alta will fund from operating surpluses if necessary. The flexible layer — nursing hours, peer support, programming — is where any temporary shortfall would be absorbed. This converts an open-ended risk into a defined, budgetable exposure of roughly $250,000–$300,000 per year in the worst case, against portfolio surpluses averaging $350,000.
- **Annual Board review.** Services funding status will be a standing item in the annual budget presentation, with a red-flag report to the Board no later than eighteen months before any funding cliff.

### 8.5 Guaranty and Balance Sheet Risk

The construction lender and tax credit investor will require completion and repayment guaranties from Vista Alta, standard for a sponsor of our size but a real contingent liability. Counsel will negotiate caps and burn-off provisions tied to completion and stabilization milestones, and the final guaranty terms will be presented to the finance committee before construction closing.

### 8.6 Operating and Reputational Risk

A poorly run PSH building would damage Vista Alta's standing with the city, the county, and our neighbors more than passing on the project ever could. The mitigations are the services model itself — the front desk, the ratios, the good-neighbor commitments — plus a decision management has already made: the site's property management will be handled by our most experienced site team, and the Program Manager will be hired six months before opening, not at opening. We will also arrange site visits for Board members and neighborhood representatives to two well-operating converted PSH properties in the region before closing.

## 9. Off-Ramps and Conditions of Approval

Management proposes that the authorization be structured with explicit off-ramps, so that approval on October 9 is not an irrevocable commitment of the full $1.2 million:

1. **Before signing (immediately after October 9):** No off-ramp; the $600,000 deposit is at risk upon signing. This is the irreducible bet the Board is asked to make.
2. **Before construction closing (January 15):** Management must certify to the finance committee that (a) the equity and loan closings are simultaneous with acquisition, (b) CDBG environmental clearance is in hand or the backstop is committed, (c) the GMP contract is executed within the construction budget, and (d) Phase II results are consistent with the property condition assessment. If any certification cannot be made, management returns to the Board before the second $600,000 tranche is wired, with a recommendation to proceed, restructure, or walk (accepting the deposit loss).
3. **During construction:** Contingency draws above 5 percent of hard costs require finance committee notification; any change order taking the project more than $400,000 over the GMP requires Board approval.
4. **Services funding:** Annual certification to the Board of services funding status, with the eighteen-month red-flag rule described in Section 8.4.

## 11. Closing

This is the largest and hardest decision this Board has faced. It asks us to commit the deepest reserve draw in our history, on a compressed timeline we did not choose, into a line of work we have not done before, in a neighborhood where some voices are against us, with the memory of Loma Verde still fresh. Every one of those objections is legitimate, and this proposal has tried to answer each with structure rather than reassurance: a guaranteed maximum price and a vacant building where Loma Verde had neither; contingencies the Board controls; a services floor the organization guarantees; and off-ramps that keep half the reserve exposure conditional until every other dollar is at the table.

Against those risks stands this: 88 permanent homes for the county's most vulnerable residents, at half the cost of building new, funded 94 percent by outside sources, on a site whose current use is itself the neighborhood's chief complaint — and an $8.6 million award that will go to someone else on April 1 if we do not act. Management recommends approval.
