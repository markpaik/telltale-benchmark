# Closing the Melt-Line Gap: A Workforce Pipeline Strategy for Great Lakes Metalcasting

**A White Paper of the Great Lakes Metalcasting Institute**

Dr. Priscilla Vandenberg, Research Director
Rafael Quintanilla, Workforce Program Lead
Dr. Sung-Min Roh, Labor Economist

Sheboygan, Wisconsin
July 2026

## Abstract

The metalcasting workforce in the Great Lakes region is losing workers faster than it can train them, and the gap is now visible in scrap rates, injury rates, and customer contracts. Drawing on the Institute's turnover census of 140 member foundries and roughly 21,400 hourly workers across eight states, this paper documents a 38 percent first-year separation rate on melt and molding lines, an average cost per separation of $11,800, and a 14-week time to fill open positions. Plants in the top quartile of turnover run scrap at nearly double the rate of bottom-quartile plants and carry recordable injury rates 60 percent higher. With more than a third of the member hourly workforce over age 55, the problem will compound over the next decade unless plants build a durable pipeline of new entrants.

This paper reviews five pipeline approaches members have tried — registered apprenticeship, high school youth apprenticeship and co-op, community college cohort partnerships, structured ninety-day in-house onboarding, and temp-to-hire conversion — and compares them on cost, time to productivity, retention, and scalability. We present a cost model and a twelve-month implementation sequence that a single plant, including one under 75 employees, can follow. We then address the four objections raised most consistently by members: poaching, the burden on small plants, seniority language in collective bargaining agreements, and supervisor resistance to releasing trainees from production. We close with a recommended sequence timed to the November 14, 2026 close of the federal apprenticeship expansion grant window and the staffing commitments now appearing in agricultural equipment supply agreements.

## 1. The Problem in the Sector

Metalcasting is a foundational supplier industry — the parts that come out of Great Lakes foundries go into tractors, trucks, pumps, valves, and machine tools sold across the country and the world. That position has historically insulated member plants from having to compete for workers the way retail or hospitality employers do. That insulation is gone.

Three forces are converging on member plants simultaneously.

**First, the workforce is aging out.** Thirty-four percent of the hourly workforce across member plants is over age 55. That figure understates the urgency, because it is an average across roles; on many melt and molding lines — the physically demanding, heat-exposed positions at the center of the casting process — the share is higher still. A worker who retires at 62 after thirty years on a pour line does not leave a vacancy that a job posting fills. That worker leaves with tacit knowledge about alloy behavior, mold conditions, and equipment quirks that no training manual fully captures.

**Second, the entrants who do arrive do not stay.** The Institute's census puts first-year separation on melt and molding lines at 38 percent. That is not a statistic about a slow labor market; it is a statistic about a broken first year of employment. Workers who leave within twelve months rarely leave because the job disappeared. They leave because the job as experienced — heat, noise, physical strain, unclear advancement, inconsistent supervision — did not match what they expected, and no one intervened before they walked out.

**Third, customers are no longer treating workforce stability as the foundry's internal business.** Two large agricultural equipment OEMs have begun requiring staffing commitments — documented plans for recruiting, training, and retention — as a condition of long-term supply agreements. This is a meaningful shift. It moves workforce planning from a plant-level HR function to a commercial function that affects contract eligibility. Plants that cannot demonstrate a credible pipeline strategy risk losing multi-year business to competitors who can.

These three forces interact. An aging workforce with no replacement pipeline produces short-staffed lines. Short-staffed lines push overtime and cross-training past sustainable limits, which degrades the new-hire experience and worsens first-year turnover. Worse turnover raises scrap and injury rates, which erodes the plant's standing with OEM customers precisely when those customers are starting to ask hard questions about staffing.

There is a narrow but real window to act. A federal apprenticeship expansion grant that offsets a meaningful share of registered apprenticeship costs closes its application window on November 14, 2026. Plants that want to use this funding to launch or expand a program need to have applications in motion well before that date. The remainder of this paper is organized to help a plant manager or HR director move from diagnosis to a funded, sequenced plan within that window.

## 2. What the Institute's Data Show

The Institute has collected turnover, cost, and outcome data from member foundries since 2019 through an annual census, supplemented by scrap and safety data reported to member quality and safety committees. The following figures represent the pooled 2025 census across 140 reporting plants and approximately 21,400 hourly employees in eight states (Wisconsin, Michigan, Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Minnesota, Iowa, and Pennsylvania).

The cost-per-separation figure of $11,800 is built from four components that member plants report separately: direct recruiting and hiring cost, supervisor and trainer time diverted from production, overtime and temporary labor to cover the vacancy, and scrap and rework attributable to reduced crew experience during the vacancy and initial ramp period. Of these, the scrap and rework component is typically the largest and the most underappreciated by plant financial reporting, because it is usually buried in general scrap accounts rather than tagged to staffing events.

The relationship in Figure 1 is not incidental. Scrap in a foundry is disproportionately driven by process consistency — mold quality, pour timing, cooling control — and process consistency depends on crew experience. A line staffed by workers with fewer than six months of tenure produces more inconsistent molds and more pour errors than a line staffed predominantly by workers with two or more years of experience, independent of equipment or alloy. The quartile gap of 4.3 percentage points in scrap rate translates, at typical member plant volumes and material costs, into a material and calculable cost of turnover that most plants do not currently attribute to their staffing programs.

The safety relationship follows a similar logic. New workers are disproportionately represented in recordable injuries industry-wide, and foundry work — with its combination of heat, moving equipment, and manual material handling — punishes inexperience more than most manufacturing settings. A 60 percent higher recordable injury rate in top-quartile turnover plants is consistent with a workforce that is, at any given time, carrying a larger share of workers still inside their first-year risk window.

Time to fill — 14 weeks on average for melt and molding roles — compounds all of the above. A 14-week vacancy is not a brief gap; it is more than three months during which the plant runs short, pushes overtime, and asks existing workers to cover unfamiliar stations. That burden on incumbent workers is itself a driver of the departures that create the next vacancy. The turnover problem is, in this sense, partly self-reinforcing, and breaking the cycle requires shortening time to fill and reducing first-year separation simultaneously — which is the design test we apply to each pipeline approach in Section 3.

## 3. Comparing Five Pipeline Approaches

Member plants have experimented, over the past several years, with five distinct approaches to building a hourly workforce pipeline. None is uniformly superior; each fits different plant sizes, local labor markets, and union environments. This section compares them on four dimensions that matter most to a plant manager: cost per hire, time to productive output, one-year retention, and scalability for plants under 75 employees.

**Table 2. Comparison of Five Pipeline Approaches**

| Approach | Cost per Hire (3-yr basis) | Time to Full Productivity | 1-Year Retention (member-reported) | Scalable Below 75 Employees? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Registered apprenticeship | ~$27,000 gross; ~$17,600 net of $9,400 offsets | 24–36 months to journey-level; productive contribution from month 4–6 | 78–85% | Difficult alone; feasible in consortium |
| High school youth apprenticeship / co-op | $4,000–$7,000 per participant | 12–18 months to full-rate hourly work | 55–65% (conversion to full hire) | Yes, with school partnership |
| Community college cohort partnership | $6,000–$9,500 per hire | 6–9 months to line-ready | 60–68% | Yes, if regional consortium shares cohort |
| Structured 90-day in-house onboarding | $2,200–$3,800 per hire | 90 days to line-ready, mentored | 58–66% at 1 year | Yes, lowest fixed cost |
| Temp-to-hire via staffing agency | $3,500–$5,000 markup per placement, recurring | Immediate placement; variable quality | 30–40% at 1 year | Yes, easiest to start, weakest retention |

### 3.1 Registered apprenticeship

Registered apprenticeship produces the strongest retention outcome in the census — 78 to 85 percent one-year retention among apprentices who complete at least the first year — because the wage progression, credentialing, and multi-year commitment structure select for workers who intend to build a career rather than fill a gap. It is also the most expensive and slowest approach to first productive output, and it depends on program infrastructure (related technical instruction, a qualified journey-level mentor bench, Department of Labor registration) that a single small plant struggles to build alone. The available federal offset — $9,400 against a roughly $27,000 three-year cost, per apprentice — meaningfully changes the economics, but the offset requires an approved program in place before the November 14, 2026 grant window closes.

### 3.2 High school youth apprenticeship and co-op

Youth apprenticeship and co-op programs are the lowest-cost route to a known, locally rooted candidate, and they build the plant's reputation with the community and with parents — a nontrivial factor in rural and small-city labor markets where reputation drives referral hiring. The weakness is conversion: many participants use the co-op as exposure rather than a firm commitment, and one-year retention among those who do convert to full hourly employment (55–65 percent) trails registered apprenticeship. This approach works best as a feeder into either registered apprenticeship or in-house onboarding, not as a standalone pipeline.

### 3.3 Community college cohort partnerships

Cohort partnerships with a regional technical or community college — where the college delivers a short, foundry-specific pre-employment curriculum (basic metallurgy, safety, blueprint reading, hand tool competency) to a cohort of 8 to 15 students, several of whom the plant then hires — produce a workforce that arrives with baseline competency and shortens time to line-ready to 6 to 9 months. This approach scales well below 75 employees specifically because the cost of curriculum development and instruction is shared across multiple participating plants in a region, which is why the Institute has begun brokering regional cohort consortia among clusters of smaller members (see Section 5.2).

### 3.4 Structured ninety-day in-house onboarding

This is the lowest-cost approach on a per-hire basis and the fastest to implement, because it requires no external partner — only a defined curriculum, a trained mentor, and management discipline to protect the ninety-day structure from production pressure. Retention (58–66 percent at one year) is respectable but below apprenticeship, largely because in-house onboarding does not carry the wage-progression and credentialing commitments that give a worker a multi-year reason to stay. Its principal value is as a foundation: every other pipeline approach eventually feeds new workers into some version of a structured onboarding process, so getting this right is a precondition for the other four approaches to work, not a competitor to them.

### 3.5 Temp-to-hire through staffing agencies

Temp-to-hire is the fastest way to fill a vacancy and requires no upfront program investment, which explains its popularity, particularly at plants managing an acute short-term gap. It is also, by a wide margin, the weakest approach on retention — 30 to 40 percent at one year — because the arrangement is transactional on both sides: the agency is paid on placement, not on retention, and workers frequently treat the assignment as a bridge rather than a destination. Recurring agency markup costs also compound over time in a way that the other four approaches, which are front-loaded, do not. Temp-to-hire has a legitimate role as a stopgap while a plant builds one of the other four approaches, but the census data do not support it as a sustainable primary pipeline strategy.

### 3.6 Reading the comparison

No single approach solves the whole problem. The pattern in the data suggests a portfolio logic: use youth apprenticeship and community college partnerships to build a visible, low-cost candidate funnel; use structured ninety-day onboarding as the universal on-ramp that every new hire passes through regardless of source; use registered apprenticeship, where scale allows or a consortium can be formed, to convert the best of that funnel into long-tenure, cross-trained employees; and treat temp-to-hire as a bridge, not a foundation. Section 4 turns this portfolio logic into a cost model and sequence a single plant can follow.

## 4. Cost Model and Implementation Sequence

### 4.1 Cost model for a representative plant

The following model applies to a representative member plant of 120 hourly employees experiencing the census-average 38 percent first-year separation rate on melt and molding roles (roughly 45 of the 120 positions), with 20 positions turning over in a given year among the affected roles.

**Table 3. Illustrative Annual Cost Model, 120-Employee Plant**

| Item | Status Quo (temp-to-hire dependent) | Portfolio Approach (Year 1 of transition) |
|---|---|---|
| Annual separations, melt/molding | 17 | 17 (unchanged in Year 1; retention gains lag cohort start) |
| Cost per separation | $11,800 | $11,800 |
| Total separation cost | $200,600 | $200,600 |
| Onboarding program build cost (curriculum, mentor training) | — | $18,000 (one-time) |
| Community college cohort partnership (10 participants) | — | $75,000 |
| Youth apprenticeship/co-op (6 participants) | — | $30,000 |
| Registered apprenticeship (4 apprentices, consortium-shared) | — | $70,400 gross; $32,800 net of $9,400/apprentice offset |
| **Year 1 total incremental spend** | **$0** | **~$155,800** |
| Projected Year 2 separation reduction (to ~28% blended, per early-adopter member data) | — | ~$45,000 avoided separation cost |
| Projected Year 3 separation reduction (to ~22% blended) | — | ~$95,000 avoided separation cost, plus scrap and injury cost avoidance not itemized above |

The model is deliberately conservative: it does not monetize the scrap-rate and injury-rate improvements documented in Section 2, both of which member plants that have completed a similar transition report as material, often exceeding the direct turnover-cost savings once fully realized in Year 3. It also assumes the plant uses the registered apprenticeship offset, which requires application before the November 14, 2026 deadline; a plant that misses that window faces the full $27,000 gross cost per apprentice, which changes the Year 1 arithmetic meaningfully and argues for early action.

### 4.2 A twelve-month implementation sequence

The sequence below is built from the practices of member plants that have executed a successful transition, adjusted to fit a single-plant timeline anchored to the current grant deadline.

**Figure 2. Twelve-Month Implementation Sequence**

```
Month 1–2   | Diagnose: separation-reason exit interviews, cost tagging by
            | department, identify feeder high schools and colleges
            |
Month 2–3   | Build the 90-day onboarding curriculum and mentor bench;
            | this is the foundation every other track depends on
            |
Month 3–4   | Sign community college cohort MOU; recruit first cohort
            | for fall term start
            |
Month 4     | File registered apprenticeship program application and
            | grant offset paperwork (target: well ahead of Nov 14
            | deadline; DOL and state review takes 8-12 weeks)
            |
Month 5–6   | Launch youth apprenticeship conversations with feeder
            | high schools for the following school year
            |
Month 6–8   | First 90-day onboarding cohort completes; measure against
            | baseline retention and scrap metrics
            |
Month 8–10  | First community college cohort completes pre-employment
            | curriculum; hire and onboard
            |
Month 10–12 | Registered apprenticeship program active with first
            | apprentice class; review Year 1 cost model against plan;
            | begin Year 2 budget and cohort planning
```

Two sequencing decisions matter more than the others. First, the 90-day onboarding redesign has to come before or alongside every other track, because a community college hire or an apprentice who arrives into an undisciplined first ninety days will fail for the same reasons a direct hire does. Second, the registered apprenticeship application has to move in month four, not month ten, because federal and state review of new program applications routinely takes eight to twelve weeks, and a plant that waits until October to apply risks missing the November 14 offset window entirely.

## 5. Working Through the Objections

Every member conversation about pipeline investment eventually reaches the same four objections. They are legitimate, and the Institute's position is not that they are wrong but that each has a workable response drawn from what other members have already done.

### 5.1 "Neighboring plants will just poach the people we train."

This is the most frequently raised objection, and it is not baseless — a trained, credentialed melt or molding worker is attractive to any plant within commuting distance, and a plant that pays to train someone else's future employee understandably resents it.

Two responses matter here. First, the census data do not support the fear at the scale members assume. Among plants that have run a structured onboarding or apprenticeship program for at least two years, member-reported poaching losses account for well under 10 percent of departures; the dominant driver of departure remains the same first-year experience problems — physical demands, unclear advancement, supervisor relationships — that drive turnover industry-wide. Investing in a better first year reduces the departures a plant actually experiences, regardless of the theoretical poaching risk.

Second, where poaching is a real regional dynamic, the answer is not to withhold training but to change the region's incentive structure. Several member clusters have adopted informal regional wage-and-benefit banding agreements — not collusion on wages, which raises legal concerns members should review with counsel, but shared transparency on where a market-competitive range sits, so that no single plant is systematically underpaying and creating an obvious poaching target. The Institute can convene these regional conversations without members disclosing confidential wage data to competitors.

### 5.2 "We're under 75 employees. We can't carry this alone."

This objection is accurate as stated: a plant with 60 hourly employees cannot build a standalone apprenticeship program, cannot justify a full-time training coordinator, and cannot negotiate favorable terms with a community college on its own. The answer is not to ask small plants to do what large plants do at smaller scale; it is consortium structure.

The Institute is actively brokering regional cohort consortia — clusters of four to eight member plants within a single labor market that share a single community college curriculum, a single registered apprenticeship program sponsor, and, where geography allows, a shared training coordinator position funded proportionally by participating plants. A plant under 75 employees that could not justify $18,000 in curriculum-build cost alone can justify a $3,000–$4,000 proportional share. This is precisely the model behind the "consortium-shared" apprenticeship line in Table 3, and it is the single most important structural recommendation of this paper for smaller members: do not attempt registered apprenticeship or cohort partnerships alone; join or form a consortium before applying for the grant offset.

### 5.3 "Seniority language in our collective bargaining agreement won't accommodate this."

Where member plants are unionized, seniority provisions governing job bidding, shift assignment, and layoff order are frequently cited as an obstacle to apprenticeship structures, which typically require defined progression steps and, in some formulations, protected training assignments that can appear to conflict with strict seniority bidding.

In practice, member plants that have navigated this successfully have not asked unions to waive seniority; they have negotiated apprenticeship and onboarding language as a distinct wage and job classification track, parallel to rather than in conflict with the existing seniority ladder, with entry into the apprenticeship track itself subject to a seniority-respecting bid process among interested current employees where the union represents incumbents, alongside a separate external-hire track for new entrants. This is a negotiated addition to the CBA, not a suspension of it, and several member plants' local agreements — available to other members through the Institute's CBA language library — provide worked templates. Locals have generally been receptive when the framing is "this creates a new, better-paying classification our members' kids can enter" rather than "this changes how your job is protected."

### 5.4 "Supervisors won't release trainees from production."

This is, in the Institute's assessment based on member interviews, the objection most likely to quietly kill a pipeline program after it launches, because it operates through everyday production pressure rather than formal policy. A supervisor facing a shift short-staffed by two people will pull a trainee off a scheduled mentoring rotation to run a station, every time, unless there is a structural reason not to. Over a ninety-day period, enough of these individually reasonable decisions add up to a trainee who never received the structured exposure the program was designed to provide — and who then leaves within the first year for the same reasons as everyone else.

The response has to be structural, not exhortative. Plants that have protected training time successfully have done one or more of the following: built a small staffing buffer (typically two to three positions above headcount) explicitly budgeted as training capacity rather than production capacity, so pulling a trainee does not create a hole; tied a portion of supervisor performance evaluation and incentive pay to training-plan completion rates for their trainees, not just to production output; and assigned training oversight to a role separate from the line supervisor — a dedicated trainer or training coordinator, shared across a shift or across a consortium of smaller plants — so that the person accountable for the trainee's ninety days does not also carry the competing daily pressure to hit the shift's production number. Plants that have relied on supervisor goodwill alone, without one of these structural protections, report the weakest of the retention outcomes in Table 2 regardless of which pipeline approach they nominally used.

## 6. Conclusion

The data in this paper describe a sector where workforce instability has stopped being a background HR concern and become a measurable driver of scrap, safety, and now commercial risk. A 38 percent first-year separation rate, a workforce more than a third over age 55, and OEM customers beginning to write staffing commitments into supply agreements together describe a problem that will not resolve through incremental hiring effort. It requires a deliberate pipeline strategy, built and sequenced with the same discipline a plant would apply to a capital project.

No single one of the five approaches examined here is sufficient alone. The evidence supports a portfolio: a disciplined ninety-day onboarding process as the foundation every new hire passes through; youth apprenticeship and community college cohort partnerships as the low-cost, locally rooted candidate funnel; registered apprenticeship, pursued through a regional consortium where a plant is too small to sustain it alone, as the retention engine; and temp-to-hire treated honestly as a bridge rather than a strategy.

The objections members raise — poaching, small-plant burden, seniority language, supervisor resistance — are real, but each has a working answer already in use somewhere in the membership. The Institute's role going forward is to make those answers, and the consortium and curriculum infrastructure behind them, available to every member plant, and to help members move quickly enough to use the federal apprenticeship offset before its window closes on November 14, 2026. Plants that act within that window will enter 2027 with both a funded program and a credible answer to the staffing commitments their customers are starting to require. Plants that wait will face the same problem, a year older, without the offset, and increasingly without the contracts.

## Appendix A: Regional Consortium Formation Checklist

Member plants exploring a shared cohort or apprenticeship consortium under Section 5.2 have asked the Institute for a practical starting checklist. The following sequence reflects the process used by the three consortia currently operating within the membership (Sheboygan–Manitowoc cluster, Fort Wayne cluster, and the Rockford–Beloit cluster).

1. **Identify the labor-shed, not the trade association boundary.** Consortium partners should draw from plants within a genuinely shared commuting radius — typically 25 to 35 miles in the Institute's member geography — rather than plants that happen to belong to the same regional trade group. A consortium built on convenience of association rather than shared labor market tends to produce a curriculum that serves no single plant's actual hiring needs well.

2. **Convene a founding meeting with HR leads, not just plant managers.** The plants that formed successful consortia report that HR or workforce leads, not plant managers alone, needed to be in the room from the first meeting, because curriculum content, wage banding conversations, and CBA coordination require HR judgment that plant managers do not always carry.

3. **Select a single fiscal or administrative sponsor.** One participating plant, or the Institute itself, should hold the registered apprenticeship program sponsorship and the community college contract, with cost-sharing formalized through a simple interlocal or membership agreement rather than each plant separately contracting with the college. This is the single administrative step that most determines whether a consortium survives its first year.

4. **Set the cohort size before recruiting.** Cohorts of fewer than eight participants rarely justify dedicated college instructor time; cohorts above eighteen strain a single instructor's ability to give hands-on attention in foundry-specific pre-employment training. The Institute recommends a target cohort size of ten to fourteen.

5. **Agree on allocation rules before the first hire is made.** The most common point of consortium failure is not curriculum design but disagreement over which plant gets first pick of cohort graduates. Successful consortia have used a rotating draft order, a skills-match placement process run by the college instructor, or a simple rule that participants choose their own employer from among consortium members based on offers received. Whichever rule is chosen, it must be agreed upon in writing before recruiting begins, not after graduates are in hand.

6. **Register the apprenticeship program under the consortium sponsor before applying for the federal offset.** Department of Labor guidance permits multi-employer program sponsorship, but the application must name the sponsoring entity and list participating employer sites at the time of filing. Plants intending to join a consortium application for the November 14, 2026 window should have the sponsor and participant list finalized no later than early September 2026 to allow time for the administrative filing and any state-level co-registration required in Wisconsin, Michigan, Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Minnesota, Iowa, or Pennsylvania.

## Appendix B: Exit Interview Question Set Used in the 2025 Census

Member plants contributing separation-reason data to the census used a common eight-question exit interview protocol, reproduced here for members who have not yet standardized their own exit process. Consistent question wording across plants is what allows the Institute to aggregate separation reasons meaningfully across the membership rather than relying on each plant's idiosyncratic exit form.

1. What was the single biggest factor in your decision to leave?
2. Before your last two weeks, had you already been considering leaving? If so, for how long?
3. Did you discuss your concerns with your direct supervisor before deciding to leave? Why or why not?
4. How would you describe your first ninety days here, compared to what you expected going in?
5. Did you feel you had someone you could go to with questions during your first ninety days?
6. Were you offered, and did you take, any cross-training or advancement opportunity while employed here?
7. If a similar opening became available here in the future, would you consider returning? Why or why not?
8. Is there one change that would have kept you here?

Plants beginning to collect this data for the first time should expect the first two or three quarters of responses to be less reliable, as exiting workers calibrate to a new process and HR staff calibrate to consistent coding of open-ended answers. The Institute's data team is available to member plants for coding guidance and for benchmarking a plant's early results against the aggregate census.

## Appendix C: Sample Ninety-Day Onboarding Curriculum Outline

Several members have asked the Institute for the underlying structure of the ninety-day onboarding curriculum referenced in Section 3.4 and Table 3. The outline below reflects the composite structure used across the eleven member plants that supplied curriculum detail to the 2025 census. Individual plants should adapt station sequencing to their own process flow, but the phase structure and the protected mentoring ratio have held constant across every plant that reported strong first-year retention gains.

**Days 1–10: Orientation and Safety Foundation**
Full-time pairing with a designated mentor, no independent station assignment. Covers PPE fitting and use, lockout-tagout, heat stress recognition, material handling technique, and a supervised walk-through of the full casting process from pattern to finishing so the new hire understands where their eventual station fits in the larger sequence. Plants that skip or compress this phase — a common casualty of production pressure discussed in Section 5.4 — show measurably weaker ninety-day completion rates in member data.

**Days 11–30: Guided Station Rotation**
New hire rotates through two to three stations related to their eventual permanent assignment, working alongside the mentor at a ratio of no less than one mentor to two trainees at any time. Formal check-ins with the training coordinator or supervisor occur at day 15 and day 30, using a written skills checklist rather than an informal verbal assessment, so that gaps are documented and addressed rather than assumed to resolve on their own.

**Days 31–60: Supervised Independent Work**
Trainee begins independent station work with the mentor present but not performing the task, available for correction and questions. Production expectations increase incrementally against a documented ramp curve rather than jumping to full-rate expectations at day 31. This is the phase most vulnerable to the "supervisor will not release the trainee" pressure described in Section 5.4, because the trainee looks capable enough to staff independently; plants with a protected training buffer maintain the mentor presence through day 60 regardless of short-term staffing pressure.

**Days 61–90: Full Independent Work with Structured Check-Ins**
Trainee works the station independently at full production expectation, with formal check-ins at day 75 and day 90 covering technical competency, safety record, and — critically, given the exit interview findings in Appendix B — a direct conversation about how the trainee's experience compares to expectations and whether they have identified a path for advancement. The day 90 check-in is the point at which several member plants have found it effective to formally discuss cross-training options or, where applicable, eligibility to bid into a registered apprenticeship track under the parallel classification structure described in Section 5.3.

Plants adopting this outline for the first time should budget mentor time explicitly rather than treating it as an informal addition to the mentor's existing production duties; census data indicate that plants which formally compensate or otherwise recognize the mentor role report more consistent curriculum adherence than plants relying on voluntary mentor participation.
