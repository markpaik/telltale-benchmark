# The Keeper's Log, Continued

The light was automated in March. Two men came out on the supply boat with a crate of equipment and stayed four days, and when they left there was a grey box bolted to the wall of the lantern room with a small green diode that never blinked. The light now turned itself on at dusk, off at dawn, and had no need of me.

Nobody told me to stop keeping the log. Nobody told me to continue, either. The Authority sent a letter thanking me for thirty-one years of service and informing me that the cottage was mine to occupy until the end of the lease, which was another eleven years, which was longer than I expected to last. The letter did not mention the log. I think they forgot it existed. For a century and a half the keepers of this station had recorded the weather, the sea state, the ships that passed, the condition of the lamp, and the letter did not mention it, so I took that as a kind of permission.

---

*April 3. Wind SW, force 4, backing. Visibility good. Sea moderate. Light functioned. Two gannets working the near water from about nine. No ships.*

---

I kept the old form for a while, out of habit, and because the form was the thing that made it a log rather than a diary. A diary is about the person writing it. A log is about everything except. But the old form had been designed around a question—*did the light work, and if not, why not*—and the question no longer had any weight. The light worked. The green diode said so. My entry confirming it was like a man standing beside a clock and announcing the hour.

So the entries began to change, not by decision but by drift. I noticed I was writing more about the gannets and less about the lamp. I noticed that when the fog came in I described the sound of the fog, which the old keepers never did, because the sound of the fog was not something the Authority needed to know.

---

*April 19. Fog from first light, dense, then thinning to a kind of milk by noon. The horn ran all morning on its own. From inside the cottage it sounds like a cow that has given up on being answered. No ships seen, though I heard engines twice, distant, heading north.*

---

My predecessor, a man named Teague whom I met only once, on the day of the handover, told me that the log was the most important thing on the station. More than the lamp. "The lamp can fail," he said. "The lamp has failed before. But if the log is kept, then somebody afterward can understand what happened." He tapped the ledger. "This is the part that remembers."

I thought at the time that this was the sort of thing an old man says when he is leaving a job. Now I am the old man, and I have come round to it. The lamp is a promise made to ships. The log is a promise made to whoever comes after. And a promise does not lapse simply because the person you made it to has stopped checking.

---

*May 6. Clear. Wind light and variable. Swell from the west, long-period, probably from weather well out in the Atlantic that never reached us. The rocks below the east face were showing more kelp than I remember for this time of year. I have no way of knowing whether this is significant. I am writing it down in case it is.*

---

*In case it is.* I found I was writing that phrase, or some version of it, more and more. The old log had not needed it, because the old log had a reader who knew what mattered. My log has a reader I cannot picture. Perhaps a researcher, decades on, who wants to know when the kelp changed. Perhaps a child of someone who buys the lease after me, bored on a wet afternoon, pulling a ledger off the shelf. Perhaps nobody. The not-knowing changes what I record. It makes me more generous. When you do not know which detail will be wanted, you have to keep more of them, and you have to keep them plainly, without deciding in advance what they mean.

---

*June 11. Wind NE, fresh. The supply boat came two days early because of the forecast. Colm brought the papers and a box of seed potatoes I did not order. He said the shop had them and he thought of me. I note this because it is the kind of thing that would not appear in the old log, and because I was moved by it, and because in thirty-one years I have never once written down that I was moved by anything, and I think that was an error of the form and not a fact about the life.*

---

I have wondered whether the green diode keeps a log of its own. I assume it does—some record of on and off, voltage, faults, sent by radio to a room on the mainland where it is stored and probably never read. I do not resent it. It is doing what it was built to do, and doing it better than I did; it does not sleep through a dawn or forget to trim a wick. But its record will say only that the light worked. It will not say that on the twenty-second of June the sun went down behind a bank of cloud the colour of a bruise healing, and that the light came on into that colour and looked, for about four minutes, as though it had been lit for a reason.

---

*June 22. Wind W, force 3. Sea slight. Light functioned. Sunset extraordinary; see above, though I have not managed to say it. One ship, a small coaster, passing south about five miles off, running lights showing. I watched it until it was gone. It did not need me, and I did not need it to, and I was glad of it all the same.*

---

The ledger I am using now is the same stock as the old ones, bound in the same green cloth, from a stationer in the town who has been supplying the station since before my time and who did not ask why I was still ordering. I have six blank ones on the shelf. At the present rate they will see me through the lease. After that I do not know who will write in them, or whether anyone will.

But there will be the shelf, and the row of spines, and the dates on the spines running unbroken from the year the tower was built to whatever year I stop. Somebody standing in front of that shelf will be able to see that the record did not end when the requirement did. They may not know why. They do not need to. The log does not exist to explain the keeper. It exists so that the weather on this rock, on days no one else was watching, was still somewhere set down.

---

*Tonight: wind dropping. Stars. The light functioned. I was here.*
