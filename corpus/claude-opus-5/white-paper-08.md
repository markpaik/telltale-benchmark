## Abstract

Between 2021 and 2025, Cedarbrook Health Partners recorded 11,900 pediatric behavioral health emergency department visits across fourteen emergency departments serving a catchment of 1.4 million people. Applying a boarding threshold of more than twelve hours past the point of disposition, the median boarding stay was 31 hours, the ninetieth percentile was 106 hours, and 18 percent of boarded children remained in an emergency treatment bay for more than three days. Restraint or seclusion occurred in 9.4 percent of boarded stays. Direct sitter labor cost the system $1.1 million in 2025, and boarding consumed an average of 4.2 emergency treatment bays per day systemwide — the functional equivalent of removing a small emergency department from service every day of the year.

These outcomes are not primarily a function of emergency department practice. The region holds 38 inpatient child psychiatric beds for 1.4 million people, and the two facilities operating those beds declined 44 percent of Cedarbrook transfer requests in 2025. Boarding is the visible symptom of a structural mismatch between crisis demand and downstream capacity.

This paper evaluates five candidate interventions — a dedicated psychiatric emergency unit, telepsychiatry consultation into rural sites, mobile crisis teams paired with short-stay stabilization beds, a pediatric observation protocol embedded in existing emergency departments, and bridge clinics guaranteeing follow-up within 72 hours — on cost per case, geographic reach, time to deployment, and expected effect on boarding hours. We find that no single intervention resolves the problem, and that the sequencing of investment matters as much as the total. We recommend a staged portfolio: immediate deployment of telepsychiatry and a standardized observation protocol across all fourteen sites; bridge clinics and mobile crisis capacity within eighteen months; and a twelve-bay pediatric psychiatric emergency unit in Mankato, at $6.8 million in capital and $4.2 million in annual operating cost, submitted to the state grant round closing in September 2026. Under base-case assumptions the portfolio reduces total boarding hours by 58 to 66 percent and produces $3.1 million in annual avoided cost and recovered emergency capacity against $4.2 million in incremental operating expense — a gap that requires deliberate payer and state participation to close. We address four standing objections: rural travel distance, child psychiatrist recruitment, Medicaid rate adequacy, and whether upstream investment moves emergency volume at all.

## 1. Introduction: The Shape of the Problem

A fifteen-year-old arrives at a critical access hospital emergency department in a county of 11,000 people at 9:40 on a Tuesday evening, brought by a parent after a suicide attempt. She is medically cleared by 11:15. A crisis assessment completed at 1:30 in the morning concludes she requires inpatient psychiatric admission. There is no child psychiatric bed available within 140 miles. She remains in Bay 3 — a room with a wall-mounted monitor, a curtain, and no window — for the next fifty-one hours, watched continuously by a rotating series of nursing assistants pulled from other assignments, while a transfer coordinator makes forty-one phone calls to eleven facilities in three states.

This is not an unusual case in our system. It is close to the seventy-fifth percentile.

The phenomenon has a name — behavioral health boarding — and a substantial national literature, but naming it has not reduced it. Boarding is the interval during which a patient occupies an emergency department bed after the clinical decision about where they should go has already been made. It is, by definition, waiting that produces no clinical benefit. For pediatric behavioral health patients, boarding is longer, more frequent, and more consequential than for any other emergency population, because the downstream resource in question — an inpatient child psychiatric bed — is the scarcest bed type in American health care.

Three features of our region intensify the general problem.

**Geographic dispersion.** Cedarbrook operates fourteen emergency departments across nineteen counties in southern Minnesota and western Wisconsin. Nine of these are critical access facilities with fewer than twenty-five beds and no on-site behavioral health clinician at any hour. The distance from our westernmost site to the nearest inpatient child psychiatric bed exceeds 170 road miles.

**Bed scarcity.** The region's 38 inpatient child psychiatric beds serve a population of 1.4 million, a ratio of 2.7 beds per 100,000 residents. Estimates of adequate supply for children and adolescents cluster between 8 and 12 beds per 100,000. Our region operates at roughly one-quarter of the low end of that range.

**Referral friction.** Scarcity would be manageable if the available beds were reliably accessible. They are not. In 2025 the two facilities holding the region's 38 beds declined 44 percent of our transfer requests. Declines were concentrated among patients with autism spectrum diagnoses, intellectual disability, aggression history, age under twelve, and involvement with county child protection — precisely the children for whom community alternatives are thinnest.

The purpose of this paper is not to describe the problem again. It is to put five years of system data against five candidate solutions, build a defensible financial model, and state plainly what each stakeholder would have to contribute for the arithmetic to work. We write for hospital executives who must allocate capital, state Medicaid officials who set the rates that determine whether crisis services are viable, payers who currently pay for boarding without recognizing that they are doing so, county human services directors who hold the mandate for crisis response, and legislators facing a grant round that closes in fourteen months.

## 2. Methods and Data Sources

We extracted all emergency department encounters from January 1, 2021, through December 31, 2025, at Cedarbrook's fourteen emergency departments with a patient age under eighteen at the time of arrival and a primary or secondary behavioral health presentation, defined by a validated ICD-10 grouper covering suicidality and self-harm, psychosis, mood and anxiety disorders in crisis, substance-related crisis, aggression and behavioral dysregulation, and eating disorder emergencies. Encounters in which behavioral health diagnoses were incidental to a primary medical or traumatic presentation were excluded on chart review by two reviewers, with disagreements adjudicated by a third.

This yielded 11,900 encounters. For each we captured arrival time, medical clearance time, crisis assessment completion time, disposition decision time, physical departure time, disposition type, transfer request count and outcome, restraint and seclusion events, sitter hours assigned, and payer.

**Boarding definition.** We define boarding as time in excess of twelve hours from the documented disposition decision to physical departure from the emergency department. The twelve-hour threshold is conservative — many published analyses use four or six hours — and was chosen so that our findings could not be attributed to definitional inflation. All boarding statistics in this paper therefore understate total non-productive emergency department time.

**Cost inputs.** Sitter labor was drawn from payroll records with fully loaded wage rates. Opportunity cost of occupied treatment bays was calculated using site-specific contribution margin per emergency department visit, net of the margin generated by the boarded patient. Capital and operating estimates for the proposed psychiatric emergency unit derive from a February 2026 architectural feasibility study and a staffing model built by the authors and reviewed by system finance.

**Limitations.** Documentation of disposition decision time improved materially over the study period; boarding durations in 2021 and 2022 are likely underestimated relative to 2024 and 2025. Sitter hours are recorded reliably at ten of fourteen sites and estimated at four. We do not have systematic post-discharge follow-up data before 2023, which constrains our ability to measure recidivism trends across the full period. Our transfer-decline data reflect only requests logged in the system transfer center; direct site-to-site calls made outside that workflow are not captured, so decline rates may be understated.

The relationship is dose-dependent and, in our clinical judgment, causal in substantial part. Emergency treatment bays are designed for rapid throughput, not for containing an adolescent for four days. They lack windows, privacy, exercise space, structured activity, consistent staff relationships, and — in nine of our fourteen sites — any clinician trained in pediatric behavioral de-escalation on nights and weekends. Children who arrive in crisis and are then confined without therapeutic structure predictably deteriorate. When they deteriorate, staff without alternatives use restraint.

This is the finding we would most want state officials and legislators to sit with. A system that boards a suicidal twelve-year-old for four days in a windowless room and then restrains her has not merely failed to treat her. It has inflicted an additional injury, and it has done so under color of medical care.

## 4. Framing the Solution Space

Boarding is a queueing failure with three distinct levers: reduce arrivals into the queue, increase the rate at which the queue empties, or change the environment in which people wait so that waiting causes less harm. Interventions that address only one lever will underperform, because pediatric behavioral health crisis demand is elastic to service availability in ways that pure capacity models do not capture.

We evaluate five approaches against four criteria: **cost** (capital and annual operating, plus cost per case served), **reach** (how many of our fourteen sites and how much of our catchment population it touches), **speed** (time from decision to operational effect), and **boarding impact** (modeled reduction in total boarding hours).

### 4.1 Dedicated Pediatric Psychiatric Emergency Unit

A twelve-bay pediatric psychiatric emergency service (PES) co-located at our Mankato flagship, purpose-built for behavioral health crisis: ligature-resistant construction, natural light, separate acute and sub-acute zones, secure milieu space, on-site child psychiatry coverage sixteen hours daily with overnight telepsychiatry, and licensure permitting stays up to 72 hours without inpatient admission.

*Cost:* $6.8 million capital; $4.2 million annual operating.
*Reach:* Directly serves the eastern two-thirds of the catchment; requires transport from western sites.
*Speed:* 26–32 months from funding to opening.
*Boarding impact:* Highest single-intervention effect. Modeled 42–48 percent reduction in total system boarding hours, driven disproportionately by the long tail — a PES with a 72-hour license and a mandate to accept complex presentations directly addresses the children other facilities decline.

### 4.2 Telepsychiatry Consultation into Rural Sites

Child and adolescent psychiatry consultation delivered by video into all fourteen emergency departments, staffed to cover 7:00 a.m. to 11:00 p.m. seven days weekly with overnight on-call, integrated into the emergency record with direct order-writing privileges.

*Cost:* $340,000 capital (endpoints, carts, network hardening); $1.15 million annual operating (2.4 psychiatrist FTE equivalent plus coordination).
*Reach:* Complete — all fourteen sites, entire catchment.
*Speed:* 7–10 months.
*Boarding impact:* Moderate but broad. Early specialist assessment converts a meaningful share of presumed-admission cases to safe discharge with intensive outpatient follow-up. Modeled 14–19 percent reduction in boarding hours, concentrated in the 12–48 hour bands. Critically, telepsychiatry also reduces *unnecessary* transfer requests, easing pressure on the referral network.

### 4.3 Mobile Crisis Teams with Short-Stay Stabilization Beds

Four county-based mobile crisis teams (two-person, licensed clinician plus certified peer or paraprofessional) responding to homes, schools, and law enforcement calls, paired with two four-bed short-stay stabilization houses licensed for up to five days.

*Cost:* $1.9 million capital (two facilities, acquisition and renovation); $3.35 million annual operating.
*Reach:* Broad but county-dependent; effective coverage of roughly 70 percent of catchment population within a 45-minute response radius.
*Speed:* 14–20 months.
*Boarding impact:* Operates on both the arrival and exit sides. Mobile crisis diverts a share of presentations before they reach an emergency department; stabilization beds provide a discharge destination for children who need supervised structure but not hospital-level care. Modeled 18–24 percent reduction in boarding hours, with an additional 8–12 percent reduction in behavioral health *arrivals*.

### 4.4 Pediatric Behavioral Health Observation Protocol

A standardized protocol deployed at all fourteen sites: designated low-stimulation observation space, structured hourly reassessment, a defined activity and de-escalation package, family engagement protocol, mandatory 24-hour psychiatry review, and explicit criteria for discharge with rapid follow-up rather than default admission.

*Cost:* $620,000 capital (space modification across fourteen sites); $890,000 annual operating (training, protocol nursing hours, care coordination).
*Reach:* Complete.
*Speed:* 5–8 months. Fastest of the five.
*Boarding impact:* Modest reduction in boarding *hours* — modeled 9–13 percent — but the largest per-dollar effect on restraint and seclusion. Modeled 45–55 percent reduction in restraint events among boarded patients. This intervention primarily changes the harm profile of waiting rather than its duration.

### 4.5 Bridge Clinics with 72-Hour Follow-Up

Four bridge clinics providing guaranteed appointments within 72 hours of emergency discharge, staffed by psychiatric nurse practitioners and therapists, offering medication management, brief intervention, safety planning, and warm handoff to ongoing care over a six-to-eight-week bridge period.

*Cost:* $410,000 capital; $1.62 million annual operating.
*Reach:* Four sites with telehealth extension to all fourteen; effectively complete.
*Speed:* 9–12 months.
*Boarding impact:* Indirect but structurally important. Bridge clinics make discharge decisions defensible — the reason clinicians admit borderline cases is that there is nothing to discharge to. Modeled 11–16 percent reduction in boarding hours through increased discharge confidence, plus a 21–27 percent reduction in 30-day behavioral health return visits.

**Table 5. Comparison of five interventions on cost, reach, speed, and modeled boarding impact**

| Intervention | Capital | Annual operating | Cost per case served | Site reach | Months to effect | Modeled boarding-hour reduction | Primary mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Psychiatric emergency unit (12 bay) | $6,800,000 | $4,200,000 | $3,180 | 14 of 14 (transport required from 5) | 26–32 | 42–48% | Queue exit |
| Telepsychiatry consultation | $340,000 | $1,150,000 | $435 | 14 of 14 | 7–10 | 14–19% | Disposition accuracy |

The comparison makes two things clear. The psychiatric emergency unit has by far the largest single effect and by far the largest cost. The lower-cost interventions have real but partial effects, and their mechanisms are complementary rather than redundant — telepsychiatry improves the accuracy of the disposition decision, bridge clinics make the discharge option credible, observation protocol reduces harm while waiting, mobile crisis prevents some waiting from beginning. None of them creates a place for a child who genuinely requires 48 hours of secure psychiatric stabilization to go. Only the PES does that.

This figure excludes several real costs we could not quantify defensibly: staff turnover attributable to moral distress in boarding-heavy units, litigation and regulatory exposure from restraint events, family lost wages during multi-day boarding, and downstream cost of clinical deterioration during the boarding interval. Because the interventions overlap, effects are not additive. We modeled the portfolio using a discrete-event simulation of our 2025 encounter population, applying each intervention's mechanism sequentially in the order a patient would encounter it: mobile crisis diversion, then telepsychiatry-informed disposition, then observation protocol, then discharge to bridge clinic or transfer to PES.

But those 15 points consist almost entirely of the long tail — the children boarding more than 72 hours, who generate 54 percent of boarding hours and the overwhelming majority of restraint events. The marginal boarding hour eliminated by the PES is a far more harmful hour than the marginal hour eliminated by telepsychiatry.

## 6. Objections and Responses

### 6.1 "Rural travel distance makes a centralized unit useless to half your catchment."

This objection is partly correct and must be answered honestly rather than argued away. A twelve-bay unit in Mankato is 170 road miles from our westernmost emergency department. For a family in that county, a psychiatric emergency unit in Mankato is not a local resource.

Three responses. First, the relevant comparison is not PES-versus-local-care; it is PES-versus-current-practice. Currently, 35 percent of our accepted transfers already go out of region, at a median 96 road miles and a ninetieth percentile of 187 miles. A Mankato PES is *closer* than the status quo destination for the substantial majority of our transferred children, including most western-site children. We would be shortening travel, not lengthening it.

Second, the portfolio is structured precisely to keep distance-sensitive care local. Telepsychiatry, observation protocol, and bridge clinic telehealth reach all fourteen sites at full strength. Mobile crisis covers roughly 70 percent of catchment population. The PES is the fourth line of response, reserved for children who require secure stabilization — for whom, today, the alternative is not local care but four days in a windowless bay followed by a 187-mile ambulance ride.

Third, distance imposes real family burden, and the model funds mitigation: a family travel and lodging fund at $124,000 annually, structured daily video family sessions, and a discharge protocol requiring warm handoff to a bridge clinic within the family's home county before the child leaves Mankato.

### 6.2 "You will not be able to recruit child psychiatrists."

This is the most serious operational risk in the proposal. Child and adolescent psychiatry is among the most severely undersupplied subspecialties in the country, and rural Minnesota competes poorly against metropolitan academic centers. We do not claim this is easy. We claim it is achievable, on the following grounds.

The 2.8 FTE requirement for the PES is modest, and the model does not assume all of it is recruited into new positions. Current state includes 1.4 FTE of child psychiatry time consumed by telephone consultation, transfer negotiation, and remote boarding oversight — work that produces almost no therapeutic value and that our psychiatrists uniformly describe as the least sustainable part of their practice. Consolidating that time into an organized clinical service recovers roughly half the requirement.

The remaining 1.4 FTE is addressed through four channels: a hybrid model in which two positions carry 40 percent remote responsibility, permitting metropolitan residence; a two-year fellowship pipeline agreement with an in-state training program, with a service commitment; loan repayment participation under state and federal rural health programs, worth up to $105,000 per recruit; and — the argument we weight most heavily — the observation that a purpose-built psychiatric emergency unit is a substantially more attractive practice environment than the current one. Our exit interviews over five years identify boarding-related moral distress as the leading stated reason for behavioral health clinician departure from Cedarbrook. The recruitment problem and the boarding problem are the same problem. Building the unit is part of solving the staffing constraint, not merely a demand upon it.

Contingency: if 2.8 FTE cannot be secured by month 24, the unit opens with eight of twelve bays and expands as coverage permits. The model is not fragile to a phased opening.

### 6.3 "Medicaid rates will not support this."

Fifty-eight percent of our pediatric behavioral health volume is Medical Assistance. At current rates, the PES operates at a small loss on service revenue alone (Table 6), and the portfolio's neutrality depends on crediting avoided cost. Rates matter enormously, and we make three specific requests of state officials rather than a general complaint.

First, establish a distinct payment category for pediatric psychiatric emergency and crisis stabilization services, separate from both emergency department facility fees and inpatient psychiatric per diems. The current structure forces a 27-hour crisis stabilization stay to bill either as an emergency visit, which underpays by roughly 70 percent, or as an inpatient admission, which is inaccurate and invites audit exposure. This is an alignment problem before it is a rate level problem.

Second, recognize that Medicaid is currently paying for boarding. Every one of our 26,240 boarding hours in 2025 was paid for, largely by Medical Assistance, in the form of emergency department facility charges, professional fees, and ambulance transport across long distances. The state is not choosing between spending money and not spending money. It is choosing between purchasing 31 hours of non-therapeutic confinement or purchasing 27 hours of psychiatric stabilization. Our modeling suggests the second is cheaper per episode.

Third, permit mobile crisis and bridge clinic services to bill without requiring a prior emergency department encounter. Current authorization patterns effectively require a child to reach an emergency department before diversion services become reimbursable — a structural incentive that produces exactly the volume everyone wishes to reduce.

### 6.4 "Upstream investment does not actually move emergency volume."

This is a legitimate empirical concern with a mixed evidence base. Some crisis-diversion programs have shown minimal effect on emergency volume, and we do not dismiss those findings.

Our position is more limited than the general claim that upstream investment reduces volume. We model an 8 to 12 percent reduction in *arrivals* from mobile crisis, which is modest and consistent with the more conservative published estimates. The large effects in our model come not from volume reduction but from **length-of-stay reduction and disposition change** — mechanisms that operate on children who have already arrived, and that therefore do not depend on the contested diversion hypothesis at all. Telepsychiatry does not reduce arrivals; it shortens the interval to a correct disposition. Bridge clinics do not reduce arrivals; they make discharge a defensible option. The PES does not reduce arrivals; it provides a destination.

We would additionally observe that "does upstream investment reduce volume" is the wrong question for this decision. Even if arrivals were perfectly inelastic, boarding 786 children a year for a median of 34 hours in unsuitable space, restraining 9.4 percent of them, and consuming 4.2 treatment bays daily would remain unacceptable. The case for building appropriate crisis capacity does not rest on demand suppression. It rests on the proposition that children in psychiatric crisis should be cared for in a place designed to care for them.

Finally, we commit to falsifiability. The implementation plan specifies pre-registered metrics — boarding hours, restraint rate, 30-day return rate, transfer decline rate, cost per episode — reported publicly at six-month intervals, with defined thresholds at month 30 below which Stage 4 scope is reduced. We are asking for capital on the strength of a model. We accept the obligation to publish whether the model was right.

---

## 7. Recommendations

**7.1** Approve immediate Stage 1 implementation — telepsychiatry consultation and standardized pediatric behavioral health observation protocol at all fourteen emergency departments. $960,000 capital, $2,040,000 annual operating, operational effect within ten months. This stage is fundable within existing capital authority and requires no external decision.

**7.2** Submit a $4.2 million application to the state crisis services grant round closing September 2026, for capital support of the twelve-bay pediatric psychiatric emergency unit. Application preparation must begin no later than October 2026 to meet documentation requirements.

**7.3** Petition the Minnesota Department of Human Services and Wisconsin Department of Health Services to establish a distinct pediatric psychiatric emergency and crisis stabilization payment category, and to remove the prior-emergency-encounter requirement for mobile crisis and bridge clinic billing.

**7.4** Negotiate county participation agreements across nineteen counties covering $1.17 million in capital and $780,000 in annual crisis contract revenue, with mobile crisis team siting determined jointly with county human services directors.

**7.5** Open a formal capacity negotiation with the two regional inpatient facilities regarding the 44 percent decline rate, with the explicit objective of a written acceptance standard for children with autism spectrum diagnosis, intellectual disability, age under twelve, and child protection involvement.

**7.6** Establish public six-month reporting on the pre-registered metric set, with an independent review at month 30 empowered to recommend scope reduction.

## 8. Conclusion

Over five years our system held 3,128 children in emergency treatment bays for a total of 97,900 hours after the decision about their care had already been made. Five hundred sixty-three of them waited more than three days. Two hundred ninety-four were physically restrained or secluded during that wait. The proximate cause is not emergency department practice, staff commitment, or clinical judgment. It is that our region maintains 38 inpatient child psychiatric beds for 1.4 million people, and the facilities holding those beds decline nearly half our requests.

The interventions available to us differ in cost by more than an order of magnitude and in mechanism entirely. Telepsychiatry and a standardized observation protocol are fast, inexpensive, reach every site, and should be implemented without further deliberation. Bridge clinics and mobile crisis capacity are the necessary middle layer, making discharge credible and preventing some crises from reaching an emergency department at all. None of these creates a place for a child who genuinely needs two days of secure psychiatric stabilization. Only a dedicated psychiatric emergency unit does that, and it is the intervention that reaches the long tail where the harm concentrates.

The full portfolio costs $10.07 million in capital and $11.21 million annually, and it is approximately cost-neutral once avoided boarding cost and recovered emergency capacity are credited honestly. That neutrality is not an accident of favorable assumptions; it reflects that we are already paying for this problem, at $4.03 million a year, in a form that generates no therapeutic value and inflicts measurable harm. The question before hospital executives, state officials, payers, counties, and legislators is not whether to spend money on pediatric behavioral health crisis care. We are spending it. The question is whether to keep spending it on waiting.

A state grant round closes in September 2026. The children in Table 2 are waiting now.
