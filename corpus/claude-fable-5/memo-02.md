The four major findings are as follows. Major 1 concerns first article inspection. The auditors pulled six FAI packages against AS9102 requirements and found three with incomplete characteristic accountability — ballooned drawings that did not capture every characteristic, and Form 3 entries signed off without supporting measurement data. One package referenced a drawing revision two levels behind the purchase order. Major 2 concerns control of nonconforming material. The audit team found parts in the MRB cage without tags, found two dispositions of "use as is" signed by a manufacturing supervisor rather than by engineering authority as our own procedure requires, and found no evidence that Meridian was notified of a nonconformance affecting a fracture-critical characteristic, which their quality clauses require without exception. Major 3 concerns calibration. Fourteen gauges on the floor were past their calibration due dates, including two bore gauges in active use at the honing cell, and the CMM in the final inspection room had a calibration sticker that did not match the record in the calibration database. Major 4 concerns supplier control, and it is the one that will demand the most from us: the auditors found that our purchase orders to Ashuelot Precision Machining, our five-axis roughing subcontractor, did not flow down Meridian's quality clauses, that we had no record of a quality system survey of Ashuelot more recent than 2021, and that we performed no source inspection or receiving verification beyond a visual check and a count on parts returning from their shop.

The seven minor findings cover training records that could not be produced for three operators performing stamped inspection operations, gaps in the FOD program including uncontrolled personal tools at two workcenters, shop travelers with white-out corrections rather than single-line-strikethrough revisions, an internal audit schedule that shows two process audits skipped in 2025 without documented rescheduling, incomplete stamp control records, packaging and preservation deficiencies in finished goods, and obsolete work instructions posted at the deburr bench. None of these is difficult to fix individually. Collectively they told the auditors that our quality system is drifting, and I do not disagree with that reading.

Here is how we will run the sixty days. I have assigned a corrective action owner to each finding; the assignment matrix is attached. Every owner will submit a containment statement to me by December 5 — what we have done immediately to stop the bleeding. Containment on the calibration finding is already complete: all fourteen overdue gauges were pulled from the floor on November 17, and product measured with the two bore gauges since their due dates is being re-verified. Containment on the MRB finding is in work; Eli has agreed to a full physical inventory and re-tagging of the cage by December 12. I will not accept "operator error" or "training deficiency" as a root cause for a major finding. If our system allowed the error, the system is the root cause, and the corrective action must change the system.

Two of the findings deserve a comment about what is coming next. Major 4, supplier control, has been overtaken by events: Ashuelot filed for Chapter 11 protection on November 28, and the company is evaluating bringing five-axis roughing in-house. You will hear more on that from Hana Ishikawa-Brennan shortly. For corrective action purposes, our response to Major 4 will address the systemic failure — flow-down, surveys, receiving verification — for the entire approved supplier list, not just Ashuelot, because Meridian will verify the fix at other suppliers. Major 1, first article inspection, will require us to re-baseline the FAI packages the auditors sampled and to audit a statistically meaningful sample of the rest. I have asked Manufacturing Engineering to support that effort with two engineers through mid-January; Hana has approved.

Verification is the step where corrective action plans die, so I am building it in from the start. Each closure package must contain the 8D or written corrective action, the revised procedure or system change, evidence of training to the change, and evidence from the floor — an internal audit sample — showing the change is effective in practice. **SUBJECT:** Second Shift Staffing Plan and Machinist Wage Structure Adjustment

This memo lays out the plan to staff the 28-person second shift required by Program Granite and the wage action I am recommending to make that staffing achievable and durable. The two are inseparable, and I want to state my central conclusion at the outset: we cannot hire and hold 28 machinists at our current wage scale, in this labor market, on any timeline that supports the program. Sullivan County unemployment is 2.1 percent. Our machinist wage scale sits an average of $3.40 per hour below the Springfield-area market based on the December survey data, wider at the top grades where the five-axis talent lives. And our own machinist turnover ran 27 percent last year, which means that even before we hire a single second-shift employee, we are replacing roughly one machinist in four annually just to stand still on first shift. A recruiting campaign layered on top of that arithmetic, without fixing the arithmetic, would be an expensive way to fail.

The staffing requirement is 28 positions: 18 CNC machinists across grades, including at least four with five-axis experience or demonstrable aptitude for our internal upgrade path; four quality inspectors for the new inspection cell, coordinated with Rasheed Mansour's requirements; two maintenance technicians; two material handlers; one shift supervisor; and one manufacturing engineer assigned to second shift for the first year of operation. The hiring timeline works backward from machine commissioning in the fourth quarter of 2026. To have a trained shift ready for production runoff, the supervisor and the first cohort of six machinists must be on board by June 1, training on first shift on existing equipment; the second cohort of ten by August 1; and the balance by October 1. That means offers going out beginning in March, which means requisitions posted and the recruiting engine running by mid-February, which is why this memo needs a decision by the end of January.

The wage recommendation has four parts. First, a market adjustment of the machinist wage structure averaging $3.40 per hour, applied across the grades with the largest increases at the top grades where our gap is widest and our turnover is worst; the grade-by-grade schedule is attached. This applies to incumbent first-shift machinists as well as new hires, effective with the first pay period in March. I want to be direct about why the incumbents must be included: if we hire second-shift machinists at market while first shift remains $3.40 under market, we will convert a hiring problem into a mass resignation, and we will teach our most experienced people that the only way to get a raise at Kearsarge is to quit and get rehired. Second, a second-shift differential of 12 percent, which is at the top of the regional range, because second shift is inherently harder to fill and because the program cannot tolerate a shift that staffs up and then bleeds out. Third, a retention structure for the incumbent workforce: a step-progression system tied to demonstrated skills — setups, offsets, five-axis operation, in-process inspection — replacing the current opaque merit process, so that a machinist can see the path from where they are to the top of the scale and knows exactly what it requires. Fourth, a referral bonus of $2,500 for machinist hires who complete six months, because in a market this tight our best recruiting channel is our own workforce, and they will not use it while they feel underpaid.

The annual cost of the full wage action, incumbents and new hires together, is approximately $1.05 million, and it is included in the $2.1 million of labor cost that Gail carried in the revised capital request pre-read, so the board case already reflects it. What the board case cannot show as a line item is the cost of not doing it: 27 percent turnover means we spent last year training people for other shops. Our exit interview data is consistent and blunt — of the nineteen machinists who left in 2025, thirteen cited pay, and eleven of those went to identifiable competitors within commuting distance. Every one of them took qualification history and tribal knowledge on our part families out the door.

Recruiting will run on three channels beyond conventional posting. First, River Valley Community College's machine tool program, where I am negotiating a sponsored-seat arrangement — we fund tuition for candidates who commit to two years of employment, giving us a pipeline of entry-grade machinists we grow into the upper grades through the step system. This does not solve the June cohort, but it solves 2027 and 2028. Second, the Ashuelot workforce. That company's bankruptcy has put skilled five-axis machinists — people who have been roughing our parts for years and know our materials — into an uncertain situation twenty minutes from our door. We have handled this carefully with counsel to avoid any interference issues, but Ashuelot has now issued WARN notices, and those employees are fair candidates. I have already had preliminary conversations with four, including two of their senior five-axis operators. Hiring even five or six Ashuelot machinists would materially de-risk the June and August cohorts and would bring direct knowledge of our part numbers in-house. Third, a relocation allowance of up to $5,000 for the scarce top-grade five-axis roles, because the honest truth is that some of that talent will have to come from outside commuting range.

The risks I am tracking: the wage action may still prove insufficient at the top grades, and I have flagged a contingency of targeted sign-on bonuses if March and April offer-acceptance rates on senior roles fall below 60 percent. Compression between the adjusted machinist scale and first-line supervision must be addressed; a modest supervisor adjustment is included in the attached schedule. And the June cohort's training load will fall on our current first-shift leads, so Eli and I will select and compensate designated trainers rather than letting the burden land informally.
