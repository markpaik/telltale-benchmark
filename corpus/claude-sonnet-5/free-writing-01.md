# The Weight of Small Rooms

My grandmother kept a drawer of rubber bands that had once held vegetables together—thin red ones from the bunches of scallions, thick tan ones from broccoli crowns—and she would not throw them away. Waste not, she said, though I never saw her use a single one. They accumulated like sediment, like she was building a geological record of every meal that had passed through that kitchen, and maybe she was.

I think about that drawer more than I think about most conversations I've had with actual people. There is something in the hoarding of small, useless things that tells you everything about a person's relationship to time—the belief, maybe superstitious, that nothing should be allowed to simply end. That the scallion becomes soup becomes memory, but the rubber band remains, stubborn evidence that a thing occurred.

I am trying to write about her without writing about her death, which is its own kind of impossible, because the drawer is only interesting to me now because she is gone and the drawer is not. Someone—my mother, probably—eventually emptied it into the trash, and I wanted to ask her not to, though I couldn't have said why. It wasn't sentiment exactly. It was closer to a fear that once the last rubber band was gone, some final proof would go with it: that a woman had stood in that kitchen for forty years, tying nothing to nothing, refusing to let the ordinary disappear without witness.

This is the trouble with small rooms in memory—the pantry, the sewing box, the coffee can of mismatched buttons. They outlast the people who filled them, and then they outlast their own meaning, and then someone like me tries to put them into sentences as if that were a way of keeping the drawer shut, the rubber bands coiled, the scallions forever bundled and fresh and waiting to be cut.

It isn't. But I keep trying anyway. That, too, might be a kind of waste not.
