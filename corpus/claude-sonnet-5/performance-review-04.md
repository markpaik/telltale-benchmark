# Wanda Chee

**Title:** Lead Press Operator, Press 3, Second Shift
**Review Period:** August 1, 2025 – July 31, 2026
**Reviewer:** Hector Salgado-Rey, Second Shift Superintendent

## Goals and Results

Wanda's goals for this cycle were set at the start of the fiscal year around three targets: hold Press 3 OEE at or above the plant goal of 72 percent, complete cross-training of at least two additional operators, and serve as a technical resource during the corporate reorganization that was already being discussed for the maintenance organization.

She exceeded all three. Press 3 finished the period at 81 percent OEE, the highest sustained mark on the floor and nine points clear of plant target, in a year when two additional presses were added to her area of responsibility as part of the maintenance reorganization — a change that increased her span of oversight without increasing her base pay grade or adding a second lead. She trained six operators to full qualification over the period, three more than her goal, including two of the four operators now running the presses that were folded into her scope. In April, she identified a die-temperature drift on Press 3 before it produced out-of-spec extrusions, stopping what maintenance engineering estimated would have been a $40,000 scrap run had it gone undetected through a full shift. That catch is now used as a training example in the die-set orientation packet.

Wanda interviewed for the maintenance supervisor opening that was posted in the spring. Corporate eliminated the position in the same reorganization cycle before a hire was made, and the two presses that would have reported through that role were reassigned to her shift lead responsibilities instead. I want this on the record plainly: she did the work of preparing for a supervisory role, performed well in the interview process by every account I have from the panel, and the position she was competing for no longer exists. That is a structural decision made above plant level, not a reflection on her candidacy.

## Safety, Quality, and Attendance Record

No recordable injuries, no near-miss reports, and no lockout/tagout deviations attributed to Wanda or her crew this period. Her attendance is full — no points on file. Scrap on Press 3 ran below the plant's 4.2 percent average for the period and below the 2.5 percent target in ten of twelve months; the two months she exceeded target correspond to the weeks she was absorbing the two additional presses and retraining crews on them, which I do not count against her.

## Customer Audit and Peer Feedback

Press 3 was not implicated in the failed June parts submission that triggered the current customer corrective action; the affected lot ran on a different press on a different shift. During the customer's follow-up audit walk, the quality manager specifically directed the auditors to Press 3's die-temperature logging practice as an example of the kind of operator-level control the corrective action plan is trying to institutionalize plant-wide. That is not a small thing given where the plant stands with this customer.

Peer feedback is consistently strong. Two of the operators she trained this year, now qualified independently, cited her by name in their own onboarding surveys as the reason they reached qualification ahead of schedule. The maintenance planner who worked the April die-temperature catch with her described her judgment as "the kind of thing you can't put in a procedure."

## Employee Self-Assessment

> "I've run Press 3 for nineteen years and I know what it sounds like and smells like when something's about to go wrong before the gauges catch it. This year I trained six people and took on two more presses with no extra help, and I did the interview for maintenance supervisor because I thought it made sense to put that knowledge somewhere official. I understand the position went away — that's not on Hector or on the plant floor, I know that's a corporate call. I'd like it noted that I did the interview in good faith and I'm still doing the work either way. What I'd ask for going into next year is some clarity on what the expanded scope means for my role on paper, because right now it's the same title covering three presses instead of one."

## Manager's Overall Assessment

Wanda is the strongest individual performer on my shift and one of the strongest in the plant by any measure I can point to — OEE, scrap, training output, and judgment under pressure. The reorganization asked more of her than it gave her, and I've said so to the plant manager directly. Her self-assessment is accurate and restrained; if anything she understates the value of the die-temperature catch, which protected not just $40,000 in scrap but goodwill with a customer relationship we are actively trying to repair.

Rick's goals for the year, set before the events described below, centered on reducing unplanned downtime across his assigned presses and supporting the plant's response to quality issues flagged in the corrective action process. That fix is one of the concrete items the plant is pointing to when we tell the customer we have addressed root cause rather than symptoms.

## Safety, Quality, and Attendance Record

I have to be plain here because the form requires it and because it matters. In February, Rick sustained a recordable hand injury tied to a skipped lockout step during a press changeover. A performance improvement plan was opened the same month, addressing both the safety procedure violation and a separate attendance issue — he carries eleven points against a twelve-point ceiling under the plant's attendance policy, one point from involuntary separation under that policy as it currently stands.

Since the plan opened, there have been no further safety incidents and no additional attendance points logged. The performance plan review meetings have been on schedule, and Rick has completed the required lockout/tagout retraining and recertification. The downtime reduction described above occurred entirely within this same window, which tells me the technical performance was never the issue — the lockout violation and the attendance record are.

## Customer Audit and Peer Feedback

The quality manager's written note on the extrusion defect fix, which I am attaching to this file, states that Rick's diagnosis "found the actual mechanical cause of the die-line defect the customer's auditor called out, not just a workaround — this is the kind of fix that holds." That fix is now cited in the plant's written response to the customer corrective action as evidence of engineering root-cause correction. I want to be clear that this carries real weight given what's riding on that relationship — 22 million dollars a year in business and a plant-wide corrective action with the customer watching our follow-through.

Peer feedback from operators on his presses is positive and consistent — several specifically noted he explains what he's doing and why when he's on their line, which matters for operator-level ownership of equipment condition.

## Employee Self-Assessment

> "I'm not going to make excuses for the lockout step. I skipped it because I was trying to keep a press from going down further into the shift and I made the wrong call, and I got hurt because of it, and it could've been worse. The attendance thing is mostly a family situation from the winter that's resolved now; I know I'm close to the line and I'm not asking for a pass on that, I'm just telling you why it happened. On the mechanical side, I think the numbers speak for themselves — downtime's down by a third and the die-line fix on the corrective action was real work, not luck. I want to finish the plan clean and I want that to be what people remember about this year, not the injury."

Rick's technical contribution this year was significant and arrived at a moment the plant needed it. I am not going to let that erase the seriousness of a recordable injury tied to a procedural violation, or the attendance record sitting one point from the ceiling. Both of those are real and both stay in this file. What I can say is that his response since February has been what I want to see from someone on a performance plan — no repeat violations, no further points, full engagement with retraining, and continued strong output. ## Appendix A: Quality Manager Memorandum — Referenced in Rick Dombrowski Review

**To:** Hector Salgado-Rey, Second Shift Superintendent
**From:** Plant Quality Manager
**Re:** Root-cause correction, extrusion die-line defect — customer corrective action item CA-2026-04
**Date:** June 18, 2026

This memo documents the mechanical corrective action taken by Rick Dombrowski on the die-line defect identified during the customer audit following the June parts submission failure at our Phoenix integrator account.

The audit team's finding traced intermittent die-line marking on extruded profiles to inconsistent bearing pressure at the die face, initially suspected to be a die-wear issue requiring replacement tooling — a fix that would have taken the affected press out of service for an estimated 60 hours and required expedited die procurement. Rick's diagnosis identified the actual cause as a worn alignment bushing in the press's die-holder assembly, a $180 part rather than a full die replacement, and one not on the standard preventive maintenance interval for that press model. The fix was implemented within a single shift with no additional production loss beyond the repair window.

This is the kind of finding that holds up under a follow-up audit because it addresses the mechanical cause rather than masking the symptom. I am recommending this fix be cited specifically in the plant's written response to the customer's corrective action request, and that the alignment bushing be added to the preventive maintenance schedule for all four presses on this line.

I want this documented in Rick's file directly, separate from whatever else is in it. This is good work and it mattered to an account we cannot afford to lose.
