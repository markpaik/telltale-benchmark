## 1. Executive Summary

Fairmount Software Group enters the second half of fiscal 2026 with $146 million in annual recurring revenue across six vertical software businesses, up from $131 million at the same point last year, with roughly $12 million of that growth attributable to the VetLedger acquisition completed in November 2025 and the balance to organic expansion at StoreVault, CivicDesk, and DentalPath. The portfolio remains cash-generative and diversified across end markets that do not share a common demand cycle, which continues to be the structural argument for the holding company model.

The headline financial fact for this review is covenant proximity. We closed the second quarter at 4.61 times net leverage against a 4.75 times maximum under the credit agreement. That is compliant, but the cushion is thin — approximately $1.9 million of trailing adjusted EBITDA at current net debt — and three of the decisions in front of the board this half interact directly with that cushion: the $4.2 million Everlight platform rewrite, the pace of remediation spending on the VetLedger billing migration, and the services investment required to work down the CivicDesk implementation backlog. This report treats leverage headroom as the binding constraint on capital allocation for the remainder of the fiscal year and evaluates every business unit decision against it.

The portfolio itself presents a familiar mixed picture. Two businesses are performing above plan: StoreVault grew 11 percent with an accelerating payments attach story, and VetLedger grew 27 percent even while its integration ran behind schedule. Two are performing to plan with identifiable problems: DentalPath is growing steadily but retention has slipped below where a business of its maturity should sit, and CivicDesk is growing fast but at negative services margin and with an implementation backlog long enough to threaten the bookings that drive the growth. Two are underperforming: RxCounter is flat and losing customers to a competitor with a structurally different offering, and Everlight is shrinking, has lost its founder and part of its engineering team, and sits on a technology platform whose vendor support ends in March 2027.

The two decisions requiring board direction at the September meeting are the Everlight rewrite (management recommends proceeding, phased, with the funding mechanics described in Section 9) and the RxCounter strategic review (management recommends initiating a formal evaluation of partnership and divestiture options alongside the product response, described in Section 4).

Our second-half outlook calls for exiting fiscal 2026 at $153 to $156 million of ARR and net leverage between 4.35 and 4.50 times, assuming the VetLedger billing migration completes by October and no further deterioration at Everlight or RxCounter. The path to that outcome, and the risks to it, are laid out in Section 11.

### 3.1 Results

|---|---:|---:|---:|
| ARR | $38.0M | $35.8M | +6.2% |

| Customer count (practices) | 6,850 | 6,610 | +3.6% |

DentalPath remains the portfolio's largest business and its most reliable cash contributor, generating roughly $18 million of annualized EBITDA. New logo bookings were on plan, with mid-size group practices (five to twenty locations) now representing 38 percent of new bookings, up from 29 percent a year ago.

### 3.2 The Problem: Retention Erosion

Gross retention of 91 percent is the weakest DentalPath has posted since we acquired the business in 2020, when it ran at 94 percent. The two-point decline is concentrated, not diffuse: churn analysis shows that 61 percent of lost ARR in the trailing twelve months came from single-location practices acquired by dental service organizations (DSOs) that standardize on an enterprise platform, typically Dentrix Ascend or a DSO's proprietary stack. This is consolidation churn — the customer is not dissatisfied; the customer ceases to exist as an independent buyer.

Consolidation churn cannot be fully defended, but it can be partially converted. Aaron Kleinfeld's roadmap response is a multi-location administration layer — consolidated reporting, centralized fee schedules, cross-location scheduling — shipping in two releases, October 2026 and February 2027, intended to make DentalPath a credible retention play when a customer sells into a small or mid-size DSO rather than an automatic rip-out. The engineering cost is absorbed within DentalPath's existing R&D envelope. We have also stood up a DSO account team of two people whose job is to establish commercial relationships with the fifteen regional DSOs most active in our customer base, so that when they acquire a DentalPath practice the default conversation is migration onto a DentalPath group agreement rather than off the platform entirely. Early evidence is modest but real: two regional DSOs signed group agreements in Q2 covering 41 locations, 26 of which were existing customers we would otherwise have expected to lose.

Management's view is that 91 percent is the trough if the group-practice strategy executes, with a return to 93 percent achievable by the end of fiscal 2027. The board should treat DentalPath retention as a standing agenda metric until that recovery is demonstrated.

## 4. RxCounter — Pharmacy Point-of-Sale

### 4.1 Results

| Metric | H1 FY26 | H1 FY25 | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| ARR | $27.0M | $27.0M | 0% |
| Gross retention | 89% | 94% | (5 pts) |
| Customer count (pharmacies) | 4,100 | 4,440 | (7.7%) |
| Adjusted EBITDA margin | 51% | 52% | (1 pt) |

RxCounter's flat ARR conceals a deteriorating unit picture: the business lost 340 of 4,440 pharmacy customers over the trailing twelve months and held revenue only through price increases averaging 8 percent on the remaining base. Pricing into churn is a strategy with a short useful life.

### 4.2 The Problem: A Structural Competitive Disadvantage

The customer losses are overwhelmingly to a single competitor, whose product bundles point-of-sale with wholesaler ordering — the pharmacy orders inventory from its wholesaler inside the same system that rings the register, with the wholesaler subsidizing the software cost as a customer-acquisition and lock-in expense. Exit interviews with 55 of the 340 lost customers found that 47 cited the ordering integration as the primary reason for switching. This is not a feature gap we are losing on; it is a business-model gap. The competitor's economics are underwritten by wholesaler distribution margin, which RxCounter does not participate in and cannot replicate through software pricing alone.

Management sees three options, which are not mutually exclusive in sequence:

**Option one: build a wholesaler partnership.** Approach the second- and third-tier wholesalers — who are losing independent pharmacy volume to the same bundled competitor and its wholesaler partner — and offer RxCounter as their integrated ordering front-end. Aaron Kleinfeld's team estimates the ordering integration at nine months and $1.1 million of development if a wholesaler partner commits to API access and co-funding. This is the only path that neutralizes the competitive dynamic rather than managing decline. We have had two exploratory conversations with regional wholesalers; one has expressed serious interest and requested a technical scoping session, now scheduled for September.

**Option two: harvest.** Cease growth investment, run the business for cash at its 51 percent margin, and accept 6 to 8 percent annual ARR decline. RxCounter would still generate roughly $12 to $13 million of annual EBITDA for several years. This is a legitimate answer for a business with a structural disadvantage, but it permanently converts 18.5 percent of portfolio ARR into a melting asset, with consequences for the multiple the lender group and any future capital partner applies to the whole.

**Option three: divest.** RxCounter's most logical buyers are the wholesalers themselves or the bundled competitor, for whom 4,100 pharmacy relationships have clear strategic value. At even a conservative multiple on its EBITDA, proceeds would materially deleverage the balance sheet — a sale at 8 times EBITDA would generate roughly $100 million and, applied to debt, take net leverage to approximately 3.9 times, resolving the covenant question outright at the cost of the portfolio's third-largest business.

**Management recommendation:** pursue option one on a strict clock — a signed wholesaler partnership term sheet by January 31, 2027 — while running a quiet parallel evaluation of option three so that a divestiture process could launch within sixty days if the partnership path fails. We are not recommending harvest as a standalone strategy. We request board authorization at the September meeting to engage an advisor for the parallel evaluation.

## 5. CivicDesk — Municipal Permitting

### 5.1 Results

| Metric | H1 FY26 | H1 FY25 | Change |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| ARR | $22.0M | $18.5M | +18.9% |
| Gross retention | 97% | 97% | — |
| New bookings (annualized) | $4.8M | $3.6M | +33% |
| Services margin | (4%) | +6% | (10 pts) |
| Implementation backlog | 14 months | 8 months | +6 months |

CivicDesk is the portfolio's organic growth engine. Municipal demand for permitting modernization remains strong, retention is effectively structural given how governments procure, and the business won 31 new municipalities in the half, including its two largest contracts to date.

### 5.2 The Problem: The Backlog Is Eating the Growth

The business is selling faster than it can deploy. The implementation backlog now stands at fourteen months — a municipality signing today goes live in the fall of 2027 — and the services organization ran at a negative 4 percent margin in the half because we surged expensive contractors into deployments while our fixed-fee implementation pricing, set two years ago, no longer covers loaded delivery cost.

The backlog is not merely a cost problem; it is a revenue-recognition and a competitive problem. Roughly $3.1 million of signed ARR is sitting in backlog not yet billing because contracts commence at go-live. And two prospective customers in Q2 cited deployment timeline as their reason for selecting a competitor — the first time CivicDesk has lost on speed rather than product. A fourteen-month backlog, left alone, converts today's bookings strength into next year's bookings weakness.

The remediation plan has three parts, jointly owned by the CivicDesk general manager and Aaron Kleinfeld:

1. **Repricing.** All new implementation contracts as of July 1 are priced at rates 22 percent higher than the prior card, with time-and-materials terms for scope beyond a defined baseline. This restores modeled services margin to positive 8 to 10 percent on new signings but does not fix the loss-making backlog already under contract, which we will work through at negative margin over the next twelve months. The remaining under-water backlog represents approximately $1.4 million of expected services losses through fiscal 2027, already reflected in our leverage forecast.
2. **Deployment productization.** The single largest driver of implementation hours is per-municipality configuration of permit types and workflow rules. Product is building a template library from the 40 most common permit configurations, targeted for November, which pilot data suggests can cut standard deployment hours by 30 to 35 percent. This is the durable fix.
3. **Capacity.** We are hiring six permanent implementation staff (replacing more expensive contractors, roughly cost-neutral in-year and accretive thereafter) and certifying two regional systems-integration partners to take overflow deployments on a revenue-share basis, with the first partner deployment beginning in October.

The target is a backlog of nine months or less and services margin at breakeven by fiscal year-end, with positive services margin in fiscal 2027. We considered and rejected the alternative of throttling sales; the bookings window in municipal modernization is open now, competitors are investing into it, and share taken today is retained for a decade at 97 percent retention.

StoreVault is the cleanest story in the portfolio and the model we intend the other businesses to follow. ### 6.2 The Problem: Concentration in the Consolidating Mid-Market

StoreVault's problem is a quieter version of DentalPath's. The self-storage industry is consolidating, and StoreVault's sweet spot — operators with three to thirty facilities — is precisely the segment being acquired by REITs and large private operators who run enterprise platforms. Our ten largest customers represent 19 percent of StoreVault ARR, and two of the ten received acquisition approaches in the half (neither has transacted). Unlike DentalPath, StoreVault has not yet felt this in the retention line — gross retention is steady at 95 percent — but the exposure is structural and we are treating it as a leading-indicator risk rather than waiting for it to appear in results. The response mirrors DentalPath's playbook run earlier in the cycle: an enterprise tier of the product (multi-facility revenue management and consolidated ownership reporting) ships in Q1 fiscal 2027, positioned to make StoreVault retainable through an acquisition event and, opportunistically, sellable to the mid-size acquirers themselves.

Everlight is the portfolio's problem child, and it has three problems that compound one another.

### 7.2 The Problems: Platform, People, and Momentum

**Platform.** Everlight runs on a commercial application framework whose vendor has announced end of support in March 2027 — nineteen months from acquisition of that knowledge, now seven months away. After end of support there are no security patches, which is untenable for a product holding decedent and family personal data, and which our customers' insurers and our own will eventually notice. A full rewrite onto Fairmount's standard stack is priced at $4.2 million over fourteen months. There is no credible patch-and-extend path; we investigated third-party extended support and the sole provider quoted terms that approach the rewrite cost over three years while leaving us on a dead platform. Section 9 treats the rewrite decision in full.

**People.** Founder Warren Sikes departed in April and took two of the five staff engineers with him. Sikes had run Everlight hands-on since we acquired it in 2021, and his departure removed both institutional product knowledge and the personal relationships that anchored several large customers. His non-solicitation and non-compete run through April 2027; we are monitoring compliance and have seen no evidence of violation to date, but the board should be aware that the non-compete's expiry falls one month after the framework's end of support — an uncomfortable alignment of dates if Sikes intends to re-enter the market. We have backfilled with three contractors, appointed DentalPath's former VP of engineering, Dana Okafor, as interim Everlight general manager, and are recruiting a permanent GM.

**Momentum.** The business shrank 3 percent, new bookings have nearly stopped — the sales team is understandably reluctant to sell a product with a publicly known platform deadline and no announced successor — and retention slipped three points as the two largest churned customers cited "uncertainty about the product's future." Uncertainty is now self-fulfilling: every quarter without a committed, communicated rewrite plan costs bookings and retention that make the rewrite economics worse.

Everlight remains meaningfully profitable — roughly $6.7 million of annualized EBITDA even in its degraded state — and funeral home management is a durable, non-cyclical niche with weak competition. The asset is worth saving. But it is worth saving only with a decision made now, which is the subject of Section 9.

VetLedger, acquired in November 2025 for $61 million (5.1 times ARR at close), is performing above the acquisition model on every commercial metric. Growth of 27 percent exceeds the 22 percent underwritten; retention improved a point; the sales team we inherited has stayed intact and beaten its bookings plan in both post-close quarters. As a business, VetLedger is validating the thesis. As an integration, it is behind.

### 8.2 The Problem: The Billing Migration Is Four Months Late

The acquisition plan called for migrating all customers from VetLedger's homegrown, founder-maintained invoicing process onto Fairmount's shared billing platform by June 2026. We will now complete in October — four months late — with 190 of 1,180 practices still invoiced by hand as of this writing, down from 410 at close.

The delay's root cause was a data-quality problem we underestimated in diligence: VetLedger's legacy contract records stored pricing as free-text notes rather than structured terms, and approximately 600 accounts required manual contract reconstruction before they could be loaded into the billing platform. The reconstruction is 85 percent complete. Consequences of the delay, quantified:

- **Cost:** approximately $0.7 million of annualized duplicative billing-operations expense (three temporary staff plus legacy system maintenance), added back under the credit agreement's integration-cost provision but real cash nonetheless. Full run-rate relief lands in Q1 fiscal 2027.
- **Cash timing:** manually invoiced accounts pay on average 19 days slower than platform-billed accounts, an estimated $0.4 million of working capital tied up at current volumes.
- **Revenue leakage:** the reconstruction uncovered approximately $210,000 of annualized under-billing on legacy accounts — services delivered but never invoiced — which we are recovering through corrected billing at renewal. This is a modest silver lining and a caution for future diligence.

The remaining 190 accounts are scheduled in three migration waves (August, September, October), each with a customer-communication sequence that Dana Okafor's team at DentalPath, which ran the same playbook in 2022, has reviewed. The dependency risk is the final wave, which contains the 60 most complex multi-site accounts. We rate on-time completion at high confidence for the first two waves and moderate for the third; a slip on the third wave costs roughly one additional month and $60,000, not a re-plan.

The broader integration is otherwise on schedule: financial consolidation completed in February, benefits and payroll harmonized in April, and the product remains on its own stack by design — VetLedger's platform is modern and requires no re-platforming. Second-half integration focus shifts from remediation to synergy: a payments attach program modeled directly on StoreVault's launches in Q4, with veterinary practices presenting a payments opportunity at least as rich as self-storage.

## 9. Decision: The Everlight Rewrite

### 9.1 The Decision Framework

The board is asked to decide, at the September meeting, whether to fund the $4.2 million Everlight rewrite. Management evaluated three options.

**Do nothing / harvest.** Run Everlight past the March 2027 support cliff, accept escalating security and insurance exposure, and manage decline. We reject this option outright. Operating a system holding families' personal and financial data on an unpatched framework is a liability posture no board should accept, and disclosure obligations to customers would accelerate churn well beyond the current 3 percent decline. Modeled outcome: ARR halves within three years and the terminal liability exposure is unquantifiable.

**Sell now.** Everlight at $16 million ARR and $6.7 million EBITDA would ordinarily command $45 to $55 million. But a buyer inherits the same platform cliff and the same rewrite bill, and will price accordingly; realistic proceeds are $28 to $35 million. Selling at a distressed price to avoid a $4.2 million investment that restores full value is poor capital allocation unless the covenant leaves us no choice. It does not, per below.

**Rewrite.** $4.2 million over fourteen months (September 2026 – November 2027), phased so that the highest-risk modules — the components with the deepest framework dependencies — migrate first, and the product runs in a supportable hybrid state by the March 2027 support deadline even though full completion comes eight months later. Aaron Kleinfeld's team has scoped the phasing with an external architecture review completed in July; the reviewer endorsed the plan and the estimate with a ±15 percent tolerance. The rewrite also unlocks the roadmap Everlight has been unable to ship — online family arrangement portals and payment plans, the features churned customers asked for — because no rational team invests features into a dying framework.

### 9.2 Covenant Mechanics

The rewrite's interaction with the leverage covenant is manageable and is the reason management can recommend proceeding despite $1.9 million of EBITDA headroom:

- Of the $4.2 million, approximately $3.4 million qualifies for capitalization as software development under the credit agreement's definitions, confirmed with our auditors in June. Only ~$0.8 million flows through EBITDA, spread over fourteen months — roughly $0.35 million of EBITDA impact within fiscal 2026.
- The cash cost is funded from operating cash flow within the existing plan; no revolver draw is required, so net debt is unaffected beyond ordinary cash seasonality.
- Modeled covenant impact: approximately 0.03 turns in fiscal 2026, against a second-half plan that builds headroom from other sources (Section 11).

### 9.3 Recommendation

**Management recommends the board approve the rewrite at $4.2 million with a 15 percent contingency ($4.8 million authorized ceiling), commencement September 2026.** We further recommend an immediate customer and prospect communication announcing the committed modernization plan — the momentum problem in Section 7.2 is being driven by silence, and the announcement itself is worth retention. Simone Baptiste concurs with the covenant treatment; Aaron Kleinfeld's organization will run the program with quarterly milestone reporting to the board, and the phase-one milestone (framework-critical modules migrated, due February 2027) is the go/no-go checkpoint ahead of the March support deadline.

The deleveraging path runs through three specific EBITDA bridges: VetLedger duplicative-cost elimination (+$0.7 million annualized), CivicDesk services margin recovery toward breakeven (+$0.9 million annualized versus H1 run rate), and normal operating leverage on organic growth (+$1.5 to $2.0 million), partially offset by the Everlight rewrite P&L component (−$0.35 million in-year) and DSO/enterprise go-to-market investments at DentalPath and StoreVault (−$0.6 million).

### 11.2 What Each Business Must Deliver

- **DentalPath:** hold gross retention at 91 percent or better; ship the October multi-location release; sign three additional DSO group agreements.
- **RxCounter:** wholesaler partnership term sheet by January 31, 2027, or the divestiture evaluation converts to a process; hold churn below 4 percent of units in the half.
- **CivicDesk:** backlog to eleven months or better by year-end (nine-month target lands early FY27); services margin at or near breakeven in Q4; ship the deployment template library in November.
- **StoreVault:** payments attach to 38 percent; tenant insurance live in Q4; enterprise tier on track for Q1 FY27.
- **Everlight:** rewrite commenced and phase-one on schedule; modernization announcement delivered to the customer base in September; permanent GM hired by year-end; stabilize retention at 90 percent.
- **VetLedger:** billing migration complete in October; payments program live in Q4; growth at or above 22 percent.

### 11.3 Risks to the Plan

Ranked by potential covenant impact:

1. **RxCounter accelerated churn.** If the competitor's bundling continues taking 7 to 8 percent of units annually and price cover is exhausted, RxCounter's H2 could turn negative, costing $0.5 to $1.0 million of EBITDA against plan. Mitigation: the January partnership deadline and the parallel divestiture readiness. This is the single largest swing factor in the portfolio.
2. **Everlight rewrite overrun or slip.** A phase-one miss against the March 2027 deadline forces expensive tactical mitigation. The 15 percent contingency and external architecture validation bound this risk; quarterly board milestones surface it early.
3. **CivicDesk backlog contracts.** If the repricing suppresses bookings more than modeled, growth moderates — an acceptable trade — but if the template productization slips, services losses persist into FY27 and the margin bridge in 11.1 loses its second-largest component.
4. **VetLedger wave-three slip.** Bounded at roughly one month and $60,000; a risk to precision, not to the plan.
5. **Covenant headroom during the transition quarter.** Q3 is the tightest modeled quarter (projected 4.55–4.60 times) before the H2 EBITDA bridges land in Q4. We do not project a breach in any modeled scenario, but a combination of risk one and risk three materializing simultaneously would compress headroom below $1 million of EBITDA. Simone Baptiste has held preliminary, informal conversations with the administrative agent regarding a covenant amendment framework as a contingency; we do not currently intend to request one, and disclosure here is in the interest of the no-surprises relationship this lender group has had from us since 2019.

### 11.4 No Acquisitions in the Second Half

For completeness: the M&A pipeline remains active and we continue to track eleven targets across current and adjacent verticals, but management has suspended acquisition activity for the balance of fiscal 2026. At 4.61 times leverage with a 4.50 times step-down approaching, the highest-return use of every incremental dollar is the organic investments and deleveraging described above. We expect to return to the board with acquisition capacity analysis in the fiscal 2027 planning cycle, by which point the plan-case balance sheet supports it.
