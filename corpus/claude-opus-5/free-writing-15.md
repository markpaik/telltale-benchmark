# The Locksmith's Apprentice

The old locksmith kept a drawer of keys that opened nothing.

Not blanks — blanks are honest, unfinished things, waiting to be told what they mean. These were cut keys. Finished. Teeth filed to precise and confident heights, each one the answer to a specific question. But the doors were gone. Buildings demolished, families dispersed, locks replaced after a burglary or a divorce or simply because the mechanism wore out and someone decided it was easier to start over.

"Why keep them," I asked, the first week.

He was working on a mortise lock, the kind with a body like a small iron book. He didn't look up.

"Where would I put them instead."

---

I had come to him because I was twenty-three and had failed at the thing I thought I was for, and locksmithing seemed like a trade where the problems were finite. A lock is a puzzle that someone already solved. There is a correct answer, and it exists, and it is made of brass.

This turned out to be about half true, which is a better ratio than most beliefs achieve.

He taught me the pin tumbler first, because everyone teaches the pin tumbler first. Five little stacks of metal, each stack cut at a different height, each one needing to be lifted exactly to the shear line — not more, not less. Lift them all at once and the cylinder turns. Lift four and fumble the fifth and you have accomplished nothing at all. There is no partial credit inside a lock.

"Feel for the binding pin," he said. "Only one is really holding. The others are just along for the ride."

I could not feel it. For three weeks I could not feel it. I raked and scrubbed and jiggled and produced only the sound of small metal objects being annoyed. He never took the tools from my hands. He would come stand beside me sometimes, close enough that I could smell the graphite on him, and say nothing, and go away again.

Then one Tuesday, in the middle of a lock I wasn't even trying on — I was thinking about lunch — a pin set. I felt it. A click that wasn't a sound, that traveled up the tension wrench into the meat of my thumb and announced itself as a fact about the world.

I said something unprintable.

"There it is," he said, from across the shop, not looking.

---

Here is what I learned, in the four years, that I did not expect to learn:

That locks are not about keeping people out. Any lock can be defeated by a person with enough time, and every lock can be defeated by a brick. Locks are about *raising the cost* — of entry, of trespass, of the casual violation. A lock is a statement that says: this will require you to become a certain kind of person. Most people don't want to become that person. That's the whole mechanism. The brass is incidental.

That there is a species of grief only locksmiths see. We are called after. After the death, when the son cannot find the key to the father's desk and will not say why he needs it opened tonight, at eleven, and pays cash. After the leaving, when a woman wants every cylinder in the house rekeyed and stands in the doorway watching, arms crossed, not because she doesn't trust me but because she needs to witness the change happen. After the mistake — the locked-out, the locked-in, the small humiliations of being outside your own life at two in the morning in a bathrobe.

We arrive at the threshold of the worst hour of someone's week and we are cheerful and we do not ask. That is the actual job. The brass is incidental.

That a good locksmith and a good thief have identical hands and different appointment books.

---

He died in the spring, which I resent, because it's the obvious season for it and he was not an obvious man.

The shop went to a nephew who sold the stock and the sign and the ancient key-cutting machine that had to be coaxed and cursed and could still cut truer than the new one. I got the tools, because he'd written it down, one line, in the handwriting of a man who did not believe in second drafts.

And I got the drawer.

I have it still. I am fifty-one years old and I have moved six times and I have thrown away furniture, photographs, an entire marriage's worth of shared objects, and I have never once considered leaving behind a drawer of keys that open nothing.

I take them out sometimes. There's one with a leather fob gone black and soft as an old dog's ear. One stamped DO NOT DUPLICATE, which is the most touching thing brass can say. One that's clearly a house key, worn thin at the shoulder from years of a thumb pressing it into a lock in the dark, a thumb that knew the exact angle, that never had to look.

Somebody came home with this key. Thousands of times. Tired, drunk, joyful, dreading it, carrying groceries, carrying a baby, carrying nothing. The door opened and there was the smell of their own house, which no one can describe and everyone would recognize in a coma.

And now the door is gone and the key is in a drawer in my apartment and I never knew them.

---

The apprentice I have now — she's better than I was, faster, felt the binding pin in nine days and had the decency to be embarrassed about it — asked me last month why I keep them.

I was working on a mortise lock. A body like a small iron book. I didn't look up.

I said: because a key is a piece of evidence. It's proof that a specific door existed, and that somebody was permitted through it. The lock is gone. The door is gone. The permission is the only part that's still made of metal.

She said that was very nice but it didn't really answer the question.

Which is fair. It doesn't. The honest answer is the one he gave me, and it took me thirty years to understand it wasn't a dodge.

*Where would I put them instead.*

There is no correct drawer for the keys to vanished doors. There is no bin marked ACCESS, REVOKED, SENTIMENTAL. You cannot throw them out, because they are not trash — trash is the thing that has no meaning, and these are pure surviving meaning with the meaning's object subtracted, which is the definition of a relic and possibly the definition of a memory. And you cannot use them. That's the entire point. They are useless in the specific, ruinous way that only a well-made thing can be useless.

So you keep the drawer. You add to it, over the years, as your own doors close behind you. And someday you hand it to somebody who will not understand, and who will keep it anyway, because you kept it, and because a drawer of keys is the closest thing to a soul that a trade can give you.

---

Last thing.

There's a thing we do — did, he did, I do it now — when we finish rekeying a house. Before we leave, we hand the customer the new key and we don't say *here's your key.* We say: **try it.**

And they do, standing in their own doorway, and it turns, and something in their shoulders comes down half an inch. Every time. Every single time, in thirty years, in four hundred doorways.

That's not the lock working. The lock was always going to work; I'm good at this. That's a person finding out they can still get in.

I'd cut a key for that feeling if I knew what door it was behind. I'd cut a hundred. I'd fill the drawer.
