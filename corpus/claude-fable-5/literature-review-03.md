# Unconditional Cash Transfers and Household Outcomes: A Review of the Evidence for the Alamo Basin Community Foundation

**Prepared by:** Tomas Iribarren, PhD, Senior Research Officer; Chidinma Okorafor, MA, Research Associate; Landon Yazzie, MPP, Program Officer

**Date:** August 9, 2026

**Status:** Final draft for board distribution

---

## 1. Introduction: The Question and Its Scope

The Alamo Basin Community Foundation's board is considering a six million dollar, three-year unconditional cash pilot serving 500 households in three San Antonio zip codes where median household income falls below $32,000. The proposal has drawn predictable and legitimate scrutiny. County Commissioner Odalys Fuentes-Nabors has declined to commit matching public funds absent evidence that unconditional transfers produce measurable improvements in household outcomes, and two donor-advised fund holders have signaled that they may relocate their accounts if the foundation proceeds. The board asked the research office a direct question: what does the evaluation literature on guaranteed income and unconditional cash pilots actually show, where does it disagree with itself, and what does it imply for a pilot of this scale in this community?

This review answers that question. It does not advocate for or against the pilot. It synthesizes findings from 63 studies of unconditional or near-unconditional cash transfer programs in high-income settings, published or released between 1997 and 2026, spanning the modern wave of American municipal pilots, the large multi-site randomized trials that concluded in 2023 and 2024, natural experiments such as the Alaska Permanent Fund dividend and tribal casino disbursements, the temporarily expanded federal Child Tax Credit, and older but still-informative experiments including Canada's Mincome and Finland's national basic income trial.

The honest headline is that the literature does not speak with one voice. Early city pilots — most prominently Stockton's SEED demonstration — reported gains in full-time employment and improvements in mental health (West & Castro, 2023). The largest and longest randomized trial to date, a three-year study of $1,000 monthly transfers, found the opposite direction on labor supply — a reduction of roughly 1.3 weekly work hours — and no detectable improvement across a battery of physical and mental health measures at three years (Vivalt, Pilkauskas, Broockman, Miller, & Rhodes, 2024; Miller, Rhodes, Bartik, Broockman, Krause, & Vivalt, 2024). Infant brain-development research generated striking early findings that subsequent measurement waves have not sustained (Troller-Renfree et al., 2022; Noble, Magnuson, Gennetian, et al., 2025). Meanwhile, housing stability effects appear steadier across studies than earnings effects, a pattern with direct relevance to the zip codes the foundation has identified.

These conflicts are not noise. They are largely traceable to identifiable design differences — transfer size relative to household income, program duration and perceived permanence, whether transfers counted against public benefits, whether outcomes were measured by self-report or administrative records, and the macroeconomic conditions surrounding each pilot. A second, equally important finding of this review is what the literature does *not* cover: mixed-status immigrant families, residents of rural colonias, and households containing a disabled adult are nearly absent from the evidence base, despite being heavily represented in the foundation's target geography.

The review proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the search. Section 3 synthesizes the evidence by theme — labor supply and earnings, mental health and wellbeing, housing stability, child development, material hardship and household finances — and then addresses directly why the studies disagree. Section 4 identifies gaps. Section 5 draws implications for a foundation weighing a pilot of this size.

## 2. How the Literature Was Searched

We searched EconLit, the Social Science Citation Index, and PsycINFO for records published between January 1997 and June 2026, using combinations of controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for guaranteed income, unconditional cash transfer, basic income, direct cash assistance, and negative income tax, crossed with outcome terms for employment, earnings, health, mental health, housing, food security, and child development. Because much of the municipal pilot literature exists outside peer review, we supplemented database searches with gray literature from twelve pilot evaluation programs, including reports from the Center for Guaranteed Income Research at the University of Pennsylvania, the Urban Institute, the Jain Family Institute, GiveDirectly's U.S. program, UpTogether, and the Stanford Basic Income Lab's evidence repository. Reference lists of eleven prior syntheses were hand-searched.

The search returned 1,410 unique records after deduplication. Two team members independently screened titles and abstracts; 268 records were read in full. Sixty-three studies met inclusion criteria: evaluation of an unconditional or minimally conditional cash transfer in a high-income country, a defined comparison strategy or systematic qualitative design, and reporting of at least one household-level outcome. The included set comprises 14 randomized controlled trials, 19 quasi-experimental studies, twelve qualitative studies, seven cost analyses, and eleven syntheses or systematic reviews. Effect estimates, transfer amounts, durations, benefit-interaction rules, and follow-up windows for each included study are recorded in the extraction workbook accompanying this review, indexed by author and year; in-text citations below correspond to workbook entries. Disagreements at full-text screening (n = 21) were resolved by discussion among all three authors.

We note three limitations. First, publication and reporting practices in the gray literature vary widely; several municipal pilots released favorable interim findings before final data collection, and we weighted final reports over interim ones wherever both existed. Second, we did not include developing-country transfer studies except where prior syntheses drew on them for design lessons (e.g., Banerjee, Niehaus, & Suri, 2019), because labor markets and benefit systems differ too sharply for direct extrapolation. Third, formal meta-analysis was not attempted; heterogeneity in transfer size, duration, and outcome measurement is severe enough that pooled estimates would obscure more than they reveal — a judgment consistent with prior cross-syntheses (Hasdell, 2020; Gibson, Hearty, & Craig, 2020).

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Labor Supply and Earnings: The Sharpest Conflict in the Literature

The disagreement most likely to surface in the commissioner's office concerns work. The Stockton SEED demonstration — 125 recipients of $500 per month for 24 months — reported that full-time employment among recipients rose from 28 percent to 40 percent over the first year, against a five-point rise in the control group, alongside qualitative accounts of recipients using the income floor to leave gig work for stable positions or to complete credentials (West & Castro, 2023; West, Castro Baker, Samra, & Coltrera, 2021). The Center for Guaranteed Income Research's Cambridge RISE evaluation reported a similar directional pattern among caregiver households receiving $500 monthly (Center for Guaranteed Income Research, 2023).

Set against this stands the largest randomized trial yet conducted: 1,000 low-income adults in Texas and Illinois receiving $1,000 per month for three years, against 2,000 controls receiving $50 monthly. Using linked administrative earnings records as well as surveys, Vivalt et al. (2024) found a decline in labor force participation of roughly two percentage points and a reduction of about 1.3 hours of work per week, with partners of recipients reducing hours as well. Annual earnings fell by roughly $1,500 relative to control, offsetting a meaningful share of the transfer. Recipients reported greater selectivity in job search and modest increases in interest in entrepreneurship, but no detectable improvement in employment quality or human capital investment large enough to register in earnings.

Three considerations prevent either finding from simply canceling the other. First, scale and identification differ enormously. Stockton's treatment group of 125, evaluated substantially through self-report during a period that included pandemic-era stimulus payments, cannot support the precision of a 3,000-person trial with administrative earnings data. Where self-report and administrative records have been directly compared, self-reported employment and income measures show substantial error that correlates with program participation (Meyer, Mok, & Sullivan, 2015). This does not make Stockton's finding wrong, but it makes the 2024 trial the stronger estimate of the average labor effect of a transfer of that size.

Second, transfer size relative to baseline income differs. A $500 monthly transfer to a working household is a supplement; $1,000 monthly to a household near the federal poverty line approaches a substantial share of earnings, and standard income effects predict modestly reduced hours at that dose. The natural experiments bracket the range: the Alaska Permanent Fund dividend — smaller and annual — produced no aggregate employment decline and a slight increase in part-time work (Jones & Marinescu, 2022); casino disbursements to Eastern Cherokee households, averaging roughly $4,000 per person annually, produced no reduction in parental work (Akee, Copeland, Keeler, Angold, & Costello, 2010); Finland's €560 monthly payment to unemployed adults produced a small *positive* employment effect, though confounded by a simultaneous national activation policy (Kangas, Jauhiainen, Simanainen, & Ylikännö, 2021; Verho, Hämäläinen, & Kanninen, 2022). Reanalyses of the 1970s negative income tax experiments concluded that labor supply reductions were real but modest and were persistently overstated in political debate (Widerquist, 2005; Marinescu, 2018; Hoynes & Rothstein, 2019).

Third, the outcome frame differs. A 1.3-hour weekly reduction is, in the words of the trial authors themselves, consistent with recipients purchasing small amounts of time — for caregiving, schooling, or search — rather than labor force exit. Whether that constitutes a cost or a benefit is a values question the data cannot settle. What the data *can* settle is that a foundation should not promise employment gains as the headline outcome of a $1,000-class transfer, and should not fear mass labor force withdrawal either. Both claims outrun the evidence.

### 3.2 Mental Health, Stress, and Wellbeing: Real Early Effects, Uncertain Persistence

The second conflict concerns psychological outcomes. Stockton reported clinically meaningful reductions in anxiety and depression on the Kessler scale at one year (West & Castro, 2023). Finland's trial found recipients reporting higher life satisfaction, lower depression, and greater trust than controls (Kangas et al., 2021). The Mincome saturation site in Dauphin, Manitoba showed an 8.5 percent decline in hospitalizations, concentrated in mental health and accident diagnoses (Forget, 2011). Quasi-experimental work on the Earned Income Tax Credit found improvements in maternal self-reported health and biomarkers of stress (Evans & Garthwaite, 2014), and studies of the 2021 expanded Child Tax Credit found reductions in parental anxiety and depressive symptoms during the payment period (Batra, Jackson, & Hamad, 2023).

Against this, the three-year randomized trial found no detectable improvement in physical health, self-reported health, or most mental health measures at endline; an initial reduction in stress and food insecurity in the first year faded by the second (Miller et al., 2024). The Baby's First Years trial similarly found no significant impacts on maternal depression or anxiety through early waves despite four years of payments (Gennetian et al., 2024).

The reconciliation here is less about measurement than about mechanism and time path. Several patterns recur across the set. Effects on *stress* and *distress* appear early, when the transfer first relieves acute scarcity, and attenuate as households adapt or as unaddressed stressors — chronic illness, neighborhood conditions, precarious housing — reassert themselves (Miller et al., 2024; Gibson et al., 2020). Effects appear stronger where the counterfactual is harsher: Finland's recipients were long-term unemployed; Dauphin's saturation design changed community-wide expectations, not just individual budgets. And short-lived programs may produce anticipatory stress at wind-down: qualitative work from Ontario's cancelled basic income pilot documented significant distress and disrupted plans when payments ended prematurely (Ferdosi, McDowall, Lewchuk, & Ross, 2020), and qualitative companion studies to several U.S. pilots report recipients deliberately declining to change major life arrangements because they knew the money would stop (West et al., 2021; Springboard to Opportunities, 2021).

The implication is not that mental health effects are illusory, but that they are front-loaded, sensitive to program duration and perceived permanence, and unlikely to persist as measured effects at a three-year endline unless the transfer is large relative to need or paired with durable changes in circumstances such as housing.

### 3.3 Housing Stability: The Steadiest Signal in the Set

If earnings effects are the literature's weakest ground, housing is its firmest. Across designs, populations, and transfer structures, unconditional cash consistently moves housing outcomes in the same direction, and the effects hold up in administrative as well as survey data.

The Denver Basic Income Project, enrolling people experiencing homelessness across three payment arms, found substantial increases in the share of participants in stable housing and reductions in nights spent in shelters at one year, with associated reductions in public service costs (Brisson et al., 2024). Vancouver's New Leaf Project, a randomized lump-sum transfer of CAD $7,500 to homeless adults, found recipients moved into stable housing faster and spent fewer days homeless, with shelter-system savings that offset a large share of the transfer cost — and no increase in spending on temptation goods (Dwyer, Palepu, Williams, Daly, & Zhao, 2023). Austin's guaranteed income pilot — the closest analogue in the set to the foundation's proposal, both geographically and in design — found participants directed the largest share of transfers to housing, with improvements in housing stability and reductions in eviction risk (Bogle, Gerken, & Salas, 2023). UpTogether's San Antonio cohort reported similar spending priorities (UpTogether, 2023). Even in the three-year trial, where health and earnings effects disappointed, housing-related outcomes — moves to better neighborhoods, increased ability to pay rent on time — were among the more durable findings (Bartik, Rhodes, Broockman, Krause, Miller, & Vivalt, 2024).

Why does housing hold when earnings do not? Three reasons emerge from the comparative record. First, rent is the largest and least compressible claim on a poor household's budget, so marginal cash flows to it almost mechanically; spending analyses across pilots consistently place housing and food at the top of expenditure categories (Liebman, Carlson, Novick, & Portocarrero, 2022; Bartik et al., 2024). Second, housing outcomes — eviction filings, shelter nights, address stability — are measurable in administrative records, avoiding the self-report noise that clouds employment findings. Third, housing stability is a threshold outcome: a transfer too small to change a labor market trajectory can still be exactly large enough to cover a rent gap and prevent a cascade of eviction, displacement, and doubled-up living. For a foundation targeting zip codes with high rent burden and eviction filing rates, this is the outcome domain where the evidence most strongly supports an expectation of measurable effect.

### 3.4 Child and Infant Development: Early Signals, Unsustained

The Baby's First Years trial — $333 monthly to low-income mothers of newborns, against a $20 control — produced the literature's most publicized early finding: at age one, infants in the high-cash group showed differences in high-frequency EEG brain activity in patterns previously associated with later cognitive development (Troller-Renfree et al., 2022). The finding was appropriately hedged by its authors and vigorously debated on robustness grounds thereafter. Subsequent waves have not sustained the promise: at age four, the study team reported no statistically detectable impacts on children's language, cognitive, or socioemotional outcomes, nor on most maternal outcomes (Noble et al., 2025; Gennetian et al., 2024). Spending analyses from the same trial found mothers increased spending on child-focused goods with no increase in alcohol or tobacco purchases (Sperber et al., 2023) — evidence against a common donor objection, even as the developmental effects failed to materialize.

Two bodies of evidence complicate a purely null reading. The Cherokee casino disbursement studies, which followed children over many years of *permanent* household transfers, found improved educational attainment and reduced criminality among children in recipient households, with the largest effects for the poorest families (Akee et al., 2010). And the 2021 expanded Child Tax Credit, effectively a near-universal child allowance for six months, produced immediate, well-identified reductions in child poverty and food insufficiency (Parolin, Ananat, Collyer, Curran, & Wimer, 2021; Shafer, Gutiérrez, Ettinger de Cuba, Bovell-Ammon, & Raifman, 2022). Cost-benefit modeling of a permanent child allowance estimates social benefits roughly an order of magnitude above costs, driven by long-run child outcomes (Garfinkel, Sariscsany, Ananat, Collyer, & Wimer, 2022).

The reconciliation is dose, duration, and distance. Baby's First Years delivered $333 monthly — modest against the depth of poverty in its sample — to a cohort whose early childhood coincided with the pandemic, and measured outcomes developmentally distant from the intervention. The casino studies involved larger, permanent income changes measured over a decade or more. The defensible synthesis: unconditional cash reliably reduces child poverty and material hardship in the present; whether transfers of pilot-typical size and duration alter developmental trajectories is unproven, and a three-year pilot should not be sold on that promise.

### 3.5 Material Hardship, Food Security, and Household Finances

Findings on immediate material conditions are more consistent than on any downstream outcome. Chelsea's pandemic-era cash card program increased food spending and food security relative to comparison households (Liebman et al., 2022). The expanded Child Tax Credit reduced food insufficiency within weeks of first payment (Parolin et al., 2021; Shafer et al., 2022). The three-year trial found increased spending on essentials and increased financial support given to others — recipients transferred resources within kin networks — though savings gains diminished over time and net worth did not durably improve (Bartik et al., 2024). Qualitative studies across pilots consistently describe reduced reliance on payday lending, ability to absorb small shocks, and — a theme with governance relevance — recipients functioning as informal safety nets for extended family (West et al., 2021; Springboard to Opportunities, 2021; Ferdosi et al., 2020).

The consistent lesson is that unconditional cash does what cash does: it is spent quickly on essentials, it buffers volatility, and it circulates through family networks. It is a hardship-reduction instrument with high fidelity and low administrative cost. What it does not reliably do, at pilot doses and durations, is build lasting wealth.

### 3.6 Why the Studies Disagree: Design Differences That Drive the Conflicts

Five design dimensions account for most of the apparent contradictions above, and each bears directly on the foundation's pilot design.

**Transfer size relative to income.** Effects scale nonlinearly. Transfers near $500 monthly show hardship and stress effects with no labor response; transfers near $1,000 monthly show larger consumption effects and small, real labor reductions (Vivalt et al., 2024); transfers at Baby's First Years' $333 level show spending effects but little else (Sperber et al., 2023; Noble et al., 2025). Comparing a $500 pilot to a $1,000 trial without adjusting expectations is a category error the board should avoid — and one relevant to the arithmetic in Section 5.

**Duration and perceived permanence.** Permanent income streams (Alaska, Cherokee) produce different behavior than time-limited pilots, whose recipients rationally decline to make irreversible changes (Jones & Marinescu, 2022; Akee et al., 2010; Ferdosi et al., 2020). Short pilots systematically understate what a permanent policy would do — in both directions.

**Benefit interactions.** Where transfers counted against SNAP, housing assistance, Medicaid eligibility, or SSI, effective transfer values fell sharply, and some eligible households declined enrollment. Marginal effective tax rates for low-income American households frequently exceed 50 percent and can approach or exceed 100 percent at benefit cliffs (Altig, Auerbach, Kotlikoff, Ilin, & Ye, 2020). Pilots that negotiated waivers or funded hold-harmless accounts show cleaner effects than those that did not — a difference rarely visible in headline results but documented across the extraction workbook.

**Measurement.** Self-reported employment gains (Stockton) sit against administrative-record employment losses (the 2024 trial). Housing and hospitalization findings, which rest on administrative data, replicate; survey-based earnings findings do not (Meyer et al., 2015; Forget, 2011).

**Macroeconomic context.** Pilots fielded during 2020–2021 coincided with stimulus payments, expanded unemployment insurance, eviction moratoria, and the expanded CTC. Both treatment and control households in that window received substantial cash from other sources, compressing measurable differences and complicating every pandemic-era estimate in the set.

## 4. Gaps: Who the Literature Has Not Studied

Three populations central to the foundation's target zip codes are nearly absent from the 63 included studies.

**Mixed-status immigrant families.** No included randomized trial reports outcomes for households containing undocumented members, and most pilots required Social Security numbers or tax filings that excluded ITIN-only filers, mirroring exclusions in the 2021 CTC. Qualitative work suggests such families face acute chilling effects — fear that receiving cash could trigger public charge consequences — but no evaluation quantifies uptake, outcomes, or effective design for this population. In the proposed service area, where a substantial share of households are mixed-status, this is not a footnote; it is a first-order external validity problem. Every effect size in this review was estimated on a population that differs systematically from a meaningful fraction of the foundation's intended recipients.

**Rural colonia residents.** The pilot literature is overwhelmingly urban. No included study evaluates unconditional cash in Texas border colonias or comparable unincorporated communities, where housing outcomes hinge on land contracts and infrastructure rather than rental markets, and where the eviction-prevention mechanism that drives urban housing findings may not operate the same way. The housing stability conclusions in Section 3.3 should be extrapolated to colonia-adjacent households with explicit caution.

**Households with a disabled adult.** Several pilots excluded SSI recipients *by design* because unconditional cash counts as unearned income against SSI's limits, risking benefit loss that could leave recipients worse off. The result is an evidence base that is silent on one of the highest-need populations in the target geography, and a hard operational warning: enrolling SSI households without individualized benefits counseling and, where possible, negotiated protections can cause direct harm.

Beyond populations, three substantive gaps persist: no U.S. study follows recipients more than a few years past program end; no study credibly estimates community-level effects at pilot scale (saturation designs like Dauphin remain unreplicated in the modern era); and cost analyses (seven in the set) rarely price the counterfactual — what the same six million dollars would produce in housing subsidy, benefits navigation, or workforce investment. Garfinkel et al. (2022) and Dwyer et al. (2023) are partial exceptions, and both favor cash, but the comparative cost-effectiveness question remains thinly evidenced.

## 5. Implications for a Foundation Weighing a Pilot of This Size

**The arithmetic comes first.** Six million dollars across 500 households over three years yields $4,000 per household per year — roughly $333 monthly — *before* administrative, evaluation, and benefits-counseling costs. That places the proposed pilot at the Baby's First Years dose, the level at which the literature shows reliable hardship reduction and spending effects but no sustained health, developmental, or employment gains. If the board wants outcomes in the range where the evidence is strongest — housing stability at $500 to $1,000 monthly — it faces a choice: fewer households at a higher dose, a shorter duration, or a larger budget. A 500-household pilot at $500 monthly for three years costs $9 million in transfers alone. This tradeoff should be put to the board explicitly rather than discovered later.

**Promise what the evidence supports.** For Commissioner Fuentes-Nabors and skeptical donors, the defensible claims are: unconditional cash reliably reduces food insecurity and material hardship; housing stability effects are consistent across the strongest studies, including the Austin pilot in a directly comparable Texas rental market; spending flows to essentials, with no evidence of increased temptation-good purchases anywhere in the set; and labor supply effects at plausible doses are small in either direction. The indefensible claims are that the pilot will raise employment, durably improve health at three years, or alter children's developmental trajectories. Early city pilots made versions of those promises; the stronger subsequent trials did not redeem them, and a foundation that repeats them invites the credibility loss now visible in the national debate.

**Design for the gaps, because no one else has.** The pilot's greatest potential contribution to the field — and its strongest answer to the commissioner's evidence demand — lies precisely in the populations the literature has ignored. Deliberate inclusion of mixed-status families (through ITIN-accessible enrollment and privacy protections), colonia-adjacent households, and households with a disabled adult (with funded benefits counseling and pursuit of state waiver protections before enrollment) would make this one of the only pilots in the country generating evidence where none exists. Conversely, replicating the standard urban pilot design would add a sixty-fourth study to a literature that no longer needs one.

**Build the evaluation the critics will believe.** The lesson of the Stockton-versus-trial conflict is unambiguous: administrative data linkage (earnings records, eviction filings, benefits records), a randomized or rigorously matched comparison group, preregistered outcomes, and commitment to publish null results. A hold-harmless fund for benefit interactions is not optional; without it, the effective transfer will vary uncontrollably across households and the pilot may harm some of them.

**Frame the pilot as a learning investment, not a proven intervention.** The literature supports a bounded conclusion: at this dose, the foundation can expect measurable reductions in hardship and probable improvements in housing stability, with honest uncertainty about everything downstream. That is a legitimate basis for a pilot — provided the board, the commissioner, and the donors are told exactly that, in advance, in writing.
