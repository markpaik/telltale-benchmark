## 1. Executive Summary

Staff requests Council authorization to proceed with replacement of the influent pump station, headworks, and both primary clarifiers at the Bellota Road Water Reclamation Facility. The total project cost is $41,600,000. Construction would run 34 months, from notice to proceed in February 2027 through substantial completion in December 2029, with the plant remaining in continuous operation on a phased bypass throughout.

The City is under Cease and Desist Order No. R8-2025-0114 from the Santa Ana Regional Water Quality Control Board, adopted November 14, 2025. The order followed four sanitary sewer overflows between June 2024 and October 2025 totaling 412,000 gallons, three of which reached Bellota Creek. Every one of those overflows originated in the same place: the 1971 influent works, where the bar screens, grit system, and clarifier mechanisms have passed the end of their service lives and can no longer be relied on during wet weather. The order requires the City to begin construction of corrective works by July 1, 2027. Missing that date exposes the City to administrative civil liability of $10,000 per day, assessed from July 1, 2027 until construction commences.

Funding is assembled from three sources: $33,000,000 in State Revolving Fund borrowing at 1.6 percent over 30 years, $4,500,000 drawn from the sewer capital reserve, and $4,100,000 generated by a sewer rate adjustment of 9 percent annually for four years beginning January 1, 2027. The rate adjustment requires a Proposition 218 majority protest proceeding, which staff proposes to notice in August 2026 with the public hearing held October 27, 2026. The median single-family household bill rises from $52.40 per month to $73.98 per month by January 2030, an increase of $21.58, phased in four steps.

The project would be delivered by a four-person owner's team using construction manager at risk. Staff has selected that delivery method deliberately and in direct response to the Council's experience with the Civic Center parking structure, which was authorized at $9.2 million in 2021 and completed at $14.8 million. Section 9 of this proposal addresses that history specifically, identifies what went wrong, and states what is structurally different here.

Three alternatives were examined and are documented in Section 7: rehabilitation of the existing works in place, regional conveyance to the Colton/San Bernardino Regional Tertiary Treatment and Water Reclamation Authority, and a deferred phased replacement. None meets the order's schedule at lower lifecycle cost.

**The action requested on March 10 is set out in full in Section 10.**

## 2. Condition of the Existing Works

### 2.1 What is there now

The Bellota Road WRF was constructed in 1970 and 1971 with a design capacity of 6.0 million gallons per day average dry weather flow. It was expanded in 1988 with the addition of secondary treatment capacity and again in 2004 with tertiary filtration and UV disinfection. The liquid stream front end — the influent pump station, headworks, and primary clarifiers — has never been replaced. Those assets are 55 years old and are the oldest continuously operating process equipment in the City's utility inventory.

Current average dry weather flow is 5.1 mgd. Peak wet weather flow recorded during the February 2024 storm sequence was 14.8 mgd, against a hydraulic capacity at the headworks of approximately 12 mgd in its present condition. That gap is the proximate cause of the overflows.

**Influent pump station.** Four dry-pit vertical centrifugal pumps, original 1971 installation, firm capacity of 9.6 mgd with the largest unit out of service. Pump No. 2 has been out of service since March 2025 pending an impeller and shaft assembly that the manufacturer no longer produces; the replacement is being fabricated from measurements. Wet well concrete shows exposed rebar over roughly 140 square feet of the north wall, consistent with hydrogen sulfide attack. The station has no standby power connection independent of the plant main; a 2023 condition assessment by Carollo Engineers rated the station 4.6 on a 5-point deterioration scale, where 5 is failure.

**Headworks.** Two mechanically cleaned bar screens with 1-inch clear spacing, hand-raked bypass channel, and a single square vortex grit chamber. The screens are chain-and-rake units whose drive assemblies have been rebuilt four times. Screenings handling is by open conveyor to a roll-off bin, with no washing or compaction, which is why the City receives odor complaints from the Almond Grove neighborhood eleven to fourteen times a year. Grit removal efficiency was measured at 31 percent of the 106-micron fraction in a 2024 sampling program, against a modern design standard of 95 percent. The consequence of that grit passing forward is measurable: the City spends approximately $310,000 a year on accelerated wear in downstream pumps, digesters, and clarifier mechanisms attributable to grit carryover.

**Primary clarifiers.** Two rectangular units, 100 feet by 25 feet, chain-and-flight collectors. Flights are wood, replaced most recently in 2009 with a nominal 15-year life. Divers inspecting Clarifier No. 1 in September 2025 found 22 broken flights, a displaced sprocket on the south chain run, and a sludge blanket depth of 4.2 feet at the influent end, indicating the collection system is no longer moving solids reliably. Both clarifiers have effluent launders with corroded weir plates that are out of level by up to 1.5 inches across their length, producing short-circuiting and uneven loading to secondary treatment.

### 2.2 The four overflows

| Date | Volume (gal) | Location | Reached surface water | Cause |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| June 8, 2024 | 68,000 | Headworks bypass channel | Yes — Bellota Creek | Screen drive failure during 1.4" rain event; manual raking could not keep pace |
| February 4, 2025 | 187,000 | Influent pump station wet well | Yes — Bellota Creek | Two pumps offline; wet well overtopped at 13.1 mgd inflow |
| July 22, 2025 | 41,000 | Primary Clarifier No. 2 | No — contained on site | Collector chain failure; solids carryover forced unit offline, hydraulic surcharge |
| October 30, 2025 | 116,000 | Headworks channel | Yes — Bellota Creek | Grit accumulation reduced channel capacity; combined with screen blinding |

Total: 412,000 gallons. The Regional Board's Enforcement Report cited the common origin of all four events and characterized the City's response after the February 2025 event as "corrective maintenance in a facility that has exhausted the useful life of the assets being maintained."

### 2.3 What the Cease and Desist Order requires

CDO No. R8-2025-0114 contains six numbered directives. In summary:

1. Submit a Corrective Action Plan identifying permanent works to eliminate the recurring overflow mechanism — **submitted January 30, 2026, accepted by Board staff February 19, 2026.**
2. Submit final design documents at the 90 percent level — **due December 1, 2026.**
3. **Commence construction of permanent corrective works no later than July 1, 2027.**
4. Achieve substantial completion no later than March 31, 2030.
5. Maintain interim operational measures — a written wet weather response protocol, 24-hour staffing during declared storm events, and standby portable pumping — until substantial completion.
6. Report quarterly on progress.

Directive 3 governs everything in this proposal. Administrative civil liability of $10,000 per day accrues from July 1, 2027 if construction has not begun. At that rate, a six-month slip costs the City $1,840,000 in penalties that purchase nothing. The Board also retains authority to seek separate liability for any additional overflow, calculated per gallon, which for an event of the February 2025 magnitude could exceed $1.8 million standing alone.

Directive 5 is not free either. Interim compliance — the storm staffing, the rented bypass pumps, the standby contractor — costs the wastewater fund approximately $640,000 a year and will continue until this project reaches substantial completion.

## 3. What Is Proposed

The project replaces the liquid stream front end in its entirety and relocates it out of the 100-year floodplain fringe that constrains the existing structures.

**Influent pump station.** New below-grade station with four submersible non-clog pumps, 4.5 mgd each, variable frequency drives, firm capacity 13.5 mgd with the largest unit out of service. Independent standby generator, 750 kW, with 72-hour on-site fuel. Fiberglass-lined wet well and corrosion-resistant coatings throughout. Two-compartment wet well design permitting one side to be dewatered for maintenance without taking the station out of service — a capability the current station lacks entirely.

**Headworks.** New structure containing three mechanically cleaned perforated plate screens with 6 mm openings, sized so that any two units handle 18 mgd peak flow. Washer-compactors on each screenings discharge, enclosed conveyance to a covered bin. Two stacked-tray or aerated grit chambers with cyclone and classifier, designed for 95 percent removal of the 106-micron fraction. The entire headworks is enclosed and served by a two-stage biological odor control system sized for 12 air changes per hour, with a carbon polishing stage. Odor control is a design requirement, not an option: the Almond Grove complaints are a standing item at Council meetings, and the new facility is intended to end them.

**Primary clarifiers.** Two new circular clarifiers, 75-foot diameter, 12-foot side water depth, with spiral rake mechanisms, energy-dissipating inlets, and stainless steel weirs and scum baffles. Combined surface area provides a peak overflow rate within Ten State Standards at 18 mgd. Circular geometry was selected over rectangular after a lifecycle analysis showed lower mechanism maintenance cost and better solids capture at variable flow.

**Ancillary work.** New influent flow metering and sampling station; replacement of 640 linear feet of 42-inch influent trunk line between the property line and the new pump station; electrical building; SCADA integration and control system migration; site civil, paving, yard piping, and demolition of the 1971 structures after cutover.

**What the project does not include.** Secondary treatment, tertiary filtration, UV disinfection, and solids handling are outside this scope. Those assets were addressed in 1988 and 2004 and have remaining useful life. The digester complex will require attention within eight to twelve years and is identified in the wastewater master plan as a separate future project; it is not folded into this request and staff does not recommend folding it in.

### 4.2 Keeping the plant in operation

The Bellota Road plant cannot stop treating. Wastewater Superintendent Gerardo Quintanilla has developed the bypass and sequencing plan with the design team, and it is the single most constrained element of the project. It carries the plant through two wet seasons — 2027–28 and 2028–29 — and a third partial season in 2029–30.

**Phase 1 (February 2027 – September 2028): New influent pump station and headworks.** The new structures are built on the northeast corner of the site, on ground currently used for equipment laydown. This location was chosen specifically so that Phase 1 requires no interruption of the existing works. The 1971 pump station and headworks continue in service, unchanged, for the entire 19 months of Phase 1 construction. Cutover occurs over a planned 96-hour window in September 2028 during the dry season, using a temporary bypass pumping system of six 12-inch diesel pumps with 100 percent redundancy, staged and tested two weeks in advance, with a full-scale dry run conducted in August.

**Phase 2 (October 2028 – May 2029): Clarifier A.** With the new headworks in service, existing Clarifier No. 1 is taken offline and demolished; new Clarifier A is constructed on an adjacent footprint. During this eight-month period the plant operates on a single primary clarifier. This is the period of greatest operational risk, and it spans the 2028–29 wet season. Three mitigations apply. First, the new headworks with 95 percent grit removal substantially reduces solids loading to primaries. Second, a temporary chemically enhanced primary treatment system — ferric chloride and polymer dosing at the new headworks — increases the effective capture rate of the single remaining unit by an estimated 35 to 45 percent, at an operating cost of approximately $18,000 per month, which is carried in the project budget. Third, the existing surviving clarifier receives a full mechanism rebuild in September 2028, before it is asked to carry the plant alone; that rebuild is in the budget at $340,000.

**Phase 3 (June 2029 – December 2029): Clarifier B and demolition.** New Clarifier A is placed in service and the second existing unit is demolished and replaced. The plant again operates on a single primary, but this time the single unit is new, at full design capacity, and the period is shorter and largely outside wet season. Demolition of the 1971 headworks and pump station follows.

**Contingency.** If a Phase 2 or Phase 3 failure occurs on the single operating clarifier, the response is a pre-positioned portable clarification package under a standby contract executed before Phase 2 begins, callable within 72 hours. The standby fee is $95,000 per year and is budgeted. Superintendent Quintanilla's operations staffing plan adds one relief operator position for the duration of Phases 2 and 3, funded from the project.

## 6. Delivery Method and Staffing

### 6.1 Construction manager at risk

Staff recommends construction manager at risk under Public Contract Code Section 22160 et seq., which authorizes CMAR for wastewater treatment facilities.

The essential feature is that the contractor is retained during design, at a fixed preconstruction fee, and commits to a Guaranteed Maximum Price before construction begins. Cost above the GMP is the contractor's; savings below it are shared on a defined split, proposed here at 60 percent City, 40 percent contractor, capped at 2 percent of GMP for the contractor's share.

**Why CMAR rather than design-bid-build.**

*Schedule.* Design-bid-build requires design completion, a 45-day advertisement, bid evaluation, and award — a sequence that would put notice to proceed in September or October 2027, three to four months past the CDO deadline, at a penalty exposure of $900,000 to $1,200,000. CMAR permits early work packages and overlaps procurement with design. This alone is close to dispositive.

*Constructability of the bypass sequence.* The phasing described in Section 4 is the hardest part of this project. Under design-bid-build, the City designs the sequence in isolation and discovers its flaws through change orders after award. Under CMAR, the contractor who will execute the sequence helps design it, during design, when changing it costs drawings rather than concrete.

*Long-lead procurement.* Screens, clarifier mechanisms, VFDs, and the standby generator carry lead times of 40 to 62 weeks. CMAR allows release of these procurements in early 2027 rather than mid-2027.

*Cost certainty at the right moment.* This is the point most relevant to Councilmember Shiraishi's concern. Under design-bid-build the Council authorizes a project against an engineer's estimate at 100 percent design and learns the real number at bid opening. Under CMAR the Council receives a GMP from the entity that will build the work, based on a design it has reviewed and priced, before construction begins — with a second Council action at that point. The Council gets a decision point with real information rather than a single decision made against an estimate.

**What CMAR does not do.** It does not eliminate change orders. Differing site conditions, owner-directed scope changes, and unforeseeable regulatory changes remain the City's risk under any delivery method. CMAR narrows the contractor's ability to claim on means, methods, coordination, and constructability. Staff states this plainly rather than overselling the method.

### 6.2 Owner's team

Four dedicated positions, in place before notice to proceed:

**Project Manager (1.0 FTE, existing position reassigned).** Senior Civil Engineer from Public Works Engineering, reassigned full time for 40 months. Single point of accountability, holds the schedule and budget, signs all change order recommendations, reports to the Public Works Director. Backfilled by a limited-term engineer at $148,000 annually loaded.

**Construction Manager (1.0 FTE, contract).** Third-party CM firm principal, retained separately from the CMAR and reporting to the City. Independence is the point: the entity checking the contractor's work cannot be the contractor. Field administration, RFIs, submittals, change order review, daily reports. $2,410,000 over 34 months, split between the CM line and the inspection line.

**Resident Inspector (1.0 FTE, contract).** Full-time on-site, quality assurance, materials testing coordination, as-built verification, daily photographic record.

**Plant Liaison / Operations Coordinator (1.0 FTE, existing position).** Senior Operator from Superintendent Quintanilla's staff, assigned to the project full time. Reviews every shutdown, tie-in, and bypass evolution before it is scheduled; holds authority to stop a planned outage if plant conditions do not permit it. This position exists because the most common source of cost and schedule damage on operating-plant projects is a construction sequence that operations cannot actually accommodate. Backfilled by an operator hired against an existing vacancy.

Superintendent Quintanilla retains full authority over plant operations throughout construction. The project does not direct the plant; the plant constrains the project. That relationship is written into the CMAR general conditions.

Total owner's team cost, $1,340,000 for City positions plus $2,130,000 for contracted CM and inspection, is 10.1 percent of construction — within the 8 to 12 percent range typical for treatment plant work of this complexity in an operating facility.

## 7. Alternatives Examined

### 7.1 Alternative 1 — Rehabilitation in place

Rehabilitate rather than replace: recoat the wet well, replace pumps within the existing structure, install new screens in existing channels, rebuild clarifier mechanisms and replace flights, add odor control to existing structures.

**Estimated cost:** $19,400,000.

**Why it was rejected.**

*It does not solve the hydraulic problem.* The existing headworks channels are hydraulically limited to approximately 12 mgd. Peak wet weather flow is 14.8 mgd and rising with infill development. New screens in undersized channels still surcharge. The October 2025 overflow would recur.

*The structures themselves are the deficiency.* Concrete testing in the 2023 assessment found chloride penetration and sulfide attack extending 2 to 3 inches into the wet well and headworks channel walls. Coating over degraded concrete addresses appearance, not section loss. Estimated remaining structural life after rehabilitation is 12 to 15 years.

*Construction risk is higher, not lower.* Rehabilitation must be performed inside the operating structures. Every rehabilitation step requires taking a channel or a clarifier out of service in a plant with no redundancy. Replacement, by contrast, builds new works on adjacent ground and cuts over once — which is precisely why Phase 1 requires no interruption at all.

*Lifecycle cost is higher.* Over 40 years, rehabilitation requires a second major intervention around year 14 at an escalated cost estimated at $34,000,000 in 2040 dollars. Net present value at 4 percent: $38,900,000 for rehabilitation against $41,600,000 for replacement — and the rehabilitation NPV assumes no overflow penalties during the 12- to 15-year interim, which is not a defensible assumption for a plant already under a CDO.

*Regional Board position.* Board staff advised in the January 30 Corrective Action Plan review that rehabilitation of the 1971 structures would not be accepted as satisfying the order's requirement for permanent corrective works.

### 7.2 Alternative 2 — Regional connection

Abandon liquid stream treatment at Bellota Road and convey raw wastewater to the Colton/San Bernardino Regional Tertiary Treatment and Water Reclamation Authority (RIX) or an equivalent neighboring agency, retaining Bellota Road as a pump station only.

**Estimated cost:** $58,700,000 capital, plus ongoing treatment charges.

Staff met with regional agency management in December 2025 and January 2026. The findings:

*Capital cost is higher.* A regional connection requires 7.4 miles of 36-inch force main, two intermediate lift stations, and a new terminal pump station at Bellota Road. Preliminary capital estimate $58,700,000, of which $46,200,000 is conveyance. Right-of-way through two jurisdictions and one railroad crossing adds cost and, more critically, schedule.

*Schedule does not meet the order.* Environmental review for 7.4 miles of new force main requires a full EIR — 14 to 18 months — followed by right-of-way acquisition with potential condemnation. Realistic construction start is 2029 at the earliest. This alternative fails the July 1, 2027 deadline by roughly two years, at penalty exposure exceeding $7,000,000, and would require the Regional Board to substantially modify its order, which Board staff has indicated it would not do.

*Capacity is not available.* The receiving agency has 3.2 mgd of uncommitted capacity against Ridgemark's 5.1 mgd average and 14.8 mgd peak. Accommodating Ridgemark requires expansion at the receiving plant, funded by Ridgemark, on the receiving agency's schedule.

*Operating cost is higher and outside City control.* Indicative treatment charges of $3.10 per thousand gallons yield approximately $5,770,000 annually against current Bellota Road liquid stream operating cost of $3,240,000. The differential exceeds $2,500,000 a year, escalating at a rate set by another agency's board.

*Loss of recycled water.* Bellota Road produces 1.8 mgd of tertiary recycled water delivered to the Ridgemark Municipal Golf Course, three school district sites, and the Vista Del Rio Homeowners Association. Regionalization ends that supply, forcing those customers to potable water and stranding $8,100,000 in recycled water distribution assets.

*Loss of local control.* Ridgemark becomes a contract customer with no vote on rates or capital decisions. This is a permanent, irreversible transfer of authority away from this Council.

### 7.3 Alternative 3 — Deferred phased replacement

Build the influent pump station and headworks now, defer clarifier replacement to a second project in 2031–2033.

**Estimated cost:** $24,900,000 now, $22,400,000 later (escalated), $47,300,000 total.

**Why it was rejected.**

*Higher total cost.* Splitting the project loses shared mobilization, site work, electrical infrastructure, and CM overhead — approximately $3,100,000 in duplicated cost, plus escalation on the deferred half at 4.5 percent annually.

*The clarifiers are failing now.* The July 2025 overflow originated at Clarifier No. 2. The September 2025 dive inspection found 22 broken flights. Deferring five to seven years asks equipment already past failure to run through two more wet seasons plus the deferral period.

*It does not satisfy the order.* The CDO identifies the clarifiers among the deficient works. Partial compliance is non-compliance.

*Two rate proceedings instead of one.* Deferral requires a second Proposition 218 process around 2030 for the second phase — a second protest risk, a second round of community meetings, and a second decision for a future Council under the same schedule pressure.

*One disruption instead of two.* The phasing in Section 4 puts the plant through a single well-planned sequence of constrained operation. Deferral means doing it twice.

## 8. Risks and Mitigations

Staff identifies eight material risks. Each is stated with its consequence and the specific mitigation, including who owns it.

**Risk 1 — Construction cost escalation and GMP exceeding estimate.** *Consequence:* GMP returns above $34,490,000, requiring either scope reduction or additional authorization. *Likelihood:* Moderate. *Mitigation:* Three independent estimates at 30, 60, and 90 percent design, including one by a cost estimator retained directly by the City and not by the designer. Early GMP for long-lead equipment in Q4 2026 locks the most volatile 31 percent of construction cost. Pre-identified deductive alternates totaling $2,400,000 — the second grit chamber train, a portion of site paving, the administrative building improvements — identified at 60 percent design and available for removal at GMP without redesign. *Owner:* Project Manager.

**Risk 2 — Proposition 218 majority protest.** *Consequence:* Rate adjustment fails; $4,100,000 funding gap; SRF coverage ratio at risk. *Likelihood:* Low, but consequence severe. *Mitigation:* Six community meetings beginning three months before the hearing; a mailed notice written in plain language that states the penalty exposure and what happens without the increase; a low-income assistance program for households below 200 percent of federal poverty level, funded at $180,000 annually from the capital reserve. *Contingency if protest succeeds:* Ms. Kovalenko has confirmed that SRF borrowing can be increased to $37,100,000 with debt service covered by a reduced rate adjustment of 6 percent for four years, requiring a second Proposition 218 proceeding in early 2027 at a lower increase. The project does not stop. *Owner:* Finance Director.

**Risk 3 — SRF financing delay or non-award.** *Consequence:* $33,000,000 unfunded; project cannot proceed as structured. *Likelihood:* Low. *Mitigation:* Application submitted and on the fundable list; CDO status confers priority. *Contingency:* Interim financing through a bank placement or the California Infrastructure and Economic Development Bank, at a rate 180 to 240 basis points higher, refinanced when SRF funds become available. Cost of a one-year interim: approximately $740,000. Staff would return to Council if this is required. *Owner:* Finance Director.

**Risk 4 — Overflow during construction, particularly Phases 2 and 3.** *Consequence:* Additional Regional Board liability; erosion of public confidence; possible order modification. *Likelihood:* Moderate during single-clarifier operation. *Mitigation:* Section 4.2 in full — CEPT chemical addition, pre-Phase 2 rebuild of the surviving clarifier, standby portable clarification under contract callable in 72 hours, and the added relief operator. Superintendent Quintanilla's wet weather protocol places the plant on 24-hour staffing whenever the National Weather Service forecasts more than 0.75 inches in 24 hours, with the CMAR contractually required to suspend tie-in work during declared storm events. *Owner:* Wastewater Superintendent.

**Risk 5 — Long-lead equipment delay.** *Consequence:* Schedule slip; potential CDO deadline exposure. *Likelihood:* Moderate. *Mitigation:* Early procurement packages released Q4 2026 through Q1 2027, before the main GMP. Liquidated damages in supply contracts tied to delivery dates. Dual-sourcing specified for screens and clarifier mechanisms — at least two acceptable manufacturers per specification, avoiding sole-source exposure. *Owner:* Project Manager.

**Risk 6 — Differing site conditions.** *Consequence:* Change orders; schedule impact. *Likelihood:* Moderate. Fifty-five-year-old plants have undocumented buried utilities and abandoned structures. *Mitigation:* Geotechnical investigation with 14 borings completed February 2026; ground-penetrating radar survey of the construction footprint scheduled April 2026; potholing of all identified utilities during design rather than construction; $1,100,000 of the owner's contingency explicitly reserved against this category and reported separately in monthly reporting. *Owner:* Project Manager.

**Risk 7 — Change order growth.** *Consequence:* The Civic Center outcome. *Likelihood:* This is the risk the Council is most entitled to be skeptical about. *Mitigation:* Section 9 in full, plus the Council-adopted change order authority thresholds in Section 10. *Owner:* Public Works Director.

**Risk 8 — Owner's team turnover.** *Consequence:* Loss of continuity; degraded oversight. *Likelihood:* Moderate over 40 months. *Mitigation:* Project Manager position designated at-risk-critical with a retention agreement; CM contract names key personnel with substitution requiring City written approval; all project records maintained in a City-hosted system rather than the CM's, so institutional memory does not leave with a consultant. *Owner:* Public Works Director.

## 9. The Civic Center Parking Structure

Councilmember Shiraishi has stated he will not vote for another public works number he cannot trust. That position is reasonable and staff does not ask the Council to set it aside. What follows is what staff believes went wrong in 2021 and what is structurally different here.

**What happened.** The Civic Center parking structure was authorized in March 2021 at $9,200,000 and completed in November 2023 at $14,800,000 — a 60.9 percent overrun. Post-project review by the Finance Department in 2024 attributed the growth to four causes:

1. **$2,100,000 — Authorization against a 30 percent design estimate.** The Council authorized the project at conceptual design. The 100 percent design was 23 percent above the concept estimate before a single bid was received.
2. **$1,700,000 — Owner-directed scope additions.** Photovoltaic canopy, EV charging infrastructure, and an elevator upgrade were added after award, none with a corresponding budget amendment brought forward at the time of the decision.
3. **$1,100,000 — Differing site conditions.** Undocumented foundations from the 1962 structure previously on the site.
4. **$700,000 — Escalation and delay.** Fourteen months of schedule growth during 2021–22 material cost escalation.

**What is different.**

*On cause 1 — authorization against an early estimate.* The Council is not being asked to make a final commitment against a 30 percent design today. Today's action authorizes design, preconstruction, and the funding plan. **The Council makes a second decision in January 2027, when a Guaranteed Maximum Price from the actual builder is in front of it, based on a design the Council's own third-party estimator has independently priced.** The Council can stop, reduce scope, or proceed at that point with real numbers. That decision point did not exist on the Civic Center project.

*On cause 2 — owner-directed scope additions.* This is the cause staff considers most correctable, because it was entirely within the City's control. The scope in Section 3 is what the CDO requires and what the hydraulics demand — nothing more. Staff has already excluded the digester complex, the administration building, and the secondary clarifier coating program from this request, though each has a reasonable claim to attention. Under the change order thresholds proposed in Section 10, **any owner-directed scope addition, at any dollar value, returns to the Council with a budget amendment before it is executed.** Not reported afterward. Approved in advance.

*On cause 3 — differing site conditions.* The Civic Center project performed no subsurface investigation of the former building footprint before construction. This project has completed 14 geotechnical borings, will complete GPR survey of the entire footprint in April 2026, and will pothole every identified utility during design. Risk 6 reserves $1,100,000 specifically against this category and reports drawdown separately every month.

*On cause 4 — escalation and delay.* The Civic Center project was design-bid-build with no early procurement. This project locks 31 percent of construction cost through early equipment procurement in Q4 2026 and holds 143 days of schedule float that staff does not propose to spend in advance.

*On oversight generally.* The Civic Center project had no dedicated owner's project manager; it was administered by a Public Works engineer carrying four other projects. This project has a full-time project manager, a full-time independent construction manager, a full-time resident inspector, and a full-time operations liaison. That structure costs $3,470,000. Staff considers it the least expensive line in the budget relative to what it prevents.

*On reporting.* Staff proposes — and requests that the Council require — a written monthly report to the finance subcommittee beginning at notice to proceed, containing: budget authorized, committed, expended, and forecast at completion; contingency drawn and remaining, by category; every change order above $25,000 with cause and disposition; schedule status against baseline with float remaining; and a narrative of the next 60 days' critical activities. Staff further proposes a quarterly presentation to the full Council. If the number is going to move, this Council will know it is moving while there is still time to do something about it, rather than at completion.

Staff does not claim this project will not encounter problems. It will. What staff commits to is that the Council will see them early, in writing, with the cost stated.

## 11. Conclusion

The Bellota Road plant has been asked to run its 1971 front end for 55 years, and in the last eighteen months it has stopped being able to do it. Four overflows, 412,000 gallons, three of them into Bellota Creek. The Regional Board has told the City to begin construction of permanent works by July 1, 2027, and has attached $10,000 a day to that date.

The project before the Council costs $41,600,000, takes 34 months, and gives the plant a new front end with 50 years of life, hydraulic capacity for peak wet weather flow, grit removal that actually removes grit, and odor control that should end the Almond Grove complaints. It costs the median household $21.58 a month more by 2030, phased over four years, arriving at a bill below three of the City's five neighbors. The alternatives were examined: rehabilitation does not solve the hydraulics and the Board will not accept it, regionalization costs more and cannot be built in time, and deferral costs more and satisfies nothing.

Staff is not asking the Council to trust a number. Staff is asking the Council to authorize a design and a funding plan, and to make the construction decision in January 2027 against a Guaranteed Maximum Price from the builder, reviewed by an independent estimator, with change order authority the Council has set itself and monthly reporting the Council has required. If this project goes wrong, the Council will know within thirty days.

Public Works Director Ruben Ontiveros, Wastewater Superintendent Gerardo Quintanilla, and Finance Director Marta Kovalenko will be present on March 10 to respond to questions.
