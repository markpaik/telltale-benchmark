# When the Plan Failed First: Delta Rise Academies and the Rebuilding of a Literacy Initiative

## A Case Study in Charter Network Instructional Reform, 2023–2025

---

## I. The Network and the Conditions It Inherited

Delta Rise Academies operates five schools in Jackson, Mississippi, serving approximately 2,700 students across elementary and middle grades. Eighty-eight percent of those students qualify for free or reduced-price meals. The network was founded on the premise that a small, tightly managed group of schools could outperform what the surrounding district had managed for the same children, and for several years it had produced modest but real gains in mathematics and in middle-grades attendance. Reading was the exception. Reading had never moved.

In the spring of 2023, 24 percent of Delta Rise third graders scored proficient on the state reading assessment. Nineteen percent were retained under Mississippi's third-grade reading gate, a provision that holds back students who fail to demonstrate adequate reading proficiency and that had, since its implementation, functioned as an annual public accounting of how well a school taught children to read. Nineteen percent meant that roughly one in five Delta Rise third graders repeated the year. In a network of 2,700 students, that figure represented something close to eighty children per cohort, and it represented them repeatedly, because the students who were retained were disproportionately the students who had been furthest behind since kindergarten.

Teacher turnover across the network stood at 41 percent. That number is worth sitting with. It meant that in a typical Delta Rise school, four out of every ten teachers who finished a school year did not return for the next one. It meant that institutional knowledge was thin, that mentoring relationships broke annually, and that any investment in professional development had a short amortization window. It also meant that whatever the network attempted in literacy would be attempted, in large part, by teachers who had not been present for the previous attempt.

Executive Director Camille Bourdeaux had been in the role for four years. Chief Academic Officer Anand Krishnamurthy had joined eighteen months earlier from a district instructional role in Tennessee. Both had reached the same conclusion by the end of the 2022–23 year: the network's reading instruction was not a matter of insufficient effort but of incoherent design. Teachers across the five schools were using a mix of materials, some purchased, some assembled, some inherited from prior administrations. Phonics instruction was present in some classrooms and functionally absent in others. The literacy block, where it existed on paper, was in practice interrupted by pull-outs, assemblies, testing, and the ordinary erosion that happens to any block of time not actively defended.

Krishnamurthy's diagnosis, delivered to the board in April 2023, was that Delta Rise did not have a reading problem so much as it had five separate reading problems, and that the network had been treating symptoms at the classroom level while the causes sat at the system level. The board accepted the framing. What followed was a commitment of $2.9 million over three years to a comprehensive literacy overhaul.

---

## II. The Original Design and the Reasoning Behind It

The plan that Bourdeaux and Krishnamurthy built had three components, and each one answered a specific piece of the diagnosis.

**A structured literacy curriculum, adopted network-wide.** The incoherence problem had an obvious remedy: choose one program, grounded in the evidence base around phonemic awareness, systematic phonics, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension, and require it everywhere. Krishnamurthy ran a selection process in the spring of 2023 involving teacher representatives from each school, and the network settled on a program with a strong scope and sequence and substantial embedded assessment. The reasoning was that a common curriculum would make coaching possible, make cross-school comparison meaningful, and reduce the preparation burden on a teaching staff that turned over at 41 percent annually. A new teacher walking into a Delta Rise classroom in August would find materials, a sequence, and a defined set of moves rather than an empty shelf.

**A protected 90-minute literacy block.** The time problem had an equally obvious remedy on paper: mandate the block, put it in every master schedule, and defend it. Ninety minutes reflected the recommendation embedded in the curriculum's design and the general consensus in structured literacy implementation that meaningful growth in early reading requires sustained daily instructional time. The network communicated the mandate to principals in May 2023 for implementation in August.

**Coaching from Meridian Literacy Partners.** This was the most consequential and, in retrospect, the most under-examined decision. Neither Bourdeaux nor Krishnamurthy believed that curriculum adoption alone would change instruction; both had watched adoptions fail elsewhere. What made the difference, in their reading of the research and their own experience, was sustained job-embedded coaching — someone in the room, watching the lesson, naming what to adjust, returning the following week. Delta Rise did not have that capacity internally. It had two instructional coaches for the entire network, both of whom split their time across subjects.

Rather than build the capacity, the network contracted for it. Meridian Literacy Partners was a regional provider with a track record in structured literacy implementation and a proposal that came in under the alternative of hiring. Contracting also offered speed: Meridian could deploy in August, whereas recruiting and training internal coaches would have consumed most of the first year. Bourdeaux later characterized this as the decision she would most want back.

The financing came together in June 2023. The Bienville Family Foundation committed $1.8 million of the $2.9 million total, structured in tranches with performance conditions, and assigned program officer Greta Lindholm as its monitor. Lindholm had funded literacy work in Mississippi and Louisiana before and had a reputation for engaged rather than passive grantmaking. The remaining $1.1 million came from network reserves and reallocated per-pupil funds. The tranche structure — an initial disbursement, a $600,000 second tranche contingent on year-one implementation indicators, and a final tranche contingent on student outcomes — was Lindholm's design, and Bourdeaux accepted it without much argument. At the time, it seemed like a reasonable discipline.

The theory of action was clean: common curriculum plus protected time plus expert coaching produces improved instruction, which produces improved reading outcomes, which reduces retention under the reading gate. Every link in that chain was defensible. The failure, when it came, was not in the theory.

## III. Year One: What Actually Happened

The 2023–24 school year began on schedule. Curriculum materials arrived at all five schools in July. Meridian conducted a three-day summer institute for approximately 170 teachers in early August. Master schedules were built with the 90-minute block. On paper, implementation was on track through Labor Day.

The first indication of trouble was structural and should have been visible earlier than it was. Meridian staffed the engagement at a ratio of one coach for every 42 teachers.

That ratio deserves examination, because it is the point at which the entire design came apart. Effective literacy coaching, in the implementations that produce results, typically runs somewhere between one coach for 15 to 25 teachers, and even that assumes the coach is not carrying other duties. At 1:42, a coach working a five-day week and doing nothing but classroom observation could reach each teacher roughly once every eight to nine weeks — and that calculation assumes no travel between schools, no planning time, no data meetings, no cancellations. Meridian's coaches were traveling among sites. The actual observation cycle in practice stretched to once per teacher per quarter, and for some teachers, less.

The contract had specified coaching support without specifying a ratio. Krishnamurthy had reviewed the staffing plan in June and had noted the coach count, but the number of teachers had not been divided into it in any document either party produced. This is a mundane failure and a common one. Nobody did the arithmetic in a way that made the implication visible, and the implication was that the coaching model — the component on which the entire theory of action depended — was under-resourced by a factor of roughly two.

**Fidelity observations came in at 38 percent.** This was the number that made the problem undeniable. Meridian's own observation instrument scored implementation fidelity across a set of instructional indicators; 38 percent meant that in fewer than four of ten observed lessons was the curriculum being delivered substantially as designed. Teachers were skipping components, substituting familiar materials, compressing the phonics sequence, or reverting to prior practice under time pressure. This was not defiance. It was what happens when a complex instructional change is introduced without sufficient support, to a staff already stretched, in a year when 41 percent of them were new to the building.

**Only two of five schools actually protected the block.** The mandate had been issued and the schedules had been built, but a mandate without enforcement is a suggestion. At three of the five schools, the 90-minute block eroded through the fall in the ordinary ways — Title I pull-outs scheduled during literacy, intervention blocks overlapping, assemblies, testing windows, special education service delivery slotted into the only open time. By November, Krishnamurthy's audit found that at those three schools, the median student was receiving between 55 and 70 minutes of literacy instruction daily, and the composition of that time was uneven. The two schools that held the block were the two whose principals had personally rebuilt their master schedules in July and had refused subsequent encroachment.

**Midyear assessment gains were three points.** Against a baseline of 24 percent proficiency, a three-point midyear movement was within the range of ordinary year-to-year noise. It did not indicate that the program was failing catastrophically; it indicated that it was not yet doing anything.

**Twenty-two teachers resigned in December 2023.** This was the cost that mattered most, and it was the one nobody had modeled. Mid-year resignations at that scale are unusual even in a high-turnover network. Exit interviews, conducted by the network's HR director and later summarized for the board, produced a consistent account: teachers described the new curriculum as demanding, the pacing as unforgiving, and the support as absent. Several used some version of the phrase "we were handed a program and left alone with it." The teachers who left were disproportionately second- and third-year teachers — the group with enough experience to feel the gap between what was being asked and what was being provided, and not enough tenure to absorb it.

The December resignations did two kinds of damage. The immediate damage was operational: twenty-two classrooms needed coverage in January, and in Jackson in mid-year, the available substitute pool was not composed of certified reading teachers. Several classrooms ran on long-term substitutes for the remainder of the year, which meant the curriculum was not being implemented in them at all. The second kind of damage was reputational and internal. The literacy initiative acquired a name inside the network — teachers referred to it as "the reason people left" — and that framing survived long enough to complicate the redesign.

## IV. February 2024: The Funder Holds the Tranche

Greta Lindholm had been receiving quarterly implementation reports from Krishnamurthy since September. The reports were honest — Bourdeaux had insisted on that — and by the January report, the fidelity figure, the block protection audit, and the December resignation count were all in front of the foundation.

Lindholm requested a site visit in early February 2024. She spent two days in Jackson, visited four of the five schools, observed eleven literacy blocks, and met with teachers without administrators present. Her conclusion, delivered to Bourdeaux and Krishnamurthy on the second afternoon and confirmed in writing a week later, was that the $600,000 second tranche was on hold.

Her stated reasoning had three parts. First, the implementation conditions specified in the grant agreement had not been met, and the tranche structure existed precisely to create a decision point when they were not. Second, releasing the tranche would fund the continuation of a model that the evidence indicated was not working, and would therefore convert a design problem into a larger design problem. Third — and this was the part Bourdeaux found most useful — Lindholm said that she was not withdrawing from the initiative, and that she wanted to see a redesign rather than a defense.

That distinction mattered. Lindholm did not terminate the grant. She held the tranche and asked for a revised plan within ninety days. She also offered something that Bourdeaux had not expected: an explicit statement that the foundation understood year-one implementation failure as a normal event in this kind of work, and that what she would evaluate was the network's response rather than the fact of the failure itself.

Bourdeaux has since said that the tranche hold was the most useful thing that happened to the initiative. Without it, the network would have continued the Meridian contract into a second year, because continuing was easier than stopping and because the sunk investment argued for patience. The hold removed that option and forced a decision in February rather than in the following August.

The internal cost, however, was substantial. The board learned of the hold at its February meeting. Two board members questioned whether the network should abandon the literacy initiative entirely and redirect the remaining funds. The network's finance director raised the possibility that a permanent loss of the second and third tranches would leave a $1.1 million hole in a three-year plan. And the news moved through the schools quickly. By late February, the working assumption among many teachers was that the literacy program was being cancelled — which had the perverse effect of further reducing implementation fidelity in the spring, as teachers reasonably declined to invest in a program they believed was ending.

## V. The Redesign

Bourdeaux and Krishnamurthy spent March and April 2024 building the revision. They involved Lindholm in the process rather than presenting to her at the end, which turned out to matter. They also involved Shauna Delgadillo, the principal at Rosewood Elementary, who ran one of the two schools that had successfully protected the block, and whose account of how she had done it became the operational basis for one of the changes.

The redesign had five components.

**Narrowing from five schools to three.** This was the hardest decision and the one that generated the most internal resistance. The argument for it was that the network's implementation capacity — measured in coaching hours, leadership attention, and available funds — had been spread across five sites at a level insufficient to change practice at any of them. Concentrating the same resources on three sites would put the coaching ratio and the leadership attention into a range where they might actually work.

The argument against it, made forcefully by the principals of the two excluded schools, was that it amounted to a decision to leave some children behind. Bourdeaux's response was that the alternative was leaving all of them behind, more slowly. The three focus schools were selected on the basis of grade-span (all served K–3), leadership stability, and the two-of-five block protection finding — Rosewood, which had protected the block, was included; so were two schools that had not, on the reasoning that the model needed to be tested where conditions were not already favorable.

The two excluded schools were not abandoned. They retained the curriculum materials, received the reduced assessment schedule, and had access to network professional development. What they did not receive was dedicated coaching. This created, unintentionally, a comparison group.

**Replacing contracted coaching with four school-based coaches.** The Meridian contract was not renewed for 2024–25. In its place, Delta Rise hired four full-time literacy coaches, based in the three focus schools — two at the largest, one each at the others.

The arithmetic changed decisively. Four coaches supporting the roughly 65 teachers in the three focus schools produced a ratio of approximately 1:16, compared to the prior 1:42. More importantly, the coaches were based in buildings rather than traveling to them. They attended grade-level meetings, they saw the same teachers weekly rather than quarterly, they were present when a lesson went badly and could address it that afternoon, and they accumulated the kind of relational credit that makes coaching feedback usable rather than threatening.

The coaching function was led by Ousmane Diallo as Literacy Coach Lead. Diallo had been one of the network's two general instructional coaches and had spent the prior year watching the Meridian model from the outside. His design principle, stated in the plan document, was that a coach who is not known by name to every teacher in the building is not coaching. He built a weekly cycle: observation, same-week debrief, a single named adjustment, and a follow-up observation the next week focused only on that adjustment. The cycle was deliberately narrow. Meridian's observation instrument had scored fourteen indicators; Diallo's coaches worked on one at a time.

Hiring internally also solved a problem the network had not initially recognized. The four coaches were recruited from within Delta Rise's own teaching staff, which meant they knew the students, the buildings, the schedules, and the specific ways in which a plan could fail in Jackson. It also created a visible internal advancement path in a network where turnover had partly been driven by the absence of one.

**Cutting required assessments from six a year to three.** The original design had embedded six assessment administrations annually. In practice, each one consumed instructional time, generated data that arrived too late to act on, and contributed to the teacher perception that the program was primarily an accountability exercise. Krishnamurthy's audit found that between administration, make-ups, and scoring, the six-assessment cycle was consuming roughly eleven instructional days per year.

Cutting to three — fall, winter, spring — recovered instructional time and, more subtly, changed the meaning of the data. Three administrations with genuine analysis and response cycles produced more usable information than six administrations that nobody had time to examine. Diallo built a two-week response protocol after each administration: coaches and grade teams reviewed data together, identified specific students and specific skill gaps, and adjusted small-group instruction accordingly.

**Making block protection a condition in principal evaluations.** This was Delgadillo's contribution. Her account of why Rosewood had protected the block was unglamorous: she had rebuilt the master schedule herself, had told the special education and Title I coordinators that literacy was not available for pull-outs, and had absorbed the resulting conflict personally. The mandate had not protected the block; her willingness to spend political capital had.

The network's conclusion was that block protection could not be a policy, because policies are enforced by whoever cares enough, and had to instead be an accountability condition attached to the person with the authority to enforce it. Beginning in 2024–25, principal evaluations at the three focus schools included a weighted measure of literacy block integrity, verified through monthly schedule audits and unannounced observation. Bourdeaux was explicit with principals that this was a genuine evaluation component and not a symbolic one.

**Rebuilding the relationship with teachers.** This was not a formal component of the plan submitted to Bienville, but it occupied significant attention. The December resignations and the February tranche news had left a staff that was skeptical of the initiative and uncertain of its future. Bourdeaux held meetings at each of the three focus schools in May 2024 in which she described what had gone wrong in specific terms, named the coaching ratio as the network's error rather than the teachers' failure, and committed to the revised plan. Krishnamurthy has said that the single most effective line in those meetings was Bourdeaux's statement that the network had asked teachers to implement a program it had not adequately supported, and that this was a leadership failure rather than an instructional one.

Lindholm reviewed the revised plan in May 2024 and released the second tranche in June, with revised conditions tied to the narrowed scope.

## VI. What the Numbers Showed

The 2024–25 school year ran the revised model. Results were measured in the spring of 2025.

**Proficiency at the three focus schools: 43 percent.** Against a 2023 baseline of 24 percent, this represented a nineteen-point gain over two years, with the substantial majority of it occurring in the second year under the revised model. In practical terms, at the focus schools, the proportion of third graders reading proficiently had gone from roughly one in four to roughly four in ten.

**Proficiency at the two non-focus schools: 29 percent.** These schools moved from 24 to 29 percent — a five-point gain over the same period, achieved with curriculum materials, the reduced assessment schedule, and network professional development, but without dedicated coaching or evaluation-linked block protection.

The comparison is the most analytically valuable thing the case produced, and it should be read with appropriate caution. The two groups were not randomly assigned; the focus schools were selected in part for grade-span alignment and leadership stability, and one of them had already demonstrated block protection. Some portion of the fourteen-point gap between 43 and 29 percent is attributable to selection rather than to treatment.

But not all of it, and probably not most of it. Two of the three focus schools had *not* protected the block in year one and were selected precisely because they had not. Both moved substantially. And the five-point gain at the non-focus schools is itself informative: it suggests that curriculum adoption alone, absent coaching and time protection, produces a real but modest effect — roughly a quarter of the gain achieved by the full model. That is close to what the implementation literature would predict, and it is a useful number for any organization weighing whether to adopt materials without funding the support structure around them.

**Retention under the reading gate fell from 19 percent to 8 percent.** This was, in Bourdeaux's assessment, the number that mattered most to families. An eleven-point reduction in retention across the network meant that in a given third-grade cohort, roughly forty-five fewer children repeated the year than would have under 2023 conditions. Retention carries documented costs in later persistence and completion, and it carries immediate costs in family stability, sibling grade placement, and student self-concept. The reduction was concentrated at the focus schools but was visible network-wide.

**Teacher turnover fell from 41 percent to 26 percent.** This was the result that surprised the leadership team most, because it had not been a stated objective. The fifteen-point reduction was network-wide, though larger at the focus schools.

The leadership team's interpretation, supported by exit and stay interviews conducted in spring 2025, was that the turnover reduction had three sources. First, coaching. Teachers who received weekly support from a coach who knew them reported substantially higher satisfaction with their professional experience than teachers who had received quarterly visits from a rotating external consultant. Second, the protected block. Teachers reported that a defended 90 minutes made their work feel possible in a way that a fragmented 55 minutes did not — the constant renegotiation of instructional time had been a persistent, low-grade source of exhaustion. Third, the reduced assessment burden, which teachers named repeatedly and specifically.

There is a fourth factor worth naming: the four coaching positions themselves. They were filled internally, which meant four teachers advanced without leaving, and which signaled to others that advancement within Delta Rise was possible.

**Spending reached $3.6 million against a $2.9 million commitment.** The overrun was $700,000, roughly 24 percent above plan. Its sources were identifiable: the Meridian contract had been paid in substantial part for a year that produced little; the four coaching positions carried salary and benefit costs above the contracted alternative; mid-year substitute coverage for the twenty-two December vacancies was expensive; and the redesign process itself consumed staff time and some external consultation.

The network absorbed the overrun through reserves and a supplemental foundation request. Lindholm's position on the overrun was that a $700,000 excess producing a nineteen-point proficiency gain at three schools represented a better use of philanthropic capital than an on-budget program producing three points, and the foundation released the third tranche on schedule.

The cost-per-outcome arithmetic is worth stating plainly, because it is rarely stated. Delta Rise spent $3.6 million over roughly two and a half years. The gains were real but partial: the majority of third graders at the focus schools still were not proficient readers, and at the non-focus schools, seven in ten were not. This was not a turnaround. It was a substantial improvement from a very low base, purchased at meaningful cost, in a network that had to fail publicly first in order to achieve it.

## VII. What the Network Would Do Differently

Bourdeaux, Krishnamurthy, Delgadillo, and Diallo participated in a structured retrospective in the summer of 2025, facilitated by Lindholm at the foundation's expense. Six conclusions emerged.

**Do the arithmetic on the support ratio before signing anything.** The single decisive error of year one was a coaching ratio of 1:42, and it was an error of omission rather than judgment — nobody performed the division. Krishnamurthy's recommendation to peer organizations is blunt: before approving any professional development or coaching contract, divide the number of practitioners by the number of supporters, compare the result against what the implementation evidence suggests is necessary, and if the gap is large, either close it or do not proceed. A coaching model that cannot reach each teacher at least twice monthly is not a coaching model.

**Build capacity rather than renting it, unless speed genuinely outweighs everything else.** The contracted model failed not because Meridian was incompetent but because external coaches traveling among sites cannot accumulate the relational knowledge that makes coaching effective. School-based coaches recruited internally solved this. Bourdeaux's revised position is that the network should have spent year one recruiting and training four internal coaches while running a smaller pilot, accepting slower initial deployment in exchange for a model that could actually work in year two.

**Attach mandates to accountability or do not issue them.** The 90-minute block was mandated in May 2023 and protected at two of five schools. It was written into principal evaluations in 2024 and protected at all three focus schools. The difference was not communication or belief; it was that in the second case, a person with authority to defend the time had a personal stake in defending it.

**Narrow before you are forced to.** The decision to concentrate on three schools was made under duress in the spring of 2024. In retrospect, the leadership team believes it should have been made in the spring of 2023, as a design choice rather than a rescue. The instinct to serve all five schools simultaneously was equitable in intention and inequitable in effect — it delivered inadequate support everywhere rather than sufficient support somewhere, and it cost a full year.

**Count the assessment burden as instructional time and price it accordingly.** Six annual assessments consumed roughly eleven instructional days and produced data that nobody had capacity to use. Three produced usable information and returned eight days to instruction. The general principle: an assessment that does not have a defined response protocol attached to it is a cost without a corresponding benefit.

**Treat teacher retention as an outcome of implementation quality, not a separate problem.** Delta Rise did not set out to reduce turnover. The fifteen-point reduction came as a byproduct of coaching, protected time, and reduced administrative burden. The inverse is equally true: the twenty-two December resignations were a direct product of asking teachers to implement a demanding program without support. Krishnamurthy's formulation is that turnover is a lagging indicator of whether an organization's demands and supports are in balance, and that a network experiencing high turnover during a reform effort should assume the reform is under-supported before assuming anything about the teachers.

A seventh point, raised by Delgadillo and endorsed by the others, concerns the funder relationship. Lindholm's decision to hold the tranche was experienced at the time as a crisis and is now understood as the intervention that made the redesign possible. What made the hold constructive rather than destructive was Lindholm's simultaneous commitment to remain engaged, her explicit statement that she would evaluate the response rather than the failure, and her willingness to participate in the redesign rather than await a submission. Bourdeaux's advice to peer executives is to report honestly to funders early, on the reasoning that a funder who learns of a problem from the grantee in month five has options, while a funder who discovers it independently in month eighteen has only one.

## VIII. What a Reader in a Similar Position Should Take From This

The temptation in reading a case like this one is to extract the redesign and skip the failure. That would be a mistake, because the failure contained the information that made the redesign correct.

Delta Rise's original plan was not foolish. Structured literacy curriculum, protected instructional time, and job-embedded coaching constitute a defensible theory of action, and the network committed real money to it. What the network got wrong was not the strategy but the implementation parameters — the coaching ratio, the enforcement mechanism, the scope, the assessment load. These are the details that strategic plans rarely specify and that determine whether the strategy functions.

For a reader running a similar initiative, the operational takeaway is that the plan is not the hard part. The hard part is the set of numbers underneath the plan: how many teachers per coach, how many minutes actually delivered versus scheduled, how many assessment days, how many schools relative to available capacity. Any of those numbers can be wrong by a factor that renders the whole design inert, and a plan document will usually not reveal it.

The organizational takeaway concerns sequencing. Delta Rise attempted five schools at once because attempting three felt like a compromise with its own mission. The result was that all five schools received a version of the program too thin to work, and the network spent a year and roughly $1.2 million discovering it. Concentration is not a retreat from ambition; it is the condition under which ambition becomes achievable. An organization that cannot adequately support five sites and chooses to support five sites anyway has not made an equitable choice. It has made an ineffective one that happens to look equitable.

The human takeaway is that implementation failure has costs that do not appear in outcome data. Twenty-two teachers left Delta Rise in December 2023 because the network asked them to do difficult work without giving them what they needed to do it. Those teachers took years of accumulated knowledge with them, and the students in their classrooms spent the remainder of the year with substitutes. The subsequent recovery — 43 percent proficiency, 8 percent retention, 26 percent turnover — is real and is worth what it cost. But some of what it cost was paid by people who were no longer there to benefit from it, and that is worth remembering when the decision is whether to launch now with inadequate support or wait a year and launch properly.

Finally: Delta Rise's results are partial. At the three focus schools, 57 percent of third graders still are not reading proficiently. At the other two, 71 percent are not. The network spent $3.6 million and produced a substantial, verifiable improvement that leaves most of the original problem in place. That is what successful instructional reform tends to look like at this scale and from this baseline, and organizations that expect otherwise are likely to abandon effective work prematurely on the grounds that it did not do enough. Nineteen points in two years, at three schools, with a year lost to a failed design — that is the realistic shape of the win.
