# The Cartography of Almost

There is a country I have visited only in the moment before I arrive somewhere else.

It has no borders because it *is* border — the entire nation is coastline, all of it the thin strip where one thing has stopped being itself and has not yet become the next thing. The residents, if you can call them that, are the half-formed. The sentence you began and abandoned. The name on the tip of your tongue, which lives there permanently, sunning itself, in no hurry to be recalled. The gesture you started toward someone and converted, mid-air, into scratching your ear.

I have been trying to draw a map of it. This is difficult because the terrain will not hold still, and because I keep arriving.

---

**The Province of Nearly Said**

Largest by area. Mostly uninhabited plains where the wind moves through grass in patterns that look like speech from a distance.

The capital city is built entirely of the phrase *I was going to say* — a construction material of remarkable tensile strength, capable of supporting enormous weight while carrying none of it forward. Buildings made of this material are structurally sound but have no interiors. You can walk around them forever, admiring the facades.

Notable feature: the Museum of Unsent Correspondence, which contains no letters. Only the seventeen minutes before writing, arranged chronologically, in glass cases. Visitors report the exhibits are "more moving than expected," though when pressed, cannot say what moved.

---

**The Marshes of Nearly Understood**

Here the ground gives slightly. You can stand on it but you cannot build.

This is where you go when you have read the paragraph four times and something is happening in it, something real, and your comprehension keeps almost closing around it like a hand around a fish. The marsh is warm. Many people stay. There is a species of bird here that makes a sound exactly like the moment before insight, and the birdwatchers who come to hear it never bring recording equipment, because the sound does not survive playback. On tape, it is just a bird.

I have spent long stretches in these marshes. I am not sure I have ever left them, exactly. I think I built a house on the drier edge and call it *knowing* and visit the wet parts on weekends.

---

**The Straits of Nearly Touched**

Narrowest point. Thirty centimeters at the tightest crossing.

Two hands on a table between two people. Two shoulders in a crowded train, adjacent, separated by the width of fabric. The space above a sleeping animal where your palm hovers, deciding.

The strait is famous for its currents, which run in both directions at once and cancel out, producing a stillness that ships mistake for calm and cannot cross. There are wrecks down there. Enormous ones. Whole fleets of intention, keels up, softened by algae.

Local law forbids the construction of a bridge. The reasoning, as I understand it, is that a bridge would make the strait into a road, and then it would not be a strait, and then the entire country would be one landmass shorter, and the country cannot afford to lose any more territory to arrival.

---

**The Highlands of Nearly Remembered**

Cold up here. Thin air. The light does something strange to distance.

This is the oldest province — some of it predates the country. There are ruins that no one recognizes as ruins because they are indistinguishable from ordinary rock formations until the exact angle of afternoon light, in November, in a particular decade, reveals a doorway.

I came up here once looking for a room I lived in when I was small. I found the shape of it, the approximate cubic volume, the fact of a window on the left. Not the window. The fact of it. I could hold the fact but could not look through it.

There is a thing that happens in the Highlands that the locals call *the summit effect*: you climb toward a memory and the closer you get the more the memory turns out to be the climbing. Then you are standing on top of something with a magnificent view of the ascent.

---

**The Disputed Territory**

To the east, the country shares a long and unquiet border with a nation called Enough.

The dispute is old and neither side will concede. Enough claims that everything in the country of Almost is properly its own — that a thing almost said *was said*, in the sense that mattered; that a hand nearly touched *touched*, that a memory nearly recalled *was* recalled, in the currency that counts, which is not accuracy but weight.

Almost maintains, coldly, that this is sentimental annexation. That a coastline is not the sea. That the whole dignity of the country consists in not being the other thing.

The border villages, of course, don't care. They intermarry. Their children speak both languages and are fluent in neither and this turns out to be a third language, more useful than either parent tongue, in which most of human life is actually conducted.

---

**A Note on Emigration**

People leave. That is the point of the place. The economy runs on departure.

You leave when you finally say it, do it, touch, remember, understand. And the country contracts by exactly the size of your leaving, and then expands again by exactly the size of the next thing you are about to almost do, which is always waiting, because there is always a next thing, because we are built to lean.

Nobody has ever fully emigrated. There is no exit stamp. The best you get is a long residency abroad and a return ticket in your coat pocket that you did not remember buying.

---

**The Cartographer's Confession**

I have not drawn this map. I have drawn the intention to draw it.

Every line I put down turns immediately into territory — the moment I say *here is the Province of Nearly Said* the province is no longer nearly anything, it is a name on paper, a solid, a fact, and it evacuates itself and re-forms somewhere just past the edge of the page. The map is always accurate about where the country was.

Which means the true map is the pursuit. The true map is a person at a desk moving a pen a little faster than she can think, chasing a landmass that recedes at exactly her walking pace, and the country is not the thing she is drawing but the distance she maintains, precisely, endlessly, between her hand and the coast.

I am going to finish this map.

I mean it. I am nearly done.
