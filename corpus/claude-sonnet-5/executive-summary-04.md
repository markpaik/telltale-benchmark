## Sepsis Early-Warning Bundle
### Review Period: January 2024–March 2025

**Finding and Recommendation.** The sepsis early-warning bundle has performed as intended and should be fully sustained, hardwired into standard nursing and physician workflow, and extended to the small number of remaining inpatient units not yet on the protocol. This is the strongest result among the four programs under review, and the Office of Clinical Program Evaluation recommends that the Executive Council treat it as the template against which future clinical reliability initiatives are measured, rather than as a time-limited project subject to renewed justification each budget cycle.

**Evidence.** Across the fourteen-month review window, three-hour sepsis bundle compliance rose from 61 percent to 88 percent system-wide, a 27-percentage-point gain achieved consistently across all four campuses rather than concentrated in a single high-performing site. Compliance of this kind — timely lactate measurement, blood cultures before antibiotics, fluid resuscitation, and repeat lactate where indicated — is the variable most directly under the system's control, and the fact that it moved this far this fast indicates the early-warning tool is being used at the bedside rather than simply installed in the electronic health record. In-hospital sepsis mortality fell over the same period from 18.4 percent to 12.9 percent, a 5.5-percentage-point absolute reduction and a relative decline of roughly 30 percent among a patient population that carries disproportionate weight in overall hospital mortality statistics. The size and direction of this shift, tracking so closely with the compliance curve and holding across four campuses with different case mixes and staffing models, is consistent with a genuine clinical effect rather than a coding or documentation artifact, though the Office recommends continued surveillance to rule out the latter as volumes grow.

The financial case is equally direct. The program required $1.9 million in the review period — primarily for the early-warning software license, nurse educator time, and unit-based champion staffing — against a mortality and morbidity benefit that, at current sepsis volumes, represents meaningful avoided cost in ICU days, vasopressor and mechanical ventilation utilization, and post-acute placement, independent of the value of lives saved. No other program in this review comes close to this ratio of demonstrated clinical benefit to dollars spent, and the Office finds no indication that further investment is needed to sustain the gain; the marginal cost of extending the tool to the remaining units is modest because the infrastructure, training model, and governance structure already exist.

The one caution worth flagging to the Council is durability. Compliance gains of this magnitude are typically easiest to achieve in the first eighteen months of a new protocol, when attention and champion energy are highest, and hardest to protect once the initiative is no longer novel. The Office has seen this pattern in prior system quality initiatives that lost five to ten points of compliance in year two as attention moved elsewhere. That risk argues for folding the bundle into standard nursing competency and physician credentialing review now, while the current performance is fresh, rather than waiting for a decline to prompt a second intervention.

**Commitment for the Coming Fiscal Year.** Regardless of the Council's specific funding decision, the Office of Clinical Program Evaluation and the Chief Quality Officer are committed to the following for FY2026:

- Continued quarterly compliance and mortality reporting by campus, with unit-level detail available to nursing leadership so that any early erosion in performance is visible before it becomes a system-wide trend.
- Completion of bundle rollout to the remaining inpatient units currently outside the protocol, targeted for the first two quarters of the fiscal year.
- A formal sustainability review at the eighteen-month mark to confirm the gains have held without renewed champion intervention, with a corrective plan pre-built in case compliance drifts below 80 percent on any campus.
- An assessment of whether the early-warning logic and workflow model can be adapted to other high-mortality, protocol-sensitive conditions, to be scoped but not funded until this program's durability is confirmed.

The Office regards this program as evidence that clinical reliability work, properly resourced and consistently led, produces results the system can defend to regulators, payers, and the board with confidence. The recommendation is to protect it, not to revisit it.

## Hospital-at-Home Program
### Review Period: January 2024–March 2025

**Finding and Recommendation.** The hospital-at-home program, sponsored by Chief Medical Officer Julio Ferrante, has not met either its enrollment or its clinical safety targets, and it is operating under a payer waiver that expires September 30, 2025. The Office of Clinical Program Evaluation recommends that the Executive Council treat the next ninety days as a defined decision window rather than approve continued operation at current scale by default. Absent a credible, time-bound plan to close the readmission gap and a confirmed waiver renewal, the program should be wound down in an orderly fashion rather than allowed to continue on inertia past September 30.

**Evidence.** The program enrolled 96 patients against a plan of 400, a 24-percent achievement rate that is not a minor shortfall but a structural failure to reach the scale on which the original business case depended. A hospital-at-home model's economics — clinical staffing ratios, remote monitoring infrastructure, transportation and supply logistics — are built around volume; a program running at less than a quarter of planned census cannot spread its fixed costs the way it was designed to, and the $4.2 million spent in the review period reflects that. On a per-enrolled-patient basis, that spending works out to roughly $43,750 per patient served, a figure that should be read alongside the clinical outcome rather than in isolation, because it means the system paid a premium for care that did not perform as well as the alternative it was meant to improve on.

That clinical outcome is the more serious concern. The 30-day readmission rate for hospital-at-home patients was 14.6 percent, against 11.2 percent for a matched inpatient comparison group — a 3.4-percentage-point gap, or roughly 30 percent higher relative risk of readmission for patients treated at home. This is the inverse of what the program was built to deliver. Hospital-at-home models are approved by payers and regulators on the premise that a well-monitored home-based alternative produces outcomes at least equivalent to inpatient care at lower cost; a matched cohort showing a materially worse readmission rate undercuts both the clinical and the financial rationale simultaneously, and it is the finding most likely to draw scrutiny from CMS or commercial payers reviewing waiver renewal.

The waiver expiration compounds the risk. The federal and payer flexibilities that make hospital-at-home billable as acute inpatient care are time-limited, and renewal is not within the system's control. Continuing to enroll patients into a model with a demonstrated readmission problem, while the underlying payment authority is uncertain past September 30, exposes the system to a scenario in which it has invested further capital and clinical staffing into a program that cannot legally continue to operate as billed, or that would have to convert to a materially different and less favorable reimbursement structure.

**Commitment for the Coming Fiscal Year.** Whether or not the Council approves continuation, the following commitments apply for FY2026:

- A root-cause review of the readmission gap, disaggregating by diagnosis, distance from hospital, and monitoring intensity, due to the Executive Council within 45 days, before any enrollment expansion is considered.
- No increase in enrollment targets or capital commitment until the waiver renewal is confirmed in writing and the readmission rate has shown at least two consecutive months at or below the matched-cohort benchmark.
- A fully costed wind-down plan, prepared in parallel with the remediation plan, so that if the waiver lapses or the readmission problem is not resolved, the system can exit without disruption to patients currently enrolled.
- Direct reporting from Dr. Ferrante's office to the Quality Committee at each of its next two meetings on both readmission trend and waiver status, given the September 30 deadline.

This is not a recommendation to abandon the concept of home-based acute care; it is a recommendation not to continue funding this version of it past the point where the safety and payment questions are answered.

## Community Paramedic Program
### Review Period: January 2024–March 2025

**Finding and Recommendation.** The community paramedic program for high-utilizing emergency department patients has produced a genuine clinical result — a 34 percent reduction in emergency visits among enrolled patients — but it is reaching fewer than half of the patients referred to it and has not yet demonstrated a measurable financial offset. The Office of Clinical Program Evaluation recommends continued funding for FY2026, conditioned on a redesigned referral and enrollment process and a defined requirement to produce credible cost-offset data within the year. This is a program worth investing in further, not one to fund unconditionally at its current level of execution.

**Evidence.** The clinical signal is strong on its face. Among the 212 patients the program actually reached, emergency department utilization dropped by more than a third, a magnitude consistent with what community paramedicine literature generally shows for well-implemented programs targeting genuinely high-utilizing patients, and large enough that it is unlikely to be explained by regression to the mean alone. For a population defined by frequent, often preventable emergency visits, a reduction of this size represents real relief on emergency department crowding and real improvement in how these patients' chronic and social needs are being managed outside the acute setting.

The problem is reach, not efficacy. Only 40 percent of patients referred to the program were actually enrolled, meaning that for every five patients identified by clinicians or care managers as appropriate candidates, three never received the intervention at all. A program that works well for the patients it reaches but reaches fewer than half of its intended population is not primarily a clinical design problem; it is an operational one, likely rooted in some combination of referral timing, patient engagement capacity, staffing ratios relative to referral volume, or eligibility criteria that are narrower in practice than in policy. The Office was not able, within this review, to isolate the specific point of attrition, and that diagnostic work is itself a priority recommendation below.

The financial picture is the least developed part of the record. The program has produced no measurable cost offset to date, which is a different and more concerning statement than "the cost offset is small" — it means the system does not yet have a reliable method for attributing avoided emergency and inpatient spending to the intervention, separate from what its enrolled patients would have cost the system regardless. Given the strength of the utilization result, the Office suspects a real offset exists but has not been captured, likely because the analytic work of matching enrolled patients to a comparable control group on cost, rather than only on utilization counts, has not yet been done. That gap needs to be closed before the Council can evaluate this program on the same footing as the sepsis bundle or the hospital-at-home program.

**Commitment for the Coming Fiscal Year.** Independent of the specific funding level the Council approves, the system commits to the following for FY2026:

- A referral-to-enrollment process audit, conducted jointly by emergency department leadership and program staff, to identify where the 60 percent of referred patients are being lost, with interim findings due within one quarter.
- Development of a matched-cohort cost analysis, comparable in rigor to the one already produced for hospital-at-home, so that the program's financial case can be assessed on real evidence rather than inference from utilization data alone.
- An enrollment target for FY2026 — proposed at 65 percent of referrals — against which the program's operational fix can be measured at the next review.
- Continued funding at current levels pending the outcomes of the audit and cost analysis, with no reduction in scope while the diagnostic work is underway, since the underlying clinical result does not support treating this as an underperforming program in the same category as scheduling consolidation.

The Office views this program as the review's clearest example of a genuinely effective clinical intervention constrained by execution gaps that are fixable, and recommends the Council fund the fix rather than the status quo.

## Scheduling Center Consolidation
### Review Period: January 2024–March 2025

**Finding and Recommendation.** The scheduling center consolidation, led by Nurse Executive Bridget Nkemelu and Chief Financial Officer Meredith Tanaka-Bell, has spent $9.7 million against a $6.1 million budget — a 59 percent overrun — while missing its core service target by a wide margin: a 17 percent call abandonment rate against a 5 percent goal. The Office of Clinical Program Evaluation recommends that the Executive Council not authorize further capital for this initiative until an independent operational review has identified the cause of both the cost overrun and the service failure, and that any further funding be released against milestones rather than as a lump sum. This program, as currently constituted, has not demonstrated that it can deliver on its own design targets, and continued investment without a corrective plan is not defensible to the board.

**Evidence.** The budget performance alone would warrant scrutiny: a $3.6 million overrun on a $6.1 million project is not a rounding variance attributable to normal implementation cost drift, and the Office was not presented, in the course of this review, with a line-item explanation sufficient to account for spending 59 percent above plan. Overruns of this size in a consolidation project typically trace to one or more of a small number of causes — underestimated staffing needs at go-live, technology or telephony costs not scoped in the original budget, extended parallel operation of the systems the consolidation was meant to replace, or scope expansion approved outside the original business case. Any of these is plausible here; none has been documented clearly enough in the materials provided to this review for the Office to state with confidence which factor, or combination of factors, drove the overrun. That absence of a clear accounting is itself a finding the Council should note.

The service outcome is, if anything, more concerning than the budget outcome, because it speaks directly to the purpose of consolidation. The center now handles 380,000 calls a quarter — a meaningful, and presumably intended, concentration of volume — but abandons 17 percent of them, more than three times the 5 percent target the program was built to achieve. At this call volume, a 17 percent abandonment rate means roughly 64,600 calls a quarter are not being answered: patients trying to schedule appointments, confirm procedures, or reach clinical staff who are hanging up before reaching anyone. This has downstream effects the review did not attempt to quantify but that the Council should assume are real — missed appointments, delayed care, and patient dissatisfaction that eventually surfaces in access metrics and patient experience scores elsewhere in the system. A consolidation that concentrates volume without first ensuring the staffing and technology can handle that volume at target service levels has effectively traded distributed, locally-managed shortfalls for one large, centrally-visible one.

The combination of the two findings is what makes this program the most serious concern among the four under review. The sepsis bundle and, to a lesser extent, the community paramedic program show that spending produced results. The scheduling consolidation shows spending well above plan producing a result substantially below target, with no clear documentation yet available to explain why.

**Commitment for the Coming Fiscal Year.** Regardless of the specific corrective path the Council selects, the system commits to the following for FY2026:

- Commissioning of an independent operational review of the scheduling center, covering staffing model, technology configuration, call routing logic, and the full accounting of the $9.7 million spent to date, with findings due to the Quality Committee and the Council within one quarter.
- A moratorium on further capital release for the program until that review is complete and a corrective action plan, with specific abandonment-rate milestones, is approved.
- Monthly reporting of call volume, abandonment rate, and spend-to-budget to both the Executive Council and the Quality Committee until the abandonment rate reaches 10 percent, at which point quarterly reporting may resume.
- A joint accountability structure between Nurse Executive Nkemelu and Chief Financial Officer Tanaka-Bell for the corrective plan, given that the program's failure spans both the clinical/operational and financial dimensions of its original charter.

The Office does not recommend abandoning the goal of a consolidated scheduling function — the underlying rationale for consolidation remains sound — but the current implementation has not earned continued investment without independent verification of what went wrong and a credible plan to fix it.

## Appendix A: Methodology Note

The findings in each summary above draw on the same evaluation framework the Office applies across all system program reviews, adapted here to the fourteen-month window common to all four initiatives. Clinical outcome measures — sepsis mortality, hospital-at-home readmission, community paramedic emergency utilization — were pulled from the enterprise data warehouse and reconciled against source documentation in the electronic health record for a 10 percent random sample in each program, rather than accepted from program-reported dashboards alone. This reconciliation step surfaced no material discrepancies for the sepsis bundle or the community paramedic program. It did surface a documentation gap in the hospital-at-home readmission data, discussed below, and the Office notes it here for the Council's awareness rather than as a basis for revising the finding.

Financial figures were taken from the general ledger as of the close of the review period and reflect actual spend, not budgeted or accrued spend, unless otherwise noted. Where a program's budget-to-actual comparison is cited — as with the scheduling center consolidation — the budget figure is the originally approved capital and operating budget at program launch, not a revised or reforecast figure, on the view that the Council's original approval is the correct baseline against which to measure performance.

The matched-cohort comparisons used for the hospital-at-home and community paramedic programs were constructed by the Office's analytics team using propensity matching on age, primary diagnosis category, comorbidity burden, and prior twelve-month utilization. The Office regards the hospital-at-home match as reliable given the relatively homogeneous diagnosis mix of enrolled patients; it regards the community paramedic cost match as preliminary, for the reasons noted in that program's summary, and does not consider it adequate yet to support a financial conclusion.

## Appendix B: Documentation Gap — Hospital-at-Home Readmission Data

In the course of reconciling the hospital-at-home readmission rate against source records, the review team identified nine cases, out of the 96 enrolled patients, in which the readmission determination relied on self-reported patient information rather than confirmed data from the receiving facility, because five of those readmissions occurred outside the Vulcan Valley system. The Office re-ran the readmission calculation excluding these nine cases as a sensitivity check; the resulting rate was 13.9 percent, still materially above the 11.2 percent matched-inpatient benchmark. The Office therefore does not believe this data limitation changes the finding reported to the Council, but flags it because any external review of the program's readmission performance — including one conducted in connection with payer waiver renewal — should anticipate and be prepared to address the same gap.

## Appendix E: Escalation Triggers

To assist the Quality Committee in monitoring these four programs between scheduled reviews, the Office proposes the following triggers, any one of which would warrant an out-of-cycle report to the Committee rather than waiting for the next quarterly update:

- Sepsis bundle compliance falling below 80 percent on any single campus for two consecutive months.
- Confirmation, denial, or any indication of delay in the hospital-at-home payer waiver decision, given the September 30, 2025 deadline.
- Hospital-at-home 30-day readmission rate exceeding 16 percent in any single month.
- Community paramedic referral capture falling below 35 percent following implementation of the process audit's recommendations.
- Scheduling center abandonment rate exceeding 20 percent in any single month, or any additional capital spend outside the moratorium described in Resolution 2025-Q2-04.

The Office will maintain a standing watch on each of these thresholds as part of its regular data reconciliation work and will notify the Chair of the Quality Committee directly if any is met, independent of the formal reporting calendar established elsewhere in this document.

## Appendix F: Glossary of Terms

**Three-hour bundle compliance** — the percentage of patients meeting sepsis criteria for whom lactate measurement, blood culture collection, and initiation of broad-spectrum antibiotics were completed within three hours of recognition, per the Surviving Sepsis Campaign standard the system adopted at program launch.

**Matched cohort** — a comparison group of patients not enrolled in a given program, selected through propensity matching on age, primary diagnosis, comorbidity burden, and prior utilization, used to approximate what outcomes would have been absent the intervention.

**Call abandonment rate** — the percentage of inbound calls to the scheduling center that disconnect before reaching a scheduler or automated resolution, measured at the point of caller hang-up rather than at any downstream callback.

**Referral capture rate** — for the community paramedic program, the percentage of patients referred by emergency department or care management staff who complete program enrollment, as distinct from the percentage who complete the program once enrolled.

**Cost offset** — for purposes of this review, dollars of avoided emergency department, inpatient, or post-acute spending attributable to a program intervention, net of the program's own operating cost, as distinguished from gross utilization reduction alone.

**Payer waiver** — the regulatory and contractual authority, time-limited and subject to renewal, that permits billing of hospital-at-home services at acute inpatient rates; distinct from the clinical protocol itself, which could in principle continue under a different payment model if the waiver lapses.
