# Speed Management Interventions on Urban Arterials: A Review of the Evidence

**City of Verde Springs, Arizona — Office of Performance and Budget**

Prepared by Anand Sundaresan, Principal Management Analyst, and Keiko Villalpando, Transportation Planner
August 2026

---

## 1. Introduction

Between 2021 and 2025, annual traffic deaths in Verde Springs rose from nine to nineteen. Fourteen of the nineteen deaths recorded in 2025 were pedestrians, and the fatal crash locations cluster on five arterial corridors that carry the city's highest traffic volumes and its highest observed operating speeds. The City Council has appropriated $6.5 million for speed management and has directed this office to review the published evidence before the Department of Transportation releases a request for proposals for automated speed enforcement. The direction reflects a genuine dispute on the Council: automated enforcement has a substantial evaluation literature behind it, but it also faces a charge — voiced by Councilmember Prosser and familiar from other jurisdictions — that camera programs function primarily as revenue devices, and a state preemption bill now in committee could constrain or eliminate the city's authority to operate cameras at all. Meanwhile, the police department reports no capacity for sustained manual speed enforcement, which removes one traditional alternative from consideration.

This review asks a practical question: **for a city of roughly 118,000 residents with pedestrian deaths concentrated on multilane arterials, what does the evidence show about the safety effects of the four intervention families the city could plausibly fund — automated speed enforcement, road diets (roadway reconfiguration), posted speed limit changes, and physical traffic calming — and how confident can the city be in that evidence?**

The review does not attempt to identify a single best intervention. The literature does not support that framing. Instead it examines what each intervention family has been shown to do, under what conditions, with what durability, and with what side effects; where the published estimates conflict; and why they conflict. The methodological section of this review matters as much as the effect estimates, because the range of reported outcomes — camera evaluations claiming injury reductions anywhere from under 10 percent to over 50 percent, road diets that succeed in one setting and fail in another — is not random noise. It is largely explained by identifiable differences in study design, and understanding those differences is what allows the city to weight the estimates sensibly.

Three constraints shape the scope. First, the review is limited to interventions applicable to urban arterial corridors, because that is where Verde Springs residents are dying; findings from freeways and rural two-lane roads are noted only where they illuminate mechanisms. Second, the review privileges injury and fatal crash outcomes over speed outcomes, while recognizing that speed is the causal pathway and that the speed–injury relationship is itself well quantified. Third, the review treats fiscal, equity, and legal-durability considerations as legitimate evaluation criteria alongside crash reduction, because a program that is repealed by referendum or preempted by statute delivers no safety benefit regardless of its effect size.

## 2. Search Strategy and Study Selection

We searched the Transport Research International Documentation (TRID) database, Scopus, PubMed, and the state transportation agency's research report series for records published between January 1995 and June 2026. Search strings combined intervention terms (automated enforcement, speed camera, photo radar, road diet, lane reduction, road reconfiguration, speed limit, traffic calming, speed hump, speed cushion) with outcome terms (crash, collision, injury, fatality, casualty, operating speed) and setting terms (urban, arterial, corridor). Reference lists of included systematic reviews were hand-searched, and we consulted the Federal Highway Administration's Crash Modification Factors Clearinghouse to identify evaluations not indexed in the academic databases.

The search returned 1,684 unique records after deduplication. Title and abstract screening excluded 1,393, most for setting (freeway or rural), outcome (compliance or attitudes only), or publication type (commentary without original data). We reviewed 291 records in full text and included 88 studies: 26 automated enforcement evaluations (including three systematic reviews and two meta-analyses), 22 road diet studies, 17 speed limit change studies, 12 traffic calming studies, and 11 studies addressing equity, citation distribution, or program governance that cut across intervention types. Crash counts, exposure measures, study designs, and effect estimates were logged in a structured extraction sheet, which is available from this office; citations in this document follow author-year convention keyed to that sheet.

Two screening decisions deserve note. We retained simple before–after studies rather than excluding them, precisely because the gap between their estimates and those of controlled studies is itself informative and is discussed at length below. We excluded red-light camera evaluations except where a program combined red-light and speed functions, because the intersection-focused mechanism and the angle-versus-rear-end crash tradeoff in that literature (Retting et al. 2003; Høye 2013) do not transfer cleanly to midblock speed management.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Automated speed enforcement: large claims, smaller corrected effects

The headline finding of the camera literature is directionally consistent and quantitatively unstable. Every systematic review concludes that automated speed enforcement reduces speeds and crashes; no credible review finds a null or harmful overall effect. The Cochrane review (Wilson et al. 2010) found reductions in average speed of 1 to 15 percent and reductions in injury crashes of 8 to 50 percent across 35 studies — a range so wide it is nearly useless for budgeting. The task for a practitioner is to understand which end of that range applies, and the answer turns almost entirely on study design.

Uncontrolled before–after evaluations sit at the high end. The United Kingdom's national safety camera program evaluation reported a 42 percent reduction in people killed or seriously injured at camera sites (Gains et al. 2005). Early North American evaluations reported injury crash reductions of 20 to 51 percent (Chen et al. 2002; Retting & Farmer 2003). These studies share a critical vulnerability: cameras are installed at sites selected *because* they recently had high crash counts, and crash counts at such sites fall toward their long-run mean even with no intervention. This regression-to-the-mean effect is not a technicality. Mountain et al. (2004), reanalyzing UK camera sites with empirical Bayes correction, found that roughly one-third to one-half of the apparent reduction at high-crash sites was attributable to regression to the mean rather than to the cameras. Christie et al. (2003) reached a similar conclusion for South Wales.

Studies that correct for site selection report effects roughly half the size of the naive estimates — and those corrected effects are still meaningful. Shin, Washington, and van Schalkwyk (2009), evaluating the Scottsdale, Arizona freeway camera demonstration with empirical Bayes methods, estimated a 44 to 54 percent reduction in target crashes on that facility, but the same authors emphasized the estimate's sensitivity to the comparison group; the Arizona setting makes this the closest analogue in the corrected literature, though the freeway context limits transfer to arterials. Li et al. (2013), applying empirical Bayes and full Bayes methods to UK sites, estimated injury crash reductions of 15 to 25 percent — half the national evaluation's figure. Høye's (2014) meta-analysis, which weighted studies by methodological quality and tested for publication bias, settled on approximately a 20 percent reduction in injury crashes and about 50 percent in killed-or-seriously-injured outcomes at conventional spot camera sites, with trim-and-fill adjustment suggesting the published literature modestly overstates even these. De Pauw et al. (2014), studying Flemish highway cameras with a comparison-group design, found a 29 percent reduction in serious injury crashes but no significant effect on total crashes. Carnis and Blais (2013) evaluated the French national program — notable for its scale and for deploying cameras partly by network coverage rather than purely by crash history, which dilutes selection bias — and estimated a 21 percent reduction in fatal crashes attributable to the program.

A reasonable synthesis: **corridor-level injury crash reductions of 15 to 25 percent at enforced locations are the defensible planning estimate for spot enforcement; claims above 40 percent almost invariably come from designs that cannot separate the camera's effect from site selection.**

Three secondary disputes matter for program design.

*Spatial spillover versus crash migration.* Some studies find safety benefits extending beyond camera sites — a "halo" of 500 meters to several kilometers (Hess & Polak 2003; Mountain et al. 2005) — while others find the opposite: drivers slowing at the camera and accelerating beyond it ("kangaroo driving"), with crashes migrating to adjacent segments or parallel corridors. Elvik (1997) and later Høye (2014) found modest evidence of migration in a minority of studies; Wong (2014) documented speed rebound within 1.5 kilometers downstream of fixed cameras in an urban network. The moderating variable appears to be enforcement architecture. Fixed, signed, conspicuous cameras produce localized compliance and are most susceptible to kangaroo effects. Mobile and covert enforcement, and especially point-to-point (average speed) enforcement, produce compliance over extended distances: Soole, Watson, and Fleiter (2013), reviewing average-speed systems, found sustained speed compliance along entire enforced segments and injury crash reductions of 30 percent or more with little evidence of migration. Montella et al. (2015) reported similar corridor-length effects on an Italian urban motorway. For a city whose problem is five specific corridors rather than isolated black spots, this distinction is arguably the most operationally important finding in the enforcement literature, though the legal availability of average-speed enforcement under Arizona statute would require separate analysis.

*Rear-end crashes.* Several evaluations report increased rear-end collisions near cameras, presumably from abrupt braking (Shin et al. 2009 found a rear-end increase alongside the target-crash reduction; Vanlaar et al. 2014 found none in Winnipeg's mobile program). The pattern mirrors the red-light camera literature, where rear-end increases are well documented but are outweighed in severity terms by reductions in angle crashes (Retting et al. 2003; Høye 2013). The inconsistency across speed camera studies again tracks program design: conspicuous fixed installations with abrupt speed transitions generate braking shockwaves; mobile, rotating, or average-speed enforcement generally does not. Because rear-end crashes are on average far less severe than pedestrian strikes, even studies reporting rear-end increases report net injury-severity benefits — but the effect is real, measurable, and mitigable through siting and signage.

*Durability and program survival.* Effects appear durable while enforcement continues — the French program's effects persisted over eight years (Carnis & Blais 2013) — and decay when it stops. Hu and McCartt (2016) found that speeding in Montgomery County, Maryland fell over seven and a half years of program operation, with the proportion of drivers exceeding limits by more than 10 mph down 59 percent at camera corridors, and that effects extended to similar non-camera roads; but studies of program termination show speeds returning toward baseline within months (Retting et al. 2010, following the termination of a demonstration program). This is the empirical basis for treating the pending state preemption bill as a safety variable, not merely a legal one: a camera program's expected benefit must be discounted by the probability of its forced termination. The governance literature reinforces the point from the other direction. Programs perceived as revenue-driven — high fines, short yellow phases in red-light programs, contractor per-ticket compensation, sited on high-volume rather than high-injury locations — attract repeal campaigns; a majority of U.S. photo-enforcement ballot measures between 2008 and 2014 resulted in program termination. The design correlates of program survival identified in the governance studies (warning periods, modest fines, revenue restricted to safety uses, public reporting of speeds and crashes rather than citations and revenue) are the same features associated with sustained deterrence rather than sustained citation volume (Delaney et al. 2005). Councilmember Prosser's objection, in other words, is answerable by program design, and the literature specifies the design.

### 3.2 Road diets: strong evidence with sharp boundary conditions

A road diet — typically converting a four-lane undivided roadway to two through lanes, a center turn lane, and reclaimed space for bicycle lanes or pedestrian refuge — has among the best-documented crash reductions in the countermeasure literature, and among the clearest limits on where the documentation applies.

The foundational studies come from small Iowa cities. Pawlovich et al. (2006), using a full Bayesian analysis of fifteen Iowa conversions, found a 25 percent reduction in crash frequency and 19 percent in injury crashes. Knapp and Giese (2001) documented speed reductions and sharp declines in high-end speeding on converted Iowa corridors. Harkey et al. (2008), in the FHWA-sponsored reanalysis that generated the crash modification factors now in national guidance, found an important split: a 47 percent crash reduction in small-town settings (populations under ~30,000, volumes generally under 12,000 vehicles per day) but 19 percent in larger urban/suburban settings on higher-volume corridors. Persaud et al. (1997), whose empirical Bayes framework underlies these corrections, established the methods that make the estimates credible.

The mechanism explains the boundary condition. On a four-lane undivided road, crashes concentrate in left-turn conflicts and multiple-threat pedestrian crashes, where a vehicle in the near lane stops and a vehicle in the far lane does not; speeds are set by the fastest driver, who can always pass. The conversion removes the passing opportunity, channels turns, and halves the number of lanes a pedestrian must cross without refuge. All of these mechanisms operate on a four-lane cross-section. **None of the North American crash evaluations in our extraction sheet involves a six-lane arterial**, and the small number of six-to-four or six-to-five conversions that have been studied report operational outcomes (delay, diversion) rather than controlled crash outcomes (Gates et al. 2007; Stamatiadis et al. 2011, which address feasibility thresholds rather than injury effects). Volume is the second boundary: above roughly 20,000–25,000 vehicles per day, four-to-three conversions begin to produce meaningful diversion to parallel streets (Gates et al. 2007), which both erodes political support and potentially exports crashes to streets less able to absorb them — a migration concern structurally identical to the one in the camera literature, and similarly under-measured because most road diet evaluations do not monitor parallel routes (Nixon et al. 2017 is a partial exception, finding modest diversion and no detectable crash migration in San José).

For Verde Springs, the implication is conditional. If any of the five fatal-crash corridors is a four-lane undivided section carrying under ~20,000 vehicles per day, the road diet evidence is strong, the effect is structural rather than behavioral (it does not decay and cannot be preempted), and the cost per corridor within a repaving cycle is modest. For corridors that are six lanes or carry higher volumes, the honest statement is that the published evidence does not cover the case, and any project there is a pilot requiring its own evaluation, not an application of established findings.

### 3.3 Speed limit changes: the sign is not the road

The speed limit literature delivers one of the most consistent findings in the review, and it is a deflating one: lowering a posted limit without changing the roadway or its enforcement reduces mean operating speeds by roughly 1 to 3 mph, regardless of the size of the posted change. Parker (1997), studying limit changes at 100 U.S. sites, found mean speed changes under 1.5 mph and no significant crash effects. Islam and El-Basyouny (2015) and the broader synthesis in Forbes et al. (2012) reach compatible conclusions: drivers respond to the road's geometry and context far more than to its signage.

Yet small speed changes are not safety-trivial, because the speed–injury relationship is steeply nonlinear. Nilsson's (2004) power model, refined by Elvik (2013) with an exponential update (Elvik et al. 2019), implies that fatal crash risk scales with roughly the fourth power of mean speed; a 2 mph reduction from a 40 mph baseline yields an estimated fatal-crash reduction on the order of 15 to 20 percent. For pedestrians specifically, Tefft (2013) estimated the probability of death at 25 percent when struck at 32 mph and 50 percent at 42 mph — the exact speed range at issue on the Verde Springs corridors. The nonlinearity is why the citywide limit-reduction evaluations, despite modest speed effects, report measurable injury outcomes: Hu and Cicchino (2020) found that Boston's citywide reduction from 30 to 25 mph cut the odds of vehicles exceeding 35 mph by 29 percent; the Seattle arterial evaluation (Hu & Cicchino 2023) found injury crash reductions on arterials where the lower limit was accompanied by sign density changes and publicity. Conversely, the natural experiment created by state-mandated limit *increases* shows the relationship running in the other direction at scale (Farmer 2017, attributing roughly 33,000 additional U.S. deaths over two decades to raised limits).

The synthesis across this theme and the previous two is a mechanism statement rather than a menu: posted limits set the legal and normative baseline, but operating speed is produced by road design and enforcement. A lower limit alone yields 1–3 mph; a lower limit that legally enables camera enforcement at meaningful thresholds, or that accompanies a reconfiguration, is a different intervention. The studies that evaluate limits in isolation and those that evaluate limits as part of packages are often cited against each other in public debate; they are not in conflict, because they measure different treatments.

### 3.4 Traffic calming: effective where it applies, and it mostly does not apply here

Vertical and horizontal deflection — humps, cushions, raised crossings, chicanes, neighborhood traffic circles — has a mature evidence base on residential streets. Elvik's (2001) meta-analysis of area-wide urban traffic calming found injury crash reductions of about 15 percent area-wide, concentrated on treated local streets (roughly 25 percent) with smaller effects on bounding main roads. Bunn et al. (2003), the Cochrane review, found an 11 percent reduction in injuries and directionally favorable but statistically fragile effects on deaths. Zein et al. (1997) documented collision reductions of 40 percent on treated Canadian residential streets. Effects are structural and durable, and the studies of individual devices (Ewing 1999) show speed reductions of 5 to 10 mph at the device with partial rebound between devices — the spot-versus-continuous problem again, in physical form.

The limitation is the setting. Nearly all of this literature concerns streets with volumes under 8,000 vehicles per day and posted limits at or below 30 mph. Conventional vertical deflection is generally contraindicated on transit and primary emergency response routes, and the emergency-response literature (Bunte 2000) documents measurable response-time penalties from hump networks — a cost that must be weighed but that also has engineering answers (speed cushions with wheel channels; raised intersections designed to fire-apparatus tolerances). A newer and thinner literature evaluates arterial-compatible calming: raised crossings at transit stops, median refuge islands, and corridor "self-explaining road" retrofits. Refuge islands carry an estimated pedestrian crash reduction near 30 percent in the FHWA countermeasure syntheses (Zegeer et al. 2017), and this is the element of the traffic calming literature most transferable to the five corridors. But the twelve traffic calming studies in our extraction sheet contribute more to the neighborhood-street program than to the arterial fatality problem, and the review should be candid that the intervention family with the most durable, least legally vulnerable effects is the one with the least evidence at the volumes and speeds where Verde Springs residents are being killed.

### 3.5 Equity: the denominator determines the conclusion

The equity literature on automated enforcement reaches conflicting conclusions that dissolve, on inspection, into a disagreement about denominators. Analyses that normalize citations by resident population find that camera citations and associated fines fall disproportionately on lower-income and majority-Black and Hispanic neighborhoods (the Chicago analyses summarized in Barajas et al. 2022; similar findings in the Washington, D.C. audit literature). Analyses that normalize by camera placement and traffic exposure find that the disparity is substantially a siting and roadway-design artifact: cameras are placed on high-injury arterials, high-injury arterials are disproportionately located in disadvantaged neighborhoods because of historical infrastructure decisions, and citation rates per vehicle passing a given camera show much smaller demographic differences (Brazil 2023 [preprint]; our extraction sheet flags the thinner peer-review status of this strand). A third denominator — who is *struck* — points the opposite direction from the citation critique: pedestrian deaths are concentrated in the same disadvantaged neighborhoods (Sanders & Schneider 2022; Governors Highway Safety Association syntheses), so both the burden of enforcement and the burden of non-enforcement fall on the same populations.

Two findings are relatively stable across the strand. First, automated enforcement removes the officer from the stop, and jurisdictions replacing manual with automated enforcement show reductions in racial disparity in *who is stopped*, because the camera cites the vehicle blind to the driver (the Fell 2023 synthesis). Second, the regressive-burden problem is a fine-structure problem, not a camera problem: flat fines, late-payment escalation, and license-suspension consequences generate the documented harms, and jurisdictions using income-scaled or capped fines, payment plans, and non-punitive escalation report reduced burden without measured loss of deterrence — though the deterrence-equivalence evidence is thin and largely from outside the United States. The honest summary for Council is that the equity literature does not adjudicate for or against cameras; it specifies fine structures, siting criteria keyed to injury rather than volume, and reporting requirements.

## 4. Why the Studies Disagree

The conflicts catalogued above reduce mostly to five design differences, and stating them explicitly is what allows the extraction-sheet estimates to be weighted rather than averaged.

**Regression to the mean and site selection.** Interventions are installed where crashes spiked. Uncorrected before–after designs attribute the natural fall from a spike to the intervention. Empirical Bayes correction (Hauer 1997; Persaud & Lyon 2007) is the accepted remedy, and across every intervention family in this review, EB-corrected estimates run one-third to one-half the size of naive estimates. This single factor explains most of the 20-to-50-percent range in the camera literature.

**Comparison group contamination and spillover.** If a camera program changes driving citywide (as Hu & McCartt 2016 found), then nearby "control" corridors are partially treated, and the comparison-group design *understates* the true effect — the mirror image of the RTM problem. Studies using distant or dissimilar comparison sites avoid contamination but import confounding. The literature's small corrected effects may therefore be modest underestimates of program-wide effects, a point rarely acknowledged by camera skeptics who otherwise favor the controlled designs.

**Treatment heterogeneity labeled as one treatment.** "Speed cameras" spans covert mobile vans, conspicuous fixed poles, and corridor-length average-speed systems; "road diet" spans a 9,000-ADT Iowa main street and a 24,000-ADT suburban arterial; "speed limit reduction" spans a sign swap and a signed-publicized-enforced package. Meta-analytic averages across these categories are averages across different interventions, which is why this review reports effects by sub-type wherever the primary studies permit.

**Outcome choice and severity weighting.** Studies finding "no effect on total crashes" alongside large reductions in serious injuries (De Pauw et al. 2014) are frequently cited as null findings. For a city whose problem is deaths, total-crash outcomes are the wrong metric; severity-stratified outcomes consistently show larger effects at the severe end, exactly as the speed–injury physics predicts.

**Follow-up duration and publication.** The median post-intervention observation period in our extraction sheet is 24 months; almost nothing is tracked past three years except the national programs. Combined with Høye's (2014) publication-bias evidence, the literature is best at measuring short-run effects of programs interesting enough to publish — a structural optimism that the corrected estimates only partially discount.

## 5. Gaps

Five gaps bear directly on decisions Verde Springs must make and cannot be resolved from the published record.

**Six-lane and high-volume arterial reconfiguration.** The road diet crash literature stops at four-lane, moderate-volume roads. If the city's fatal corridors exceed those bounds, no controlled crash evaluation covers the treatment the city would be buying.

**Crash and traffic migration measured at network scale.** Both the camera and road diet literatures raise migration concerns and neither resolves them, because evaluations almost never instrument parallel corridors. A city that treats five corridors without monitoring the streets beside them will replicate the gap rather than fill it.

**Long-run durability.** Effects past three years are essentially unmeasured for everything except the French and UK national camera programs. Whether road diets' structural effects hold as land use changes, whether camera deterrence erodes as fines lose salience, and how quickly effects decay after program termination are all thinly documented.

**Combined-treatment evaluation.** Cities deploy packages; researchers evaluate components. Almost no study in the extraction sheet evaluates cameras *plus* reconfiguration *plus* limit changes against any single component, so the additivity of the effects reported here is assumed, not demonstrated.

**Equity outcomes, as opposed to equity burdens.** The citation-distribution literature is developing rapidly; the literature on whether program design changes (income-scaled fines, injury-based siting) preserve deterrence is nearly empty in the U.S. context. Similarly, no study we identified tracks whether camera-program *removal* — the preemption scenario — differentially harms the neighborhoods where pedestrian deaths concentrate, though the death-concentration literature strongly implies it would.

**Small-city external validity.** Most rigorous camera evaluations come from national programs, large counties, or cities several times Verde Springs' size. The fixed costs of program administration, adjudication, and public reporting per camera are higher for a small program, and the literature offers no evaluated model of a program at the 10-to-20-camera scale the appropriation implies.

## 6. Implications for Verde Springs

The evidence supports several statements at planning-level confidence.

**First, a properly designed camera program on the five corridors is the intervention with the strongest evidence of near-term fatal and serious-injury reduction — on the order of 15 to 25 percent at enforced locations, not the 40-to-50-percent figures in vendor materials, which derive from uncorrected designs.** The RFP should be written to the design features the literature associates with both larger effects and program survival: enforcement keyed to injury locations rather than volume; corridor-length or rotating mobile coverage rather than isolated conspicuous poles, to blunt kangaroo-driving and rear-end effects; a warning period; modest fines with payment accommodations; contractor compensation decoupled from citation volume; and annual public reporting of speeds and injuries, not revenue. These features constitute the substantive answer to the revenue-device objection.

**Second, the preemption risk argues for pairing enforcement with structural change, because camera effects decay on termination and reconfiguration effects do not.** Corridors meeting the road diet boundary conditions — four-lane undivided, under roughly 20,000 ADT — should be programmed for conversion within the appropriation, with the strong Harkey et al. (2008) and Pawlovich et al. (2006) evidence behind them. Corridors outside those conditions should receive the arterial-compatible structural elements with independent support (median refuge islands, raised crossings at transit stops, signal-timing speed management), explicitly framed as monitored pilots.

**Third, speed limit reductions are worth adopting only as enablers, not as standalone treatments.** The 1-to-3-mph standalone effect is real but small; the value of a lower posted limit is that it lowers the enforcement threshold and the design speed for reconstruction, and the nonlinear injury physics converts even small operating-speed changes into meaningful fatality changes.

**Fourth, the city should not replicate the literature's evaluation failures.** The appropriation should reserve funds to instrument parallel corridors before treatment, commit to at least five years of follow-up, apply empirical Bayes correction using the state's crash database, and report severity-stratified outcomes. A city this size treating five corridors is, in effect, running the study the literature is missing; a modest evaluation budget converts that from a liability into a contribution.

The disagreements in this literature are real but legible. They do not counsel paralysis; they counsel buying the corrected effect sizes, designing for the failure modes the studies document, and measuring what the studies did not.
