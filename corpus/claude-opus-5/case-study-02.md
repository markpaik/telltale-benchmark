# Consolidating Emergency Dispatch in Cahaba Bend County, Alabama

## A Case Study in Multi-Jurisdictional Public Safety Consolidation, 2021–2025

---

## I. The County and Its Four Centers

Cahaba Bend County occupies a stretch of central Alabama where the river bends west before rejoining the coastal plain, and its 264,000 residents are distributed unevenly across a county seat of 71,000, three smaller incorporated municipalities, and a large unincorporated remainder of farmland, timber tracts, and a growing band of exurban subdivisions along the interstate corridor. Like most American counties of its size, it grew its emergency communications infrastructure the way sediment accumulates: one deposit at a time, each responding to a local need, none of them planned in relation to the others.

By 2021 the county operated four separate public safety answering points. The largest, run by the Sheriff's Office, handled all unincorporated calls and served as the fallback for the others. The county seat operated its own center inside the police department. Two of the three remaining municipalities operated a joint center under an intergovernmental agreement dating to 1997. The fourth municipality, the smallest and the most recently incorporated, contracted with a regional fire district whose dispatch floor sat eleven miles outside the county line.

Eighty-eight telecommunicators staffed these four rooms. They worked on four dispatch platforms that could not exchange data. The Sheriff's Office ran a computer-aided dispatch system installed in 2009 and last meaningfully upgraded in 2016. The county seat ran a different vendor's product purchased in 2014. The joint center ran a system that its vendor had placed in end-of-life status in 2019 and continued to support only under a custom maintenance agreement priced at a premium. The fire district ran a fourth platform entirely.

The practical consequence was that a call crossing a jurisdictional boundary — which in a county with this geography happened constantly — had to be transferred by voice. A caller reporting a rollover on the interstate might reach the county seat's center, which would determine the incident sat 400 yards past the city limit, and would then conference in the Sheriff's Office, repeat the location verbally, and hang up. Every transfer reintroduced the possibility of error in the address, the callback number, and the nature code. Every transfer added time. There was no shared incident number, so the same crash could exist as three separate records in three separate systems, and reconciling them for after-action review or for state reporting required someone to sit down with printouts.

The numbers described a system under strain. Average time from call answer to unit assignment stood at three minutes and 41 seconds across the four centers, a figure Emergency Communications Director Ramon Escalante had begun tracking uniformly in 2020 after discovering that each center defined the interval differently and that two of them had been reporting a shorter measurement that excluded queue time. Abandoned calls — callers who hung up before a telecommunicator answered — ran at 7.9 percent countywide, with the joint center's rate approaching 11 percent during evening peaks. Annual dispatch overtime across the four centers totaled $2.1 million. Annual turnover among telecommunicators reached 34 percent, meaning the county was continuously training replacements for roughly thirty positions a year, and a substantial share of the floor at any given moment consisted of people in their first eighteen months.

Escalante had been in the role since 2018. He was not a career dispatcher — he had come out of county emergency management — and his early tenure had been spent largely on the discovery that he did not actually direct three of the four centers. His authority ran to the Sheriff's center by delegation and to countywide coordination by title, but the municipal centers reported to municipal chiefs, and the fire district center reported to a board on which the county held one seat of seven. When he began publishing a single quarterly performance report covering all four, in early 2021, two of the four center supervisors initially declined to submit data.

County Administrator Delphine Roussard had arrived in 2019 from a larger county in another state. She inherited a general fund under pressure from a jail expansion and a road resurfacing backlog, and she inherited the dispatch overtime line as a recurring embarrassment in budget hearings, where it had exceeded its appropriation in each of the preceding six years. Her interest in consolidation began as a cost question and only later became an operational one.

## II. Building the Case, 2021–2023

The consolidation proposal that Roussard and Escalante brought forward in late 2022 was not their first attempt. A 2015 study commissioned by a prior administration had recommended merging the county seat's center with the Sheriff's, and had died without a vote. A 2018 discussion had reached the point of a draft intergovernmental agreement before the joint center's two municipalities withdrew over governance terms. Escalante was aware that anyone he spoke to about consolidation had heard the pitch before and had watched it fail, and he shaped his approach accordingly: he assumed skepticism was the baseline and that the burden of proof sat entirely with him.

The first eighteen months were spent almost entirely on measurement rather than persuasion. Escalante's view — which turned out to be correct — was that the previous attempts had failed because they argued from principle. Consolidation was more efficient; consolidation was best practice; consolidation was what peer counties had done. None of that moved a police chief who believed his officers were dispatched well and who suspected that a county center would deprioritize his calls. Escalante decided the argument had to be made in numbers specific to Cahaba Bend and in scenarios that local officials recognized.

The measurement program ran from early 2021 through mid-2022. It produced four categories of evidence.

**Transfer volume and transfer cost.** Escalante's staff sampled 11,400 calls across the four centers and coded each for whether it required a voice transfer. Nineteen percent did. Median time added by a transfer was 47 seconds. In 3.4 percent of transferred calls, the receiving center recorded a location that differed materially from the location captured by the originating center, meaning roughly one in thirty transferred calls carried a demonstrable address error into the field.

**Mutual aid failure.** The county's fire departments operated under automatic aid agreements that were, in principle, comprehensive. In practice the sampling showed that the closest appropriate unit was dispatched in 71 percent of structure fire responses. In the remainder, a unit from the dispatching jurisdiction was sent past a closer unit belonging to a jurisdiction on a different dispatch system, because the dispatching telecommunicator could not see the other jurisdiction's unit status.

**Staffing arithmetic.** Four centers required four minimum staffing floors. The joint center's overnight minimum was two positions; its overnight call volume averaged 3.1 calls per hour. The fire district's arrangement was similar. Escalante calculated that a single center could cover the county's aggregate overnight volume with five positions where the four centers collectively staffed nine, and that the overtime driver was not aggregate volume but the inability to flex staff across facilities when someone called in sick.

**Turnover cause.** Escalante's team conducted exit interviews for every departing telecommunicator during the study period — 41 interviews. The most frequently cited reason was schedule instability driven by mandatory overtime, cited in 27. The second was lack of advancement, cited in 19; in centers of eight to twelve people, there was essentially nowhere to be promoted to.

By mid-2022 Roussard and Escalante had a proposal. A single consolidated center, purpose-built in a county-owned facility, running one dispatch platform, staffed by the existing 88 telecommunicators under a unified county employer with a common pay scale and a common promotional ladder. Projected cost: $18.6 million, comprising facility construction and hardening, the dispatch platform and its integration work, radio system interfaces, furniture and consoles, backup power, and a training and transition budget.

The funding structure was where the political work happened. Eleven million dollars would come from county general obligation bonds. Five point two million would come from a state homeland security and emergency communications grant for which Escalante had been assembling an application since 2021 and which required demonstrated multi-jurisdictional participation — a requirement that, usefully, made municipal buy-in a precondition of the largest single outside contribution. The remaining $2.4 million would be assessed to the four municipalities, allocated by a formula weighted 60 percent on call volume and 40 percent on population, phased across four fiscal years.

The case was made differently to each audience, which Escalante later described as the single most important tactical decision of the project.

**To the County Commission**, the argument was fiscal and risk-based. The $2.1 million overtime line was structural and would grow. The joint center's end-of-life platform represented an unquantified liability — if it failed, the county would be the fallback, and the county had no capacity to absorb it. The 2016-vintage Sheriff's system would require replacement within four years regardless of any consolidation decision, at an estimated $4.8 million, meaning a meaningful share of the consolidation cost was money the county would spend anyway.

**To the municipalities**, the argument was service and cost-avoidance. Each municipality received a jurisdiction-specific analysis showing its own transfer volume, its own mutual aid gaps, and its own projected assessment against its current dispatch operating cost. For two of the four, the assessment was lower than what they currently spent. For the county seat, it was higher in the first two years and lower thereafter. For the smallest municipality, the assessment was substantially higher than its fire district contract, and Escalante negotiated a five-year phase-in and a guaranteed service-level agreement with financial remedies to close the gap.

**To the telecommunicators**, the argument was pay, schedule, and career. The unified pay scale placed every telecommunicator at or above their current rate, with 61 of the 88 receiving an increase. The single facility meant a shift bid across 88 people rather than across 12, and Escalante committed publicly that mandatory overtime would be capped and that the cap would be enforced by the county rather than by a shift supervisor's discretion. Three supervisory and two training positions would be created that did not exist in the fragmented structure.

**To the public**, the argument was made almost entirely through the single most legible number Escalante possessed: the three-minute-41-second call-to-assignment interval, presented against the national benchmarks and against three peer Alabama counties that had consolidated. He avoided the word "efficiency" in public materials. The framing was time.

## III. The Sheriff, the Council Chair, and the May 2023 Vote

Sheriff Wanda Pruitt opposed the consolidation from the moment it was formally proposed and did so consistently, publicly, and on grounds that were substantially more coherent than proponents initially credited.

Pruitt's position rested on three arguments. The first was command and control: her deputies operated across 700 square miles of largely rural territory, frequently out of radio range, frequently alone, and she held that the dispatchers who managed their safety needed to sit inside her chain of command and needed to know her deputies personally. She had specific incidents to cite, including a 2019 case in which a dispatcher who recognized a deputy's voice stress on a routine traffic stop initiated a welfare check that turned out to be warranted. Her contention was that this kind of judgment does not survive scale.

The second was governance. Under the proposal, the consolidated center would report to Escalante, who reported to Roussard, who served at the pleasure of the Commission. Pruitt, an independently elected constitutional officer, would have no line authority over the people dispatching her deputies. She proposed instead a governance board with law enforcement majority representation; Roussard resisted, on the grounds that a board with operational authority and no budgetary accountability would reproduce the joint center's dysfunction at larger scale.

The third was risk. Four systems fail independently; one system fails once. Pruitt argued that the county was proposing to concentrate every dispatch function in a single building running a single platform, and that the redundancy inherent in fragmentation was a genuine asset that the business case treated purely as waste.

The proposal failed twice. In September 2022, the Commission deadlocked and the item was tabled for revision. In January 2023, a revised version with an expanded governance annex failed again when a commissioner who had signaled support voted no after the fire district's board formally objected to the loss of its dispatch revenue.

The version that came forward in May 2023 differed from its predecessors in four respects, each traceable to Pruitt's objections. It created a Communications Governance Board with nine seats — Sheriff, four municipal police chiefs or designees, two fire representatives, the EMS medical director, and the county administrator — holding approval authority over operating protocols, dispatch priority schemes, and the annual center budget prior to its submission to the Commission. It committed to geographic dispatch assignment, meaning telecommunicators would be assigned to consistent radio channels rather than rotated countywide, preserving the familiarity Pruitt valued. It funded a fully equipped backup center at the Sheriff's existing facility, capable of assuming full countywide dispatch within fifteen minutes, staffed during activation by recalled personnel. And it added a provision allowing any participating municipality to withdraw after year three with eighteen months' notice and a defined exit payment, which converted the arrangement from a permanent surrender of authority into something closer to a terminable contract.

Council Chair Elias Mwangi cast the deciding vote on May 16, 2023, in a 4–3 outcome. Mwangi had been publicly uncommitted for eight months. His stated reason, delivered in a floor statement, focused on the backup center and the withdrawal provision: he said he had been unwilling to vote for a proposal that could not be reversed and could not fail safely, and that the May version could do both. He also noted that the joint center's platform had experienced two unplanned outages in the preceding four months and that he did not believe the status quo was actually the stable option it was being portrayed as.

Pruitt, to her credit and to the project's considerable benefit, did not continue to fight after the vote. She stated publicly that she had lost the argument, that her deputies' safety now depended on the new center succeeding, and that her office would participate fully. She took her seat on the Governance Board and attended. Escalante later said that this single decision probably saved the project during the events of February 2025.

## IV. Procurement and the First Slip

The county issued its request for proposals in July 2023 and received five responses. Evaluation ran through October. The award went to Lattimore Public Safety Systems, a mid-sized vendor with roughly 140 agency installations, at a contract value of $7.9 million covering the dispatch platform, mapping, mobile data integration, radio interfaces, records interfaces to four existing law enforcement records systems, data migration from the four legacy platforms, and implementation services.

Lattimore was not the lowest bidder. It ranked second on price and first on technical evaluation, driven largely by its multi-agency dispatch model and by reference checks from two counties with comparable jurisdictional complexity. The selection committee, which included Pruitt's chief deputy and the county seat's police chief, was unanimous.

The contract included a go-live date of October 14, 2024, and liquidated damages of $30,000 per week for vendor-caused delay, capped at $1.2 million.

Facility work proceeded ahead of schedule. The county renovated a 14,000-square-foot county-owned building, installing 24 dispatch positions with room for 30, redundant generators, a hardened communications room, and a training suite. Construction completed in June 2024, four weeks early.

The software implementation did not go as well. The first significant problem was data migration. Four legacy systems held between them approximately 2.9 million historical incident records, 340,000 address points, and premise hazard information — the notes attached to an address warning that a resident is combative, that a dog is present, that a household member is oxygen-dependent — that had been entered over fifteen years with no common format and, in the joint center's case, substantial free-text entry into fields not designed for it. Lattimore's migration tooling assumed structured data. Reconciling the address point files alone consumed four months and required the county to hire two temporary GIS analysts.

The second problem was interface complexity. The RFP had specified interfaces to four records management systems. During design it emerged that two of those systems had been locally customized to a degree their own vendors could not fully document, and that one interface would require a middleware layer nobody had scoped.

By August 2024 the project was visibly behind. Escalante convened the Governance Board and recommended against attempting the October date. The Board agreed. A revised go-live of February 18, 2025 was set, and the county began assessing liquidated damages, ultimately accruing $540,000 for the delay period.

Information Technology Director Vikram Chaudhary, who had been involved but not central through 2024, moved into a more active role during the slip. He raised a concern in a September memo that would prove prescient: he noted that the failover testing plan for the integrated environment tested each component's failover individually but did not test failover of the mapping service under production-equivalent load with all dependent components live. Lattimore responded that the mapping service failover was a standard configuration validated across its installed base. The item was recorded as closed.

## V. February 18, 2025

The cutover began at 2:00 a.m. on Tuesday, February 18, 2025 — a deliberately chosen low-volume window. The plan called for all four centers' call-taking to shift to the consolidated facility at 2:00, with dispatch functions following at 3:00, and with the legacy systems remaining live in read-only mode.

The first ninety minutes went well. Call-taking cut over on schedule. Twenty-two telecommunicators were on the floor, roughly double normal overnight staffing. Lattimore had six implementation staff on site and four more available remotely. Chaudhary was present. Escalante was present. Pruitt was present, in the observation room, and stayed for the duration.

At 3:47 a.m., approximately forty minutes after dispatch functions transferred, the mapping service became unresponsive.

The consolidated system used the mapping service for more than displaying a map. It resolved addresses against the county's master address point file, determined which agency's jurisdiction contained a given location, calculated recommended units based on unit position and road network travel time, and populated the incident record with geographic identifiers. When it stopped responding, telecommunicators could still answer calls and could still type into the incident form, but the system could not validate an address, could not determine jurisdiction, and could not recommend units. The dispatch screens showed incidents with blank location validation and no recommended response.

The failover did not engage. The secondary mapping service instance was running and healthy. The mechanism that should have redirected requests to it did not, and the primary instance did not fail cleanly — it continued accepting requests and holding them, so from the perspective of the monitoring configuration the service appeared alive.

At 3:52 Escalante made the decision to go to manual operations. Telecommunicators moved to paper — physical incident cards, radio dispatch by voice, unit status tracked on a magnetic board that had been installed in the new center at Pruitt's insistence during design review and that several people had privately regarded as a nostalgic gesture.

The outage lasted four hours and forty minutes, resolving at 8:27 a.m. During that window the center handled 812 calls on paper. Volume climbed steeply from 6:00 a.m. as the county woke up, and the last ninety minutes of the outage overlapped the beginning of the morning peak.

The manual operation worked, in the sense that no call went unanswered and no incident was lost. It worked because Escalante's transition plan had included manual operations training for all 88 telecommunicators — eight hours each, which had been the single most criticized element of the training budget — and because the veteran dispatchers on the floor had spent parts of their careers doing exactly this. Two telecommunicators hired within the previous year later said in after-action interviews that they had found the training tedious and had not expected to use it, and that on the morning of February 18 it was the only reason they were functional.

There were consequences nonetheless. Average call-to-assignment time during the outage exceeded eleven minutes. A structure fire in the county seat's northern district received an initial response from a station 2.4 miles farther away than the closest available unit, because the dispatcher working from a wall map did not have current status on the closer station's engine, which had returned from a call twelve minutes earlier. There was no injury and property loss was not materially affected, but it was recorded and it was reported.

A cardiac arrest call at 6:52 a.m. was dispatched in two minutes and eight seconds — within acceptable range — but the patient did not survive, and the family subsequently and understandably asked whether the county's computer problems had contributed. Review concluded they had not. The county's public communications about that call, in the days that followed, were among the most difficult parts of the entire episode.

Reconstruction of the paper records into the system took nine days and required 340 hours of overtime.

## VI. Political Fallout

The outage became public within hours, initially through a scanner-following social media account and then through local television by midday.

Two municipalities threatened to withdraw. The smallest — the one that had left a fire district contract and taken on a higher assessment — sent a formal notice of intent to invoke the withdrawal provision, and its mayor stated publicly that the city had been sold a system that did not work. The joint center's larger partner municipality passed a council resolution demanding a third-party review and suspending its next assessment payment pending the outcome.

The state grant administrator opened a review on February 25, notifying the county that $5.2 million in awarded funds were subject to potential recovery if the review found the county had failed to meet the operational readiness conditions of the award.

Two commissioners who had voted yes in May 2023 publicly expressed regret. A citizens' group formed and appeared at the March Commission meeting.

Pruitt did not join the criticism. Asked directly by a reporter on February 19 whether the outage validated her opposition, she said that she had voted no, that she had lost, that she had spent the morning of the eighteenth watching dispatchers keep the county safe with paper and a magnet board, and that the question of whether consolidation was correct had been settled twenty-one months earlier while the question of whether it would work was being settled now. She said her office would continue to participate. Roussard later described that statement as the moment the project stopped being politically survivable-in-theory and became politically survivable-in-fact.

Chaudhary's technical investigation produced findings within eleven days. The failure originated in the mapping service's health check configuration. The monitoring probe validated that the service process was running and accepting TCP connections; it did not validate that the service was returning correct responses within a threshold. When the primary instance entered a state in which it accepted connections but could not complete geocoding requests — triggered, the investigation determined, by a resource exhaustion condition arising from the volume of concurrent requests generated by 22 simultaneously active positions against a county-scale address file — the health check continued reporting healthy and the failover logic, which keyed on the health check, never triggered.

This was the untested failover path Chaudhary had flagged in September. His memo had asked for failover testing under production-equivalent load with all dependent components live. That test had not been performed. The individual failover test that was performed had killed the primary service process outright, which produced a clean failure the health check detected correctly.

The finding was significant in three ways. It established vendor fault, since the health check configuration was Lattimore's standard implementation and the concurrency condition was foreseeable. It established a county process failure, since Chaudhary had identified the gap and the county's own governance had accepted a vendor assurance in place of a test. And it established that the cause was narrow and correctable, which mattered enormously for the political survival of the project — the county could say truthfully that the system had not failed broadly.

Roussard's handling of the March Commission meeting drew on all three findings. She presented Chaudhary's report in full, including the part unfavorable to the county. She stated that the county had accepted a vendor assurance where it should have required a test, that this was her responsibility, and that the county's remediation plan assumed nothing further would be taken on assurance. Escalante presented the operational data from the outage: 812 calls, none unanswered, none lost, one documented mutual aid error. The Commission did not take action against the project.

## VII. Remediation

The county's response had four elements, and their sequencing mattered more than any of them individually.

**Vendor accountability.** The county held Lattimore to $900,000 in total penalties — the $540,000 in accrued delay damages plus $360,000 negotiated in settlement of the outage, structured as $200,000 cash credit and $160,000 in additional services including eighteen months of extended on-site support. The county did not pursue termination. Roussard's reasoning, presented to the Commission, was that the county had a partially migrated system, 2.9 million converted records, and four legacy platforms of which two were already decommissioned, and that termination would mean starting over at full cost with an eighteen-month gap. The relevant question was not whether Lattimore had performed acceptably but whether any alternative produced a better outcome.

**Parallel operation.** The county restored the legacy systems where they had been decommissioned and ran old and new in parallel for eleven weeks, from late February through mid-May 2025. This was expensive — it required maintaining two sets of infrastructure, retaining legacy vendor support, and asking telecommunicators to enter incidents into both systems during a defined validation window. It was also the single change that most restored confidence, because it meant a failure of the new system had a fallback that did not involve paper.

Parallel operation surfaced eleven defects that had not appeared in testing, including two that would have produced incorrect unit recommendations in specific geographic edge cases along the river where jurisdiction boundaries follow the historic channel rather than the current one.

**Retraining.** All 88 telecommunicators went through a second training cycle, 24 hours each, delivered in small groups on the production system with live-but-parallel data. The retraining was designed around the actual failure: it emphasized recognizing degraded system states, escalating early, and transitioning to manual operations as a deliberate decision rather than a panic response. Manual operations training was increased from eight hours to sixteen.

**Phased functional migration.** The most consequential change. The original plan had moved all dispatch functions in a single cutover. The revised plan moved them in three waves — law enforcement first, then fire, then emergency medical — separated by four to five weeks.

The logic was that each discipline exercises the system differently. Law enforcement dispatch is high-volume, short-duration, and heavily dependent on unit status and officer safety functions. Fire dispatch is lower-volume but depends critically on run cards, automatic aid, and multi-unit assignment. EMS dispatch depends on medical priority protocols and hospital status. A single cutover exercised all three simultaneously against a system that had never carried the full combination. Phasing meant each wave carried a fraction of the total load, that defects surfaced in a context where the discipline affected could be isolated, and that the two disciplines not yet migrated remained on known-working legacy systems.

Law enforcement migrated March 25, 2025. Fire migrated April 29. EMS migrated June 3. Each wave was preceded by a load test at 150 percent of projected concurrent transaction volume, with all dependent services live, and by a deliberate failover test in which Chaudhary's team induced the specific failure mode that had caused the February outage. The mapping service health check had been reconfigured to validate response correctness and latency rather than process liveness.

The fire wave surfaced a defect in automatic aid run card logic affecting three stations. It was corrected in nine days and the wave completed on schedule. The EMS wave was clean.

## VIII. Results

The county's June 2025 measurement, taken thirty days after full migration and covering a full month of consolidated operation across all disciplines:

| Measure | Before (2022 baseline) | After (June 2025) | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Call answer to unit assignment | 3 min 41 sec | 2 min 14 sec | −39.4% |
| Abandoned call rate | 7.9% | 2.1% | −73.4% |
| Annual dispatch overtime | $2.1M | $1.3M | −38.1% |
| Annual telecommunicator turnover | 34% | 19% | −44.1% |
| Voice transfers between agencies | 19% of calls | 1.2% of calls | −93.7% |
| Closest-unit dispatch, structure fires | 71% | 94% | +23 pts |
| Telecommunicators | 88 | 84 | −4 |
| Dispatch centers | 4 | 1 (+1 backup) | −3 |

Several of these deserve unpacking.

The **87-second improvement in call-to-assignment** is the headline number, and roughly 40 percent of it is attributable to the elimination of voice transfers. The remainder comes from automated unit recommendation, from address validation that no longer requires the telecommunicator to confirm a location verbally, and from queue management across a single larger pool of answering positions.

The **abandoned call rate** improvement is the largest proportional change and the one Escalante considers most meaningful, because abandoned calls are the ones nobody measures the consequences of. A 7.9 percent abandonment rate against the county's annual volume meant roughly 21,000 callers per year hung up before reaching anyone. At 2.1 percent that figure is about 5,600. The driver is queue pooling: under fragmentation, a caller reaching a two-position center at a busy moment waited regardless of whether eighteen positions sat available eleven miles away.

The **overtime reduction** of $800,000 was below the $1.1 million the business case had projected. Escalante attributes the shortfall to two things: the county staffed the consolidated center more conservatively than modeled, at 84 telecommunicators against a modeled 78, and the phased migration and parallel operation consumed overtime that a clean cutover would not have. He expects the figure to decline further as the center matures.

The **turnover reduction** from 34 to 19 percent is, in Escalante's assessment, the result that will matter most over ten years. Turnover at 34 percent meant continuous training load, continuous inexperience on the floor, and a recruiting pipeline that could never get ahead. At 19 percent the center trains roughly sixteen replacements a year instead of thirty. The drivers appear to be the unified pay scale, the enforced overtime cap, and the promotional ladder — three supervisory and two training positions were created, and four of the five were filled internally.

The **headcount reduction of four** was accomplished entirely through attrition. The county committed at the outset that no telecommunicator would lose employment, and none did. Two of the four eliminated positions were vacancies that were simply not filled. This commitment was expensive relative to the modeled staffing and it is the primary reason the overtime savings underperformed. Roussard regards it as the correct trade: the project could not have survived February 2025 with a workforce that believed the county was maneuvering to cut jobs.

The **closest-unit dispatch improvement for structure fires**, from 71 to 94 percent, is the result with the clearest connection to outcomes. The remaining 6 percent consists largely of cases where the closest unit was unavailable for reasons the system correctly accounted for.

The state grant review closed in August 2025 with no recovery of funds. The reviewer's report cited the county's after-action documentation, the phased remediation, and the absence of any lost or unanswered call during the outage. It included a finding regarding pre-cutover testing adequacy and required the county to submit a revised testing protocol, which it did.

Both municipalities that had threatened withdrawal remained. The smaller one withdrew its notice in May 2025 after the law enforcement wave completed successfully. The larger resumed assessment payments in June and released its demand for third-party review after receiving Chaudhary's report.

## IX. The True Cost

The project's final cost was $21.4 million against an $18.6 million projection — an overrun of $2.8 million, or 15.1 percent.

| Component | Projected | Actual | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Facility construction and hardening | $6.2M | $6.0M | −$0.2M |
| Dispatch platform, licensing, integration | $7.9M | $7.9M | — |
| Radio interfaces and console equipment | $2.4M | $2.6M | +$0.2M |
| Backup center | $0.9M | $1.1M | +$0.2M |
| Data migration | $0.6M | $1.9M | +$1.3M |
| Training and transition | $0.4M | $1.2M | +$0.8M |
| Parallel operation (unbudgeted) | — | $0.9M | +$0.9M |
| Project management and contingency | $0.2M | $0.7M | +$0.5M |
| Vendor penalties | — | −$0.9M | −$0.9M |
| **Total** | **$18.6M** | **$21.4M** | **+$2.8M** |

The $900,000 in vendor penalties offsets roughly a third of the overrun. Absent them the overrun would have been $3.7 million, or 19.9 percent.

The dominant overrun is **data migration**, at more than triple its budget. This was underestimated at the RFP stage, and Roussard's retrospective assessment is that the county could not have estimated it correctly without work it did not perform: nobody profiled the four legacy databases before writing the specification. The county specified migration of four systems' data without knowing what condition that data was in. The premise hazard records alone — the notes about combative residents and oxygen-dependent patients — required manual review of approximately 31,000 entries because free-text content could not be reliably parsed and because a wrong premise hazard record is worse than none.

The **training and transition** overrun of $800,000 is almost entirely retraining after February 18. The **parallel operation** cost of $900,000 was wholly unbudgeted; the original plan had no parallel period.

There are costs outside the capital number. The eleven-week parallel operation consumed staff attention that had operational cost not captured on any ledger. The reputational cost of February 18 is unquantifiable but real, and Roussard notes that the county's next bond issue drew questions from underwriters about project management capacity. The 340 hours of overtime for paper record reconstruction appears in the operating budget, not the project budget.

Against these, the offsetting item the business case identified holds: the $4.8 million the county would have spent replacing the Sheriff's aging platform by 2026 was not spent. Netting that against the overrun, the county spent $2.8 million more than projected to avoid $4.8 million it would otherwise have spent.

The recurring picture is favorable. Consolidated annual operating cost, including the platform maintenance agreement, facility operations, and the backup center, runs approximately $1.6 million below the aggregate of the four legacy centers' operating costs. The county projects payback on the incremental capital within roughly nine years, against a facility and platform expected to serve fifteen to twenty.

## X. What the County Would Sequence Differently

Roussard, Escalante, and Chaudhary each provided a retrospective assessment. Their conclusions converge on sequencing rather than on the decision itself. All three maintain that consolidation was correct. All three believe the county arrived at the right destination through an unnecessarily damaging route.

**Profile the data before writing the specification.** The single largest cost overrun and a meaningful share of the schedule slip trace to a specification written without knowledge of what was actually in the four legacy databases. A three-month, roughly $150,000 data profiling engagement conducted before the RFP would have produced a specification with realistic migration scope, a realistic timeline, and vendor proposals priced against known rather than assumed conditions. The county instead discovered the condition of its data after signing a contract that assumed a different condition.

**Phase the functional migration from the beginning, not as a remedy.** The three-wave migration was adopted after the failure. It should have been the plan. There was never a defensible reason to move law enforcement, fire, and EMS dispatch simultaneously; the disciplines exercise different system functions, and moving them together meant the first production test of the integrated system was also the moment of maximum exposure. Escalante's assessment is that this was a failure of imagination reinforced by a bias toward a clean break — a belief that a single cutover would be less confusing than a staged one. The staged migration proved less confusing, because each stage was smaller.

**Plan for parallel operation as a budgeted certainty.** The eleven-week parallel period was the most effective confidence-restoring measure the county took, and it was entirely unplanned and unbudgeted. Roussard's conclusion is that any dispatch consolidation should budget parallel operation from the outset, treat it as a normal cost of transition rather than an admission of doubt, and define in advance the criteria for exiting it.

**Never accept a vendor assurance in place of a test on a failure path.** Chaudhary identified the exact gap in September 2024 and was answered with an assurance that the configuration was standard and validated elsewhere. The assurance was true and irrelevant: it was standard, it was validated elsewhere, and it did not hold at Cahaba Bend's concurrency and address-file scale. Chaudhary's revised protocol now requires that every failure path in the critical set be tested under production-equivalent load with all dependent components live, that tests be witnessed by county staff, and that no test be closed on vendor representation. The county's testing protocol also now requires that induced-failure tests use realistic failure modes rather than clean process termination — the February failure was invisible precisely because the service did not die, and a test that kills a process cannot detect a health check that cannot distinguish a hung service from a healthy one.

**Preserve manual capability and train it seriously.** The eight hours of manual operations training was the most criticized item in the training budget and it was the reason 812 calls were handled without loss. The magnet board Pruitt insisted on during design review, regarded by several people as sentimental, was in continuous use for four hours and forty minutes. Escalante's conclusion is that manual capability in an emergency communications center is not a legacy artifact but a required layer of the resilience architecture, and it should be budgeted, trained, and exercised on a schedule.

**Give the opposition something real, and then keep them inside.** The governance board, the geographic assignment commitment, the funded backup center, and the withdrawal provision were all concessions to Pruitt's objections, and all four were operationally correct on their own merits — the backup center in particular, which the original proposal treated as optional. The concessions also converted an opponent into a participant, and Pruitt's conduct on February 19 was worth more to the project than any communications strategy the county could have executed. Roussard's assessment is that the county was fortunate rather than skillful in this: Pruitt behaved better than she had to, and a project should not depend on that.

**Guarantee employment, and understand what it costs.** The commitment that no telecommunicator would lose their job cost the county something real — the consolidated center is staffed six positions above model, and that gap is the principal reason overtime savings underperformed projection by $300,000 annually. Both Roussard and Escalante regard it as the right decision anyway, and specifically as the reason the workforce performed on February 18 rather than treating the failure as vindication. Workforce commitments in consolidation are not gestures; they are the purchase of discretionary effort at the moment discretionary effort determines whether the project survives.

## XI. What a Reader in a Similar Position Should Take

Cahaba Bend County's consolidation succeeded. Call-to-assignment time fell by 87 seconds. Abandoned calls fell by nearly three-quarters. Turnover fell by fifteen points. Closest-unit dispatch for structure fires improved by 23 points, which means the county's fire response now sends the nearest engine in situations where it previously did not, and that is the kind of improvement that eventually appears in an outcome nobody attributes to a dispatch project.

It also cost 15 percent more than projected, went live four months late, failed publicly and badly on its first morning, and spent three months in genuine danger of political collapse.

The instructive part is that the failure was neither random nor unforeseeable. It was identified in writing five months in advance by the county's own IT director, and it was closed on a vendor's assurance. The gap between knowing and testing is where this project nearly died, and it is a gap that exists in most implementations of most systems, because testing a failure path under production-equivalent load is expensive, schedule-consuming, and always competing against a go-live date that someone has already announced.

The second instructive element is that the recovery measures — phased migration, parallel operation, retraining, real failure-path testing — were all available before the cutover and all cost less then than after. The county spent $2.8 million in overrun, and roughly $1.7 million of it was remediation of a failure that a fraction of that amount, spent earlier, would likely have prevented. This is the ordinary economics of pre-implementation rigor, which is always more expensive than it appears and always cheaper than the alternative.

The third is that consolidation projects are political before, during, and after they are technical, and that the political structure built in advance determines whether the technical failure is survivable. Cahaba Bend survived February 18 because it had a governance board that gave stakeholders standing, a withdrawal provision that made the arrangement reversible, a workforce that had been treated well, a sheriff who had lost the argument honorably and said so, and an administrator who presented the county's own failings alongside the vendor's. None of that was in the technical plan. All of it was load-bearing.

The final point concerns what "success" means on this timeline. In February 2025 this project was, by any contemporaneous account, a disaster. By June 2025 it was delivering every operational improvement it had promised. The four-month distance between those two assessments is the interval in which most troubled public projects are killed — not because they cannot be fixed, but because the fixing happens in public, slowly, while the failure remains vivid. Escalante's summary, offered to a peer county considering a similar consolidation, was this: build the political structure that lets you survive the bad morning, because you will have one, and the only question is whether the arrangement you built in advance is strong enough to hold while you fix it.
