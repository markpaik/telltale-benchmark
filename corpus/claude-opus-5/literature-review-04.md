# Speed Management on Verde Springs Arterials: A Review of the Evidence

**Prepared for:** Mayor and Council; City Manager
**Prepared by:** Anand Sundaresan, Principal Management Analyst, Office of Performance and Budget; Keiko Villalpando, Transportation Planner, Development Services
**Date:** July 29, 2026
**Reference:** Council Resolution 2026-14 (speed management appropriation, $6,500,000); pre-RFP evidence review

---

## 1. Introduction: The Question and Its Scope

Nineteen people died in traffic crashes in Verde Springs during calendar year 2025, against nine in 2021. Fourteen of the nineteen were on foot. Fourteen of the nineteen died on five arterial corridors that together carry perhaps eleven percent of the city's centerline mileage. At a population of 118,000, nineteen deaths is a rate of roughly 16 per 100,000 residents — above the national municipal average and in the range reported by the worst-performing Sunbelt cities of comparable size. A pedestrian share of 74 percent is roughly three to four times the national share. Those two facts, taken together, describe a specific problem rather than a general one: people walking are being killed by vehicles traveling at high speeds on wide, signalized, commercially fronted arterials.

Council appropriated $6.5 million for speed management and directed this office to review the evidence before staff release a request for proposals for automated enforcement. This review is written to that charge. It asks a bounded question:

> For a city of roughly 118,000 people with a concentrated arterial pedestrian fatality problem, what magnitude and durability of speed and injury reduction does the published evidence support for (a) automated speed enforcement, (b) roadway reconfiguration including road diets, (c) posted speed limit changes, and (d) physical traffic calming — and under what design and deployment conditions?

Three boundaries deserve statement at the outset.

**First, this is a review of effect estimates, not a recommendation on procurement.** The RFP will be drafted by staff and approved by Council. What this office can supply is a defensible expectation of what each intervention buys, so that the specifications, the evaluation clauses, and the Council's own performance expectations are calibrated to the evidence rather than to vendor literature or to the strongest published figures.

**Second, the 2021-to-2025 comparison should not anchor expectations.** Annual fatality counts in a city this size are small numbers. If the underlying annual risk in Verde Springs were stable at fourteen deaths per year, ordinary Poisson variation would produce years with nine deaths and years with nineteen deaths without any change in the underlying hazard whatsoever. That does not mean nothing has changed — the multi-year trend, the pedestrian share, and the corridor concentration all point to a real and worsening problem — but it does mean that any program launched at a peak will appear to succeed for reasons that have nothing to do with the program. This phenomenon, regression to the mean, is not an abstraction in this literature. It is the single largest source of disagreement among the studies reviewed below, and it is the reason a substantial share of the automated enforcement literature reports effects that later, better-designed work cannot reproduce (Hauer 1997; Persaud and Lyon 2007).

**Third, the interventions in scope are not substitutes.** They operate on different mechanisms, at different speeds of implementation, with different legal exposure, and different failure modes. Automated enforcement changes driver behavior through the threat of a citation and depends entirely on continued statutory authority and continued political support. Reconfiguration changes the road so that the behavior is harder to perform and requires no continuing enforcement, but costs more and cannot be reversed cheaply. Posted limit changes cost almost nothing and, absent other changes, do almost nothing. Traffic calming has the strongest evidence base of the four on residential streets and the weakest applicability to six-lane arterials. Council should read the sections below as describing a portfolio problem, not a selection problem.

The review proceeds as follows: the search and screening protocol (§2); the synthesis, organized by intervention and by the points of conflict within each (§3); an account of why the studies disagree (§4); gaps in the evidence base (§5); and implications for Verde Springs specifically (§6).

## 2. How the Literature Was Searched

Searches were run in April and May 2026 across four sources: the Transport Research International Documentation (TRID) database, Scopus, PubMed, and the report series of the state transportation agency together with the report series of comparable state agencies indexed in TRID. The date range was January 1995 through March 2026. The 1995 start was chosen because automated speed enforcement in North America is essentially a post-1995 phenomenon and because evaluation practice in road safety changed materially with the diffusion of empirical Bayes methods in the mid-1990s (Hauer 1997).

Search strings combined intervention terms (*speed camera*, *automated speed enforcement*, *photo radar*, *point-to-point*, *section control*, *road diet*, *lane reduction*, *4-to-3 conversion*, *roadway reconfiguration*, *speed limit reduction*, *posted speed*, *traffic calming*, *speed hump*, *road narrowing*, *curb extension*) with outcome terms (*crash*, *collision*, *injury*, *fatality*, *pedestrian*, *operating speed*, *85th percentile*) and setting terms (*urban arterial*, *urban street*, *city*). Reference lists of included studies and of eight prior systematic reviews were hand-searched. No language restriction was applied at the search stage; two non-English records were excluded at full-text stage for want of translation capacity, and both are noted in the extraction sheet.

**Screening.** The search returned 1,684 unique records after de-duplication. Title and abstract screening excluded 1,393. Two hundred ninety-one records were read in full. Eighty-eight studies were included.

**Inclusion criteria.** Studies were included if they reported a quantitative effect estimate on crash frequency, crash severity, injury outcomes, or operating speed; if the intervention was implemented on a public road rather than simulated; and if enough detail was reported to classify the evaluation design. Simulator studies, stated-preference studies, and modeling exercises without an implemented treatment were excluded. Studies reporting only citation counts or only revenue were excluded unless they also reported speed or crash outcomes.

**Included studies by category.** Twenty-six automated enforcement evaluations; twenty-two roadway reconfiguration and road diet studies; seventeen posted speed limit change studies; twelve traffic calming studies; and eleven cross-cutting studies covering the speed–injury risk relationship, evaluation methodology, and distributional analysis. The eleven cross-cutting studies are not intervention evaluations and are used in this review to interpret the other seventy-seven.

**Quality coding.** Every included evaluation was coded on evaluation design using a five-level scheme: (1) naive before-after, no comparison; (2) before-after with a comparison group but no regression-to-the-mean adjustment; (3) before-after with empirical Bayes or full Bayes adjustment; (4) interrupted time series or panel with adequate pre-period; (5) randomized or quasi-randomized allocation. Studies were also coded for treatment-site selection rule (crash-based, speed-based, political, or unstated), length of follow-up, whether spillover to adjacent untreated sites was measured, whether the comparison group could have been contaminated by spillover, and the exposure denominator used. These codes carry most of the interpretive weight in §4.

**Known limitations of the search.** Three should be stated. Grey literature coverage is uneven; state agency reports are indexed inconsistently and it is likely that some evaluations with null results were never published in any indexed form. Publication bias in road safety meta-analysis is documented and non-trivial (Elvik 2011). And the transferability of effect estimates across countries and roadway environments is itself contested; the majority of the automated enforcement evidence comes from the United Kingdom, Australia, the Netherlands, and Norway, where road design, fleet composition, default urban speed limits, and enforcement law differ from Arizona in ways that are not adjustable by statistical means.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 The speed–injury relationship: the premise both sides accept

Before turning to the interventions, it is worth establishing what is not in dispute. Across the eleven cross-cutting studies, the relationship between impact speed and pedestrian injury outcome is steep, non-linear, and consistent. Tefft (2013), using a nationally representative U.S. sample, estimates the risk of death for a struck pedestrian at approximately 10 percent at 23 mph, 25 percent at 32 mph, 50 percent at 42 mph, and 75 percent at 50 mph. Rosén and Sander (2009) and Rosén, Stigson, and Sander (2011), working from European data, place the curve slightly lower but with the same shape. The practical implication is that on a corridor where free-flow speeds run 45 mph, moving the striking-speed distribution down by five miles per hour is worth substantially more in survival terms than the same five miles per hour on a corridor running 30 mph.

The aggregate relationship between mean traffic speed and crash frequency is more contested but points the same direction. Nilsson's power model, as extended by Elvik (2009, 2013), holds that fatal crashes scale roughly with the fourth power of the speed ratio and injury crashes with roughly the second. Under those exponents, a one mile per hour reduction in mean speed on a 35 mph corridor implies roughly a 5 to 6 percent reduction in injury crashes and roughly an 11 percent reduction in fatal crashes. Aarts and van Schagen (2006), reviewing the same literature, accept the direction but caution that the exponents were estimated largely from rural and high-speed environments and that urban arterial applications extrapolate beyond the estimation range. Elvik's own 2013 reparameterization moves from power functions to exponential functions partly for this reason.

This matters for §3.3. The power model implies that even the small speed reductions produced by sign changes are worth something. The crash-outcome studies of sign changes frequently find nothing. Both cannot be straightforwardly true, and the resolution is discussed below.

### 3.2 Automated speed enforcement: the effect size depends on the design of the study

Twenty-six evaluations were included. Sorted by design quality, they do not tell one story; they tell two, and the boundary between them is methodological rather than geographic.

**The high estimates.** Naive and comparison-group before-after studies, particularly from the first decade of camera deployment, report large reductions. The UK national safety camera programme's four-year evaluation reported a 42 percent reduction in killed and seriously injured casualties at camera sites (Gains et al. 2005). Christie et al. (2003) reported substantial reductions from mobile cameras in South Wales. Chen, Meckle, and Wilson (2002) reported reductions on the order of 20 percent in a British Columbia photo radar corridor. The Cochrane review (Wilson et al. 2010), which synthesized thirty-five studies, reported reductions in injury crashes in the range of 8 to 50 percent and fatal and serious crashes of 11 to 44 percent in the camera vicinity — but graded the evidence as low quality precisely because so few studies controlled for regression to the mean or for secular trend.

**The lower estimates.** Studies that apply empirical Bayes adjustment, use matched comparison sites, or model the underlying trend produce consistently smaller numbers. Høye's (2014) meta-analysis, which explicitly corrected for publication bias and study design, found effects on injury crashes of roughly 20 percent for fixed cameras within the immediate camera zone but substantially less when measured over the surrounding corridor, and found that a meaningful fraction of the raw effect in earlier studies was attributable to trend and regression artifacts. Mountain, Hirst, and Maher (2005), comparing camera sites against other speed management treatments in the UK, found that roughly a third of the apparent camera effect in unadjusted analyses disappeared under regression-to-the-mean correction. De Pauw et al. (2014), evaluating fixed cameras in Belgium, found injury crash reductions in the range of 10 to 15 percent — real, but far from the headline figures.

The North American evidence closest to Verde Springs' setting sits in this lower band. Retting, Farmer, and McCartt (2008) and Hu and McCartt (2016), evaluating the Montgomery County, Maryland, program over successive periods, found reductions in the likelihood that a crash involved a fatality or incapacitating injury on the order of 19 percent on camera-eligible roads, with speed reductions of roughly 8 to 10 percent in the proportion of vehicles exceeding the limit by more than 10 mph. Directly relevant to this jurisdiction, Shin, Washington, and van Schalkwyk (2009), evaluating the Scottsdale Loop 101 demonstration under state agency sponsorship, found significant reductions in total and injury crashes within the enforcement zone alongside a measured increase in rear-end collisions — a mixed result that the earlier phase report (Washington, Shin, and van Schalkwyk 2007) presented with appropriate caution.

**The migration question.** Several studies find that crashes do not simply disappear; they move. Høye (2014) documents "kangaroo jumping" — drivers decelerating at the camera and reaccelerating past it — as a mechanism by which point cameras compress the effect into a short segment and can displace risk downstream. Where comparison sites are adjacent corridors, migration contaminates the comparison group in a direction that inflates the estimated treatment effect. Conversely, several studies find the opposite: a generalized "halo" of reduced speeds extending beyond the enforced segment in both time and distance (Champness, Sheehan, and Folkman 2005; Goldenbeld and van Schagen 2005). The two findings are not necessarily inconsistent — halo effects appear more common with covertly or randomly deployed mobile enforcement, migration more common with fixed, signed, single-point installations — but the literature has not resolved the conditions under which each dominates, and no included study measures both mechanisms on the same network with an adequate untreated control.

**Rear-end collisions.** Six of the twenty-six studies report statistically significant increases in rear-end crashes at or near camera sites; nine report no significant change; the remainder do not report crash type separately. The parallel red-light camera literature is more consistent on this point: Erke's (2009) meta-analysis found rear-end increases sufficient to offset a meaningful share of the angle-crash benefit, while Council et al. (2005) and Persaud et al., in the FHWA multi-jurisdiction evaluation, found a net positive benefit-cost result because the rear-end crashes were substantially less severe than the crashes prevented. Applied to speed cameras, the pattern in the extraction sheet suggests rear-end increases are associated with abrupt, poorly signed, single-point installations at locations with existing congestion, and are largely absent from average-speed or section control installations.

**Point versus section control.** This is one of the clearer signals in the literature. Høye (2014) found section control (average speed enforcement over a segment) associated with larger and more spatially uniform injury crash reductions than point cameras, without the kangaroo-jump artifact. Carnis and Blais (2013), evaluating the French national program, found effects that scaled with deployment density rather than with the presence of any single camera. If Verde Springs deploys, the evidence favors treating a corridor rather than a point.

**Durability.** Almost nothing is known past three years, a gap discussed in §5. Of the twenty-six studies, seventeen report follow-up of two years or less. Four report three years. Five report longer, and of those, three report attenuation of the speed effect over time while two do not. There is no included study with a follow-up long enough to characterize what happens to a mature camera program in year seven.

### 3.3 Posted speed limit changes: a small, real, and frequently misattributed effect

Seventeen studies examined changes to posted limits. Their central finding is uncontroversial and, from the standpoint of a Council considering a sign-change ordinance, disappointing: lowering a posted limit without changing the road produces a reduction in mean operating speed of approximately one to three miles per hour.

Hu and Cicchino (2020a), evaluating Boston's citywide reduction from 30 to 25 mph, found mean speeds essentially unchanged but meaningful reductions in the odds of vehicles exceeding 35 mph — a distributional effect at the tail rather than a shift of the whole curve. Their companion Seattle analysis (Hu and Cicchino 2020b) found reductions in injury crashes on treated arterials, though the treatment there was accompanied by signal retiming and signage changes, so attribution to the limit alone is not clean. Islam and El-Basyouny (2015), applying full Bayes methods to a residential limit reduction in Edmonton, found reductions in crashes that were smaller than the naive comparison suggested and were sensitive to the choice of reference population.

The apparent conflict with the power model (§3.1) resolves in two ways. First, a one to three mph reduction in mean speed is genuinely worth something — under Elvik's exponents, on the order of 5 to 15 percent on fatal crash risk — but it is a small effect that crash-count studies in individual cities are statistically underpowered to detect. Absence of a detected crash effect in a single mid-size city is weak evidence of absence. Second, the effect concentrates in the upper tail of the speed distribution, which is precisely where pedestrian fatality risk lives (Tefft 2013). A treatment that removes the 45-mph drivers from a 35-mph corridor while leaving the 33-mph drivers alone will barely move the mean and may matter a great deal for survival.

The deeper finding across these seventeen studies is that the posted limit is a weak instrument relative to design speed. Where a corridor's geometry — lane width, lane count, sight lines, setback, signal progression — communicates 45 mph, a 30 mph sign changes the legal standard and not the road. Retting, Ferguson, and McCartt (2003) make this argument explicitly in the pedestrian context. The practical corollary is that limit changes function well as a *complement* to enforcement and reconfiguration (they set the threshold that enforcement enforces, and they legitimize the design change) and poorly as a standalone intervention.

### 3.4 Road diets and reconfiguration: strong evidence in the wrong setting

Twenty-two studies addressed lane reduction and reconfiguration. The evidence for the classic four-lane-undivided to three-lane conversion is among the strongest in the traffic safety literature, and it is also the evidence least transferable to Verde Springs' problem.

**What the strong evidence says.** Pawlovich et al. (2006), using Bayesian methods on Iowa conversions, found crash frequency reductions of roughly 25 percent and crash rate reductions of approximately 19 percent. Huang, Stewart, and Zegeer (2002), comparing conversions in California and Washington against matched sites, found a smaller effect — roughly 6 percent — a discrepancy that the profession has generally attributed to differences in city size, traffic volume, and comparison group construction. Harkey et al. (2008), in NCHRP 617, synthesized these into the crash modification factors that entered the *Highway Safety Manual*: approximately 0.71 for small urban areas and approximately 0.81 for suburban and larger-city arterial settings. Noland et al. (2015) found favorable benefit-cost results for a New Jersey conversion. Knapp and Giese (2001) established the operational thresholds still in use: conversions generally perform well below roughly 20,000 vehicles per day, with diversion and delay concerns rising sharply above that.

**Why it may not transfer.** Verde Springs' five corridors are, per the Development Services inventory, four- to six-lane facilities carrying between 24,000 and 41,000 vehicles per day. The Iowa evidence was generated on four-lane undivided streets in cities of 5,000 to 40,000 people carrying 8,000 to 15,000 vehicles per day. Nothing in the twenty-two included studies establishes that a 0.71 crash modification factor survives that extrapolation, and the Huang et al. result — the study whose setting is closest to a larger arterial context — is the weakest of the set. The FHWA road diet informational guide is candid on this point: the technique is documented for four-to-three conversions and the evidence for six-to-four or six-to-five reconfiguration is thin.

**What may transfer.** The mechanism underlying the road diet effect is not lane count as such. It is the removal of the multiple-threat crossing conflict, the reduction in speed differential between lanes, the shortening of pedestrian crossing distance, and the provision of a protected left-turn refuge. Those mechanisms can be delivered on a six-lane arterial by other means, and there the evidence is better. Chen et al. (2013), evaluating New York City's arterial safety program, found meaningful crash reductions from packages combining medians, refuge islands, signal timing changes, and geometric modification on high-volume arterials. Fitzpatrick and Park (2010) found that pedestrian hybrid beacons reduced total crashes by roughly 29 percent and pedestrian crashes by roughly 69 percent at treated crossings. Zegeer et al. (2005) established that marked crosswalks alone on multilane roads with high volumes are associated with *higher* pedestrian crash rates absent a refuge island or signal — a finding directly applicable to any Verde Springs proposal to add crossings without adding protection. Fayish and Gross (2010) found leading pedestrian intervals associated with reductions of roughly 59 percent in pedestrian-vehicle crashes at treated intersections.

The honest synthesis is this: the road diet literature does not tell Verde Springs what a six-lane reconfiguration will do. The crossing treatment literature tells Verde Springs, with reasonable confidence, what refuge islands, hybrid beacons, and leading pedestrian intervals will do at specific locations — and those treatments target exactly the crash type that killed fourteen people last year.

### 3.5 Traffic calming: the strongest evidence, the narrowest application

Twelve studies addressed physical traffic calming. The Cochrane review (Bunn et al. 2003) found area-wide traffic calming schemes associated with reductions in road traffic deaths of roughly 11 percent, with wide confidence intervals reflecting the heterogeneity of the schemes. Elvik's (2001) meta-analysis of area-wide urban schemes found injury crash reductions of approximately 15 percent overall, with roughly 25 percent on residential streets and roughly 10 percent on main roads — a distinction that matters enormously here. Tester et al. (2004), in a matched case-control design, found that residence near a speed hump was associated with roughly 50 to 60 percent lower odds of child pedestrian injury. Zein et al. (1997) and Ewing (1999) documented speed reductions of 20 to 25 percent at vertical deflection devices.

The gradient in Elvik (2001) is the finding Council should retain. Physical calming works best where it is politically and operationally feasible — local and collector streets — and least on the arterial mainline, where emergency response times, transit operations, and freight movement constrain vertical deflection and where the treatments that remain feasible (lane narrowing, curb extensions at intersections, median installation, access management) have a thinner evidence base. Roundabout conversion is the exception with strong evidence for vehicle occupants: Persaud et al. (2001) found injury crash reductions of roughly 76 percent at U.S. conversions. But the pedestrian evidence at multilane roundabouts is genuinely contested, and Verde Springs' problem is a pedestrian problem.

### 3.6 Equity and distributional effects: the denominator decides the answer

Six of the included studies, plus material from the cross-cutting set, examine who receives automated enforcement citations and who is struck. They reach opposing conclusions, and the opposition is arithmetic rather than substantive.

Analyses using **residents per neighborhood** as the denominator consistently find that citation burden falls disproportionately on lower-income neighborhoods and on neighborhoods with higher shares of Black and Latino residents. The ProPublica Illinois analysis of Chicago's speed camera program (Sanchez and Kambhampati 2022) is the most widely cited example, and its finding of higher per-household ticketing in Black and Latino ZIP codes is not seriously disputed as a description.

Analyses using **vehicles passing the camera** as the denominator generally find citation rates that are similar across neighborhoods, because the disparity in the first analysis is driven substantially by camera siting and by traffic volume rather than by differential enforcement of individual drivers.

Analyses using **crash and fatality burden** as the denominator find the reverse of the equity objection: pedestrian fatalities in U.S. cities fall disproportionately on lower-income neighborhoods and on neighborhoods with higher shares of residents of color, so the neighborhoods receiving the most enforcement are, in many programs, the neighborhoods bearing the most death. Ferenchak and Marshall (2016) demonstrate that the *method of identifying* safety projects — reactive, crash-based site selection versus proactive, systemic selection — itself produces different distributional outcomes, independent of any intervention.

None of these three framings is wrong. They answer different questions. The consequence for Verde Springs is procedural rather than substantive: the city must choose and publish its denominator *before* deployment, and must report against it consistently. A program that selects its equity metric after the first year of data will be accused, correctly, of choosing the metric that flatters it.

Two further findings are consistent across the distributional literature. First, the burden of a flat fine is regressive in proportion to income, and the downstream consequences of unpaid camera debt — late penalties, collections, registration holds, and in some jurisdictions license suspension or vehicle immobilization — are where the real harm concentrates, not in the citation itself. Second, the enforcement-avoidance argument runs both ways: automated enforcement removes officer discretion from the stop, which the policing literature identifies as a principal locus of disparity (Barajas 2021), while simultaneously extending the reach of fine-based sanction into places officers were not going. Neither point is settled by the traffic safety literature and this office does not claim to settle it here.

### 3.7 Public acceptance and program discontinuation

Public opposition to automated enforcement is highest before implementation and declines afterward in most evaluated programs; Hu and McCartt (2016) found majority support in Montgomery County after several years of operation, including among drivers who had received citations. This is a real and replicated pattern, but it should not be oversold: it comes from programs that survived long enough to be surveyed, which is a selected sample.

The discontinuation literature is small and pointed. Where camera programs have been terminated by referendum or legislation, evaluations of the post-termination period generally find crash increases at formerly enforced locations (Ko, Geedipally, and Walden 2013, in the red-light context). The mechanism is unsurprising: behavioral effects that depend on continuing enforcement end when the enforcement ends. For a city facing a state preemption bill in committee, this is the operative finding of the section. Any benefit purchased through cameras is rented, not owned.

## 4. Why the Studies Disagree

The conflicts described above are not random. Six design differences account for most of the variance in reported effect sizes, and all six can be checked in the extraction sheet.

**Regression to the mean.** Camera sites, road diet sites, and calming sites are selected because they had a bad recent crash history. Bad recent history is partly signal and partly noise, and the noise component reverts regardless of treatment. Hauer (1997) established the magnitude of this bias; Persaud and Lyon (2007), reviewing two decades of empirical Bayes application, found that RTM commonly accounts for a third to a half of the apparent effect in naive before-after studies of crash-selected sites. This is the principal reason the studies reporting 40 and 50 percent reductions are the studies without EB correction, and the reason the EB-corrected studies cluster in the 10 to 20 percent range.

**Comparison group contamination.** Where the comparison sites are adjacent corridors, and where either migration or halo effects operate, the comparison group is itself treated. Migration inflates the estimated effect; halo deflates it. Only four of the twenty-six enforcement studies explicitly tested for comparison group contamination.

**Exposure denominator.** Crash counts, crash rates per vehicle-mile, crash rates per capita, and crash rates per pedestrian crossing produce different answers, particularly across periods with changing travel volume. Studies spanning 2020 and 2021 are especially vulnerable, since exposure collapsed while speeds and fatality rates rose. Nine included studies span that period; five use raw counts.

**Outcome definition.** "Injury crash" is not a stable construct. Some studies use police-reported injury of any severity; some use KABCO A-and-above; some use hospital admission; some use fatalities alone. Reported effect sizes are systematically larger for more severe outcome definitions, which is consistent with the speed–injury relationship in §3.1 but also means that comparing a 42 percent KSI reduction against a 12 percent all-injury reduction is comparing different quantities.

**Follow-up duration and secular trend.** Short follow-up periods capture novelty effects. Long follow-up periods risk confounding with vehicle fleet changes, economic cycles, and other programs. Studies without a modeled trend attribute the entire secular decline in crash rates over a study period to the treatment.

**Bundling.** Real cities do not implement one thing. Seattle lowered limits, retimed signals, and added signage in the same period. New York City's arterial program bundled a dozen treatments. Bundled evaluations produce larger effects and weaker attribution. The single-intervention studies with clean attribution are disproportionately the small, low-volume, four-to-three conversions in Iowa — which is exactly why the cleanest evidence is the least transferable.

To these six should be added publication bias, which Elvik (2011) has demonstrated operates in road safety meta-analysis, and which Høye (2014) explicitly corrected for in the enforcement literature with the effect of reducing pooled estimates.

## 5. Gaps

The following are gaps in the literature, not gaps in this review. Each constrains what Verde Springs can claim to know.

1. **Effects beyond three years are essentially untracked.** Twenty-one of twenty-six enforcement studies report two years or less. No included study characterizes a mature program at year seven or ten. Whether the behavioral effect decays, stabilizes, or is sustained by continued citation volume is unknown.

2. **Six-lane arterials.** The reconfiguration evidence is a four-lane evidence base. Effect estimates for six-to-four and six-to-five conversions at 30,000-plus ADT are not established. Verde Springs would be extrapolating.

3. **Sunbelt urban form.** The North American evidence is disproportionately from the Northeast, Midwest, and Pacific Northwest. Arterials with 200-foot rights of way, 600-foot signal spacing, deep commercial setbacks, and year-round pedestrian exposure to extreme heat are underrepresented. Heat exposure as a determinant of crossing behavior — where people cross relative to shade — appears in no included study.

4. **Pedestrian-specific outcomes.** Most evaluations report total or injury crashes. Pedestrian crashes are a small share of the total and are frequently underpowered within study samples. For a city whose problem is 74 percent pedestrian, the directly relevant effect estimates are the thinnest.

5. **Combined packages.** No included study provides a clean estimate of the incremental effect of adding automated enforcement to a corridor that has already been reconfigured, or the reverse. Practitioners assume sub-additivity; the literature does not test it.

6. **Mid-size cities.** The evaluation literature is bimodal: very small cities (the Iowa road diet work) and very large ones (New York, Chicago, London, Melbourne). Cities of 100,000 to 250,000 are underrepresented across all four intervention categories.

7. **Distributional outcomes with a pre-specified denominator.** No included distributional study registered its denominator in advance. Nor does any included study track the downstream consequences of unpaid camera debt as an outcome.

8. **Night.** Nationally, roughly three-quarters of pedestrian fatalities occur in darkness. Lighting appears as a treatment in only two included studies. Whether Verde Springs' fourteen pedestrian deaths follow the national nighttime pattern is a question for the Police Department's crash file, not for this literature.

9. **Full program cost.** Very few evaluations report the complete cost of ownership — capital, vendor fees, adjudication, court time, collections, public information — against the benefit. Benefit-cost claims in vendor materials are generally not derived from the peer-reviewed evidence base.

## 6. What This Means for Verde Springs

### 6.1 What the evidence supports

**Calibrate to the corrected estimates, not the headline ones.** If Council proceeds with automated enforcement, the defensible planning assumption is an injury crash reduction on the order of 10 to 20 percent within the enforced segments, not 20 to 50 percent citywide. A program promised at 40 percent will be judged a failure at 15 percent, which would be a mistake, because 15 percent on these five corridors is a meaningful number of people.

**Deploy along corridors, not at points.** The section-control and deployment-density findings (Høye 2014; Carnis and Blais 2013) both argue against a small number of fixed single-point installations, which is the configuration most associated with kangaroo jumping, rear-end increases, and displaced risk. If state law permits average-speed enforcement, it is the better-evidenced technology. If it does not, mobile deployment rotated across a defined corridor set is better supported than a handful of permanent poles.

**Select sites by a published rule, and use empirical Bayes to do it.** Site selection by recent crash count guarantees regression to the mean, which guarantees an inflated apparent effect, which guarantees a credibility problem when the effect fades. An EB-based site ranking using multi-year data and a reference population of comparable Arizona arterials is more defensible technically and more defensible in front of Councilmember Prosser, because it can be published in advance and audited.

**Do not expect a sign change to do the work.** A limit reduction on the five corridors will buy one to three miles per hour and a somewhat larger reduction in the share of drivers exceeding the limit by ten or more. That is worth doing — the tail is where the fatalities are — but it is a complement to enforcement and design, not a substitute, and it should not be presented to the public as a safety program.

**Put real money into the crossings.** The treatments with the best evidence against the specific crash type killing people in Verde Springs are not the ones in the RFP. They are median refuge islands, pedestrian hybrid beacons at long uncontrolled gaps, leading pedestrian intervals at signalized intersections, lighting, and lane narrowing (Zegeer et al. 2005; Fitzpatrick and Park 2010; Fayish and Gross 2010; Chen et al. 2013). These are also preemption-proof: no legislature can turn off a refuge island.

### 6.2 Evaluate on speed, not on deaths

This is the most important operational recommendation in the review, and it follows from arithmetic rather than from any study.

To detect a 20 percent reduction in a crash count with 80 percent power at conventional significance, an evaluation needs roughly 350 events in the before period. Verde Springs records fourteen to nineteen traffic deaths per year. Detecting a 20 percent change in fatalities would require roughly two decades of data. Detecting a 40 percent change would require roughly 80 events, or five to six years. **Fatality counts cannot serve as the program's performance measure.** Any year-over-year fatality comparison Council is offered in 2027 will be noise.

The measures that can support inference within the appropriation period are:

- **Operating speed distributions** — mean, 85th percentile, and share exceeding the limit by 10+ mph — measured continuously at treated and untreated sites. Thousands of observations per site per week. A one mph change is detectable within weeks.
- **All police-reported injury crashes on the five corridors**, pooled. If the corridors carry on the order of 100 to 140 injury crashes per year collectively, a three-year before period supplies enough events to detect an effect in the 25 to 30 percent range.
- **Pedestrian crashes of all severities**, reported but interpreted cautiously given small numbers.

Council should be told this explicitly, in writing, before the program launches. It is far easier to explain statistical power in 2026 than to explain in 2028 why a program that reduced speeds by four miles per hour coincided with a bad year for fatalities.

### 6.3 Build the evaluation into the deployment

The gaps in §5 exist because cities deploy first and evaluate second. Verde Springs can do better at negligible cost by staggering deployment across the five corridors — treating two corridors in year one, two in year two, one in year three — which produces a stepped-wedge design in which each untreated corridor serves as a control for the treated ones and each corridor eventually receives treatment. This costs nothing in equity terms (all five corridors are treated) and converts an ordinary capital program into an evaluable one. If the sequencing can also vary which treatment comes first — engineering before enforcement on one corridor, the reverse on another — the city would generate the first local evidence on the combined-package question identified in §5.

The evaluation plan, including the outcome measures, the comparison strategy, the equity denominator, and the analytic method, should be written and published *before* the first camera is energized.

### 6.4 RFP provisions the evidence supports

1. **No per-citation vendor compensation.** Fixed-fee or fixed-unit contracting removes the structural incentive that gives the revenue-device argument its force. This is a political and institutional judgment more than an empirical one, but it is the single provision most likely to determine whether the program survives five years.
2. **City ownership of all speed and citation data**, in machine-readable form, including non-citation speed observations, which are the program's most valuable evaluation asset and are routinely retained by vendors.
3. **A warning-only period** of 30 to 60 days at each new site, with signage, consistent with the practice in the better-documented programs.
4. **Publication of the site-selection rule and the ranked site list** in advance of deployment.
5. **Revenue disposition specified in the enabling ordinance** — dedication of net revenue to the engineering program described in §6.1 is the most defensible arrangement and directly answers the revenue objection.
6. **Fine structure with income-based relief**, payment plans, and an explicit prohibition on registration holds, license consequences, or immobilization arising from camera debt.
7. **Sunset and re-authorization at five years**, tied to the evaluation.

### 6.5 The preemption contingency

The bill in committee is a reason to weight the portfolio toward engineering, not a reason to abandon enforcement. The discontinuation literature indicates that camera-derived benefits reverse when programs end. A $6.5 million appropriation spent predominantly on a camera program could, in the event of preemption, leave the city with stranded equipment and no durable change to the five corridors. A portfolio weighted toward crossing treatments, lighting, medians, and signal retiming — with a smaller, corridor-based enforcement component that can be discontinued without stranding the capital program — is the configuration most robust to the legal risk.

This office does not offer a specific dollar split; that requires engineer's estimates that Development Services has not yet produced. It does note that indicative costs for pedestrian hybrid beacons, refuge islands, leading pedestrian intervals, and lighting on five corridors will likely consume the majority of the appropriation, and that Council should see those estimates alongside the camera program costs before the RFP is released rather than after.

### 6.6 What this review does not settle

It does not establish what a six-lane reconfiguration will do on Verde Springs arterials; the evidence does not exist. It does not establish whether cameras or crossing treatments deliver more safety per dollar in this city. It does not establish how long a camera effect persists. It does not resolve the equity question, which is a question about values and about fine administration at least as much as about traffic safety. And it cannot tell Council whether nineteen deaths in 2025 represents a step change or a bad draw from a worsening distribution — only that the answer matters for how the program is judged, and that the city should stop measuring itself by a number too small to measure anything.
