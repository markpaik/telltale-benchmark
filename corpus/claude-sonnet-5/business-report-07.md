## 1. Executive Summary

Brandywine Medical Group enters the fourth quarter of fiscal 2026 carrying two intersecting pressures that will shape the organization's near-term financial trajectory and its longer-term competitive position in New Castle County: an unresolved commercial payer contract that expires September 30, and a still-unfolding revenue cycle disruption traceable to the April electronic health record (EHR) conversion. Neither issue is contained to a single service line or department; both touch every one of the practice's 26 sites and all 310 providers, and both will occupy a substantial share of the finance and quality committees' joint attention at this meeting.

Operationally, the quarter was mixed. Orthopedics continued to perform as the group's financial engine, growing case volume 9 percent to 6,100 cases and contributing $31 million, aided by a favorable and growing shift of cases into the ambulatory surgery center (ASC). Primary care, by contrast, remains structurally strained: panels averaging 2,180 patients per provider, a third-next-available appointment wait of 22 days, and a $4.2 million loss before referral contribution reflect a service line operating at capacity without adequate throughput or compensation design to match. Cardiology enters the fourth quarter in the most acute distress of any division, having lost three physicians effective August 1 and facing a projected 14 percent volume decline against a 31-day echocardiogram backlog. Behavioral health continues to expand access through virtual care — 68 percent of visits are now delivered by video — but a 24 percent no-show rate and nine unfilled clinician positions cap the division's ability to convert demand into revenue. Imaging processed 218,000 studies across 11 sites and posted strong magnetic resonance (MR) utilization of 74 percent, though the six-week downtime of the Middletown scanner due to a helium compressor failure has created a capacity and access problem that overlaps uncomfortably with the cardiology backlog.

Underlying all of these service-line results is the financial drag from the EHR conversion. Initial claim denial rates have nearly doubled, from 6.9 percent pre-conversion to 12.8 percent post-conversion, and days in accounts receivable (AR) have risen from 41 to 58. Management estimates $19 million in cash is currently stuck behind this backlog. This is a liquidity and working-capital issue as much as a billing issue, and it is compounding at the same time the practice heads into negotiation on its largest commercial contract.

The report that follows works through each service line in turn, details the EHR-related revenue cycle problem, summarizes the state of payer negotiations, and closes with management's assessment of where the practice stands entering the fourth quarter, along with the decisions the board should expect to face over the next 90 days.

## 2. Service Line Performance

### 2.1 Primary Care

Primary care remains the front door to the practice and the single largest source of referral volume into orthopedics, cardiology, imaging, and behavioral health, but its standalone economics continue to erode. The division operates with 98 providers carrying panels that average 2,180 patients — well above the range most benchmarking cohorts consider sustainable for full-service primary care without significant care-team support. Third-next-available appointment access sits at 22 days, a figure that limits the practice's ability to capture same-quarter acute and follow-up volume and pushes patients toward urgent care or competitor systems for anything time-sensitive.

| Metric | Q3 FY2026 | Prior Quarter | Prior Year Q3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Providers (FTE) | 98 | 99 | 101 |
| Total visits | 412,000 | 405,000 | 418,000 |
| Average panel size | 2,180 | 2,140 | 2,050 |
| Third-next-available appointment (days) | 22 | 19 | 15 |
| Direct financial result (before referral contribution) | ($4.2M) | ($3.6M) | ($2.9M) |

The direct loss of $4.2 million reflects the ongoing structural reality that primary care visit reimbursement, particularly for the evaluation-and-management codes that dominate panel-based care, does not cover the fully loaded cost of the care team model the practice has built, including embedded behavioral health liaisons, care coordinators, and pharmacy support at several sites. The division has historically been evaluated — and should continue to be evaluated — on total contribution including downstream referrals it generates into higher-margin specialty and imaging services. Preliminary attribution modeling, still being refined by the analytics team, suggests primary care-originated referrals contributed in the range of $9–11 million in specialty and ancillary margin this quarter, which would net the division to a modestly positive total contribution. Committee members should treat that referral-attribution figure as directional; a more rigorous methodology is in development and will be presented at the December board meeting.

Two access-related dynamics warrant board attention. First, the 22-day third-next-available figure has moved unfavorably for three consecutive quarters, coinciding with panel growth that has outpaced net provider additions — the division has lost one net FTE year over year even as the group added sites in Newark and Bear. Second, primary care access constraints appear to be a contributing factor in the higher-than-desired volume seen in urgent care and, indirectly, in emergency department utilization among attributed patients under the group's value-based contracts, a linkage the quality committee should track given its implications for shared-savings performance.

Recruitment for primary care remains difficult in the current Delaware and greater Philadelphia labor market; the division has six open requisitions, four of which have been posted for more than 120 days. Management's working view is that primary care will not close its direct-cost gap through volume growth alone and will require either a panel-size ceiling tied to added support staff, a revised compensation model that more explicitly rewards access and referral generation, or both. These options will be brought to the finance committee as a discrete agenda item in the fourth quarter.

### 2.2 Orthopedics

Orthopedics had the strongest quarter of any division and remains the clearest example within the practice of a service line whose site-of-service strategy is working as designed. Case volume grew 9 percent year over year to 6,100 cases, and the division moved 61 percent of eligible cases to the practice's ambulatory surgery center rather than hospital-based settings, up from 54 percent in the prior-year quarter. That shift matters financially in two directions: it reduces facility costs borne by payers and patients, which supports the division's competitive positioning heading into payer negotiations, and it increases the practice's own facility-fee capture, since Brandywine owns the ASC outright.

| Metric | Q3 FY2026 | Prior Quarter | Prior Year Q3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total cases | 6,100 | 5,780 | 5,596 |
| Case growth (YoY) | +9% | +7% | — |
| Share of cases at ASC | 61% | 58% | 54% |
| Division contribution margin | $31.0M | $28.4M | $27.1M |

The $31 million contribution figure represents the largest single contribution among the five service lines detailed in this report and is the primary offset the finance committee should keep in mind when reviewing primary care's standalone loss. Volume growth was broad-based across joint replacement, sports medicine, and spine, with joint replacement showing the largest absolute increase, consistent with regional demographic trends and the division's continued build-out of same-day and next-day total joint pathways at the ASC.

The division's principal constraint going forward is not demand but capacity: the ASC is operating at an estimated 84 percent of available block time, and the division chair has flagged that further volume growth beyond the current pace will require either additional operating room time — a capital and staffing decision — or continued referral of overflow cases to hospital-based settings, which reduces the facility-fee benefit described above. Management recommends the finance committee consider an ASC expansion feasibility study as part of the fiscal 2027 capital planning cycle, given the return this division has demonstrated on its current physical plant.

### 2.3 Cardiology

Cardiology is the division requiring the most immediate board attention. Effective August 1, three physicians left the group, a departure the division absorbed with essentially no lead time for recruitment or panel redistribution. Division Chair Stefan Marek has attributed the departures directly to dissatisfaction with call coverage burden, which he describes as having intensified over the past 18 months as the division's on-call panel shrank relative to patient volume and as neighboring hospital systems increased demand for cardiology coverage in ways that pulled unevenly on Brandywine's roster.

| Metric | Q3 FY2026 (Actual/Reported) | Q4 FY2026 (Projected) |
|---|---|---|
| Physicians on staff (as of report date) | Reduced by 3, effective 8/1 | — |
| Projected volume impact | — | -14% |
| Echocardiogram backlog | 31 days | Expected to widen absent intervention |

The projected 14 percent volume decline has not yet fully materialized in the Q3 numbers reported here, since the departures occurred one month after quarter-end, but it is the division's own forward estimate and should be treated by the committee as the base case for Q4 planning purposes. A 14 percent volume decline in cardiology, one of the practice's higher-margin specialties, represents a meaningful revenue-at-risk figure; the finance team's preliminary estimate, pending division-level confirmation, places the annualized contribution impact in the $6–8 million range if the decline holds and is not offset by recruitment or coverage redesign.

The 31-day echocardiogram backlog predates the physician departures and is a function of both echo-technician staffing and reading-physician capacity; it will almost certainly worsen through the fourth quarter as remaining physicians absorb the volume of departed colleagues in addition to their own panels. This backlog also interacts with the Middletown imaging outage discussed in Section 2.5, since a portion of cardiology's imaging demand is routed through the same enterprise imaging infrastructure.

Dr. Marek has requested, and management supports bringing to the finance committee, a proposal to restructure call coverage — potentially including a shared coverage arrangement with a regional locum tenens partner and a revised call-pay differential — as a retention measure for the remaining cardiology staff. Recruitment for the three vacated positions is underway but is not expected to yield placements before the second half of fiscal 2027, given typical cardiology search timelines of 9–14 months. The quality committee should also be aware that the backlog and coverage strain carry clinical risk implications, particularly for time-sensitive echo referrals from primary care and the emergency department; Dr. Fashola's team is monitoring turnaround-time-related safety events and will report separately if any pattern emerges.

### 2.4 Behavioral Health

Behavioral health continues to demonstrate the clearest access transformation of any division in the practice, driven almost entirely by telehealth adoption. Sixty-eight percent of visits this quarter were delivered by video, allowing the division's 41 clinicians to serve a geographically dispersed patient population without the site-based constraints that limit other specialties. That said, the division is running with nine vacant clinician positions — roughly 18 percent of authorized staffing — and a no-show rate of 24 percent, both of which materially limit the division's ability to convert market demand into completed, billable visits.

| Metric | Q3 FY2026 |
|---|---|
| Clinicians on staff | 41 |
| Vacant positions | 9 |
| Visits delivered by video | 68% |
| No-show rate | 24% |

The combination of high vacancy and high no-show rates suggests two distinct but related problems rather than one. The vacancy rate is primarily a labor-market and compensation issue consistent with national behavioral health staffing trends; the division has struggled particularly to recruit master's-level licensed clinical social workers and psychiatric nurse practitioners, both in high demand across the Mid-Atlantic. The no-show rate, by contrast, appears to be at least partially a byproduct of the division's own access model: same-week video appointment availability, while a genuine strength, has made scheduling frictionless enough that patients cancel or simply do not log on at a higher rate than is typical for in-person behavioral health care, where the effort of physically traveling to an appointment tends to reinforce follow-through.

Management's near-term recommendation is twofold: continue targeted recruitment incentives for the nine open positions, informed by a compensation benchmarking exercise now underway, and pilot a revised scheduling and reminder protocol — including deposit-free double-booking for high-no-show-risk time slots and a 48-hour and 2-hour reminder cadence — in two behavioral health sites during the fourth quarter, with results to be reported to the quality committee in December. If the pilot reduces no-shows meaningfully, the division's effective capacity would increase without any additional headcount, which is a materially better return than recruitment alone can offer in the current labor market.

### 2.5 Imaging

Imaging performed 218,000 studies across the practice's 11 imaging sites this quarter, with magnetic resonance utilization at 74 percent — a strong figure by industry benchmarks and consistent with the division's positioning as a high-throughput, lower-cost alternative to hospital-based imaging for payers steering patients toward freestanding sites.

| Metric | Q3 FY2026 |
|---|---|
| Total studies (all modalities, 11 sites) | 218,000 |
| MR utilization | 74% |
| Scanner downtime (Middletown) | 6 weeks, ongoing |
| Cause of downtime | Helium compressor failure |

The Middletown scanner outage is the most consequential single operational event in the imaging division this quarter. A helium compressor failure took the unit offline in mid-June, and as of this report the unit remains down; the replacement compressor has been ordered but delivery and installation are not expected until late September at the earliest, meaning the outage will extend to roughly 14–15 weeks by the time service is restored — well beyond the six weeks reported as of quarter-end and continuing into the fourth quarter. Volume normally handled at Middletown has been redistributed to the Newark and Christiana Crossing sites, both of which are now operating at or near capacity themselves, which has contributed to lengthening MR scheduling windows practice-wide and interacts with the cardiology echo backlog described above, since several cardiac MR referrals have been affected as well.

Given the extended timeline, management recommends the finance committee consider approving expedited procurement of a backup compressor unit or, alternatively, a temporary mobile MR unit for the Middletown site, at an estimated incremental cost of $180,000–$240,000 for the remaining outage period, against an estimated $310,000 in monthly imaging revenue currently at risk from the site being offline. This is a straightforward return-on-investment case and staff will bring a formal capital request to the committee following this meeting.

## 3. Electronic Health Record Conversion: Denials and Receivables

The April go-live of the practice's new electronic health record system has produced the most significant revenue cycle disruption in Brandywine's recent history, and it remains unresolved as of this report. The core metrics are stark and have not yet shown a sustained improving trend.

| Metric | Pre-Conversion Baseline | Current (Q3 FY2026) | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial claim denial rate | 6.9% | 12.8% | +5.9 pts |
| Days in accounts receivable | 41 | 58 | +17 days |
| Cash held up behind conversion issues | — | $19.0M | — |

The denial rate increase is concentrated in a handful of root causes that the revenue cycle team has now isolated with reasonable confidence: coding and charge-capture mapping errors introduced during the data migration, particularly for modifier usage and place-of-service codes across the ASC and imaging sites; eligibility and authorization workflow breaks where the new system's payer connectivity does not yet match the prior system's coverage; and a wave of claims rejected for missing or malformed National Provider Identifier (NPI) and taxonomy data at the group level, a configuration issue that has since been corrected prospectively but has left a substantial volume of claims from April and May requiring manual rework. Denials attributable to these three causes account for an estimated 70–75 percent of the total increase; the remainder is dispersed across smaller, longer-tail issues that the team is working through claim by claim.

The rise in days in accounts receivable, from 41 to 58, is a direct consequence of the denial pattern: claims that deny on first submission take substantially longer to resolve than clean claims, both because of the rework cycle itself and because the practice's billing staff — sized and trained for the prior system's workflows — has needed time to build new denial-management routines in the new platform. The $19 million cash estimate represents management's best current calculation of the incremental AR balance attributable specifically to the conversion, isolated from normal seasonal and payer-mix AR fluctuation, and should be understood as a liquidity issue: this is cash the practice has earned and expects to collect, but which is not yet available to fund operations, capital projects, or debt service.

Management has taken the following steps and reports them to the committee for visibility rather than as a request for new approval, since all were executed within existing operating authority:

- A dedicated conversion remediation team of 14 billing and coding staff, including four temporary contract coders, has been assembled and is working the aged denial queue in priority order by dollar value and payer.
- The vendor has been engaged under the existing implementation contract to correct the NPI/taxonomy configuration issue, which was resolved at the system level in early July; claims submitted after that date show an initial denial rate of 9.8 percent, still elevated versus baseline but a meaningful improvement over the 12.8 percent quarter average.
- Weekly denial-rate and AR-aging reporting has been instituted at the division level so that service line leaders can see the direct effect of the conversion on their own financials, separate from the operational results discussed in Section 2.

The trajectory since the July fix is encouraging but not yet sufficient to call the problem resolved. At the current pace of remediation, staff project the denial rate will return to a range of 8–9 percent by the end of the fourth quarter and to baseline by mid-fiscal-2027, with the $19 million AR balance working down over a similar timeframe as older claims are resolved. The finance committee should treat this as a multi-quarter recovery, not a single-quarter event, and should expect the practice's cash position to remain tighter than budgeted through at least December.

## 4. Payer Contract Negotiation

The practice's contract with the state's largest commercial payer expires September 30, 2026 — three weeks after this board meeting — and remains unsigned. This is the single most consequential open item on the practice's agenda for the remainder of the calendar year, given that this payer represents approximately 31 percent of Brandywine's net patient revenue across all service lines, making it the largest single commercial contract in the practice's portfolio.

Negotiations have been underway since March and have narrowed to two substantive open issues. First, the payer has proposed a global rate adjustment below the practice's target, citing regional cost benchmarks; management's counter-position leans heavily on the orthopedics division's ASC migration data described in Section 2.2, arguing that Brandywine's site-of-service shift has already generated meaningful savings for the payer that justify a more favorable rate than the payer's benchmark-based offer reflects. Second, the payer has raised the practice's rising denial and AR metrics, discussed in Section 3, as a point of friction in discussions about administrative simplification provisions the practice has requested; management's position is that the denial increase is a temporary, self-identified, and actively remediated conversion issue rather than a structural billing practice, and that the July configuration fix and improving post-fix denial rate support that framing. Staff recommend the committee be prepared for the payer to continue raising this point through the remainder of negotiations, and recommend the practice proactively share the weekly remediation reporting described in Section 3 with the payer's contracting team as evidence of good-faith progress.

No further substantive terms remain in open dispute; both parties have signaled willingness to extend the current contract on existing rates for a defined bridge period — 60 to 90 days has been discussed informally — if a final agreement is not reached by September 30, which would avoid an immediate network disruption for the roughly 140,000 attributed lives the practice currently serves under this contract. Management does not believe termination without a bridge extension is the payer's preferred outcome, given the payer's own network adequacy obligations in New Castle County, but the committee should be aware that a lapse, even a brief one, would require patient and referring-provider notification under state regulation and would create administrative and reputational disruption regardless of how quickly a bridge or final agreement follows.

Management's recommendation to the committee is to authorize continued negotiation toward the practice's target rate with the ASC utilization data as primary leverage, to pursue the bridge extension as a fallback protecting continuity of care, and to keep the board's executive committee informed on a weekly basis as September 30 approaches. A closed session update, without materials circulated in advance, may be appropriate at this meeting given the sensitivity of specific rate terms; staff defer to the CEO and general counsel on format.

## 5. Fourth Quarter Outlook and Positioning

Taken together, the third quarter leaves Brandywine Medical Group in a position of real strength in some areas and real exposure in others, with the two largest open items — the payer contract and the EHR-driven revenue cycle disruption — both converging on the fourth quarter in ways that will demand sustained board and management attention.

On the strength side, orthopedics and, to a lesser extent, imaging continue to demonstrate that the practice's site-of-service and access strategies work when adequately resourced: the ASC migration in orthopedics is generating both margin and a competitive argument the practice is actively using in payer negotiations, and imaging's MR utilization reflects a well-run core operation even as the Middletown outage creates a near-term capacity gap. Behavioral health's telehealth adoption likewise shows the practice can expand access rapidly when the care modality allows it, even though staffing and no-show challenges are limiting the division from fully capitalizing on that strength.

On the exposure side, cardiology enters the fourth quarter as the division most likely to produce a negative financial surprise, given the confirmed physician departures and the division's own 14 percent volume decline projection; management views stabilizing cardiology coverage and beginning recruitment in earnest as the single highest-priority service line action for the next 90 days. Primary care's structural loss, while partially offset by referral contribution, is not improving and will require a compensation and staffing model decision from the finance committee in the near term rather than continued monitoring alone. The EHR-driven denial and AR problem, while trending in the right direction since the July fix, still has $19 million of cash outstanding and will not fully normalize before the second half of fiscal 2027, meaning the practice should plan its fourth-quarter and fiscal 2027 budget with continued AR-day pressure built in rather than assuming a rapid snapback. And the payer contract, the most time-sensitive item on this agenda, will likely not be fully resolved by the board's next scheduled meeting, making the bridge-extension contingency the most probable near-term outcome.

Management's overall assessment is that Brandywine remains financially and operationally sound at the enterprise level — orthopedics' contribution alone substantially offsets primary care's loss, and imaging and behavioral health are fundamentally healthy divisions working through solvable capacity and staffing constraints — but that the fourth quarter will be defined by how well the practice executes on three specific fronts: cardiology stabilization, revenue cycle remediation, and payer contract resolution. Staff will bring discrete action items and, where applicable, capital or compensation proposals tied to each of these fronts to the board's next regularly scheduled meeting in December, with interim written updates to the finance and quality committee chairs in October and November given the time-sensitivity of the payer contract in particular.

## Appendix E: Glossary of Terms Used in This Report

**Third-next-available appointment:** The number of calendar days between the date a scheduling request is made and the third open appointment slot offered to the patient, a standard access metric that avoids distortion from single anomalous cancellations.

**Contribution margin (division level):** Net revenue less direct variable costs and allocated fixed overhead, as detailed in Appendix A; does not reflect enterprise-level fixed costs such as corporate administration, which are reported separately in the practice's consolidated financial statements.

**Initial claim denial rate:** The percentage of claims rejected or denied on first submission to a payer, prior to any appeal or resubmission, measured against total claims submitted in the same period.

**Days in accounts receivable (Days in AR):** A standard revenue cycle metric calculated as ending net AR divided by average daily net patient revenue, reflecting the average time required to collect on billed services.

**Ambulatory surgery center (ASC) migration:** The practice's ongoing strategic initiative to shift eligible surgical cases from hospital-based or hospital-outpatient settings to Brandywine's own freestanding ambulatory surgery center, discussed in Section 2.2.
