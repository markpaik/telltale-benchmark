# Does the Heart Failure Transitional Care Program Lower 30-Day Readmissions?

### A Research Brief for the September 15 Budget Committee

**Prepared by:** Anjali Deshmukh, Senior Clinical Outcomes Analyst
**Commissioned by:** Loretta Sizemore, Vice President of Care Management
**Cost analysis requested by:** Grant Pelletier, Chief Financial Officer
**Prepared for:** Kwabena Mensah, Chief Medical Officer, and the Budget Committee
**Mountain Laurel Health System — Beckley, West Virginia**

---

## The Question

Mountain Laurel Health System launched a transitional care program for heart failure patients in 2024. The design is simple: a pharmacist calls the patient by telephone within 48 hours of discharge to review medications, and a clinician sees the patient in person within seven days. The program was built to reduce 30-day readmissions, both because readmissions represent poor outcomes for patients with a difficult chronic disease and because the system currently faces a federal readmissions penalty of $1.2 million against next year's Medicare payments. On September 15, the budget committee will decide whether to fund six additional care coordinator positions, at a cost of $840,000, to extend the program's reach. Dr. Mensah asked a direct question: did the program lower 30-day readmissions, and is the evidence strong enough to justify that expansion?

## What We Found

Patients enrolled in the transitional care program were readmitted within 30 days at a rate of 14.1 percent, compared with 19.6 percent among patients who were not enrolled — a gap of 5.5 percentage points that, taken at face value, would be one of the larger effects reported anywhere for this kind of intervention. That headline comparison, however, mixes two very different groups. Nineteen percent of patients had no working telephone number on file and were never offered enrollment at all; they could not have participated regardless of how sick, engaged, or well-supported they were. When the comparison is restricted to patients who *could* have enrolled — those with a reachable phone number — the gap narrows considerably, to 16.8 percent readmitted among the enrolled versus 18.9 percent among the unenrolled, a difference of 2.1 percentage points. Among patients living more than 45 minutes from a hospital, there was no detectable difference between enrolled and unenrolled patients at all. And 22 percent of index discharges — those going to skilled nursing facilities — were excluded from the program by design and are not reflected in any of these figures.

The honest answer to Dr. Mensah's question is: the data are consistent with a real, modest benefit, on the order of two percentage points, for patients who have a phone and choose to participate, but they cannot rule out that some or most of even that smaller gap reflects the kind of patient who agrees to enroll rather than the effect of the program itself. The larger, more dramatic 5.5-point figure that will be tempting to cite in a budget presentation is not a credible estimate of what the program does; it is substantially inflated by the fact that patients without a working phone number — who are systematically different, and by several measures already known to be at higher risk — could never appear in the enrolled group. This brief lays out the data, the sources, the comparison problem, and what the subgroup results do and do not support, so that the committee can weigh the expansion decision and the penalty exposure against evidence appropriately qualified rather than evidence oversold.

## Data and Methods

### The Sample

The analytic population is 2,308 index heart failure discharges from Mountain Laurel's three hospitals between July 2024 and December 2025, an 18-month window chosen to give the program time to mature past its initial rollout and to allow a reasonable number of 30-day follow-up windows to close before this analysis was run. Heart failure was identified using the discharge diagnosis coding the system already uses for its own readmission reporting, so the case definition is consistent with what the system reports elsewhere, including to CMS for penalty purposes.

Of the 2,308 discharges, 508 (22 percent) went to skilled nursing facilities. These patients were excluded from the transitional care program by design, on the reasoning that SNF patients already receive structured post-acute monitoring and that the program's two touchpoints — a home telephone call and an outpatient visit — do not fit a patient who has not yet gone home. That is a defensible design choice, but it means that roughly a fifth of the system's heart failure discharges, a group that tends to be older, frailer, and at higher baseline readmission risk than patients discharged directly home, is entirely absent from any comparison in this brief. Nothing here speaks to whether a differently designed intervention would help that population, and the overall hospital-wide readmission rate the system reports to CMS — the rate the $1.2 million penalty is based on — includes SNF discharges even though this evaluation does not.

Of the remaining discharges eligible for the program by disposition, coordinators did not attempt outreach to patients without a working telephone number, roughly 19 percent of the eligible population. This is a structural exclusion, not a patient choice: those patients were never asked. Among those with a reachable number, enrollment was voluntary. Patients who agreed to the pharmacist call and the follow-up visit were enrolled; patients who could have been reached but declined, could not be scheduled, or were reached but did not complete both components were treated as unenrolled for this analysis. We did not have a reliable way to separate "declined" from "reached but not completed" in the data extract available, which is itself a limitation noted below.

### Two Data Sources, Two Sets of Gaps

Readmission status was assembled from two sources, and each has a different blind spot.

**Readmissions to any of Mountain Laurel's own three hospitals** come from the electronic health record. This source is complete and current for anything that happens inside the system: if a patient comes back through the emergency department or is admitted to any of the three facilities, it is captured in real time with no lag and no coverage gap. The limitation here is not completeness but scope — it only sees what happens inside Mountain Laurel's walls.

**Readmissions to hospitals outside the system** come from West Virginia's state all-payer claims database (APCD), a feed the system does not control and cannot speed up. Three characteristics of that feed matter for this analysis. First, it lags actual events by roughly five months, meaning that for discharges toward the end of the study window — broadly, the second half of 2025 — the external readmission count may not yet be fully mature at the time an analysis is pulled, and even for this brief, prepared in mid-2026, the very last cohorts in the sample are the ones most likely to still be missing late-arriving claims. Second, the feed excludes several Medicare Advantage plans outright; those enrollees' out-of-system readmissions are invisible to us regardless of how much time passes. Third, even setting aside the excluded plans, the feed's own reporting entities estimate its overall coverage at about 78 percent of discharges statewide, meaning that even for plans it does include, some fraction of claims never appear.

The practical effect is that the true 30-day readmission rate for both the enrolled and unenrolled groups is understated in this analysis, because some fraction of out-of-system readmissions — plausibly on the order of a fifth to a quarter of them, before even accounting for the excluded Medicare Advantage plans — never shows up. That is a meaningful qualification on its own, but it becomes a harder problem for comparing the two groups specifically if the *rate* of undercounting differs between them, which is addressed in the limitations section below.

### Follow-Up Window and Outcome Definition

The outcome is any inpatient readmission, to any hospital captured by either source, within 30 days of the index discharge date, following the same definition the system uses for CMS reporting so that the internal analysis and the penalty exposure are speaking the same language. Observation, emergency-department-only, and outpatient encounters were not counted as readmissions, again consistent with CMS methodology.

## Enrollment and What It Does to the Comparison

The central methodological problem in this brief is that enrollment was not assigned; it happened. Two separate processes determined who ended up in the "enrolled" group, and both push in the same direction — toward making the enrolled group look healthier and more likely to do well regardless of the program.

The first process is the phone-number exclusion. Patients without a working telephone number are disproportionately patients who have moved recently, who have unstable housing, who have had phone service disconnected for nonpayment, or who otherwise face material and social circumstances associated with worse health outcomes generally, not just worse readmission risk specifically. Coordinators did not choose to exclude these patients for clinical reasons; the exclusion was purely operational, a consequence of the program's telephone-first design. But the effect on the comparison is the same either way: a group already known to carry more risk factors was never eligible to be counted as "enrolled," which mechanically inflates the unenrolled group's readmission rate and widens the apparent gap between the two arms.

The second process is voluntary enrollment among patients who did have a working phone. Patients who agree to a pharmacist call and a follow-up visit within a week of a hospital stay are, almost by definition, patients with some baseline capacity to engage with their own care: they answer calls from unfamiliar numbers, they have a way to get to an appointment, they may have a family member helping coordinate their care, and they may simply be patients whose health status allows them to participate in the first place. None of that is observable in claims or EHR data in a way that lets us adjust for it directly. This is the textbook problem with voluntary-enrollment program evaluations, and it is not a flaw in how the analysis was run; it is a limit on what an observational comparison like this one can support without either a randomized design or much richer data on why patients did or did not enroll.

The phone-access restriction used in this brief — comparing enrolled patients only to unenrolled patients who also had a working phone — removes the first source of bias but not the second. It is a meaningful improvement over the headline 5.5-point comparison, because it holds constant the one selection factor we can actually observe and control for in the data (whether the patient could have been reached at all). It does not, and cannot, hold constant the harder-to-observe factors that separate a patient who says yes to enrollment from a similarly reachable patient who says no or cannot be scheduled. For that reason, the 2.1-point gap in the phone-access-restricted comparison should be read as an upper bound on the plausible effect of the program, not as a clean causal estimate. The true effect could be smaller than 2.1 points, including zero; it is very unlikely to be larger than what the unrestricted comparison suggests, and the direction of the remaining bias — toward making the program look more effective than it is — is more confidently signed than its magnitude.

## Results

The table below summarizes the comparisons available in this dataset. All figures are 30-day all-cause readmission rates as defined above.

| Comparison | Enrolled | Unenrolled | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overall (all eligible discharges) | 14.1% | 19.6% | 5.5 pts |
| Restricted to patients with a reachable phone | 16.8% | 18.9% | 2.1 pts |
| Patients living more than 45 minutes from a hospital | No detectable difference | No detectable difference | ~0 pts |
| Discharges to skilled nursing facilities (22% of total) | Not eligible for program | Not eligible for program | Not applicable |

### Overall Comparison

The unrestricted, overall comparison — 14.1 percent versus 19.6 percent — is the number most likely to appear in a slide deck, and it is the number we would caution most strongly against using as the basis for the September 15 decision on its own. As described above, roughly a fifth of the "unenrolled" group in this comparison consists of patients who were never offered the chance to enroll because they lacked a working phone number, a group with independent reasons to run a higher readmission risk. This comparison answers a narrower question than it appears to: it tells us that patients who both could be reached and chose to participate did better than everyone else, including patients who structurally could never have participated. It does not by itself tell us what would happen if the program's reach were extended, which is the actual decision in front of the committee.

### Restricted to Reachable Patients

The phone-access-restricted comparison — 16.8 percent versus 18.9 percent — is the more defensible figure for describing what the program appears to be doing among patients who had a realistic chance to enroll. This comparison is still consistent with a real benefit, and a gap of roughly two percentage points, if it reflects the program's actual effect, would be clinically meaningful and would matter for both the penalty exposure and the standard of care. But as discussed above, this comparison still cannot separate the effect of the intervention from the effect of being the kind of patient who agrees to it. We would characterize this result as suggestive of benefit, not as confirmation of it.

### By Distance from a Hospital

Among patients living more than 45 minutes from a hospital — a substantial subgroup in a health system serving a rural area around Beckley — there was no discernible difference in readmission rates between enrolled and unenrolled patients. There are two very different explanations for this finding, and the data available here cannot distinguish between them. One possibility is that the program genuinely does not help patients who live far away, plausibly because the in-person follow-up visit within seven days — a component that requires travel — is harder for these patients to complete even when they are nominally enrolled, or because a distant patient's access to timely emergency and outpatient care generally is a stronger driver of readmission than anything the program can offset. The other possibility is simply that this subgroup is smaller than the full sample, and a modest true effect of the size seen elsewhere (roughly two points) may not be detectable with the number of far-away patients available in 18 months of discharges. We do not have subgroup sample sizes precise enough in the extract used for this brief to say with confidence which explanation is closer to correct, and we would flag this as a priority for a focused follow-up analysis before any decision is made about whether the six proposed positions should be geographically targeted.

### By Discharge Disposition

Skilled nursing facility discharges — 508 patients, 22 percent of the total sample — were excluded from the program by design and therefore do not appear in either the enrolled or unenrolled comparison groups. This is worth stating plainly because it will otherwise be easy to overlook in a budget conversation: none of the readmission results in this brief say anything about whether the transitional care model, or some adapted version of it, would help or hurn the roughly one in five heart failure patients discharged to a SNF. If the committee's interest in the six new positions extends to reducing the hospital-wide readmission rate that determines the $1.2 million penalty — which, unlike this evaluation, includes SNF discharges — that population represents an unaddressed gap, not a demonstrated non-issue.

## Limitations

We see five limitations serious enough to affect how this brief should be used, beyond the enrollment selection problem already discussed at length above.

**The claims lag and coverage gap likely understate readmissions for both groups, and the direction of any resulting bias between groups is genuinely uncertain.** Because the state APCD lags real-world events by roughly five months and covers an estimated 78 percent of discharges even before accounting for the excluded Medicare Advantage plans, some true readmissions to non-Mountain Laurel hospitals are missing from this analysis entirely, and some that occurred late in the study period may not yet have arrived in the feed at all. It is tempting to assume this affects both groups equally and therefore washes out of the comparison, but there is a plausible reason it might not. Enrolled patients, by virtue of completing an in-person follow-up visit at a Mountain Laurel facility within a week of discharge, may be somewhat more likely to return to a Mountain Laurel facility if they are readmitted at all, simply because they have a recent, active relationship with the system. If so, a larger share of enrolled patients' true readmissions would be captured completely and immediately through the EHR, while a larger share of unenrolled patients' true readmissions might depend on the lagging, incomplete claims feed — which would make the unenrolled group's *measured* rate understated relative to its true rate, and would tend to narrow the true gap relative to what we report. We flag this as a real possibility, not a settled conclusion; the same mechanism could in principle run the other way if enrolled patients, for reasons related to language, insurance, or personal preference, actually use non-system facilities at a different rate than unenrolled patients. We do not have the data in this extract to test this directly, and we recommend it as a next step before treating either the 5.5-point or the 2.1-point gap as precise.

**We cannot distinguish declined enrollment from incomplete enrollment.** Patients coded as unenrolled in this dataset include both those who were offered the program and said no, and those who agreed but did not complete both components — the 48-hour call and the seven-day visit. These are likely to be different patients with different risk profiles, and collapsing them into a single "unenrolled" category may understate the effect for patients who fully complete the intervention as designed and overstate it for patients who receive a partial version of it. A future analysis that separates full completers from partial or non-starters would give the committee a cleaner picture of what a fully delivered program achieves.

**Sample size limits the confidence we can place in subgroup results, particularly the distance finding.** With 2,308 total discharges, and after removing the SNF-excluded population and the phone-inaccessible population, the number of patients available for the distance-based subgroup analysis is meaningfully smaller than the headline figures suggest. We have not been able to calculate formal confidence intervals or a statistical test for the distance subgroup within the scope of this brief, and we would not want the committee to read "no detectable difference" as equivalent to "no effect exists" for that population.

**The comparison groups may differ in illness severity in ways not captured by readmission diagnosis alone.** We do not have ejection fraction, comorbidity burden, or prior admission history in the extract used for this analysis. Heart failure readmission risk varies substantially by these factors, and if enrolled patients are systematically less sick at baseline — which the voluntary nature of enrollment makes plausible — some of the observed gap, even in the phone-restricted comparison, could reflect baseline severity differences rather than the program itself. Risk adjustment using these variables, where available in the record, would strengthen any future version of this analysis considerably.

**This is an 18-month, single-system observational study, not a controlled trial.** Even setting aside every issue above, an evaluation of this kind cannot rule out that some portion of the observed improvement reflects other changes occurring in heart failure care at Mountain Laurel over the same period — updated discharge protocols, staffing changes elsewhere in the system, or secular trends in heart failure readmissions that are occurring regardless of this specific program, locally or nationally. We have no comparison hospital or pre-program baseline period built into this design that would let us separate a program effect from a general trend.

## Implications for the September 15 Decision and the Penalty

Dr. Mensah's question was whether the program lowered readmissions. The honest answer is: probably, by a modest amount, for the specific slice of patients who have a working phone and choose to participate — but the strength of that evidence is considerably weaker than the headline 5.5-point gap implies, and the program's design leaves a substantial share of the system's heart failure population, including all SNF discharges and a group with no phone access, entirely untested.

For the budget committee, we would frame the decision this way. The case for the six additional care coordinator positions should not rest on the claim that the program has been shown to cut readmissions by nearly a third, because that is not what a careful reading of this data supports. The more defensible claim is that the program shows a real, if smaller, signal of benefit among patients who can be reached and choose to engage, that the phone-access barrier itself — the reason 19 percent of eligible patients were never offered the program at all — is a concrete and fixable problem that additional coordinator capacity could directly address, and that extending outreach methods beyond telephone-only contact (home visits, text-based outreach, or coordination through family members) could close a documented, structural gap rather than simply enrolling more of the same voluntarily-engaged patients who are already doing relatively well. If the committee's goal is to move the readmission rate that drives the $1.2 million penalty, the SNF-excluded population — 22 percent of all heart failure discharges — deserves explicit consideration in how the new positions are deployed, since no result in this brief speaks to whether the current program design would help them, and they are not carved out of the penalty calculation the way they are carved out of this evaluation.

We would recommend three things before or alongside the September 15 vote. First, treat the 2.1-point, phone-restricted comparison, not the 5.5-point overall comparison, as the working estimate of the program's plausible effect, and be explicit in committee materials that even this figure likely still includes some voluntary-selection bias. Second, request a short follow-up analysis — feasible within the existing data — that separates full program completers from partial participants, adds available severity measures, and produces formal subgroup estimates with uncertainty ranges for the distance comparison, so that any decision to target the new positions geographically rests on firmer ground. Third, if the positions are approved, build an evaluation design into the expansion itself — ideally a staggered or randomized rollout to the currently-unreached, no-phone population — so that a year from now the system has a cleaner answer than this observational comparison can provide, both for internal decision-making and for any future conversation with CMS about the drivers of the readmissions penalty.

## Appendix A: Notes on the Phone-Access Restriction

The restriction used in the body of this brief — comparing enrolled patients only against unenrolled patients who also had a working telephone number — was constructed by pulling the contact-attempt field from the care coordination log rather than relying on enrollment status alone. Every discharge in the program-eligible population (that is, excluding SNF dispositions) carries a coordinator-entered flag indicating whether an outreach attempt was made and, if not, why. "No working number" was the single largest reason for no attempt, at 19 percent of the eligible population, followed by smaller categories — deceased before contact, transferred to hospice, and a residual "other" category each under 3 percent — that were left out of both the numerator and denominator of every comparison in this brief because they represent a handful of patients and a mix of reasons unrelated to the phone-access question. Readers reproducing these figures from the raw extract should expect the eligible denominator for the restricted comparison to be slightly smaller than eligible discharges minus the 19 percent no-phone group, on the order of two to three percentage points smaller, for that reason.

It is worth being explicit that the phone-access flag reflects whether *a* number was on file and reachable at the time of the outreach attempt, not whether that number remained reachable throughout the seven-day follow-up window. A small number of patients who were successfully contacted at 48 hours but could not be reached again to schedule the in-person visit are coded in the coordination log as "enrolled, visit not completed." For the purposes of this brief we treated any patient with a completed 48-hour call as enrolled regardless of whether the seven-day visit was completed, which means the enrolled group as defined here is somewhat broader, and the intervention somewhat less uniformly delivered, than "pharmacist call plus in-person visit" implies. This is the same issue flagged in the limitations section under incomplete enrollment, and it cuts toward understating rather than overstating the program's apparent effect, since patients coded as enrolled but who received only half the intervention are pooled with those who received the full sequence.

## Appendix B: Specification for a Follow-Up Evaluation

If the committee wants a firmer answer before the next penalty cycle, we would propose the following design, all of it achievable with data the system already collects or could begin collecting immediately.

*Completer analysis.* Re-run the core comparison three ways: full completers (call and visit both done) against unenrolled patients with a phone; partial completers (call only) against the same comparison group; and full completers against partial completers directly. This requires no new data collection, only a cleaner pull from the coordination log than was used for this brief, and it would tell the committee whether the visit component is doing meaningful work on its own or whether the phone call accounts for most of the observed gap — a question that matters directly for staffing, since the visit is the more resource-intensive half of the model and the one that six new coordinators would primarily be hired to deliver.

*Severity adjustment.* Pull ejection fraction category, count of heart failure admissions in the prior twelve months, and a basic comorbidity count (diabetes, chronic kidney disease, COPD) from the EHR problem list for the full 2,308-patient sample, and re-estimate the phone-restricted gap after adjusting for these factors. None of this requires linkage to any outside source; it is a matter of pulling fields already documented at the index admission.

*Staggered rollout for the no-phone population.* Rather than treating the 19 percent of patients without a working number as permanently unreachable, we would recommend the six new positions be deployed first against this group specifically, using alternative contact methods — a designated home visit within 48 hours in place of the telephone call, or outreach through an emergency contact or caregiver on file — with enrollment phased across hospitals or discharge months so that early and late cohorts can be compared. This would generate the closest approximation to a controlled comparison available without a formal randomized trial, and it would directly test whether the phone-access barrier is fixable in the way this brief suggests it might be, rather than assuming it.

*Claims reconciliation check.* For a defined subset of patients — perhaps a random sample of 200 enrolled and 200 unenrolled patients — manually reconcile EHR-documented follow-up contact after discharge (any Mountain Laurel encounter of any kind, not just readmission) against the claims feed once it has had a full year to mature, to get an empirical estimate of whether enrolled patients really do return to the system at a different rate than unenrolled patients, which would let the analysis in Appendix A's companion limitation be resolved with evidence rather than left as a caveat.

We estimate the completer analysis and severity adjustment could be completed within four to six weeks using existing data and would not require committee approval or new expenditure. The staggered rollout and claims reconciliation would need to be built into the design of the expanded program itself, which is a decision for the committee to make in conjunction with, not after, the September 15 vote if it wants the next round of evidence to be materially stronger than this one.

## Appendix C: Glossary

**Index discharge** — the hospitalization from which the 30-day readmission clock starts; in this analysis, any hospitalization at a Mountain Laurel facility with a heart failure discharge diagnosis during the study window.

**All-payer claims database (APCD)** — a state-maintained repository of medical claims submitted by insurers operating in West Virginia, used here as the only available source of information about hospitalizations occurring outside the Mountain Laurel system.

**30-day all-cause readmission** — any inpatient hospitalization, for any cause, occurring within 30 days of an index discharge date, following the same definition used in CMS's Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program methodology.

**Phone-restricted comparison** — the subset analysis in this brief limiting both the enrolled and unenrolled groups to patients who had a documented, reachable telephone number at the time of discharge, used to remove the structural exclusion of no-phone patients from the overall comparison.

**Voluntary selection bias** — the general problem, present in any evaluation of an opt-in program, that patients who choose to participate may differ systematically from those who do not, in ways that affect the outcome being measured independent of the program itself.
