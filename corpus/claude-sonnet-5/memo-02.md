**TO:** Quality and Engineering Staff
**FROM:** Rasheed Mansour, Quality Director
**DATE:** November 14, 2025
**SUBJECT:** Solvern Aerosystems Supplier Audit — Findings and 60-Day Corrective Action Schedule

Solvern Aerosystems completed its scheduled supplier audit on November 10–12. Solvern accounts for 44 percent of our revenue, and this was a for-cause audit tied to two customer complaints filed earlier this year on nickel-alloy housing lots. The audit team issued eleven findings, four of them major. Solvern's supplier quality manager confirmed in the closing meeting that our corrective action plan is due in 60 days, by January 13, 2026, and that Solvern is placing a hold on the release of any new part numbers to Kearsarge until the plan is submitted, reviewed, and accepted. Existing production releases are not affected, but three part numbers we were expecting to quote in Q1 will not move forward under hold.

I want every finding in front of this group before we assign root cause owners, because several of them touch the same underlying gaps and I don't want four separate teams solving one problem four different ways.

**Major Findings**

1. First article inspection records for six of fourteen sampled part numbers did not carry traceable linkage to the raw material heat lot certification. The FAI packets were complete on dimensional data but the material cert reference number was either missing or did not match the router.
2. Three coordinate measuring machine probes used for final acceptance inspection on Solvern hardware were operating 11, 19, and 26 days past their calibration due date. All three showed in-tolerance on subsequent verification, but the audit team treated the lapse as a systemic gap in our calibration tracking, not an isolated clerical miss.
3. The nonconforming material segregation cage on the shop floor lacked positive physical identification separating open MRB dispositions from hardware pending disposition. The auditor found one tote with mixed tags.
4. A corrective action from Solvern's 2024 audit, finding 7, concerning inspection plan revision control, was closed in our system but the auditor could not find objective evidence the fix had actually been verified in production. That finding has been reopened.

**Minor Findings**

The remaining seven cover document control revision mismatches between two inspection plans and their governing drawings, incomplete operator training records for four employees qualified on Solvern work in the last six months, missing calibration stickers on two torque wrenches, a gap in our internal audit schedule for Q3, an out-of-date organization chart in the quality manual, insufficiently detailed root cause language on two closed corrective actions from last year, and a records retention question on archived FAI packets older than five years.

**Schedule and Ownership**

I am assigning root cause owners this week for each major finding, with containment actions due November 21 and root cause analysis due December 5. That gives us five weeks to validate corrective actions before the January 13 submission. The four majors will each get a dedicated cross-functional review — engineering, quality, and the responsible production supervisor — because I expect the FAI traceability finding and the reopened 2024 finding are connected to the same document control weakness.

I am instituting a weekly review every Wednesday at 9:00 a.m. starting November 19 until submission. Attendance is not optional for the assigned owners. I will brief Hana and the plant leadership team every other week, and I will flag to Gail immediately if any containment action requires capital or contract labor we have not budgeted.

The hold on new part numbers is a real cost to this business, and it will stay in place until Solvern accepts our plan and verifies closure through their own follow-up audit, which they have indicated will occur within 30 days of acceptance. I need this group's full attention on getting this right the first time. We do not have room for a second round.

**FROM:** Hana Ishikawa-Brennan, Vice President of Manufacturing
**DATE:** December 10, 2025
**SUBJECT:** Insourcing Five-Axis Roughing — Program Kickoff

Timberline Precision Machining, which has performed the five-axis roughing on our titanium and nickel-alloy structural parts for the past nine years, filed for Chapter 11 protection on December 3. As of this week, 2,200 pieces of our work in process are sitting inside Timberline's shop, and their bankruptcy counsel has told us we do not have a clear timeline for release. Combined with the volume already delayed ahead of the filing, 38 percent of our open orders are now past promise date. This has happened three weeks after Solvern's audit put us on a corrective action clock and a part-number hold. I don't need to tell any of you what it means to be managing both at once, but I want to be direct about it in writing: this is the most exposed our delivery performance and our largest customer relationship have been at the same time in this company's history.

The decision in front of us is not whether to recover the stranded work. We will recover it, and Eli's team is already working the logistics with Timberline's trustee and two alternate shops. The decision is whether we continue to depend on an outside shop for roughing at all. I am recommending, and the board has now discussed informally, that we do not. We are bringing five-axis roughing in-house, permanently.

The scope is two five-axis machining centers, a rebuilt inspection cell sized to support in-house roughing acceptance rather than the outsourced receiving inspection we do today, and a second shift of 28 machinists to run it. Total capital is $6.8 million. The machine tool lead time quoted to us is 34 weeks from purchase order, which means if we place the order by early January, we are looking at delivery in late August 2026, installation and qualification through the fourth quarter, and a production ramp that does not reach full rate until early calendar 2027.

That gap between now and machine delivery is the hard part, and it is where I need each of you.

Eli — I need a bridge plan for the 2,200 stranded pieces and the ongoing roughing volume through next August. That means working the trustee for release of finished and in-process material, qualifying at least one additional outside roughing source in parallel so we are not dependent on a single shop again even during the bridge, and a recovery schedule I can show Solvern and our other customers with dates I can stand behind.

Rasheed — the inspection cell rebuild has to be qualified against both our own procedures and Solvern's audit expectations, given where we stand on the corrective action plan. I want the inspection cell scope reviewed against finding 1 and finding 3 specifically, because I don't want us building a new capability that repeats a documentation gap Solvern just cited us for.

Gail — you and I need to talk before I put anything further in front of the board. The October approval covered $4.5 million, and this scope is $6.8 million. I know where that leaves us and I know it's your job to tell the board how we close it, not mine.

Ingrid — 28 machinists on a second shift, in this labor market, is the single biggest execution risk in this plan. I want your read on feasibility before we finalize the capital request, not after.

I am setting up a weekly steering committee, Mondays at 8:00 a.m., starting December 15. I will run point on the overall program. This is now the top operating priority for this plant.

**TO:** Marguerite Okonkwo-Reyes, Chief Executive Officer; Kearsarge Board Finance Committee
**FROM:** Gail Dombrowski, Chief Financial Officer

**SUBJECT:** Revised Capital Request — Roughing Insourcing Funding Gap and FY28 Forecast Impact

The board approved $4.5 million in October for a single five-axis machining center, sized at the time to reduce our dependence on outside roughing capacity and to give us a second source alongside Timberline Precision. Timberline's bankruptcy filing on December 3 has overtaken that scope. Hana's program, which I support, now calls for two machining centers, a rebuilt inspection cell, and a second-shift workforce build to run them, at a total capital cost of $6.8 million. That leaves a funding gap of $2.3 million against what the board has approved.

**Where the Gap Sits**

The additional $2.3 million breaks down as follows: the second machining center itself, which the October plan did not include, at approximately $1.4 million; expanded inspection cell scope, driven partly by Rasheed's need to qualify new procedures against the Solvern corrective action plan rather than the narrower receiving-inspection rebuild originally scoped, at roughly $500,000; and rigging, foundation work, electrical service upgrades, and an initial spare tooling package sized for two machines instead of one, at approximately $400,000.

I recommend the board approve the additional $2.3 million as an amendment to the October authorization rather than as a new request, given the timeline pressure. The 34-week machine tool lead time means we need purchase orders placed no later than mid-January to hold an August delivery window. Missing that window pushes delivery into the fourth quarter and the full production ramp into the second half of FY28 rather than the first.

**Financing**

I recommend funding the gap through a draw on our existing revolving credit facility rather than an equipment lease. Our facility has sufficient headroom, the rate is favorable relative to lease financing at our current credit profile, and I would rather carry this on the balance sheet given that we intend to own and run this equipment for the next 15 to 20 years, not treat it as a bridge asset.

**FY28 Forecast Impact**

I want to walk through the effect on the forecast in three pieces, because this program is going to touch the P&L before it helps it.

First, the near-term cost of the Timberline disruption. Between expedited freight, overtime premium at our two alternate roughing sources, and the cost of qualifying a second outside shop as Eli's bridge plan requires, I am carrying $1.8 million of incremental cost in the first two quarters of this fiscal year that was not in the original budget. This is recovery cost, not investment, and it does not buy us anything permanent.

Second, the Solvern part-number hold. Three part numbers we expected to quote and book in Q1 are frozen until the corrective action plan is accepted and verified. I have removed approximately $2.1 million of expected FY26 bookings from the forecast pending that resolution. If the hold extends past Solvern's stated 30-day post-acceptance verification window, that number grows.

Third, the timing of insourcing benefit. With machines ordered in January, delivered by late August, installed and qualified through Q4 calendar 2026, and ramping through FY27, I am not underwriting full run-rate margin benefit from insourced roughing — lower per-piece cost, reduced freight, tighter cycle time — until the first quarter of FY28. That is a full year later than the original October plan assumed, which had a single machine ramping by mid-FY27. I have adjusted the FY28 forecast to reflect $3.2 million of annualized margin improvement beginning in Q1 FY28 rather than phased in earlier, and I have removed the interim benefit the October plan had assumed for FY27.

I recognize this is a harder number to bring to the board than the one we approved in October. It is also the accurate one. I would rather amend the request now than come back in June asking for a second amendment because we underscoped it twice.

I am available to walk the finance committee through the model in detail before the January board meeting.

**TO:** Hana Ishikawa-Brennan, Eli Castellanos, Plant Leadership
**FROM:** Ingrid Halvorsen, Director of Human Resources
**DATE:** January 20, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Second Shift Staffing Plan and Wage Adjustment for Roughing Insourcing

Hana asked me in December to give a candid read on the feasibility of hiring 28 machinists for a new second shift ahead of the roughing insourcing ramp. I have spent the last five weeks pulling labor market data, talking to our staffing agency partners, and reviewing our own turnover and exit interview history. I want to give the plan leadership team the full picture before we finalize headcount assumptions tied to the August machine delivery.

Sullivan County unemployment sits at 2.1 percent. That is not a tight labor market in the ordinary sense — it is close to the floor of what a labor market can register outside of a genuine crisis. There is no meaningful pool of unemployed, qualified five-axis machinists sitting in Claremont or the surrounding towns waiting to be hired. Every credible candidate we would want is currently employed somewhere else, most of them at shops within a 45-minute drive of here.

That brings me to the wage problem. Our current shop wage scale sits $3.40 an hour below the Springfield-area market rate for comparable five-axis machining work. Springfield is close enough — under an hour for most of the county — that it functions as a competing labor market for us, not a separate one. We are not just failing to attract new machinists at our current scale; the gap is a documented driver of the 27 percent machinist turnover we posted last year. Four of the twelve exit interviews I reviewed from experienced machinists cited pay relative to Springfield-area shops by name.

I do not believe we can hire 28 machinists onto a new second shift at our current wage scale, in this labor market, on this timeline, without also solving the retention problem on our existing shift. If we raise wages only for the new shift, we will accelerate turnover on days, not just fail to fix it.

**Recommendation**

I am recommending a wage scale adjustment effective March 1, 2026, applied across all machinist classifications on both shifts: an immediate increase of $2.50 an hour, followed by an additional $0.90 an hour on September 1, 2026, timed to land after the new machines arrive and the second shift is up and running. That closes the full $3.40 gap to Springfield-area market by the time we need the workforce fully trained and qualified. I am also recommending a second-shift differential of $1.25 an hour on top of the adjusted base scale, consistent with regional practice for off-hours machining work.

To support recruiting specifically, I am proposing a $2,500 sign-on bonus for qualified five-axis machinists, paid half at 90 days and half at one year, an employee referral bonus of $1,000, and a formal training partnership with River Valley Community College's advanced manufacturing program to build a pipeline of machinists we train ourselves rather than compete for in the open market. That pipeline will not produce fully qualified five-axis operators by August, but it starts building the bench we will need for the following two years of turnover and growth.

**Cost and Timing**

The wage scale adjustment costs approximately $1.1 million annualized across our current machinist workforce, rising to roughly $1.5 million once the second shift is fully staffed. This is a labor cost increase, not a capital cost, and I understand it needs to be reflected in Gail's forecast work separately from the machine and inspection cell capital.

On timing, I want to be candid: even with the wage adjustment, recruiting, vetting, and qualifying 28 machinists by the time the machines arrive in late August is an aggressive target. I am building the plan around a staggered hire, bringing on roughly 8 to 10 machinists per month starting in April so that the first cohort is trained and cross-qualified on our existing equipment before the new machines land, rather than trying to hire the full complement in a single push in July.

I need approval on the wage scale change by early February to begin recruiting against the new rate before the spring hiring season, which is when Springfield-area shops typically post their own openings.
