# Verification of Unknown-Material Service Lines: A Comparison of Predictive Records Against Field Findings

## Licking Valley Regional Water Authority — Research Brief

**Prepared for:** Sun-Hee Park, Chief Engineer; Alma Restrepo, Deputy Director
**Prepared by:** Reginald Boateng, Data Analyst
**Date:** July 30, 2026

---

## The Question and What We Found

Chief Engineer Park asked a direct question: do the Authority's paper records predict what crews actually find in the ground, and can the Authority trust those records to guide the next phase of lead service line replacement before the board commits a $19 million bond tranche in August? A related question, raised publicly by Heath council member Dale Hutchins, is whether the fourteen months of field verification have been distributed fairly across the service territory or have instead concentrated in wealthier, newer neighborhoods while the oldest and highest-risk blocks went unchecked.

The short answer to both questions is that the records are unreliable in ways that matter for compliance and spending, and that the verification effort to date has *not* been distributed in proportion to risk. Overall, 42 percent of connections that records listed as "unknown material" turned out on inspection to be lead or galvanized and therefore require replacement — a rate far above what a random sample of the full 96,400-connection system would suggest and high enough that "unknown" cannot be treated as a low-priority category. That overall figure, however, conceals a stark split: verified connections in the four oldest wards came back lead or galvanized 68 percent of the time, while connections in areas platted after 1960 came back positive only 19 percent of the time. Records that affirmatively said "copper" were wrong 9 percent of the time, meaning the inventory has false negatives as well as unknowns. Most consequentially, 71 percent of all completed potholes fall in just three of the Authority's eleven wards, and those three wards are not the four oldest, highest-risk wards — they are newer, higher-turnout, higher-homeownership wards where the lead find rate is close to the systemwide low. Meanwhile, verification in majority-renter blocks has been slowed by a 28 percent refusal rate for interior inspection, a pattern concentrated in the same older wards that show the highest lead rates in the data that does exist.

Put together, these findings mean the Authority does not yet have a statistically defensible basis for the verified lead service line inventory it must file with the state by December 31, 2026, and it does not yet have a defensible basis for directing the next $19 million tranche to specific neighborhoods. The brief below lays out the record sources, the verification results, the coverage problem, and what each of these implies for the two decisions in front of the board.

---

## Background

Licking Valley Regional Water Authority serves 96,400 connections across three counties from its Newark headquarters, with 214 employees supporting operations, engineering, and customer service. The Authority operates under a 2024 consent order that requires submission of a verified lead service line inventory to the state by December 31, 2026, consistent with federal lead and copper rule revisions that require utilities to resolve "unknown" material classifications through physical or documented verification rather than continued estimation. The Authority holds $58 million in bond authority for replacement work, of which the board has so far released $39 million in two prior tranches; a third tranche of $19 million is scheduled for a board vote in August 2026.

Of the system's 96,400 connections, 31,200 — just under a third — carry no reliable material classification in the Authority's records and are coded "unknown." Over the past fourteen months, field crews have potholed and visually or physically verified 4,850 of those unknown connections, or about 15.5 percent of the unknown population. Deputy Director Restrepo commissioned this comparison after Council Member Hutchins alleged, at a public Heath council meeting in June, that verification crews had been directed preferentially to wealthier streets rather than to the oldest and most likely-lead-bearing infrastructure. That allegation, combined with the December filing deadline and the pending tranche vote, is the immediate occasion for this brief, but the underlying question — whether the Authority's inherited records can be trusted to plan a nine-figure capital program — would need answering regardless of the political context.

## Data and Methods

### Three overlapping and incomplete record sources

The Authority's material classification for any given connection is derived from up to three sources, none of which was built for this purpose and none of which covers the full system on its own.

**The 1978 tap card file.** This is the oldest and, for the oldest parts of the system, the only documentary record of original installation material. It consists of handwritten index cards recording tap date, size, and material for connections made from the utility's founding through 1978, when the practice was discontinued in favor of permit records. The cards were scanned in 2009 as a preservation measure, but the scanning project did not include re-transcription, and the Authority estimates that 6 percent of cards are illegible in the scanned images — faded ink, water damage, or handwriting that optical character recognition and manual review both failed to resolve. Illegible cards are coded "unknown" by default, meaning a meaningful share of the "unknown" population is unknown not because the material was never recorded, but because the record exists and cannot be read. This matters because illegibility is not randomly distributed: the cards most likely to be damaged or faded are disproportionately the oldest cards, which correspond to the oldest wards — the same wards where lead is most likely to be present. Data loss and lead risk are therefore correlated, not independent, which is a first reason to distrust an "unknown" classification as a neutral, low-risk residual category.

**The permit database.** Beginning in 1994, the Authority moved to an electronic permit system that captures material for new taps, replacements, and any connection touched by a permitted plumbing or excavation project. This is the most reliable of the three sources for connections installed or altered after 1994, but it has two structural gaps relevant here. First, it says nothing about connections untouched by a permitted event since 1994 — a connection installed in 1955 and never replaced or permitted since has no permit-database record at all, regardless of whether its tap card is legible. Second, and more troubling for present purposes, the permit database is not infallible even where it does have an entry: crews verifying connections that the permit database coded "copper" still found lead 9 percent of the time. Some of this is plausible explanation rather than data error — partial replacements where a copper line was installed at the curb stop but a lead gooseneck or pigtail was left in place at the main, a pattern common nationally and consistent with what inspectors have described anecdotally in Newark's core wards. But some share of the 9 percent almost certainly reflects transcription error, permit records applied to the wrong parcel, or contractor self-reporting that was never field-checked at the time. The Authority cannot currently distinguish between these explanations at scale, which is itself a limitation discussed below.

**Meter replacement notes.** The third source is the least structured: handwritten or free-text notes recorded by meter technicians at the time of routine meter changeouts, which since the early 2000s have periodically included an observation of the visible service line material at the meter pit or basement entry point. These notes were never intended as an inventory input, are inconsistently completed, use inconsistent terminology ("gray pipe," "lead?," "unk" all appear), and were only systematically extracted into the current database in a 2019 data cleanup effort. Where meter notes conflict with the tap card or permit database, the Authority's existing protocol has been to default to "unknown" rather than adjudicate the conflict, which means that a meaningful number of the 31,200 unknown connections are unknown specifically because two of the three sources disagreed with each other — not because no data existed.

### Verification protocol

Field verification proceeds by excavating a small pit ("potholing") to the curb stop or, where necessary, the water main tap, allowing a crew member to visually confirm material by direct observation and, where ambiguous, a scratch test to distinguish lead from galvanized steel or copper. Where the property owner permits it, crews also attempt an interior inspection at the point of entry, which is faster, cheaper, and less disruptive than a full pothole and is the preferred first method; potholing is used where interior access is refused or where the interior inspection is inconclusive (common in older homes with interior plumbing changes that obscure the original service material). This distinction matters for the refusal analysis below: refusal is a refusal of *interior* access, not of the pothole itself, and crews have in nearly all cases proceeded to pothole verification regardless of refusal, which is why the Authority has a verified answer for 4,850 connections despite an elevated refusal rate in some areas.

Verification assignments over the fourteen-month period were made by a combination of engineering judgment, contractor route efficiency, and — as discussed in the Coverage section below — an unstated set of practical constraints (permit backlog, traffic control availability, main-line replacement project overlap) that were not designed with a sampling frame in mind. This is the central methodological limitation of this brief: the 4,850 verified connections are not a random or stratified sample of the 31,200 unknown population, and the results below must be read as a description of what verification has found *where it has occurred*, not as a projectable estimate for the full unknown population without adjustment. Where this brief reports rates by ward or by housing age, those are rates within the verified subset for that category, not rates re-weighted to represent the unverified remainder of that category.

## Results

### Overall find rate

Of the 4,850 connections verified, 2,037 — 42 percent — were found to be lead or galvanized and therefore require replacement under the consent order's definitions. The remaining 58 percent resolved to copper, plastic, or other non-lead materials. A 42 percent positive rate on a population the records already flagged as uncertain is high in absolute terms: if that rate held across the full 31,200 unknown connections, it would imply roughly 13,100 additional lead or galvanized lines beyond what the Authority's currently confirmed inventory shows, a figure large enough on its own to affect both the December filing and the tranche sizing conversation. But — and this is the central finding of this brief — the 42 percent figure is an average across a highly uneven distribution, and where verification has occurred is correlated with where lead is least likely to be found.

### Results by ward and housing age

The verified sample splits cleanly along two related but distinct lines: the age of the housing stock (a proxy for original installation era) and the ward in which verification actually took place. The table below summarizes the two comparisons the Authority currently has clean numbers for.

| Category | Share of verified sample | Lead/galvanized find rate |
|---|---|---|
| All verified connections (systemwide) | 100% (4,850) | 42% |
| Four oldest wards (pre-1920 platting) | ~13% (approx. 610–650) | 68% |
| Areas platted after 1960 | ~64% (approx. 3,100) | 19% |
| Connections where permits recorded "copper" | subset of above | 9% found lead |
| Three highest-coverage wards (of 11 total) | 71% (approx. 3,444) | approximates post-1960 rate |

The four oldest wards — the historic core of Newark and its immediately adjoining platted sections, largely built out before 1920 — show a 68 percent find rate among verified connections, more than three and a half times the rate in areas platted after 1960 (19 percent). This gap is exactly what an engineer familiar with national lead service line patterns would expect: lead and galvanized installations were standard practice through the mid-twentieth century and effectively phased out by federal restriction only after 1986, so pre-1960 housing stock — and especially pre-1920 stock, installed before galvanized steel became the more common budget alternative — carries systematically higher lead risk. The magnitude of the gap in the Authority's own verified data (68 percent versus 19 percent) confirms that housing age is doing real predictive work here, which is useful: it means that where the Authority *has* looked, the pattern is coherent and not noise. The problem, developed in the next section, is where the Authority has looked.

The copper-mislabeling finding deserves separate attention because it changes the interpretation of "unknown" as a category. Among connections where the permit database affirmatively stated the material was copper — connections the Authority would ordinarily treat as resolved and not prioritize for verification at all — crews still found lead 9 percent of the time when those connections happened to be checked (typically because they were incidentally potholed as part of a main replacement project or a customer complaint, not because they were flagged for verification). A 9 percent error rate on records the Authority currently treats as *definitively resolved*, as opposed to merely unknown, suggests the 31,200 figure the Authority is working from likely understates the true scope of the problem. If even a fraction of the roughly 65,200 connections currently coded as confirmed non-lead by permit record carry a similar 9 percent latent error rate, the true number of lead or galvanized lines in the system could exceed what either the "unknown" count or the currently confirmed count would suggest on their own. This finding did not come from a systematic recheck of copper-coded connections — the Authority has not run one — and should be read as a flag for a targeted audit rather than as a system-wide estimate.

### What the ward-level find rate implies when combined with coverage

Because 71 percent of all potholing to date occurred in three wards, and because those three wards are demonstrably not the four oldest wards, the 42 percent overall find rate is pulled downward by heavy sampling in low-risk areas. A rough reconstruction makes the point concretely: if the three high-coverage wards produced roughly 3,444 of the 4,850 verifications at something close to the 19 percent post-1960 rate (these wards are predominantly post-1960 subdivisions), that alone accounts for on the order of 650 of the 2,037 total lead findings. The remaining 1,406 verifications, spread across the other eight wards — including the four oldest — produced the remaining roughly 1,387 findings, an implied find rate well above 90 percent in that residual pool once the high-coverage wards are backed out. That residual figure is almost certainly overstated by this back-of-envelope arithmetic — the "other eight wards" are not uniformly old, and the true distribution is more complicated than a two-bucket split — but the direction of the distortion is unambiguous and important: the systemwide 42 percent average is being generated disproportionately by a small number of high-find-rate verifications concentrated in under-sampled older wards, while the *volume* of verification work has gone overwhelmingly to areas least likely to need replacement.

## Coverage and Refusal Patterns

### Geographic concentration of verification effort

Seventy-one percent of the 4,850 completed potholes — roughly 3,444 verifications — fall in three of the Authority's eleven wards. Those three wards correspond to subdivisions platted predominantly between the mid-1960s and mid-1980s, areas of comparatively high homeownership, lower renter turnover, and — per the housing-age comparison above — a lead find rate close to the systemwide low of 19 percent. The four oldest wards, where the verified find rate is 68 percent, together account for a substantially smaller share of completed verification work, on the order of 12 to 13 percent of the total, despite containing a disproportionate share of the 31,200 unknown connections to begin with (the Authority's own records show the four oldest wards holding roughly a third of the unknown-material population, since these are precisely the areas most likely to predate the permit database and most likely to have illegible tap cards).

This pattern is directly responsive to Council Member Hutchins's allegation and, on the data available, substantially corroborates its factual premise, whatever its motivation. Verification effort has not tracked lead risk; if anything, it has moved somewhat inversely to it. This does not appear from the data reviewed here to be the result of a deliberate policy decision to prioritize wealthier areas — no directive to that effect appears in the work order records reviewed for this brief — but the practical drivers of route assignment (contractor efficiency, ease of traffic control, alignment with concurrent main-replacement projects, and, as discussed next, ease of securing resident cooperation) have produced that outcome regardless of intent. For a public utility operating under a consent order and a board answerable to member municipalities including Heath, the distinction between deliberate inequity and inadvertent inequity produced by operational convenience is important for accountability purposes but does not change the remedial obligation: the verification program to date has not distributed effort in proportion to need, and continuing on the same operational logic for the remaining 26,350 unknown connections would repeat the pattern.

### Refusal rates and the renter-occupancy problem

A second, related coverage issue compounds the geographic one. Refusal of interior inspection access reached 28 percent on majority-renter blocks, well above the systemwide average refusal rate implicit in the Authority's ability to complete verifications on the large majority of attempted connections elsewhere. Majority-renter blocks are disproportionately concentrated in the older, denser housing stock near the Newark core — the same four wards with the highest lead find rate and the lowest verification coverage. The mechanism is intuitive and worth stating plainly: interior inspection requires either an owner-occupant's consent or a landlord's cooperation in scheduling access with a tenant, and absentee landlords with units in older, lower-value housing stock have less financial incentive to prioritize scheduling than an owner-occupant does, particularly where the landlord may reasonably anticipate that a lead finding could trigger a replacement obligation, temporary service disruption, or future disclosure requirement. Where interior access is refused, crews have generally proceeded to pothole verification, which does not require interior access — this is why the Authority has any verified data at all from these blocks — but pothole-only verification is slower and more expensive per connection than interior inspection, and it appears from route-completion records that pothole-only jobs have been more likely to be deferred or dropped from a given week's route when schedules tightened. The refusal pattern therefore does not fully explain the coverage gap in the oldest wards on its own, but it is a contributing and compounding factor: the areas hardest to verify quickly are the same areas most in need of verification, and the Authority's operational practice to date has tended to route around difficulty rather than through it.

## Limitations

Several limitations bear directly on how much weight the board should place on these findings.

**Non-random verification sample.** As stated above, the 4,850 verified connections were not selected through a stratified or random sampling design. The 42 percent overall rate, the 68 percent and 19 percent ward/era rates, and the 9 percent copper-mislabeling rate are all accurate descriptions of what has been found *where crews have gone*, but none of them is a statistically valid projection to the full 31,200 unknown population without further adjustment. A defensible systemwide estimate would require either a retrospective reweighting analysis (feasible, using the ward and era composition of the unverified population as weights) or a prospective stratified sample of the remaining connections designed explicitly to be representative. Neither has been done to date.

**Small subgroup sizes.** The four-oldest-wards figure of 68 percent and the copper-mislabeling figure of 9 percent are each based on verification subsets in the low hundreds, not thousands, of connections. These are directionally credible given their consistency with known national patterns for pre-1920 housing stock, but confidence intervals around figures of this size are wide enough that a rate reported as 68 percent should be read as "clearly and substantially higher than the newer-area rate," not as a precise point estimate suitable for budgeting to the connection.

**Tap card illegibility is not random.** The 6 percent illegibility rate in the 1978 tap card scans is concentrated in the oldest cards, meaning the "unknown" population is itself enriched for old, high-risk connections in a way that is difficult to quantify precisely without a targeted re-transcription effort on the physical cards (the originals are retained in Authority archive storage and have not, per records reviewed, been consulted since the 2009 scan).

**No systematic recheck of "confirmed" records.** The 9 percent copper-mislabeling finding rests on incidental verifications, not a designed audit. The Authority does not currently know whether that rate is representative of all copper-coded connections, higher in older sections of the permit database, or an artifact of a specific data-entry period or contractor. This is a significant open question given that the confirmed-copper population (roughly 65,200 connections) dwarfs the unknown population in size; even a modest true error rate there has larger absolute implications than errors within the unknown category.

**Refusal is measured at the block level, not resolved to individual outcomes.** The 28 percent refusal figure describes interior-access refusal on majority-renter blocks; it does not by itself establish that those same connections were never verified by other means, since pothole verification frequently proceeded regardless. The brief's characterization of the oldest wards as "under-verified" rests on the combination of low pothole counts and high refusal rates together, not on refusal alone, and a more precise accounting of pothole-completed-despite-refusal versus fully-unverified-due-to-refusal would sharpen this picture.

**No cost or engineering-feasibility data included.** This brief addresses record accuracy and verification coverage only. It does not address the comparative cost of interior inspection versus potholing at scale, replacement cost by ward, or engineering sequencing constraints (main condition, street reconstruction schedules, traffic control availability) that would properly inform how a prioritized verification and replacement plan should be sequenced even after coverage gaps are corrected.

## Implications for the December Filing and the August Tranche

### For the December 31, 2026 inventory filing

The consent order requires a *verified* inventory, and the state's expectation, consistent with federal guidance on lead service line inventories, is that "unknown" cannot be used as a permanent or unexamined catch-all — utilities are generally expected either to verify unknown connections or to apply a defensible presumption (often, treating unknowns in a given category as lead until proven otherwise) where verification is incomplete. With five months remaining before the filing deadline and 26,350 of the 31,200 unknown connections still unverified, full physical verification of the remaining population in the time available is not realistic at the crews' demonstrated pace (4,850 over fourteen months implies a rate that would require roughly six years to complete the remainder at the same tempo). Two consequences follow directly from the findings in this brief.

First, the Authority should not file a December inventory that extrapolates the 42 percent systemwide find rate uniformly across the remaining unknown population, because that rate is now demonstrably a blend of a low-risk, over-sampled subpopulation and a high-risk, under-sampled one; applying it uniformly would both understate risk in the unverified portions of the four oldest wards and, if the state later audits ward-level outcomes against the filed inventory, expose the Authority to a credibility problem given that its own data show the flat rate is not representative of the pattern within its own sample. A defensible filing approach would instead apply the 68 percent rate as the working presumption for unverified connections in the four oldest wards and pre-1920-platted areas generally, the 19 percent rate as a starting presumption for unverified connections in post-1960 areas, and an intermediate, clearly-flagged-as-estimated rate for the remaining mid-century areas not separately reported in the data reviewed here — with all three presumptions explicitly labeled as provisional pending verification, consistent with how other utilities operating under similar consent orders have handled partial inventories.

Second, the tap card illegibility problem and the copper-mislabeling finding both suggest the Authority's classification system needs a documented correction protocol before December, independent of new field verification. Re-transcribing the illegible 6 percent of tap cards from originals, and running even a modest targeted audit of copper-coded connections in the oldest wards (where the 9 percent error rate is most likely to be concentrated, on the pattern seen elsewhere in this brief), are lower-cost interventions than additional potholing and could materially improve the defensibility of the December filing on their own.

### For the August $19 million tranche

The board is being asked to commit the next tranche of a $58 million bond authority against a body of prioritization data that, per the findings above, has been shaped substantially by operational convenience rather than by risk. If the tranche is allocated using the same route-planning logic that produced the existing 71 percent concentration in three low-risk wards, the Authority risks directing a significant share of a $19 million replacement commitment toward connections that a stratified verification effort would very likely show are disproportionately non-lead — while the four oldest wards, already shown to carry a 68 percent find rate on the connections checked so far, continue to receive a small fraction of both verification and, by extension, replacement dollars.

This brief does not recommend a specific allocation formula — that is an engineering and financial planning exercise beyond its scope — but it does support three conclusions relevant to the August vote. First, the tranche should not be sized or geographically pre-allocated on the assumption that the current 42 percent systemwide rate applies evenly across the service territory; doing so would misallocate resources in a way the Authority's own data already contradicts. Second, some portion of the tranche should reasonably be directed toward closing the verification gap in the four oldest wards and on majority-renter blocks specifically — including funding for a revised interior-access strategy (tenant-direct outreach, landlord incentive or compliance mechanisms, or a default-to-pothole protocol that does not depend on interior consent) — before the bulk of replacement dollars are committed to specific streets. Third, the board and Deputy Director Restrepo should be prepared to address Council Member Hutchins's allegation directly and factually rather than by disputing its premise: the coverage data show a real concentration of effort in higher-income, newer wards relative to the oldest wards, whatever its cause, and a public accounting of that pattern alongside a stated correction plan is likely to be more defensible, both politically and in any subsequent state review of the December filing, than a response that treats the allegation as unfounded.

Taken together, the record comparison Chief Engineer Park requested does not answer whether the Authority will meet its December obligation or how the August tranche should ultimately be spent — those remain engineering, legal, and financial decisions for the Authority's leadership and board. What the comparison does establish is that the records cannot currently be trusted to make those decisions on their own, that the verification effort to date has not tracked risk, and that the coverage gap has fallen most heavily on exactly the properties — old, occupied by renters, previously undocumented — that both the consent order and basic public-health logic would suggest should have been checked first.

The immediate operational question this raises is what a corrected verification protocol should look like for the remaining five months before the filing deadline, given that full physical verification of all 26,350 outstanding unknown connections is not achievable at the demonstrated crew pace. Three adjustments follow directly from the findings above and could be implemented without waiting for a broader planning process.

The first is a re-weighting of route assignment away from contractor efficiency and toward risk-stratified sampling. Rather than continuing to fill weekly route sheets with whatever connections are easiest to reach — which is what has produced the 71 percent concentration in three wards — crews should be assigned a minimum weekly quota of verifications in the four oldest wards and on majority-renter blocks specifically, even where those jobs take longer per connection or require repeat visits to secure access. This would not close the coverage gap by December, but it would materially improve the statistical basis for whatever presumption rates the Authority applies to the connections that remain unverified at filing time, because the sample underlying those presumption rates would better reflect the population it is meant to represent.

The second is a change to how refusals are handled procedurally rather than simply recorded. At present, refusal of interior access appears to function as a soft deprioritization signal — jobs requiring pothole-only verification are more likely to slip from a given week's schedule when time is short. A written protocol that treats pothole verification as the default method on any block where interior refusal exceeds a defined threshold, rather than treating pothole verification as a fallback attempted only after an interior refusal is already recorded, would remove the scheduling penalty that appears to be compounding the coverage gap in older, renter-heavy areas. This is a dispatch and workflow change, not a new funding request, and could be implemented within the current crew capacity.

The third is the targeted records correction work described above: re-transcription of the illegible tap cards from the original 1978 file, and a bounded audit — not a full recheck, but a defined sample large enough to produce a defensible confidence interval — of copper-coded permit records in the four oldest wards, where the 9 percent mislabeling rate found incidentally in this review is most likely to be concentrated. Both of these are desk exercises rather than field exercises and could proceed in parallel with the route changes above, using existing staff rather than additional crew capacity.

None of these three adjustments resolves the underlying tension between the December deadline and the scale of the remaining unknown population, and none of them substitutes for the allocation decision the board must make in August. But together they would materially improve the quality of the presumption-based estimates the Authority will need to rely on for whatever share of the 26,350 remaining unknown connections cannot be physically verified in time, and they would give Deputy Director Restrepo and Chief Engineer Park a factual, documented response to Council Member Hutchins's allegation that goes beyond acknowledging the pattern — one that shows a specific, already-underway correction to it. Given that the same coverage and refusal patterns identified here would, left uncorrected, likely reproduce themselves in however the August tranche is spent, addressing them now is lower-cost than addressing them after $19 million has already been committed to a plan built on the existing, risk-inverted verification record.

A final point bears on sequencing between the two deadlines. Because the December filing is a state compliance obligation with a fixed date and the August tranche is a board decision with more procedural flexibility, it may be reasonable for the board to treat the two on different timelines: proceeding with the filing in December using clearly labeled, ward-stratified presumption rates as described above, while deferring final geographic allocation of the full $19 million tranche until the risk-stratified route adjustment has had at least two to three months to generate a more representative verification sample. A partial or conditional tranche release in August — sized to fund both continued replacement in already-verified high-find-rate areas and the expanded verification effort in the four oldest wards — would let the Authority act on the money it already has confidence in without locking in an allocation formula built on the coverage gap this brief has identified. That is a financial and political judgment for the board to make, not a data question, but it follows directly from what the verification record does and does not currently support.
