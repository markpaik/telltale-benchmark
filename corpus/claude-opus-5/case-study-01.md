# The Pooler Retrofit
## How Coastal Harvest Alliance Nearly Broke Itself Solving a Capacity Problem

---

### I. The Organization

Coastal Harvest Alliance occupies a particular niche in the American charitable food system: large enough to matter, small enough that a single bad decision reaches every corner of the operation. From a warehouse on the western edge of Savannah, the food bank supplies a twelve-county service area that runs from the Atlantic beaches west into the pine flats of Screven and Candler counties and south toward the Altamaha River. It is a territory of roughly 690,000 people, about a fifth of whom fall below 185 percent of the federal poverty line, and it contains within it two distinct hunger problems that the organization has never been able to solve with the same tools.

The urban problem sits in Chatham County, where a tourism-and-port economy generates a large number of jobs that pay too little to cover Savannah's rising housing costs. The food bank's urban partners are relatively dense, relatively sophisticated, and able to receive frequent deliveries — a soup kitchen on Bull Street can take a pallet three times a week and turn it over. The rural problem is the opposite. In the outer counties, partner agencies are church pantries operating out of fellowship halls, staffed by volunteers in their seventies, open one Saturday morning a month. They cannot receive frequent deliveries, cannot store much of anything cold, and have no capacity to absorb variation. A truck that comes late doesn't come at all, because the volunteers have gone home.

By 2023 the organization employed 118 people and ran a $41 million budget, of which roughly $31 million was the assessed value of donated product moving through the building and about $10 million was cash — government commodity administration, USDA reimbursements, foundation grants, a corporate giving program built largely around the port's logistics companies, and an individual donor base of about 14,000 households that gave reliably if not lavishly. Chief Executive Marguerite Ostrowski had run the organization for nine years. Chief Financial Officer Lucia Sandoval-Reyes had been there for six. Chief Operating Officer Terrence Achebe was the newest of the three, hired in 2021 out of a third-party logistics firm in Jacksonville, and Warehouse Director Nadia Haddad had come up through the building itself, starting as a forklift operator in 2013.

The organization's reputation in the region was strong and, importantly, was built on reliability rather than on scale. Coastal Harvest had spent a decade telling rural pantries that the truck would come when it said it would. That promise was the entire basis of the partner relationship, and it turned out to be the thing most vulnerable to what happened next.

---

### II. The Squeeze

The pandemic years did to Coastal Harvest what they did to most food banks: they broke the relationship between the building and the volume.

In fiscal 2021 the organization distributed 17 million pounds. Federal emergency commodity programs were flooding the system, retail donation had surged as grocery supply chains thrashed, and demand in the service area had roughly doubled from pre-pandemic levels. By fiscal 2023 distribution had reached 24 million pounds — a 41 percent increase in two years — and every pound of it had to pass through a 60,000 square foot warehouse built in 1994 for an organization moving perhaps 9 million pounds a year.

Haddad's capacity numbers by early 2023 were unambiguous. The building was running at 96 percent of usable pallet positions on an average day, which in practice meant it was over capacity, because a warehouse at 96 percent has no room to stage, sort, or recover from a bad delivery. Trucks were being turned away or asked to wait. Volunteer sorting sessions were being canceled because there was nowhere to put the sorted product. The organization was renting outside dry storage in two locations, one of them eleven miles away, and paying to shuttle product between them.

The refrigeration constraint was worse and mattered more. Of the 60,000 square feet, only 4,000 were refrigerated or frozen — about 6.7 percent, against a sector norm closer to 25 percent for a food bank of that profile. This was not merely inconvenient. It was strategically disabling, because the direction of travel in charitable food was toward fresh product: produce recovered from farms and packers, dairy, protein, prepared foods rescued from retail. Those categories were where the donated supply was growing, where the nutritional value was, and where partner agencies were asking Coastal Harvest to go. The organization was turning down produce donations weekly. Haddad kept an informal log of refusals in 2022 and estimated the organization had declined or failed to place roughly 2.6 million pounds of fresh product, most of it because there was no cold space to hold it for the two or three days it took to build out delivery routes to the rural counties.

So the problem was defined, correctly, as a facilities problem. What followed was a failure not of problem definition but of option evaluation.

---

### III. Two Options, One Deadline

Ostrowski and Sandoval-Reyes brought the board two options at the March 2023 meeting.

**Option A** was a purpose-designed distribution center on a parcel the organization had been quietly tracking near the intersection of I-16 and the Jimmy DeLoach Parkway. Roughly 105,000 square feet, of which 28,000 would be refrigerated and frozen, designed around the flow the organization actually had rather than the flow it had inherited. Estimated cost $22 million. Estimated timeline four years from board approval to occupancy, including a capital campaign the development team assessed as feasible but demanding — it would require securing at least two lead gifts in the $2.5 million range from donors who had never given at that level.

**Option B** was a retrofit. A 78,000 square foot building in Pooler, ten miles west of the existing facility, formerly used as a consolidation warehouse by a regional building-products distributor. Available on a fifteen-year lease with a purchase option in year seven. The plan called for building out 19,000 square feet of refrigeration and freezer space, replacing the dock apron, adding four dock doors, upgrading electrical service, and reconfiguring racking. Estimated cost $14.5 million. Estimated timeline fourteen months.

The board packet ran 43 pages. In retrospect — and both Ostrowski and Sandoval-Reyes have been direct about this — the packet was asymmetric in a way that predetermined the outcome. Option A had been under development for nearly two years and was documented accordingly: a site study, a schematic design package from an architecture firm with food bank experience, a traffic analysis, a capital campaign feasibility study, and a cost estimate carried to a level of detail that included allowances for site work and a 12 percent contingency.

Option B had been under development for eleven weeks. Its cost estimate came from a single general contractor who had walked the Pooler building twice, in the company of a broker representing the landlord. There was no independent mechanical engineering assessment of the refrigeration build-out. There was no structural assessment of the roof, which would need to carry the weight of new condensing units. There was no phase-one environmental report. The contingency was 5 percent. The $14.5 million figure was, functionally, a contractor's budgetary number dressed in the typography of a board document.

Sandoval-Reyes has since described the moment she should have intervened and didn't. "I looked at a five percent contingency on a retrofit of a thirty-year-old industrial building and I thought, that's low. And then I thought, Cornelius has been in commercial real estate for forty years, and if it were dangerously low he would say so. I outsourced my own professional judgment to somebody else's résumé. That's the whole mistake, right there."

Board Chair Cornelius Bledsoe advocated forcefully for the retrofit. His argument had three parts, and two of them were good.

The first was demand urgency. People in the twelve counties needed food now, and a four-year timeline meant four more years of declining produce donations and canceled volunteer sessions. Bledsoe framed this in moral terms and the framing landed, because it was true.

The second was capital risk. A $22 million campaign was roughly triple anything the organization had ever raised. If it stalled at $13 million, Coastal Harvest would be sitting on a partially funded project, having spent two years of executive attention, with donors fatigued and nothing built. The $14.5 million retrofit could be funded from a combination of the state grant, a $4 million bridge from reserves, a $5 million tax-exempt bond placement, and a $2 million campaign that the development team considered close to certain.

The third argument was the one that broke the process. Georgia had awarded Coastal Harvest a $3.5 million grant from a state food-security infrastructure program funded through federal recovery dollars. The appropriation carried a hard spending deadline: funds had to be expended, not merely committed, by June 30, 2024. Under Option A, in March 2023, nothing would be expended by June 2024 except soft costs. Under Option B the grant would be fully absorbed by the refrigeration build-out.

"Three and a half million dollars that walks out the door if we dither," Bledsoe said, in language several attendees remember nearly verbatim.

The board voted 11–3 for the retrofit. The three dissenting votes came from a retired hospital facilities director who wanted an independent mechanical assessment first, a pastor from Jesup who worried about the ten-mile move lengthening rural routes, and a member who abstained in substance and voted no on process grounds — she thought eleven weeks of diligence was not enough to compare against two years of it.

Ostrowski's retrospective assessment is the most pointed statement any of the leadership team has made about the episode:

> "We let a three-and-a-half-million-dollar grant deadline set the schedule for a fifteen-year facility decision. When you say it that plainly it sounds insane, and it is insane, but it did not feel insane in the room. It felt like stewardship. It felt like we were being careful with money. The grant wasn't a resource in that meeting. It was a clock, and the clock was making the decision, and none of us said out loud that the clock was making the decision."

### IV. What Went Wrong, In Order

Construction started in June 2023 with a projected occupancy of August 2024.

**The refrigeration specification.** The first and most consequential failure was baked in before a nail was driven. The contractor's mechanical subcontractor sized the refrigeration system — four compressors serving the cooler and freezer envelopes — against a load calculation derived from the square footage of the space and a standard assumption about product turns.

The assumption was wrong for a food bank, and specifically wrong for this food bank. A commercial cold storage facility receives product at temperature, on pallets, from refrigerated trucks, and holds it stably. A food bank receives produce warm off a farm truck in August, in mixed-size cartons, at unpredictable volumes, and has to pull the field heat out of it fast. It opens and closes its dock doors constantly. It has volunteers walking in and out of the cooler for two-hour repack sessions. Its peak load is not its average load, and its peak load arrives exactly when ambient temperature is highest.

Nobody in the design chain caught this, because nobody in the design chain had built cold storage for a food bank, and Coastal Harvest had no one on its side of the table qualified to review the calculation. Haddad, who understood the operational reality better than anyone, was not shown the mechanical drawings. "I saw a floor plan," she said later. "I saw where the walls were going. I never saw a load calculation. I wouldn't have been able to check the math, but I could have told them about August. I could have told them about the dock doors. Nobody asked me and I didn't know there was something to ask about."

**The schedule.** The contractor was nine months late. Occupancy slipped from August 2024 to May 2025 through an accumulation of causes: the roof required $1.1 million of unbudgeted structural reinforcement to carry the condensing units, discovered in month four; electrical service upgrades required a utility interconnection that took nineteen weeks against a projected eight; a subcontractor default on the dock apron cost eleven weeks; and the sequencing of the refrigeration build-out was rearranged twice to accommodate the roof work. The contract had liquidated damages of $2,500 per day, which sounds meaningful until you calculate that nine months of delay generated a theoretical $675,000 against actual consequential damages that ran to several times that, and until you note that the contractor contested the majority of the delay days as owner-caused or excusable.

**The grant.** The grant deadline that drove the whole decision was met, technically. Coastal Harvest expended $3.5 million on refrigeration components and installation labor by June 30, 2024. What it had on that date was $3.5 million of correctly-purchased, incorrectly-specified equipment installed in a building it could not yet occupy.

**July 2024.** In the last week of July 2024, with the cold storage envelope substantially complete and the organization having begun moving perishable product into it to relieve pressure on the Savannah cooler, two of the four compressors failed within thirty-one hours of each other during a stretch of days that ran above 97 degrees.

The freezer envelope held. The cooler envelope rose from 38 degrees to 61 degrees over roughly nine hours overnight and was not discovered until the 5:30 a.m. shift, because the temperature monitoring system's alarm was configured to call a phone number belonging to a project superintendent who was no longer on the job.

One hundred forty thousand pounds of product was destroyed. The mix mattered as much as the tonnage: roughly 61,000 pounds of fresh produce, 44,000 pounds of dairy including a large donated yogurt load, 28,000 pounds of protein, and the balance in prepared and refrigerated dry goods. At the organization's assessed value it represented approximately $310,000 of product. But the operational meaning was worse than the accounting meaning, because it was two weeks of fresh distribution to the entire twelve-county network, and it disappeared in a single night in the middle of summer, when school meal programs were down and demand was at its annual peak.

Haddad had to make the calls. "I called forty-one agencies in two days to tell them there was no produce coming. I got asked, more than once, whether we still knew what we were doing. That's the question you can't recover from quickly. A truck being late is a logistics problem. Somebody asking whether you still know what you're doing is something else."

### V. The Cost of Eighteen Months

The first eighteen months of the project — roughly June 2023 through November 2024 — cost the organization in four currencies, and only one of them showed up cleanly on the financial statements.

**Partner attrition.** Active partner agencies fell from 214 to 189, a loss of 25 agencies representing 11.7 percent of the network.

The composition of that loss was the damaging part. Eighteen of the 25 were rural pantries in the outer counties — precisely the agencies with the least slack, the least capacity to absorb a late or short delivery, and the least ability to source product from anywhere else. These were not agencies that shopped around and found a better supplier. Several of them closed outright. A pantry in Screven County that had operated out of a Methodist fellowship hall since 1997 shut down in October 2024 after four consecutive months of short or missed deliveries; its two coordinators, both in their late seventies, told Achebe they no longer had the energy to keep explaining to their neighbors why the shelves were empty. Others reduced their hours, dropped from monthly to quarterly distribution, or stopped ordering perishables entirely — which meant that even the ones who technically remained active had shrunk what they could offer.

The organization's own analysis estimated that the 25 lost agencies had been serving approximately 9,400 individuals annually, and that roughly 5,800 of those had no alternative pantry within fifteen miles.

**Service reliability.** On-time order fulfillment — defined as a partner order delivered in full within its scheduled window — fell from 91 percent in fiscal 2022 to 78 percent by mid-2024. The number understates the damage in two ways. It counts a partial delivery as on-time if the truck arrived within the window, and it averages urban and rural performance. Broken out, urban partners saw on-time rates around 86 percent while rural routes ran at 68 percent, and rural partners were the ones for whom a missed window meant a missed month.

**Direct financial cost.** The emergency cold chain expense was the visible wound: $600,000 spent on rented refrigerated trailers over roughly fourteen months. At peak in August 2024 the organization had nine reefer trailers parked at two locations, generator-powered, requiring a staff member to check temperatures four times daily and fuel to be delivered on a standing schedule. Trailers are the worst possible cold storage — expensive per pallet position, hard to pick from, temperature-unstable when opened, and impossible to organize. The organization estimated that trailer-based picking consumed roughly 2.3 times the labor hours per pallet of cooler-based picking.

Beyond the $600,000: approximately $210,000 in overtime attributable to the disruption, $310,000 in destroyed product, and $145,000 in legal and consulting fees related to contractor disputes.

**Staff.** The organization lost 21 employees to voluntary departure between June 2023 and December 2024, against a normal run rate of about eight. Fourteen were warehouse and transportation staff. Two of Haddad's four supervisors left within six weeks of each other in the autumn of 2024. Exit conversations pointed consistently at the same thing: not the hours, which had been long before, but the futility. People were working harder to deliver worse service and could not see the end of it.

Ostrowski's own account of that period is unsparing:

> "I spent the summer of 2024 managing a construction dispute and I was the worst version of myself as a chief executive. I was in the weeds on change orders. I was not talking to donors. I was not talking to partner agencies. And the thing about a fourteen-month project that becomes a twenty-three-month project is that you can always tell yourself you're two months from the end, so you never stop and reassess. You just keep being two months from the end for a year."

---

### VI. The Correction

The turn came in October 2024, and it came because Sandoval-Reyes forced a reckoning that was financial in form and strategic in substance.

She built a model that answered a question nobody had asked in eighteen months: what does full completion of the original scope actually cost from here, and what does the operating gap cost us in the meantime? The answer was that the remaining scope would push total spend past $19 million, that occupancy under the current sequencing was realistically nine to eleven months out, and that the organization would burn another $700,000 to $900,000 in emergency cold chain and overtime getting there. She presented it to Ostrowski and then, at Ostrowski's insistence, to the full board on October 22, 2024, with a recommendation that the board delegate operational control of the project to Achebe and Haddad and authorize a renegotiation of scope.

The board did so. The delegation was explicit and unusual: Achebe was given authority to commit up to $1.8 million without further board approval, to hire outside representation, and to modify the project scope, with reporting obligations to a three-member board committee rather than to the full board. Bledsoe supported the motion. He also, at the same meeting, offered to step aside as chair, and the board declined the offer — a decision Ostrowski has described as correct, because "the person who has to live with the consequences of their advocacy is the person most motivated to fix it, and Cornelius worked harder on the correction than anyone on that board."

The correction had four components.

**An owner's representative.** Within three weeks Achebe engaged a Charleston firm specializing in owner-side representation on cold chain and food processing construction. The engagement cost $240,000 through project close. Its first deliverable, delivered in nineteen days, was the document Coastal Harvest should have had in February 2023: an independent assessment of the refrigeration system against the organization's actual operating profile.

The findings confirmed what the July failure had already demonstrated. The system was undersized by roughly 30 percent against realistic peak load. The design assumed 1.4 air changes per hour at the dock interface against realistic food bank operation closer to 4. There was no provision for pre-cooling warm produce, which meant field-hot product was being loaded directly into a holding cooler and dragging the whole envelope up. The controls were configured for a stable-load commercial application with no capacity to modulate against the swings a food bank actually generates.

The rep's recommendation was not to replace the system, which would have cost $4.1 million and delayed occupancy another eight months. It was to reconfigure: add a dedicated pre-cooling bay with independent refrigeration for warm inbound produce, install air curtains and a vestibule at the dock interface, replace the controls package, add a fifth compressor for peak capacity, and re-commission the whole system with an eight-week performance verification period. Total cost $1.55 million. Timeline five months, running concurrently with other work.

Achebe's view of the retainer:

> "Two hundred forty thousand dollars is what we thought we couldn't afford in early 2023. It's what would have prevented every single thing that went wrong. I don't have a more useful sentence than that. The cheapest thing we ever bought was somebody whose only job was to be skeptical on our behalf."

**Phased occupancy.** The single most valuable move was also the simplest, and it required no construction at all. The original plan called for occupying Pooler in one move once everything was complete. Haddad proposed instead moving dry goods immediately — in December 2024, five months before the refrigeration work would finish — into the completed dry storage portion of the building.

The logic was that the binding constraint on the Savannah facility was total space, and dry goods were consuming most of it. Moving 34,000 square feet of dry storage out of Savannah freed the old building to be reconfigured around cold chain, allowing the organization to expand its Savannah cooler footprint from 4,000 to 9,500 square feet using temporary insulated panel construction at a cost of $290,000. That, in turn, allowed the organization to release six of its nine rented reefer trailers by February 2025, saving roughly $31,000 a month.

The dry goods move happened over eleven days in December 2024, using volunteer labor and three rented trucks, and it did not disrupt a single scheduled delivery.

"That was Nadia's idea and nobody senior to her had thought of it in eighteen months," Achebe said. "Because we were all thinking about the project as a thing that finished. She was thinking about the building as a thing you could use in pieces. When you're in a hole, the question isn't when do we get out, it's what can we do from in here."

**Renegotiated scope.** Achebe and the owner's rep went through the remaining contract line by line against the question of what the organization needed to operate versus what the drawings said. The result was $2.9 million of deferrals and deletions:

- A second-floor office mezzanine, 6,200 square feet, deferred indefinitely. Administrative staff remained in Savannah. Saving $1.4 million.
- A volunteer engagement center with classroom and demonstration kitchen, deferred to a future campaign. Saving $780,000.
- Exterior finishes, landscaping, and signage reduced to code minimum. Saving $260,000.
- Four of twelve planned additional dock doors deleted after throughput modeling showed eight sufficient through 2030. Saving $340,000.
- Miscellaneous scope reductions. Saving $120,000.

The deferrals were traded against the $1.55 million refrigeration reconfiguration, which is the trade the organization would have made at the outset had it understood the choice. "We bought a mezzanine we didn't need with money that should have bought compressors we did," Sandoval-Reyes said. "The correction was just reversing that."

**Contractor resolution.** Rather than litigate, Coastal Harvest negotiated a global settlement in January 2025: the contractor accepted $420,000 in liquidated damages against the theoretical $675,000, absorbed $310,000 of the roof reinforcement cost, and completed the remaining base scope at a fixed price with a $2,000-per-day incentive for early completion. Sandoval-Reyes estimated litigation would have cost $600,000 to $900,000 over two to three years with uncertain recovery, against a settlement that produced $730,000 of value in six weeks.

**Partner communication.** Running alongside all of it, Achebe instituted something the organization had conspicuously failed to do during the bad period: telling partners the truth on a schedule. Beginning in November 2024, every active and lapsed partner received a monthly one-page update with actual on-time performance numbers, actual product availability, and specific commitments for the following month. Achebe and Haddad personally visited all 25 lapsed agencies between November 2024 and March 2025.

"We had gone quiet because we were embarrassed," Achebe said. "That was the worst possible instinct. The pantry in Jesup didn't need us to be competent, they needed us to be predictable. If we'd told them in July 2024, here is exactly what you're getting for the next four months and it isn't much, most of them would have stayed. We lost agencies to silence more than to shortage."

### VII. Results

By June 2025 the picture had changed comprehensively.

| Measure | Baseline | Trough | June 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pounds distributed (annual) | 24M (FY23) | 22.1M (FY24) | 31M |
| Cost per pound | $0.41 | $0.52 | $0.33 |
| On-time fulfillment | 91% | 78% | 94% |
| Active partner agencies | 214 | 189 | 221 |
| Refrigerated square feet | 4,000 | 4,000 | 21,500 |
| Total project spend | $14.5M budget | — | $17.9M actual |

**Volume.** Thirty-one million pounds against 24 million at baseline — a 29 percent increase, and a 40 percent increase over the fiscal 2024 trough. The growth was disproportionately fresh: produce, dairy, and protein rose from 31 percent of total poundage to 48 percent. This was the strategic objective the whole project existed to serve, and it was finally achieved.

**Cost per pound.** From $0.41 to $0.33, a 19.5 percent reduction, driven by the elimination of trailer rental and off-site shuttling, the labor efficiency of picking from racked cooler space rather than trailers, and simple fixed-cost absorption across a larger denominator. On 31 million pounds the eight-cent reduction represents about $2.5 million in annual operating value — which, against $3.4 million of budget overrun, means the corrected facility repays its own excess cost in roughly sixteen months of operation.

**Reliability.** Ninety-four percent on-time, three points above pre-project baseline. Rural route performance, the number Haddad watches, reached 92 percent against a pre-project 89 percent — the first time rural reliability had exceeded urban-adjacent reliability in the organization's history, a function of the pre-cooling bay allowing product to be staged for route building rather than pushed out the door the day it arrived.

**Partners.** Two hundred twenty-one active agencies, seven above the pre-project peak. Nineteen of the 25 lapsed agencies returned. Six did not, including four that had closed permanently. The organization also added 27 new partners, weighted toward the rural counties, made possible by cold chain capacity that let it serve pantries that had previously been unreachable with perishables.

Achebe's caution about that number: "Two twenty-one is a better number than two fourteen and I won't pretend otherwise. But four pantries closed and the people they served are still out there. There's no line in the recovery that undoes that. I want that in the record because I don't want us to tell this story as though it came out fine."

**Financial.** Total spend $17.9 million against a $14.5 million budget — 23 percent over. Against the $22 million purpose-built alternative, the organization spent $4.1 million less and got a facility with 21,500 refrigerated square feet against a planned 28,000, in a leased building rather than an owned one, without administrative or volunteer space. Sandoval-Reyes's assessment is that the retrofit was defensible as an outcome and indefensible as a process:

> "We landed at seventeen-nine and people want to say, well, that's still cheaper than twenty-two, so the decision was right. That's the wrong way to read it. We got to seventeen-nine by cutting a mezzanine and a volunteer center we will eventually have to build, and by spending a million and a half fixing something that should have been right the first time, and by burning through a million two in emergency operating costs that bought us nothing. And we lost twenty-five agencies and twenty-one staff. If you priced the whole thing honestly, including the things that don't appear on a balance sheet, the two options were much closer than the headline numbers suggest. We chose on the basis of a comparison that was never valid."

### VIII. What the Leadership Team Would Do Differently

The four executives were interviewed separately. Their accounts converge on five things.

**Never let a funding deadline set a facility timeline.** All four named this first.

Ostrowski: "The right move in March 2023 was to say to the state, we cannot responsibly spend this by June 2024, can we return it or can we get an extension, and if the answer is no then we forfeit three and a half million dollars and we build the right building. I have run the numbers on that counterfactual more times than is healthy. Forfeiting the grant would have cost us three and a half million. Taking it cost us three point four in overruns, one point two in emergency operations, twenty-five agencies, twenty-one staff, and two years of my attention. The grant was the most expensive money we ever received."

Sandoval-Reyes adds a procedural remedy the organization has since adopted: any restricted funding whose deadline would compress a capital timeline must be presented to the board as a separate decision, in writing, with the question stated explicitly — *are we willing to accept this money on this schedule, given what the schedule requires?* "It has to be its own vote. Otherwise it hides inside the project vote and nobody examines it."

**Diligence must be symmetrical.** Achebe: "We compared two years of work against eleven weeks of work and pretended it was a comparison. The rule now is that no capital option comes to the board until every option in the packet has been developed to the same depth. If we can't afford to develop them equally, we're not ready to decide."

Coastal Harvest's revised capital policy requires that any project over $5 million include, before board presentation: independent mechanical and structural assessment for any retrofit; a contingency of no less than 15 percent for retrofit and 10 percent for new construction; a written statement of the operating profile the facility must serve, signed by the operations director; and cost estimates from at least two independent sources.

**Hire the owner's representative before you need one.** Sandoval-Reyes: "The board asked in 2023 whether we needed one, and I said we could manage it internally. I said that because $240,000 sounded like a lot against $14.5 million. It is 1.7 percent. Every dollar of the $3.4 million overrun traces back to a question an owner's rep would have asked in week one. I will never again run a capital project of any size without one, and I will present it as a non-negotiable line item rather than an option."

**The people who will use the building must review the drawings.** Haddad: "I ran that warehouse for eleven years and nobody showed me a load calculation. And I want to be fair — I didn't ask, because I assumed the engineers knew things I didn't. They did know things I didn't. But I knew things they didn't, and the only one of those two gaps that killed us was mine going unspoken."

The organization now requires that operational staff at supervisor level and above formally sign off on mechanical, electrical, and workflow drawings for any capital project, with a structured review protocol that asks specific questions: What happens on the hottest day of the year? What happens when a donation arrives unannounced? What happens when a volunteer group of thirty is in the building? What happens when this piece of equipment fails at 2 a.m.?

**Overcommunicate during failure, especially when it's humiliating.** Achebe: "We went quiet in the exact period when our partners needed us to be loud. Not because we decided to, but because there was no good news and we didn't have a habit of communicating without good news. The habit is the fix. Monthly, on a schedule, whether or not there's anything good to say."

There is a sixth item that only Ostrowski raised, and it concerns governance rather than construction.

> "Cornelius was the most experienced person in that room on real estate, and his experience made him confident, and his confidence made the rest of us stop thinking. That is not his failure. That is the board's failure and it is mine, because I chaired the process. What I've put in place is that on any capital decision, we assign someone to argue the other side — formally, in writing, in the packet. Not a devil's advocate in the room, a written dissent that the board has to read. The three people who voted no in March 2023 were right, and they were right for the right reasons, and nothing in our process required us to engage with why. We just counted the votes and moved on."

### IX. For a Reader in the Same Position

If you run an organization approaching a capital decision under time pressure, the Pooler retrofit offers a small number of transferable lessons, and they are not the obvious ones. The obvious lesson — retrofits are riskier than new construction — is true but not very useful, and in fact Coastal Harvest's retrofit produced a facility that works, at a defensible cost. The retrofit was not the mistake.

The mistake was that a deadline attached to a small pool of restricted money was allowed to function as the decision-maker for a fifteen-year commitment, and that the organization's diligence process was structured in a way that made this invisible. Restricted funding with a spending deadline is not neutral. It is a force acting on your timeline, and it will pull you toward whatever option can absorb it fastest, regardless of whether that option is right. The only defense is to name the force out loud and vote on it separately.

The second lesson concerns asymmetric information. When one option in a decision packet has been studied for two years and another for eleven weeks, the shorter-studied option will almost always look better, because diligence surfaces problems and eleven weeks of diligence surfaces fewer of them. A 5 percent contingency does not mean a project is low-risk; it means nobody has yet found the risks. Boards read confidence intervals as information about the world when they are usually information about how much work has been done.

The third lesson is that expertise adjacent to your problem is not expertise in your problem. The mechanical engineers who sized Coastal Harvest's compressors were competent cold storage engineers. They had simply never designed for an operation that receives field-hot produce in unpredictable volumes through constantly-opening doors with volunteers walking in and out. The gap between commercial cold storage and charitable cold storage is not obvious from the outside, and it cost $1.55 million plus 140,000 pounds of food to discover. Whatever your sector's version of that gap is, someone on your side of the table has to be qualified to find it, and if nobody on your payroll is, you buy one.

The fourth is Haddad's insight, which generalizes further than the food bank world: a project is not a binary. Coastal Harvest spent eighteen months treating occupancy as a single future event and consequently extracted zero value from a partially complete building. Moving dry goods in December 2024 unlocked a chain of benefits — reconfigured Savannah cooler, released trailers, $31,000 a month — that had been available for months and that nobody saw because everyone was looking at the finish line. When a project is late, the highest-value question is not *when will this be done* but *what can we use right now.*

The fifth is about silence. Coastal Harvest lost 25 partner agencies, and its own leadership believes most of them were lost not to shortage but to the absence of information about shortage. A rural pantry with volunteers in their seventies can plan around bad news. It cannot plan around no news. The instinct to go quiet during failure is nearly universal and nearly always wrong, and the remedy is structural rather than attitudinal: a communication cadence that runs on a schedule and does not depend on having something good to report.

Finally, and least comfortably: the recovery was real and the damage was permanent. Coastal Harvest moves 31 million pounds at $0.33 a pound to 221 agencies, and by every measure on the dashboard it is a stronger organization than it was in 2023. It also presided over the closure of four food pantries in counties where there was nothing else, and those pantries are not on the dashboard. Achebe asked that this be stated plainly, and it should be, because the temptation in a case study like this one is to let the final numbers absolve the process that produced them. They don't. The organization got to a good place by a route it should not have taken, and the people who paid for the route were not the people who chose it.
