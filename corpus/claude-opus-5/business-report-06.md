## 1. Purpose and Framing

This report documents the operations of Susquehanna Rising Charter Schools across the 2025–26 school year. It is written for two audiences at once, and the tension between them shapes how it is organized. The first audience is this board, which needs an accurate internal account of where the network is strong, where it is not, and what decisions are coming. The second audience is the Harrisburg School District Board of Directors, which will consider the network's charter renewal in October and will receive this document as part of the renewal packet.

Those two audiences are usually served by different documents. The executive team's instruction — stated separately by Mr. Lemieux, Ms. Steinmetz, and Mr. Bishop — was that this report should not average the four campuses into a single network narrative. That instruction is worth stating plainly at the front, because it produces a report that reads differently from a conventional renewal submission. A network-level summary of Susquehanna Rising would show 3,120 students, 380 staff, a $47 million budget, and a set of aggregate outcomes that are neither alarming nor impressive. That summary would be true and would tell the reader almost nothing. The four campuses are in materially different conditions. One is performing above the city on the measure the authorizer cares most about. One is losing students and teachers simultaneously. One graduates students at a respectable rate but cannot account for what happens to four in ten of them. One is roughly 280 students short of the enrollment its building and staffing model assume, and that gap is the single largest financial fact in this document.

Presenting those four situations honestly carries some risk in a renewal year. It is the position of the executive team, endorsed by the board chair, that the risk of not presenting them is greater. Authorizers read enrollment files, state assessment results, and audited financials independently. A report that smooths over a 640-student middle school with 22 percent chronic absenteeism and six unfilled teaching positions will be read against the underlying data and will cost the network credibility on every other claim it makes. The approach taken here is to state each campus's condition, state what the network is doing about it, and let the record of candor do some of the work that a polished narrative cannot.

Section 2 covers the four campuses individually. Section 3 covers network financial position. Section 4 covers staffing and turnover. Section 5 addresses the Steelton Prep enrollment shortfall in detail, as the board specifically requested. Section 6 sets out what trustees should understand and be prepared to discuss going into the October hearing.

## 2. Campus Performance

### 2.1 Network Enrollment at a Glance

| Campus | Grades | Enrollment 2025–26 | Enrollment Target | Percent of Target | Prior-Year Enrollment | Change |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Riverfront Elementary | K–5 | 780 | 810 | 96% | 772 | +8 |
| Cameron Street Middle | 6–8 | 640 | 720 | 89% | 720 | −80 |
| Allison Hill Academy | 9–12 | 1,010 | 1,020 | 99% | 1,004 | +6 |
| Steelton Prep | 6–12 | 690 | 970 | 71% | 612 | +78 |
| **Network Total** | **K–12** | **3,120** | **3,520** | **89%** | **3,108** | **+12** |

The network total conceals its own components. Systemwide enrollment is essentially flat year over year — twelve students of growth on a base of 3,108 — but that flat line is the product of Steelton Prep adding 78 students while Cameron Street Middle lost 80. The two movements nearly cancel, and the cancellation is not a sign of stability. It reflects a network in which one campus is filling slowly toward a target it has never met while another is shedding students it previously held.

### 2.2 Riverfront Elementary

Riverfront Elementary enrolled 780 students in kindergarten through grade five, or 96 percent of its 810-student target. Attendance held at 92 percent for the year. Third-grade reading proficiency reached 41 percent against a city average of 29 percent, a margin of twelve percentage points.

That third-grade reading figure is the strongest single result in this report and deserves specific attention, because it is the result most likely to matter at the renewal hearing. Third-grade reading proficiency is the metric authorizers and state officials return to most often, both because it is an early predictor of later outcomes and because it is comparatively hard to manipulate through selective enrollment or scheduling. Riverfront's 41 percent is not high in absolute terms — a majority of its third graders are not proficient readers, and the report should not obscure that. It is high relative to the comparison group the authorizer will use. Against the city average of 29 percent, Riverfront's students are proficient at roughly 1.4 times the rate of their peers.

The 92 percent attendance rate is consistent with the campus's performance over the prior three years and sits above the elementary average for the district. Attendance and early literacy tend to move together, and the board should understand these two results as related rather than independent. The instructional model at Riverfront — protected morning literacy blocks, a full-time reading interventionist, and grade-level teams that meet weekly on student-level data — depends on students being present. It does not survive high absenteeism.

Riverfront is 30 students below target. In a network with an aggregate 400-student gap to target, a 30-student gap at one campus is not a management concern. It is within the range of ordinary attrition and mobility in a K–5 population in this city. No corrective action is recommended.

The board should be aware that Riverfront will carry a disproportionate share of the renewal argument. When the authorizer asks whether Susquehanna Rising delivers academic results its students would not otherwise get, Riverfront is the campus with the cleanest affirmative answer. That is a strength and also a concentration risk. If Riverfront's results decline, the network's academic case declines with them, because no other campus currently produces a comparable margin over the comparison group.

### 2.3 Cameron Street Middle

Cameron Street Middle enrolled 640 students in grades six through eight, down from 720 the prior year — a loss of 80 students, or 11 percent of the campus. Chronic absenteeism reached 22 percent. Six of 44 teaching positions were vacant from January through June, covered by long-term substitutes.

These three facts are not separate problems. They are one problem observed from three angles, and the board should read them together.

| Cameron Street Middle — Key Indicators | 2025–26 | Prior Year | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Enrollment | 640 | 720 | −80 (−11%) |
| Chronic absenteeism rate | 22% | 16% | +6 pts |
| Teaching positions authorized | 44 | 44 | — |
| Positions vacant, January–June | 6 | 1 | +5 |
| Percent of instructional staff vacant | 13.6% | 2.3% | +11.3 pts |
| Students affected by long-term substitute coverage | ~430 | ~85 | +345 |

Six vacancies out of 44 positions means that from January forward, roughly one in seven Cameron Street classrooms was staffed by a long-term substitute rather than a permanent teacher. Long-term substitutes in this market are, with occasional exceptions, uncertified or provisionally certified adults who provide supervision and continuity of a schedule but cannot be expected to deliver a coherent instructional sequence across a semester. Because middle school students rotate through multiple teachers, the number of students touched by those six vacancies is far larger than six sections' worth. Master schedule analysis indicates approximately 430 of the campus's 640 students — about two-thirds — had at least one long-term substitute in their daily schedule for the second half of the year.

Chronic absenteeism at 22 percent means that roughly 141 students missed ten percent or more of enrolled school days. The rate rose six points from the prior year. Families notice when a child's math teacher leaves in December and is replaced by a rotating series of adults; attendance is one of the ways they express that observation. The enrollment loss is the other. Eighty students did not disappear from the city — they enrolled elsewhere, predominantly in district middle schools and one other charter operator, per the withdrawal codes recorded in the student information system.

The causal sequence, as best the administration can reconstruct it, runs as follows. Cameron Street entered the year with elevated turnover from the prior spring, filled positions late and thinly, lost additional teachers at the semester break, could not backfill in a January market where every district in the region is competing for the same small pool of certified middle-grades candidates, and absorbed the consequences in attendance and enrollment. Each element made the next more likely. A campus with six vacancies is harder to recruit into, and a campus losing students has a shrinking budget with which to recruit.

Academic results at Cameron Street are consistent with this account and are not presented in detail here because the state has not yet released final grade-level proficiency data for the year. Internal benchmark assessments administered in May showed declines in grades six and seven mathematics against fall baselines, with grade eight roughly flat. The administration expects the released state results to show decline. The board should be prepared for that and should not be surprised by it at the renewal hearing.

Cameron Street is the campus most likely to draw sustained questioning from the authorizer. It is a campus that lost 11 percent of its students, staffed one in seven classrooms with substitutes for half a year, and saw absenteeism rise. There is no framing that makes those facts favorable. The board's position should be that the network identified the problem, understands its mechanism, and has committed specific resources to it — not that the problem is smaller than it appears.

Actions taken and committed for 2026–27 are: all six vacancies posted in March rather than June, with four filled as of this writing; a $4,000 mid-year retention payment for Cameron Street instructional staff who complete the full year, funded from the network reserve; reassignment of a network-level instructional coach to full-time deployment at the campus; and a family re-engagement effort targeting the 80 withdrawn students, of whom 14 have submitted re-enrollment applications.

### 2.4 Allison Hill Academy

Allison Hill Academy enrolled 1,010 students in grades nine through twelve, essentially at its 1,020-student target. It graduated 84 percent of its senior class, documented postsecondary plans for 61 percent of graduates, and enrolled 210 students in dual credit coursework. It lost its only school counselor to a district position in February and had not filled the position as of June 30.

| Allison Hill Academy — Outcomes | Result | Comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Four-year graduation rate | 84% | City average: 73% |
| Graduates with documented postsecondary plan | 61% | Network goal: 85% |
| Students enrolled in dual credit | 210 (20.8% of enrollment) | Prior year: 176 |
| Dual credit courses passed with C or better | 87% of enrollments | Prior year: 84% |
| Counselor positions filled, Feb–Jun | 0 of 1 | — |
| Student-to-counselor ratio, Feb–Jun | 1,010:1 | ASCA recommendation: 250:1 |

The graduation rate of 84 percent exceeds the city average by eleven points and is a defensible result. Dual credit participation at 210 students — better than one in five enrolled students — is genuinely strong for a school with this demographic profile, and the 87 percent pass rate indicates the participation is not nominal. Students are taking college coursework and completing it.

The 61 percent postsecondary plan documentation rate is the problem, and the board should understand exactly what it does and does not mean. It does not mean that 39 percent of Allison Hill graduates have no plans. It means the school has no record of what those graduates are doing. The distinction matters internally and does not matter at all to an authorizer, which will read an undocumented graduate as an unknown outcome and will be right to do so.

The counselor vacancy is the direct cause. A single counselor serving 1,010 students is already a ratio four times the professional standard; the American School Counselor Association recommends 250 students per counselor. From February 1 through the end of the year — the precise window in which seniors finalize college applications, complete financial aid filings, confirm enrollment deposits, and make decisions about workforce entry or military service — Allison Hill had no counselor at all. Postsecondary plan documentation is a counselor function. It did not happen because there was no one to do it.

The counselor left for a district position. The salary differential was approximately $11,000 on a base of $58,000. This is the same market dynamic that produced the Cameron Street vacancies, applied to a different job category, and it is discussed further in Section 4.

Actions for 2026–27: the counselor position has been reposted at a revised salary band of $66,000–$71,000, funded by reallocating a vacant network-office position; a second counselor position has been added to the Allison Hill budget for 2026–27, bringing the ratio to approximately 505:1; and the postsecondary plan documentation process has been moved from a counselor-only workflow to a shared workflow involving senior advisory teachers, so that a single vacancy cannot again produce a documentation gap of this size. A retrospective outreach effort to the Class of 2026 is underway and has raised documented plans from 61 percent to 68 percent as of July 25; the administration expects to reach approximately 75 percent before the October hearing but does not expect to close the gap entirely.

### 2.5 Steelton Prep

Steelton Prep, opened in 2023 under principal Yesenia Duarte, enrolled 690 students against a 970-student model. The shortfall drives a $2.3 million operating deficit at a campus whose building costs $1.4 million a year to lease. Section 5 treats this campus in full. What follows is the summary that belongs alongside the other three campuses.

| Steelton Prep — Enrollment Trajectory | Students | Model Assumption | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 (2023–24) | 418 | 520 | −102 |
| Year 2 (2024–25) | 612 | 745 | −133 |
| Year 3 (2025–26) | 690 | 970 | −280 |
| Year 4 (2026–27, projected) | 745 | 970 | −225 |

Steelton Prep is growing. It added 78 students this year and 194 the year before. The problem is that the financial model assumed it would add 225 this year, and the building was leased against that assumption. Growth is real and the gap is widening, because the model's ramp is steeper than the campus's actual recruitment.

Academic results at Steelton Prep are early and mixed. As a school in its third year serving grades six through twelve, it has not yet graduated a class; its first cohort of seniors enters in 2027–28. Attendance stands at 89 percent, below Riverfront but above Cameron Street. Principal Duarte's leadership has been steady through a difficult financial period, and staff retention at Steelton Prep — 78 percent, against a network figure of 69 percent — is the best in the network. That is a meaningful achievement at a campus where the budget conversation has been visible to staff all year.

### 3.2 Cash Position

The network holds 38 days of cash. This is the figure the board should hold in mind above all others in this section.

Thirty-eight days means that if all revenue stopped, the network could meet obligations for thirty-eight days. Revenue will not stop. But the figure is a standard measure of financial cushion, and most authorizers, lenders, and rating frameworks treat 60 days as a floor for a stable charter operator and 90 days as healthy. Susquehanna Rising is below the floor. It was above the floor a year ago at 61 days.

The 23-day decline has two sources, in roughly equal measure. The first is the Steelton Prep deficit, which consumed $2.3 million of cash that would otherwise have accumulated. The second is the timing of Pennsylvania's charter payment reconciliation, which shifted approximately $1.1 million of expected receipts from June into the following fiscal year. The second source is a timing issue and will reverse. The first will not reverse on its own.

Ms. Steinmetz's projection, reviewed with the audit committee in June, is that days of cash will trough at approximately 31 days in late October 2026 — coinciding with the renewal hearing — and recover to 44 days by June 2027 assuming the Steelton Prep deficit narrows as projected in Section 5. The trough is a function of the annual payment cycle rather than a deterioration in operations, but it is an unfortunate coincidence of timing, and trustees should expect to be asked about it in October.

### 3.3 Federal Relief Wind-Down

The network eliminated 14 positions funded by expiring federal pandemic relief money. This was a planned action, executed on the schedule the board approved in 2024, and it was accomplished without layoffs — all 14 reductions were achieved through attrition and non-replacement.

| Position Category | Positions Eliminated | Annualized Cost Avoided |
|---|---|---|
| Instructional aides | 6 | $312,000 |
| Interventionists (reading and math) | 4 | $268,000 |
| Social-emotional learning coordinators | 2 | $154,000 |
| Family engagement liaisons | 2 | $118,000 |
| **Total** | **14** | **$852,000** |

The absence of layoffs is a legitimate management accomplishment and should be stated as such. The absence of consequences is not the same thing, and this report should not claim it. Six instructional aides and four interventionists left the network's service delivery this year. Those roles supported the students furthest behind. Riverfront's literacy results were achieved with a reading interventionist still in place; two of the four eliminated interventionist positions were at Cameron Street. It is not possible to isolate the effect of that reduction from the effect of six teaching vacancies at the same campus, and this report does not attempt to. The board should understand the relief wind-down as one of several simultaneous pressures at the network's weakest campus.

### 3.4 Debt and Facilities

The network's debt service coverage ratio stands at 1.08, down from 1.31. The covenant floor in the network's 2021 facilities financing is 1.10. The network is in technical breach of that covenant as of June 30, 2026.

Ms. Steinmetz has been in contact with the lender since April. The lender has indicated in writing that it will grant a one-year covenant waiver conditioned on (a) delivery of an audited FY26 financial statement by November 30, (b) a written Steelton Prep enrollment and financial recovery plan approved by this board, and (c) a return to 1.10 coverage or better in FY27. The waiver letter is expected in August and will be presented to the board alongside this report. The recovery plan referenced in condition (b) is under development and is scheduled for board review in September, before the October hearing.

The board should treat the covenant breach as a serious but manageable item. It is manageable because the lender has signaled cooperation and because the breach is narrow — two hundredths of a ratio point. It is serious because a covenant breach must be disclosed in the renewal packet, and because the authorizer will read it as confirmation of financial strain rather than as a technicality.

| Cameron Street Middle | $495,000 | $773 | Leased; 2019 term |

At the modeled 970 students, Steelton Prep's facility cost per student would be $1,443 — high, but within a defensible range for a purpose-fitted secondary building. The building is not too expensive. It is too empty.

## 4. Staffing and Turnover

### 4.1 The Network Figure

Teacher turnover across the network was 31 percent. Of approximately 245 teaching positions, roughly 76 teachers did not return for 2026–27.

Thirty-one percent is high. The national charter sector average runs in the low twenties; the Pennsylvania public school average is under fifteen percent. It is not, however, an outlier for urban charter networks in markets with aggressive district hiring, and the board should be ready to place it in that context without treating the context as an excuse.

### 4.2 Turnover by Campus

| Campus | Teaching Positions | Departures | Turnover Rate | Prior Year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Riverfront Elementary | 62 | 15 | 24% | 21% |
| Cameron Street Middle | 44 | 19 | 43% | 27% |
| Allison Hill Academy | 78 | 27 | 35% | 29% |
| Steelton Prep | 61 | 15 | 25% | 31% |
| **Network** | **245** | **76** | **31%** | **27%** |

The network figure again conceals its components. Cameron Street lost 43 percent of its teachers — nineteen of forty-four. Steelton Prep, the newest and most financially strained campus, improved from 31 percent to 25 percent under Principal Duarte. These are opposite trajectories inside the same average.

### 4.3 Destination of Departing Staff

Exit interviews were completed with 61 of 76 departing teachers, an 80 percent response rate.

| Destination | Teachers | Share of Responses |
|---|---|---|
| Harrisburg School District or neighboring district | 29 | 48% |
| Another charter operator | 8 | 13% |
| Left teaching entirely | 11 | 18% |
| Relocated out of region | 7 | 11% |
| Retirement | 4 | 7% |
| Other / not disclosed | 2 | 3% |

Nearly half of departing teachers went to a traditional district. The cited reasons, in order of frequency, were compensation (mentioned by 41 of 61), pension and benefit structure (28), workload and hours (24), and class size or support staffing (19).

The compensation gap is real and quantified. A teacher at year five of the Susquehanna Rising scale earns $56,400. The comparable Harrisburg School District step is $63,800 — a gap of $7,400, or 13 percent. The gap widens at later steps. The network cannot close a 13 percent compensation gap at 38 days of cash while carrying a $2.3 million deficit at one campus. This is the central constraint on the network's staffing position, and the board should understand that no retention initiative available within the current budget will fully offset it.

### 4.4 What Is Being Done

The measures below are what the budget supports. They are targeted rather than comprehensive, and they are concentrated where turnover is worst.

- **Cameron Street retention payment.** $4,000 per instructional staff member completing the full 2026–27 year, funded from reserve. Cost: approximately $176,000.
- **Scale compression at years three through seven.** The 2026–27 scale raises steps three through seven by an average of 4.1 percent, targeting the range where the network loses the most teachers. Cost: approximately $410,000.
- **Early hiring.** All known 2026–27 vacancies posted by March 15 rather than the historical May–June pattern. This is a process change with no direct cost and has already produced a fill rate of 71 percent as of July 25, against 44 percent at the same date last year.
- **Counselor and specialist repricing.** The Allison Hill counselor position reposted at $66,000–$71,000, above the prior $58,000 base, funded by network office reallocation.
- **Exit interview protocol.** Now mandatory and conducted by a party outside the departing teacher's chain of command, which raised the response rate from 52 percent to 80 percent and produced the destination data above.

The board should not expect these measures to bring turnover below the mid-twenties. A realistic target for 2026–27 is 26 to 28 percent network-wide, with Cameron Street improving to the low thirties. That would be progress and would still be above the sector norm.

## 5. The Steelton Prep Shortfall

### 5.1 The Structure of the Problem

Steelton Prep enrolled 690 students against a model that assumed 970. The 280-student gap produces a $2.3 million operating deficit.

The arithmetic is straightforward. Per-pupil revenue in Harrisburg for 2025–26 averaged approximately $13,100 across regular and special education students. A gap of 280 students is therefore roughly $3.67 million in revenue the model expected and the campus did not receive. Against that, the campus reduced staffing below model — operating with 61 teachers rather than the modeled 78 — recovering approximately $1.37 million in avoided salary and benefit cost. The net is a $2.3 million deficit.

| Steelton Prep Financial Bridge | Amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue at model enrollment (970 students) | $12,707,000 |
| Revenue at actual enrollment (690 students) | $9,039,000 |
| Revenue shortfall | $(3,668,000) |
| Staffing reductions below model (17 positions) | $1,368,000 |
| **Net operating deficit** | **$(2,300,000)** |

What that bridge shows is that the campus has already absorbed most of the variable cost it can absorb. The remaining deficit is concentrated in costs that do not scale with enrollment: the $1.4 million lease, administrative staffing, utilities, transportation contracts with fixed minimums, and the instructional positions required to offer a full secondary course catalog regardless of how many students take each course. A 6–12 school must offer chemistry, precalculus, and a world language sequence whether it enrolls 690 students or 970. Those courses run with smaller sections at 690.

### 5.2 Why the Model Was Wrong

The board authorized Steelton Prep in 2022 on a model projecting 520 students in year one, 745 in year two, and 970 in year three. Actual enrollment was 418, 612, and 690. The model was wrong in three identifiable ways.

**The ramp was too steep.** The model assumed the campus would open with grades six, seven, and nine simultaneously and add roughly 225 students annually. In practice, secondary schools in this market grow primarily through sixth and ninth grade entry points; lateral transfers into grades seven, eight, ten, and eleven are far less common than the model assumed, because families are reluctant to move a student mid-sequence. The campus is filling its entry grades adequately and its middle grades poorly.

**The catchment was overstated.** The model drew on district enrollment data for the Steelton and south Harrisburg area without adjusting for two competing charter schools that opened or expanded in the same corridor during the 2022–2024 period. Roughly 340 seats of competing capacity entered the market after the model was built.

**Transportation was underestimated as a barrier.** Approximately 30 percent of surveyed families in the target catchment cited transportation as a factor in not enrolling. Steelton Prep sits at the edge of the district's transportation zone, and students from the neighborhoods with the largest unmet demand face rides exceeding 45 minutes.

These are the administration's findings, and they should be stated to the authorizer directly. A network that cannot explain why its own projection missed by 280 students will not be believed when it presents a revised projection.

### 5.3 The Lease

The building is leased for $1.4 million annually on a twelve-year term signed in 2023, with nine years remaining. The lease was sized to the 970-student model. There is no early termination clause and no rent abatement tied to enrollment.

The administration has explored three options.

**Sublease of unused space.** The building has approximately 22,000 square feet of unoccupied instructional and support space. Preliminary conversations with two potential subtenants — a workforce training provider and an early childhood operator — suggest a market rent of $9 to $12 per square foot, or $198,000 to $264,000 annually. The landlord's consent is required and has been informally indicated as likely. This is the most promising option and would offset 14 to 19 percent of the lease cost.

**Renegotiation with the landlord.** The landlord has declined to reduce rent but has offered to defer $200,000 annually for three years, repayable in years seven through nine at interest. This improves cash but increases total cost and is not recommended except as a contingency.

**Relocation.** No suitable alternative facility has been identified in the catchment area at a materially lower cost. Relocation would also require re-recruiting the campus's families and would almost certainly cost more enrollment than it saves in rent. Not recommended.

### 5.4 Recovery Plan Outline

The full recovery plan comes to the board in September. Its outline is as follows.

| Element | Target | Financial Effect (FY27) |
|---|---|---|
| Sublease 22,000 sq ft | Executed by January 2027 | +$120,000 (partial year) |
| Enrollment growth to 745 | September 2026 count | +$720,000 |
| Grade 6 and 9 recruitment intensification | 40 additional entry-grade students | Included above |
| Transportation pilot (two routes) | Launch September 2026 | −$180,000 |
| Course catalog consolidation | Reduce low-enrollment sections | +$210,000 |
| Administrative reduction (1.0 FTE) | July 2026 | +$96,000 |
| **Projected FY27 deficit** | | **$(1,334,000)** |

The plan does not eliminate the deficit. It reduces it from $2.3 million to approximately $1.3 million in FY27, with a path to approximately $600,000 in FY28 and breakeven in FY29 at roughly 870 students. That trajectory assumes the campus never reaches 970 students, and the administration recommends the board formally revise the Steelton Prep model to a stabilized target of 870 rather than continue to plan against a number the campus is unlikely to achieve.

The board should be clear-eyed about what this means. Steelton Prep will require cross-subsidy from the other three campuses for at least three more years, totaling roughly $2.2 million in cumulative operating support beyond FY26. That subsidy comes out of the same pool that would otherwise fund compensation increases at Cameron Street and Allison Hill. This is the network's central resource allocation tension, and it is not resolvable through better management. It is a consequence of a decision made in 2022 on a projection that did not hold.

## 6. Renewal Readiness: What Trustees Should Understand

### 6.1 The Shape of the Renewal Question

The authorizer will evaluate the network against three broad standards: academic performance, organizational viability including governance, and financial health. Susquehanna Rising's position differs sharply across the three.

| Standard | Network Position | Primary Evidence | Primary Vulnerability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Academic | Mixed, trending positive at two campuses | Riverfront third-grade reading (41% v. 29%); Allison Hill graduation (84% v. 73%); dual credit (210 students) | Cameron Street decline; Allison Hill postsecondary documentation at 61% |
| Organizational | Sound | Stable board; clean audits; no compliance findings; enrollment lottery in order | 31% teacher turnover; six-month counselor vacancy; six-month teaching vacancies at Cameron Street |
| Financial | Strained | Balanced network budget within $400K; planned relief wind-down without layoffs | 38 days cash; covenant breach at 1.08; $2.3M Steelton deficit |

### 6.2 The Six Questions Trustees Should Be Prepared to Answer

Trustees will be asked to speak at the October hearing. The following are the questions the administration expects, with the substance of the recommended answer. Trustees should answer in their own words; the point is that trustees should not be hearing these facts for the first time in the hearing room.

**1. Why did Cameron Street lose 80 students?** Because six of 44 teaching positions went unfilled from January through June, families noticed, and they left. The network did not fill those positions because it could not compete with district salaries in a mid-year market. It has responded with early posting, a retention payment, and scale compression at the steps where it loses the most teachers. Four of the six positions are already filled for 2026–27.

**2. Is 38 days of cash sustainable?** It is below the network's own policy floor of 45 days and below the sector norm of 60. It reflects the Steelton Prep deficit and a $1.1 million payment timing shift that will reverse. Projected recovery is to 44 days by June 2027. The board has adopted no dividend, no reserve draw beyond the approved retention payment, and no new facility commitments until cash returns above 60 days.

**3. What happened at Steelton Prep?** The 2022 enrollment model was wrong in three specific ways: too steep a ramp, an overstated catchment that did not account for 340 competing seats, and an underestimated transportation barrier. The campus is growing but will not reach 970. The board is revising the target to 870 and has a recovery plan reducing the deficit from $2.3 million to $1.3 million in FY27.

**4. Why doesn't Allison Hill know what 39 percent of its graduates are doing?** Because its only counselor left in February for a district job paying $11,000 more, and postsecondary plan documentation is a counselor function performed in exactly those months. The position has been reposted at a higher band, a second counselor has been added for 2026–27, and the documentation process has been redistributed so a single vacancy cannot cause this again. Retrospective outreach has raised the figure from 61 to 68 percent.

**5. Is 31 percent turnover acceptable?** No. It is driven by a 13 percent compensation gap against the district that the network cannot close at current cash levels. It varies widely by campus — 43 percent at Cameron Street, 24 percent at Riverfront, 25 percent at Steelton Prep, which improved. The network has committed approximately $586,000 to targeted retention and scale measures and expects 26 to 28 percent in 2026–27.

**6. Why should this charter be renewed?** Because Riverfront's third graders read proficiently at 1.4 times the city rate; because Allison Hill graduates students eleven points above the city and puts one in five into college coursework they pass; because the network absorbed a $852,000 federal funding cliff without laying off a single employee; and because the network's problems are identified, quantified, and under active management rather than discovered by the authorizer. The board should be prepared to add that the network does not ask to be renewed on the strength of Cameron Street or Steelton Prep this year, and does not claim those campuses are where they should be.

### 6.4 Closing Note

The honest summary of this year is that Susquehanna Rising is a network with two campuses performing well, one campus in visible decline, one campus financially underwater, and a cash position that leaves little room for error. That is not a comfortable position to bring to a renewal hearing. It is, in the judgment of the executive team and the board chair, a position that can be defended — provided the board defends it accurately.

The network's strongest asset in October will not be its results, which are mixed. It will be the demonstrated capacity to see its own situation clearly and act on it. This report is intended as evidence of that capacity. It should be read that way by the trustees who adopt it, and it will be read that way by the authorizer that receives it.
