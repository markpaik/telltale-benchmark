# Reduced Hospital Use Among Medically Tailored Meal Recipients: A Twelve-Month Utilization Analysis

**Riverbend Community Table, Evaluation Unit**

**Reviewed by:** Harriet Lindqvist-Moore, Executive Director
**July 2026**

## The Question and What We Found

Riverbend Community Table asked the question the foundation posed directly: did the medically tailored meal program, now sixteen months into a three-year, $3.8 million grant, reduce hospital admissions among the adults with congestive heart failure or type 2 diabetes it was designed to serve? We answer that question using linked hospital-encounter data for 1,412 of the program's 1,903 enrolled participants, compared against 2,640 pantry clients with matching diagnoses who never enrolled.

The topline answer is yes, and the effect is not small: participants averaged 0.71 inpatient admissions in the twelve months following enrollment, against 0.94 among comparison-group members with the same qualifying diagnoses — a reduction of roughly one admission for every four people served. But the topline number understates what the data actually show, because the effect is not evenly distributed. It is concentrated almost entirely among participants who received a full or near-full course of deliveries, is close to absent among people over 70, and is roughly twice as large among people who live alone as among people in larger households. Two structural problems in the data — a partner health system that captures only four of the county's six hospitals, and an eleven-week delivery gap in two zip codes caused by a refrigerated van failure — complicate both the size of the estimate and our confidence in the mechanism behind it.

We think the honest summary for the foundation is this: the program appears to be doing something real for the subset of participants it reaches consistently and who live without in-home support, and appears to be doing much less for older enrollees, but the overall averaged effect is more fragile than the headline number suggests, and the evaluation cannot yet rule out that some of what looks like program benefit is really a story about who manages to keep receiving deliveries. The sections below lay out the enrollment picture, how the comparison group was assembled, the results in full, what the data gaps do to the findings, and what we think this means for a renewal decision.

## Background

Riverbend Community Table operates a twelve-site pantry network across Cincinnati and, since March 2024, a medically tailored meal program serving adults with a diagnosis of congestive heart failure or type 2 diabetes. The program delivers up to 26 weeks of home-delivered, condition-specific meals to enrolled participants, funded by a three-year, $3.8 million grant from a regional health conversion foundation. As the grant approaches its midpoint, the foundation's program officer, Renata Kowal, asked the organization to assess whether the program has measurably reduced hospital utilization among enrollees, ahead of a renewal decision expected in September 2026.

This brief was commissioned by Executive Director Harriet Lindqvist-Moore and carried out by Evaluation Manager Adaeze Okoro, with analytic support from graduate fellow Tomas Villalobos-Reyes. It is intended to answer Kowal's question as directly as the available data allow, and to be candid about where the data allow less than a direct answer.

## Data and Methods

### Enrollment and the Linkage Sample

As of this analysis, 1,903 adults have enrolled in the medically tailored meal program since its March 2024 launch. Utilization data for this brief come from a data-use agreement Riverbend Community Table holds with a single partner health system, which operates four hospitals in the county. Under that agreement, we can observe inpatient admissions for any enrolled participant who consented to the linkage and who receives care within that system.

Of the 1,903 enrolled participants, 1,412 — about 74 percent — are represented in the linked utilization data. The remaining 491 are not included, for two reasons that we cannot fully disentangle from each other: some declined the data-linkage consent outright, and others simply receive their care outside the partner system, at one of the county's two other hospitals or at facilities entirely outside Cincinnati. We do not have a clean breakdown of how these 491 split between "declined" and "goes elsewhere," because the intake form does not ask people who decline linkage why they are declining, and we have no visibility into where non-linked participants actually receive care, if they receive it at all.

This distinction matters more than it might first appear. If most of the 491 are people who get care outside the partner system, the analytic sample of 1,412 is a reasonably representative slice of the enrolled population, just missing a hospital-choice dimension we discuss below. If a meaningful share instead actively declined the linkage — out of privacy concern, distrust of data sharing, or some other disposition — then the 1,412 could differ from the full enrolled population in ways correlated with the outcome we care about. People with more complicated relationships to the health system, more chaotic health histories, or lower general trust in institutions might be simultaneously more likely to decline linkage and more likely to be frequent hospital users, in which case our analytic sample would understate true admissions among enrollees. We flag this as an open question rather than a solved one; the intake process should be revised to record a reason for declining linkage so that future evaluations can characterize this gap.

### Construction of the Comparison Group

The 2,640-person comparison group consists of pantry clients — drawn from the same twelve-site network — who carry a matching diagnosis of congestive heart failure or type 2 diabetes but who never enrolled in the meal program. Both groups were identified from the same referral lists used to recruit meal-program participants, which means the comparison group was, in principle, eligible for the program and simply did not take it up, rather than being a population that was never offered or never aware of it.

This is a reasonable starting point for comparison, but it is not a randomized comparison, and the difference matters. People who enroll in a 26-week home-delivery program and people who are referred but never enroll are likely to differ from each other in ways connected to health outcomes independent of the meals themselves. Enrollment requires being reachable by phone or mail, being willing to have a stranger deliver food to one's home on a recurring schedule, and being well enough, at intake, to complete an enrollment conversation. Non-enrollees may skew toward people who are harder to reach, more mobile (and thus less interested in home delivery), sicker or more institutionalized at the point of referral, or simply more resistant to a new relationship with a nonprofit. Any of these could independently affect hospital use in either direction. We do not have baseline health-status measures, baseline utilization in the year prior to referral, or demographic detail (age distribution, income, insurance status) for the comparison group at the level of granularity we have for enrollees, so we cannot statistically adjust for these differences or verify that the two groups look similar before the meal program's clock starts running. The comparison here is best described as a matched-diagnosis observational comparison, not a controlled one, and the topline gap between 0.71 and 0.94 admissions should be read with that qualification attached throughout.

### Outcome Measure and Follow-Up Window

The outcome is inpatient admissions, counted at the partner health system's four hospitals, over the twelve months following each individual's enrollment (for participants) or referral (for comparison-group members, as the closest available anchor date). We did not have access to emergency-department visits, observation stays, or admissions at the two non-partner hospitals for either group, a limitation discussed at length below.

### Subgroup and Dose Analyses

Beyond the overall comparison, we examined three ways the effect might vary:

- **Dose**, measured as the number of weeks of meal deliveries actually received out of the 26-week course, dividing participants into those who completed the full course, an intermediate group, and those who discontinued before week 10;
- **Age**, comparing participants over 70 to younger enrollees;
- **Household size**, comparing participants living alone to those in households of three or more.

These are descriptive subgroup comparisons rather than a formal statistical model with covariate adjustment; we did not have the person-level control variables (income, insurance type, baseline utilization, disease severity or staging) needed to run a proper adjusted regression, and we did not have standard errors or confidence intervals calculated for any of the differences reported below. We treat these as suggestive patterns worth the foundation's attention, not as statistically confirmed effects, and we recommend a follow-up analysis with a proper adjusted model before the findings are used for anything beyond the current renewal conversation.

## Results

### Overall Effect

Among the 1,412 linked participants, average inpatient admissions in the twelve months after enrollment were 0.71. Among the 2,640 comparison-group members, the average was 0.94. That is a difference of 0.23 admissions per person, or a reduction of about 24 percent relative to the comparison group's rate. If this difference reflects the program's effect and not some other explanation, it implies the meal program is preventing roughly one admission for every four to five people enrolled over the course of a year — a magnitude consistent with what medically tailored meal programs elsewhere in the country have reported, and large enough to matter both clinically and financially for a health-system-facing funder.

That said, the overall figure is an average across a population whose experience of the program varied enormously, and the subgroup results below make clear that the "average participant" implied by 0.71 admissions does not actually describe most participants well.

### Results by Dose

The clearest and most consequential pattern in the data is the dose-response relationship between weeks of delivery received and hospital use.

| Group | Weeks of Delivery Received | Average Admissions (12 months) |
|---|---|---|
| Full-course completers | 26 weeks | 0.52 |
| Partial/intermediate | 10–25 weeks | (between full and early-stop groups; not separately reported) |
| Early discontinuers | Fewer than 10 weeks (38% of enrollees) | 0.98 |
| Comparison group | N/A (no deliveries) | 0.94 |

Participants who completed all 26 weeks of deliveries averaged 0.52 admissions — a reduction of nearly 45 percent relative to the comparison group. Participants who discontinued before week 10, who make up 38 percent of all enrollees, averaged 0.98 admissions, statistically indistinguishable from the 0.94 rate among people who never enrolled at all.

In plain terms: more than a third of the people enrolled in the program received less than ten weeks of a 26-week intervention, and that group shows no detectable benefit over not enrolling in the first place. The entire measured benefit of the program is being generated by the roughly two-thirds of participants who stayed engaged past the early weeks, and disproportionately by those who completed the full course.

This raises an immediate and important interpretive question that we cannot fully resolve with the data in hand: does staying enrolled cause lower hospitalization, or does lower likelihood of hospitalization (i.e., being more stable, more mobile, less acutely ill) cause people to stay enrolled? Both directions are plausible. A person who is hospitalized in week 6 of the program will likely show up in claims data as an "early discontinuer" not because they lost interest in the meals but because a hospital stay disrupts a home-delivery routine, may involve a discharge to a skilled nursing facility where the program does not deliver, or may simply mean the person did not answer the door for a few weeks running while recovering. If hospitalization causes discontinuation as often as discontinuation causes (or fails to prevent) hospitalization, then the 0.98-versus-0.52 gap partly reflects reverse causation rather than program impact, and the "dose" variable is contaminated by the very outcome we are trying to explain.

We also cannot rule out a more conventional "healthy adherer" pattern, well documented in medication-adherence research: people who follow through on a recommended course of any health intervention — whether or not the intervention itself does anything — tend to differ from people who do not follow through, in ways connected to health behavior generally (transportation reliability, cognitive function, social support, health literacy, motivation). If early discontinuers are simply higher-risk, less-resourced people to begin with, the dose gradient would appear even in a program with zero true effect.

We flag one further complication specific to this data set: the eleven-week van-related delivery interruption in two zip codes in early 2025, discussed in full below, means that some fraction of the "early discontinuer" group stopped receiving meals not because of their own health trajectory or motivation but because Riverbend's delivery infrastructure failed them. This is a second source of contamination in the dose variable, working in the opposite direction from the reverse-causation concern — it would tend to make some genuinely engaged, health-stable participants look like early discontinuers through no fault or health event of their own, which should if anything narrow rather than exaggerate the observed dose gradient. That the gradient is nonetheless large suggests the underlying pattern is not solely an artifact of the van failure, but we cannot quantify how much of the gap is explained by supply disruption versus health-driven attrition versus true program effect with the data currently available.

### Results by Age

Participants over 70 showed almost no difference in hospital admissions compared to the matched comparison group. This is a striking and, we think, important finding, because older adults with congestive heart failure or diabetes are generally the highest-utilization population and the group for whom a nutrition intervention might most plausibly be expected to help.

Several explanations are consistent with this pattern, and we cannot distinguish among them with the data available. One possibility is a ceiling or competing-risk effect: older adults with these diagnoses often have multiple additional comorbidities, and hospitalization drivers unrelated to diet — falls, infections, medication complications, other organ system decline — may simply swamp any nutritional benefit the meals provide. A second possibility is that older participants are more likely to have their nutrition needs already met through other channels (family caregiving, existing home health services, congregate meal programs, or transitions to assisted living), such that the marginal contribution of Riverbend's meals is smaller for this group even if the meals themselves are just as clinically appropriate. A third, more concerning possibility is delivery-completion differences: if older participants are disproportionately represented among early discontinuers — because of mobility barriers to answering deliveries, cognitive impairment affecting engagement, or higher rates of hospitalization or facility placement during the study window — then the null age effect could simply be another expression of the dose-response pattern described above, rather than a distinct age-related finding. We do not currently have a cross-tabulation of age against weeks of delivery received, and we recommend that the next round of analysis produce one, because it would help clarify whether "age" and "dose" are really capturing two different phenomena or the same one.

Whatever the mechanism, the practical implication is the same: as currently designed and delivered, the program's measured benefit is not reaching its oldest enrollees, even though this group is likely to be a significant share of the CHF-diagnosed population the grant is meant to serve.

### Results by Household Size

The estimated effect among people living alone was roughly twice the size of the estimated effect among people in households of three or more. This is one of the more intuitively plausible findings in the brief: a person living alone with heart failure or diabetes has no one else in the home to notice a missed meal, prepare an alternative, prompt medication adherence around mealtimes, or notice early warning signs of decompensation. A home-delivered, condition-tailored meal may substitute directly for support that a larger household would otherwise provide informally. For someone in a household of three or more, the same meal delivery may be one of several sources of appropriate nutrition already available, making its marginal contribution smaller.

This pattern is also, however, consistent with a confound we cannot rule out: household size correlates with social isolation more broadly, and social isolation is itself an independent risk factor for hospital readmission, documented extensively in the health-services literature independent of nutrition. It is possible that people living alone who enroll and stay engaged in a recurring-contact program like this one are getting a benefit from the routine social contact of a weekly delivery — someone checking in, noticing decline, prompting a call to a provider — rather than, or in addition to, a benefit from the nutritional content of the meals themselves. If so, the finding is still genuinely good news for the program's value, but the active ingredient may be different from what the grant was designed around, with implications for how the program should be described, staffed, and potentially expanded (for example, whether the check-in/social-contact function could be strengthened deliberately for isolated participants, independent of meal content).

We again note that this comparison, like the age comparison, has not been cross-checked against dose. If people living alone are also more likely to complete the full 26-week course — plausible, since they may have fewer competing obligations or more consistent daily routines than people managing a larger household — then some of the household-size effect could again be a restatement of the dose-response finding rather than an independent driver.

## Limitations

We want to be direct with the foundation about the two structural data problems that most affect how much weight this brief's numbers can bear, and about several smaller limitations that compound them.

### The Missing Hospitals

The partner health system operates four of the county's six hospitals. Any admission at the other two hospitals is invisible to this analysis, for both the participant group and the comparison group. This is a genuine and unresolved gap, and it could cut in either direction.

If patients route to the two non-partner hospitals at similar rates regardless of program enrollment, the missing data simply understates absolute admission counts for both groups roughly proportionally, and the relative gap between 0.71 and 0.94 — the number that actually matters for assessing program effect — would be largely preserved. This is the most optimistic reading, and it may well be correct if hospital choice in the county is driven mainly by insurance network, geography, and long-standing patient relationships that have nothing to do with meal-program enrollment.

But we cannot confirm that assumption, and there is a specific reason to worry it might be wrong. Enrollment in the meal program required a consent process tied to the data-use agreement with the partner system, and it is plausible that participants who already have an existing relationship with the partner system's clinics or providers were both more likely to be referred into the program in the first place and more likely to have their subsequent hospitalizations occur within that same system, simply because that is where their physicians admit patients. If comparison-group members are, on average, less embedded in the partner system's outpatient network — perhaps because the referral lists draw more broadly, or because non-enrollees are, almost by definition, people with a weaker existing relationship to organized care — then comparison-group admissions could be systematically undercounted relative to participant admissions, which would make the program look more effective than it is. We do not have the data to test this directly (we would need, at minimum, outpatient utilization or primary-care attribution data for both groups), but it is the single limitation we think most warrants caution before treating 0.71-versus-0.94 as a clean estimate of program effect.

A related and more mechanical problem: the 26 percent of enrolled participants who are not in the linked sample at all (491 of 1,903) are excluded from every number in this brief. We do not know their hospitalization experience. If people who declined linkage or who receive care outside the partner system differ systematically from the 1,412 who are included — and we have reason to think they might, given that declining linkage is itself a nonrandom choice — then the entire analysis describes three-quarters of the enrolled population, not all of it, and we cannot characterize the missing quarter's experience at all.

### The Delivery Interruption

Deliveries stopped for eleven weeks in early 2025 in two zip codes when a refrigerated van failed. This has at least three consequences for the findings in this brief.

First, as discussed above, it contaminates the dose variable: some participants in those two zip codes who would otherwise have completed the full 26-week course were involuntarily interrupted, and depending on how the discontinuation is recorded and how close they were to the ten-week threshold at the time of the failure, some may have been classified among the "stopped before week 10" group for reasons entirely unrelated to their own engagement, health status, or the program's clinical value to them.

Second, it introduces a geographic confound. The two affected zip codes may differ systematically from the rest of the service area — in housing density, distance from partner-system hospitals, baseline socioeconomic conditions, or the demographic composition of who lives there — any of which could independently affect hospitalization rates. If those two zip codes happen to have higher- or lower-than-average baseline utilization for reasons unconnected to the meal program, the eleven-week gap both reduces program dose there and mixes an unrelated geographic effect into the dose-response comparison.

Third, and more constructively: this interruption is also an opportunity. Because it was an operational failure rather than a participant choice, it created something close to a natural experiment — a group of participants whose exposure to deliveries was reduced for reasons plainly unrelated to their own health trajectory or motivation. A dedicated analysis comparing hospitalization before and after the interruption, specifically within those two zip codes, and comparing that pattern to zip codes that experienced no interruption, could help disentangle the reverse-causation problem described in the dose-response section above. We recommend this as a priority for the next phase of evaluation work, because it is one of the few pieces of this dataset that could speak to causation rather than mere association, and it would cost little to construct since the affected zip codes and dates are already known.

### Other Limitations Worth Naming

The comparison group was not randomly assigned, and we lack baseline data (prior-year utilization, disease severity, insurance status, income) that would let us adjust for the ways enrollees and non-enrollees likely differ before the program's effects could plausibly begin. We do not have confidence intervals or significance tests for any of the differences reported here, so a foundation reviewer should not treat, for instance, the household-size finding as established at a conventional level of statistical confidence; it is a pattern in descriptive data, not a confirmed effect. We have only twelve months of follow-up, which cannot speak to whether benefits persist, fade, or grow beyond the first year, a particularly relevant question given that the full effect appears concentrated among people who received a full 26-week course that itself lasts only half the follow-up window. Finally, we have not yet examined emergency-department utilization, medication adherence, self-reported health status, or cost data, all of which would materially strengthen a renewal case if they pointed the same direction as the admissions data, and all of which are, in principle, addable to future rounds of this evaluation.

## Implications for the Foundation's Decision

Kowal's question was whether the meals reduced hospital use. Our answer is a qualified yes: the descriptive data are consistent with a real reduction in inpatient admissions, plausible in size, and directionally supported by a sensible dose-response pattern — people who received more of the intervention show a larger benefit, which is the pattern one would want to see if the program were working as intended. That pattern, together with the household-size finding, gives us more confidence in a real effect than a single topline comparison would justify on its own.

At the same time, the foundation is being asked to renew a $3.8 million, three-year investment based on evidence that has three significant gaps: a comparison group that was not randomly assigned and cannot be adjusted for baseline differences; a hospital-utilization measure that may not capture two of six county hospitals and may undercount the comparison group disproportionately; and a program-delivery record disrupted by an operational failure that complicates the very dose-response finding that is doing most of the work in making the overall result credible. None of these gaps is fatal to the case for renewal, but together they mean the 24 percent topline reduction should be presented to the foundation's board, if it is presented at all, with the caveats above attached, not as a clean causal estimate.

We think the more defensible and more useful message for the September decision is not "the program reduced admissions by 24 percent" but rather: the program shows a real and clinically meaningful signal concentrated among participants who complete the full course and among people living alone, shows little to no detectable benefit for participants over 70 and for the 38 percent of enrollees who discontinue in the first ten weeks, and needs two structural fixes — broader hospital data coverage and resolved delivery reliability — before the size of the effect can be stated with real confidence. If the foundation renews, we would recommend that a portion of the next grant period fund the data and operational improvements needed to answer this question properly: a data-sharing arrangement covering the two non-partner hospitals, or at minimum a sample audit of comparison-group and participant care-seeking patterns outside the partner system; a formal analysis of the natural experiment created by the van failure; a retention-focused operational push aimed specifically at the 38 percent early-discontinuation rate, since that group is currently receiving grant-funded resources without a demonstrated benefit; and a targeted look at what is and is not working for participants over 70, given that this population is both clinically central to the program's mission and currently showing no measured benefit. We would also recommend collecting baseline utilization and basic demographic data on the comparison group going forward, so that the next report can adjust for pre-existing differences rather than relying on a raw, unadjusted comparison.

In short: there is a real story here, and it is a reasonably encouraging one for a program in its second year, but it is not yet a fully resolved one, and we think the foundation's renewal decision should treat it accordingly — as strong enough to justify continued investment, but not yet strong enough to treat as settled evidence of the program's clinical value across the full population it enrolls.

## Appendix A: Cohort Construction in Detail

For reviewers who want to trace exactly how the two analytic groups were assembled, we lay out the construction here in more granular form than the main text allows.

**Participant cohort.** The starting population is every adult enrolled in the medically tailored meal program between the program's March 2024 launch and the twelve-month mark preceding this analysis, a total of 1,903 individuals. Enrollment requires a qualifying diagnosis of congestive heart failure or type 2 diabetes, documented either through the referral source or through intake screening, and a completed intake process that includes a data-use consent conversation. Of the 1,903, 1,412 consented to the linkage and were successfully matched to the partner health system's records using a deterministic match on name, date of birth, and, where available, medical record number. We did not attempt probabilistic matching for records that failed the deterministic match, which means some fraction of the 491 excluded participants may in fact be present in the partner system's data under a name variant, address change, or transcription discrepancy that our matching process did not catch. We regard this as a modest additional source of undercount layered on top of the linkage-consent gap discussed in the main text, though we have no way to estimate its size without a more resource-intensive fuzzy-matching pass.

**Comparison cohort.** The 2,640-person comparison group was constructed by pulling the full universe of individuals on the same referral lists used to generate meal-program enrollment who (a) carry a matching CHF or type 2 diabetes diagnosis, and (b) never appear in the meal program's enrollment records. Because referral lists were generated on a rolling basis across the same window as enrollment, comparison-group members do not share a single common start date; each was assigned an index date equal to the date of their original referral, and the twelve-month outcome window runs from that date. This detail matters because it means "twelve months of follow-up" is measured from different calendar starting points across the comparison group, and if referral volume or hospital-system behavior shifted over the program's first sixteen months — for instance, if the two non-partner hospitals expanded capacity, or if regional COVID-era care-seeking patterns continued to normalize during this window — comparison-group members referred earlier versus later could have systematically different admission experiences for reasons having nothing to do with the meal program. We did not test for this kind of secular drift in the comparison group, and we recommend it as a straightforward addition to the next phase of work, since the referral dates are already recorded.

**Diagnosis matching.** Both cohorts were required to carry a CHF or type 2 diabetes diagnosis code, but we did not stratify results by which diagnosis a participant carried, nor did we examine participants who carry both diagnoses separately from those who carry only one. Given that CHF and diabetes have different typical admission drivers and different expected responses to a nutrition intervention, this is a meaningful simplification. A participant enrolled for diabetes management alone may show a different response profile than one enrolled primarily for heart failure, and pooling them into a single 0.71 average could mask two quite different underlying stories. We recommend that any future iteration of this analysis report results separately by qualifying diagnosis, and, where a participant carries both, treat that as its own subgroup rather than folding it into either single-diagnosis category.

## Appendix B: Proposed Phase 2 Analysis Plan

Should the foundation renew funding in September, or even independent of that decision, we propose the following sequence of work over the next two quarters, roughly ordered by the ratio of analytic value to additional data-collection burden.

**1. Cross-tabulate dose against age and household size (low burden, high value).** All three of the subgroup variables discussed in this brief already exist in the data Riverbend currently holds. Producing a simple cross-tab — dose by age band, dose by household size — would tell us within weeks whether the age and household-size findings are independent phenomena or restatements of the dose-response pattern. This should be the first task undertaken, before any new data collection begins, because it directly affects how the rest of the findings should be interpreted.

**2. Construct a pre/post analysis around the van failure (low burden, moderate-to-high value).** The two affected zip codes and the eleven-week interruption window are already documented in delivery logs. Comparing hospitalization rates for participants in those zip codes in the weeks immediately before, during, and after the interruption, against a matched set of unaffected zip codes over the same calendar period, would give the evaluation its first piece of evidence that speaks to the direction of causation rather than mere association. We would want at least three months of post-interruption data beyond what is captured in the current twelve-month window to see whether any admission increase during the gap reversed once deliveries resumed.

**3. Pursue expanded hospital data coverage (moderate burden, high value).** We recommend Riverbend's leadership approach the county's two non-partner hospital systems about a limited data-use agreement, even one restricted to aggregate admission counts by zip code and diagnosis category rather than a full individual-level linkage. Failing that, a manual chart-review sample — for instance, contacting a random sample of 100 participants and 100 comparison-group members directly to ask about any hospitalization in the study period, and comparing self-report to what appears in the partner-system data — would let us estimate how much undercount exists and whether it differs between the two groups.

**4. Record reasons for declining data-linkage consent (low burden, moderate value, long time horizon).** We recommend a one-line addition to the intake form asking participants who decline linkage to indicate, optionally, a reason (privacy concern, receives care elsewhere, prefers not to say, other). This will not help characterize the 491 participants already excluded from this analysis, but it will let every future cohort analysis characterize its excluded population rather than treating it as an unexplained gap.

**5. Add baseline utilization and severity measures to the comparison group (moderate burden, high value).** If Riverbend can obtain even one year of pre-referral utilization data for the comparison group — plausible if the partner health system can run a retrospective query using existing identifiers — the next iteration of this brief could report an adjusted estimate that accounts for baseline differences between enrollees and non-enrollees, which would substantially strengthen the causal interpretation of any overall gap.

**6. Extend the follow-up window (ongoing, high value, long time horizon).** As additional months of program operation accumulate, we recommend re-running this analysis with an 18- and then 24-month follow-up window for the earliest-enrolled cohorts, to see whether the benefit associated with full-course completion persists, fades, or changes shape once the meal deliveries themselves have ended for six months, twelve months, and longer.

We believe items 1 and 2 can be completed within six weeks using data Riverbend already holds, well ahead of any subsequent foundation reporting cycle, and we recommend prioritizing them regardless of the September funding outcome.

## Appendix C: Glossary of Terms Used in This Brief

**Linked participant** — An enrolled meal-program participant who consented to data sharing under the data-use agreement and was successfully matched to records at the partner health system's four hospitals. There are 1,412 linked participants out of 1,903 total enrollees.

**Comparison group** — Pantry clients with a matching CHF or type 2 diabetes diagnosis, drawn from the same referral lists as meal-program enrollees, who never enrolled in the meal program. N = 2,640.

**Dose** — The number of weeks, out of a possible 26, during which a participant actually received meal deliveries, used here as a proxy for program exposure.

**Early discontinuer** — A participant who stopped receiving deliveries before completing ten weeks of the 26-week course; 38 percent of enrollees fall into this category.

**Index date** — The date from which the twelve-month outcome window is measured: enrollment date for participants, referral date for comparison-group members.

**Partner health system** — The regional health system with which Riverbend holds a data-use agreement, operating four of the county's six hospitals; the sole source of the utilization data used in this brief.
