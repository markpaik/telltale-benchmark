# Executive Summary — Street Resurfacing Program

**Prepared for:** Mayor Andre Toussaint and the City Council Budget and Finance Committee, Councilmember Sylvia Rocha, Chair
**Prepared by:** Priscilla Vandermeer, City Auditor
**Audit period:** Fiscal Year 2025 (July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025)
**Hearing date:** October 21, 2025

The finding of this audit is favorable and, in the auditor's experience, unusually so: the decision to bring street resurfacing back in house in 2023, made under Public Works Director Guadalupe Renteria, has produced measurably more output at a materially lower unit cost while improving the condition of the roads the public actually drives on. On the strength of that record, the auditor recommends that the committee sustain the in-house resurfacing model in the coming budget, fund it at a level consistent with the throughput the program has demonstrated, and direct Public Works to formalize the practices that appear to be driving the result so that the performance does not depend on the tenure of any single manager. This is a recommendation to protect and institutionalize a success, not to correct a failure, and the committee should weigh it in that light against the three programs that follow in this set.

The evidence supporting that recommendation is straightforward and rests on figures that Public Works and the auditor's office reconciled against work orders, payroll distributions, and material invoices for the fiscal year. Against a plan of 90 lane-miles, the program completed 128 lane-miles. That is not a marginal overachievement; it is roughly 42 percent more surface than the plan contemplated, delivered within the same fiscal window and without a supplemental appropriation. When a program exceeds its own plan by that margin, the auditor's first obligation is to test whether the additional output was purchased by degrading quality, deferring other obligations, or drawing down a reserve that will have to be replenished later. The audit found no such offset. The additional lane-miles were resurfaced with the same crews, equipment, and material specifications applied to the planned miles, and the program closed the year within its appropriated operating budget.

The cost evidence is equally clear and, for a budget committee, the more consequential of the two. In-house resurfacing cost the city $61,000 per lane-mile during the audit period. The prior arrangement, under which the work was contracted out, cost $84,000 per lane-mile. The difference is $23,000 per lane-mile, a reduction of roughly 27 percent against the contracted baseline. Applied across the 128 lane-miles actually completed, the in-house unit cost implies a total program expenditure on the order of $7.8 million, whereas the same volume of work at the prior contract rate would have cost approximately $10.75 million. The spread of nearly $3 million on this single year's output is the plainest financial statement the auditor can make, and it holds even if one assumes the contract rate would have been negotiated somewhat lower on renewal. The auditor cautions the committee against reading the per-mile figures as a permanent entitlement — asphalt, fuel, and labor markets move, and a mild season can flatter a paving program's numbers — but the gap between $61,000 and $84,000 is wide enough that ordinary cost drift would not erase it.

The performance evidence extends beyond output and cost to the experience of residents, which is where a resurfacing program either earns or loses public confidence. Pavement complaints fell 44 percent over the period. The auditor treats complaint volume with appropriate caution, because complaint counts respond to reporting channels and public attention as much as to road conditions, and a drop in complaints can sometimes reflect resignation rather than repair. Here, however, the decline in complaints coincides with a documented 42 percent increase in lane-miles resurfaced, and the two figures corroborate one another: more miles restored, fewer miles generating grievances. When output and public sentiment move in the same direction and are supported by independent records, the auditor is prepared to credit both.

What management says in response supports the finding without overstating it. Director Renteria attributes the results to three operational choices: scheduling crews to keep equipment and personnel productive across the full paving season rather than in the compressed windows a contractor typically works; sequencing streets by condition and by proximity to hold down mobilization and setup time; and retaining institutional knowledge of the city's street network within the department rather than transferring it to and from a vendor each cycle. Management further states that the eliminated contractor margin and the reduced administrative burden of procuring and overseeing an outside contract account for a meaningful share of the per-mile savings. The auditor finds these explanations consistent with the cost structure observed in the records and does not dispute them.

The auditor's residual concern is not with the program's performance but with its durability. A result this good, concentrated in a single fiscal year and closely associated with one director's management, carries the risk that it is not yet a system the city owns but a performance the city is enjoying. The audit accordingly recommends that Public Works document its scheduling, sequencing, and cost-tracking methods in a form that would survive a change in leadership, and that the department establish a multi-year pavement-condition baseline so that future councils can judge whether the program is maintaining the network or merely addressing its worst segments first. The auditor also notes that a 42 percent overdelivery against plan, while welcome, indicates that the plan itself was set conservatively; the committee may wish to direct Public Works to build next year's target from the demonstrated throughput rather than from the prior plan, so that the budget request and the work program describe the same city.

Taken together, the record on street resurfacing is the clearest positive result in this year's audit cycle. The program produced more lane-miles than planned, at a unit cost well below the contracted alternative, with a measurable decline in resident complaints, and management's account of why is credible on the evidence. The auditor recommends that the committee fund the in-house model in the coming budget and direct the modest documentation and baselining steps described above, so that a good year becomes a reliable practice.

# Executive Summary — Downtown Outreach Program

**Prepared for:** Mayor Andre Toussaint and the City Council Budget and Finance Committee, Councilmember Sylvia Rocha, Chair
**Prepared by:** Priscilla Vandermeer, City Auditor
**Audit period:** Fiscal Year 2025 (July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025)
**Hearing date:** October 21, 2025

The finding of this audit is that the downtown outreach program is failing against the outcome it was funded to achieve and, separately, is out of compliance with the reporting terms of its own contract. On that basis the auditor recommends that the committee withhold renewal of the $2.8 million vendor contract in its current form, condition any further payment on the immediate delivery of the overdue outcome reports, and either restructure the engagement around enforceable, outcome-based terms or redirect the appropriation to a delivery model the city can hold accountable. The auditor does not recommend that the city abandon downtown outreach as a public objective; the recommendation is that it stop paying for activity it cannot verify and outcomes it is not receiving.

The central evidence is the gap between contact and result. The program logged 1,900 street contacts during the audit period, and by the volume of that number one might conclude the vendor is present and working. But the program was not funded to make contact; it was funded to move people off the street and into housing, against a target of 300 placements for the period. It placed 47. That is roughly 16 percent of the target — fewer than one placement for every forty contacts logged — and it is the figure on which the auditor rests the finding of failure. Contacts are an input; they measure the vendor's activity and cost the city money whether or not anything follows from them. Placements are the outcome the contract exists to purchase. A program that generates inputs in the thousands while delivering outcomes in the dozens is not, on this evidence, converting the city's money into the result the city bought, and the committee should not be reassured by the 1,900 figure. If anything, the volume of contacts set against the scarcity of placements sharpens the concern, because it establishes that the shortfall is not a failure to find people in need — the vendor found them 1,900 times — but a failure to help them into stable housing once found.

Compounding the outcome shortfall, and in the auditor's judgment aggravating it, is a straightforward contract-compliance failure: the vendor has not filed the quarterly outcome reports its contract requires since December 2024. As of the close of the audit period and the date of this report, that is three consecutive quarters of missing reports on a live $2.8 million contract. The significance of this failure runs deeper than paperwork. The quarterly outcome reports are the instrument through which the city is supposed to know what it is getting; without them, the city has been paying against a contract while blind to the contract's performance, and the 47-placement figure had to be assembled by the auditor's office rather than furnished by the vendor as required. A vendor that is both missing its outcomes and withholding the reports that would document those outcomes has removed the city's ordinary means of oversight, and the coincidence of the two failures should weigh heavily in the committee's deliberations. When a contractor stops reporting at roughly the same time its results collapse, the auditor cannot treat the reporting lapse as a clerical matter.

The cost evidence frames the stakes. The contract is valued at $2.8 million. Measured against the 47 placements actually achieved, the implied cost is approximately $59,000 per person housed — a figure the auditor offers not as a precise unit price, since outreach costs are not incurred per placement, but to convey to the committee the scale of the mismatch between what the city is spending and what it is receiving. Even the most conservative reading of the arrangement leaves the city having committed $2.8 million against 16 percent of the outcome that sum was meant to secure, with no vendor reporting for three quarters to explain the shortfall or evidence any mitigating value delivered in its place. The auditor notes that the city cannot even fully test the vendor's own account of the 1,900 contacts, because the reporting that would substantiate contact quality, follow-up, and disposition is precisely what has not been filed.

What management says in response is, at this stage, incomplete, and that incompleteness is itself part of the finding. Program management acknowledges that placements have fallen far short of target and characterizes the population as harder to house than the target assumed, citing housing-market scarcity and the prevalence of behavioral-health and substance-use conditions among those contacted. Management states that it is in discussions with the vendor about the missing reports and about a corrective plan. The auditor takes the point that street outreach among an unsheltered population is genuinely difficult work and that a 300-placement target may have been set without adequate grounding in the local housing supply. But two things must be said in reply. First, difficulty explains a shortfall against target; it does not explain a shortfall of this magnitude, and it does not explain the total absence of the placements that other jurisdictions achieve under comparably hard conditions. Second, and more pointedly, difficulty does not explain the missing reports. If the population is harder to serve than anticipated, the quarterly outcome reports are exactly the vehicle in which the vendor was obligated to say so, quarter by quarter, so that the city could adjust the target, the method, or the contract. The vendor instead went silent. Management's explanation, in other words, addresses the easier of the two failures and leaves the harder one — the breakdown of accountability itself — unanswered.

The auditor's recommendation follows directly. The committee should not renew this contract on its existing terms, under which the city pays for activity and receives neither outcomes nor reporting. Any continued payment should be conditioned on the vendor's immediate production of the overdue quarterly reports covering the period since December 2024, so that the city can reconstruct what its money bought. Going forward, if the city continues to pursue downtown outreach through a vendor, the engagement should be rebuilt around outcome-based terms — with a meaningful share of payment tied to verified placements and housing retention rather than to contact counts — and with reporting obligations backed by withholding provisions that take effect automatically when reports are late, so that a lapse like the present one cannot recur without financial consequence. Alternatively, the committee may direct staff to evaluate whether the outreach function is better delivered through a different provider or brought within a city or county program that the city can supervise directly. In any of these paths, the objective is the same: the city should stop spending $2.8 million a year on a program whose outcomes it cannot see and whose vendor it cannot presently hold to account.

# Executive Summary — Curbside Composting Pilot

**Prepared for:** Mayor Andre Toussaint and the City Council Budget and Finance Committee, Councilmember Sylvia Rocha, Chair
**Prepared by:** Priscilla Vandermeer, City Auditor
**Audit period:** Fiscal Year 2025 (July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025)
**Hearing date:** October 21, 2025

The finding of this audit is mixed, and the auditor states it as such rather than forcing the program into a verdict its evidence will not support: the curbside composting pilot is diverting real tonnage and holding its participants, but a persistent contamination problem is undermining the economics and the credibility of the diversion it achieves. The auditor therefore recommends that the committee neither expand the pilot to citywide service nor terminate it in the coming budget, but instead extend it for a defined further period conditioned on a contamination-reduction plan with numeric targets, and defer any scaling decision until the program demonstrates that it can bring contamination down to a level at which the diverted material is reliably accepted. This is the one program in the cycle for which the responsible recommendation is to continue and prove, rather than to sustain or to stop.

The evidence that argues for keeping the program is genuine and should not be discounted. The pilot reached 3,400 households and diverted 610 tons of organic material from disposal during the audit period. That is a working collection system, not a paper proposal: households are participating, material is being set out, and trucks are moving it. Participation held at 22 percent, which the auditor regards as a meaningful data point precisely because it held. New voluntary programs frequently show an enthusiastic sign-up followed by attrition as the novelty fades and the discipline of separating organics wears on households. A participation rate that stays at 22 percent across the pilot period, rather than eroding, indicates that the households who joined have largely stayed, and that the behavior the program depends on is sustainable for those who adopt it. For a program whose central risk is that residents will not keep it up, stable participation is a real and favorable signal.

The evidence that argues against expansion, and that prevents the auditor from returning a clean positive finding, is the contamination rate. Eighteen percent of the collected material was contaminated — that is, mixed with non-compostable material that does not belong in an organics stream — and that contamination has twice during the period been serious enough to trigger load rejection fees, meaning the processor refused loads and charged the city for the refusal. This is the fact that reorganizes the entire assessment. Contamination at 18 percent is not a cosmetic quality issue; it is an operational and financial one. It degrades the value of the diverted material, because contaminated organics are worth less to a processor and may be downgraded or refused outright. It exposes the city to rejection fees that convert the program's environmental benefit into a fiscal liability at unpredictable intervals. And it hollows out the diversion claim itself, because tonnage that is rejected at the processor is not in fact diverted from disposal — it is collected, hauled, refused, and ultimately landfilled anyway, at additional cost and with the added mileage of a round trip. The 610-ton diversion figure, in other words, is only as good as the share of it that the processor accepts, and an 18 percent contamination rate places a standing question mark over that figure.

The interaction between the two sets of evidence is what defines the finding. A program can tolerate a modest contamination rate; every organics program contends with some. But 18 percent is high enough, and has already produced load rejections often enough, that the auditor cannot recommend committing the city to citywide service on the current performance. Scaling a contamination problem does not dilute it — it multiplies it, across more households, more loads, and more rejection fees, and it does so in a service the whole city would then depend on. At the same time, the stable participation and the substantial gross tonnage indicate that the underlying concept is sound and the residents are willing, which is why the auditor also declines to recommend termination. The problem the pilot faces is a solvable one — contamination is driven by how clearly residents are told what belongs in the cart, how consistently that is reinforced, and whether the program monitors and corrects setouts — and the pilot exists precisely to work such problems out before the city commits at scale. To end it now would discard the participation the program has built; to expand it now would export a defect the program has not fixed. Extension with conditions is the path the evidence supports.

The cost evidence available to the auditor centers on the rejection fees, which are the clearest financial consequence of the contamination and the metric the committee can most readily track. The two rejection events during the period are documented, and they establish that contamination is already reaching the city's ledger, not merely its diversion statistics. The auditor notes for the committee's planning that the pilot's per-ton cost of diversion cannot be fairly judged until the contamination rate falls, because every rejected load raises the effective cost of every accepted ton; a program evaluated on its net accepted tonnage will look materially different from one evaluated on gross collection. The committee should insist that any renewal be accompanied by cost reporting stated in net accepted terms.

What management says in response is constructive and, in the auditor's assessment, correctly focused. Program management does not dispute the contamination rate and identifies it as the pilot's principal problem. Management attributes the contamination primarily to residents' uncertainty about which materials are accepted, points to the difficulty of sorting compostable from lookalike non-compostable packaging, and states that it is preparing targeted education, clearer labeling, and setout monitoring intended to bring the rate down. The auditor credits management for naming the right problem and not defending the 18 percent figure. But the audit also observes that education and monitoring are means, not results, and that the committee should fund them against a measurable end. The auditor accordingly recommends that any extension require management to commit to a specific contamination target — a defined percentage, by a defined date — and to report the contamination rate and any rejection events on a recurring basis, so that the eventual scaling decision rests on demonstrated improvement rather than on the promise of it. If the program brings contamination to an acceptable level within the extension period, it will have earned the case for expansion on evidence; if it cannot, the city will have learned that at pilot scale and pilot cost, which is exactly what a pilot is for.

# Executive Summary — Permitting System Replacement

**Prepared for:** Mayor Andre Toussaint and the City Council Budget and Finance Committee, Councilmember Sylvia Rocha, Chair
**Prepared by:** Priscilla Vandermeer, City Auditor
**Audit period:** Fiscal Year 2025 (July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025)
**Hearing date:** October 21, 2025

The finding of this audit is that the permitting system replacement, sponsored by Chief Information Officer Kwame Boateng, has failed on both dimensions by which such a project must be judged: it has overrun its approved budget substantially, and the service it was meant to improve has instead grown worse. On that combined basis the auditor recommends that the committee halt further spending on the current effort pending an independent project review, appoint that review to determine whether the initiative can be recovered or should be restructured, and refuse any additional appropriation until the review reports and until the project is placed under governance capable of controlling its cost and delivering its outcome. The auditor states plainly that this is the most serious finding in the cycle, because a permitting system touches every resident and business that builds in Marston Bluff, and the project has managed to spend far more than approved while making that service slower.

The cost evidence establishes the first half of the failure and does so without ambiguity. The project was approved at $4.6 million. It has spent $7.9 million. That is an overrun of $3.3 million, or roughly 72 percent above the approved figure — not a variance to be reconciled at year-end but a doubling-toward of the original commitment. The overrun is bound up with a vendor failure: the city engaged a first vendor, terminated that vendor in August 2024, and has since proceeded with a second, so that the $7.9 million represents payment across two vendors for a single system the city still does not have in the improved state it was promised. Termination of a primary implementation vendor partway through a project is among the most expensive events that can befall a technology initiative, because work is frequently duplicated, transition consumes time and money, and the second vendor must absorb or rebuild what the first left behind. The audit found this pattern here: a material portion of the $3.3 million overrun is attributable to the cost of changing vendors mid-course, which means the city has paid twice for progress it should have bought once. For a budget committee, the essential fact is that $7.9 million has left the treasury against a $4.6 million authorization, and the authorization was never amended to contemplate the spending that actually occurred.

The performance evidence establishes the second and, in the auditor's view, more damning half of the failure. The purpose of replacing a permitting system is to make permitting faster and more reliable; the justification for spending millions of dollars is the service improvement residents and builders are supposed to receive. Here the metric has moved decisively in the wrong direction. Residential permit turnaround has worsened from 21 days to 34 days — an increase of 13 days, or roughly 62 percent longer, over the course of a project intended to shorten it. This is the figure the committee should hold in mind above all others, because it means the city has spent $7.9 million and, so far, purchased a slower service than it started with. Every one of those additional 13 days is borne by a resident waiting to build, a contractor carrying financing while a permit sits, or a small business unable to open. A cost overrun alone might be defended if the delivered system were transforming the service; a service decline alone might be excused if it were the temporary disruption of a cutover about to pay off. What the auditor cannot reconcile, and what the committee should not accept, is the combination: more money spent and worse service delivered, at the same time, on the same project.

The interaction of the two failures is the heart of the finding. A troubled project usually presents one problem — it is late, or it is over budget, or it has disappointed on scope — and the presence of any single problem invites a management plan to address it. This project presents the full set at once. It is over budget by $3.3 million; it has already burned through and discarded one vendor; and the service outcome it exists to improve has degraded by 62 percent. When cost control and service delivery fail together, the auditor's concern moves past the project's particulars to the governance around it, because two simultaneous failures of this magnitude indicate that the mechanisms meant to catch either one — budget oversight, vendor management, and outcome monitoring — were not functioning as they should. The audit's recommendation for an independent review flows from exactly this concern. The city needs to know not only whether the current system can be salvaged but why the spending was allowed to reach $7.9 million against a $4.6 million authorization without the matter being brought back to the council for reauthorization, and why turnaround was allowed to lengthen to 34 days without triggering a halt.

What management says in response acknowledges the difficulties while framing them as the transitional cost of an eventual improvement. CIO Boateng attributes the overrun principally to the failure and termination of the first vendor and to the additional work required to recover from it, and characterizes the current turnaround degradation as a temporary condition arising from operating in a partially converted environment, expected to reverse once the second vendor completes implementation and the new system is fully in service. The auditor takes this account seriously and does not dismiss the possibility that turnaround will improve once the system stabilizes. But the auditor must also test that account against what the project has demonstrated rather than what it forecasts, and on that test the response is not yet sufficient. The first vendor's failure explains part of the overrun but does not explain how the project's governance permitted spending to reach 172 percent of authorization without a return to the council; the promise that turnaround will improve after full implementation is a forecast from a project whose forecasts have so far not held, given that it is over budget, has changed vendors, and has produced the opposite of the intended service result. Management is asking the committee to fund the completion of a project on the strength of assurances that the project's own record does not support, and the auditor's obligation is to say so.

The recommendation is therefore deliberately restrictive. The committee should not approve additional appropriation for this initiative in the coming budget on the current showing. It should commission an independent review — technical and financial, conducted by parties without a stake in the current vendor relationship — to establish the true remaining cost to complete, the realistic timeline, the recoverability of the second-vendor path, and the specific governance failures that allowed the overrun and the service decline to occur unchecked. Pending that review, further spending should be limited to what is necessary to preserve the city's position, and any resumption of full funding should be conditioned on new project governance with hard reauthorization thresholds, mandatory reporting of turnaround times and expenditures to this committee, and a defined point at which the project will be stopped if the service metric does not recover. The auditor's aim is not to abandon the city's need for a modern permitting system, which is real, but to ensure that the next dollars are spent under controls the last $7.9 million plainly lacked, and that the committee does not authorize the completion of a project until it understands why the project reached this condition.
