# Chronic Absenteeism Interventions Across Grade Bands: A Review of the Evidence for Network-Level Decision-Making

**Tres Rios Preparatory Schools — Office of Research and Accountability**
**Thandiwe Mokoena, Ph.D., Director of Research and Accountability**
**Ernesto Palafox-Ruiz, M.S., Data Specialist**
**August 2026**

---

## 1. Introduction

Chronic absenteeism at Tres Rios Preparatory Schools reached 34 percent in the 2024–25 school year, up from 19 percent in 2018–19. The increase mirrors national and state trends, but it has now become an operational and existential problem for the network: our authorizer has flagged attendance as a focus area in the charter renewal review due in April 2027, and the board has allocated $1.8 million in state funds to address it. Three proposals are before the board. The first is a mailed informational nudge campaign at approximately $40 per family. The second is a school-based mentoring model at approximately $1,600 per student served. The third funds dedicated attendance teams with structured home visits at approximately $210,000 per campus. These options differ by two orders of magnitude in per-student cost, and they rest on different theories of why students miss school. The board cannot fund all three at scale, and it should not fund any of them without understanding what the evidence actually supports.

This review addresses a deceptively simple question: **What interventions reduce chronic absenteeism, for which students, under which conditions, and at what cost?** Answering it requires more than cataloguing effect sizes. The absenteeism literature is unusually sensitive to context. Effects that replicated reliably before 2020 have attenuated or vanished in post-pandemic samples. Effects concentrated in elementary grades have failed to appear in high schools — a concern raised pointedly by our own high school leadership, and one the literature largely validates. Effects estimated in samples of moderately absent students may not generalize to the severely absent students who now dominate our distribution. A network serving 6,400 students, 78 percent of whom qualify as low-income, in a post-pandemic Phoenix labor and housing market, cannot assume that a 2015 effect size from a Philadelphia mailing experiment describes what will happen here in 2026.

The review therefore does three things. First, it synthesizes the causal evidence on the three intervention families before the board — informational nudges, mentoring, and intensive attendance-team/home-visit models — organized by intervention type and grade band, setting studies against one another rather than summarizing them serially. Second, it explains why the literature disagrees with itself: shifting baselines, threshold effects, grade-band moderation, selection into treatment, and the difference between informational and structural causes of absence. Third, it identifies the populations and conditions the evidence does not cover — most importantly, students in unstable housing and severely chronically absent adolescents — and translates the synthesis into implications for the board's allocation decision.

A note on terminology. Following the dominant convention, "chronic absence" refers to missing 10 percent or more of enrolled school days for any reason, excused or unexcused. Some studies report effects on days absent (a continuous measure) and others on the probability of crossing the chronic-absence threshold (a binary measure); the two are not interchangeable, and the distinction turns out to matter for interpreting the disagreements in this literature, as discussed in Section 3.4.

## 2. Search Strategy and Study Base

We searched ERIC, PsycINFO, Web of Science, and the What Works Clearinghouse for studies published between January 2000 and June 2026, using combinations of terms for the outcome (chronic absenteeism, chronic absence, school attendance, truancy) and intervention families (nudge, informational intervention, parent messaging, mailing, mentoring, success mentors, check and connect, home visit, attendance team, early warning). We supplemented database searches with backward and forward citation chasing from six prior reviews and meta-analyses and with hand searches of working-paper series where attendance experiments frequently appear before journal publication.

The search returned 1,760 unique records after de-duplication. Title and abstract screening excluded 1,528, most for lacking an attendance outcome, lacking a comparison group, or addressing school refusal in clinical rather than school settings. We read 232 records in full and included 67 studies: 18 randomized controlled trials, 24 quasi-experimental studies (difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, matched comparison, and instrumental-variables designs), 13 implementation and mixed-methods studies, and 5 cost or cost-effectiveness analyses. Inclusion required a K–12 sample, a measured attendance outcome, and, for causal claims, a credible identification strategy. Implementation studies without comparison groups were retained to inform delivery conditions but are not treated as evidence of effectiveness. Effect sizes, baseline chronic-absence rates, sample grade bands, and cost data (where reported) were extracted into a standardized workbook, available from this office. Because outcome metrics vary, we report effects in the original study's metric — typically percent reduction in absences or percentage-point change in chronic-absence rates — rather than forcing a common standardized effect size, which prior meta-analytic work has shown to obscure more than it reveals in this literature (Eklund et al., 2022).

Two limitations of the base should be flagged at the outset. First, the causal literature is heavily weighted toward elementary and middle grades; only 11 of the 42 causal studies include high school samples, and only 4 report high-school-specific estimates. Second, only 9 causal studies use post-2020 data, a period in which baseline absence rates roughly doubled nationally. Both limitations bear directly on the board's decision and are treated at length below.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Informational Nudges: A Robust Early Literature Meets a Disappointing Replication Record

The nudge literature is the best-developed causal evidence in the field, and it tells a story in two acts.

The first act is genuinely impressive. Rogers and Feller (2018), in a randomized trial of over 28,000 Philadelphia households, found that mailings correcting parents' underestimates of their child's absences reduced total absences by roughly 10 percent and chronic absence by 15 percent, at a marginal cost of about $6 per family — implying a cost per additional day of attendance under $10, far below any competing intervention. Robinson, Lee, Dearing, and Rogers (2018) replicated the design with elementary families in a California district and found reductions of similar magnitude, with effects concentrated among students whose parents most underestimated absences. Lasky-Fink, Robinson, Chang, and Rogers (2021) refined the messaging in a ten-district trial and confirmed that simplified, absence-total-focused mailings outperformed both standard district letters and no contact. Smythe-Leistico and Page (2018) and Kalil, Mayer, and Gallegos (2021) extended the logic to text messaging with kindergarten and pre-K families, respectively, finding meaningful reductions at trivially low cost. Bergman and Chan (2021), while focused on parent alerts about missed assignments and classes rather than full-day absence, found that automated information to parents of middle and high schoolers reduced class absences by roughly 17 percent — one of the few nudge results with any secondary-grades traction. By 2019, a reasonable reader of this literature would have concluded that parental information gaps were a meaningful, cheaply correctable driver of absence.

The second act complicates the story considerably. Post-2019 replications and scale-ups report effects roughly half the size of the originals, or null. Guzman and Ortega (2022), replicating the Rogers and Feller design across four districts in 2021–22, found a 4 to 5 percent reduction in absences — statistically significant but a third of the original effect on chronic absence. Nguyen, Castleman, and Patterson (2023), in a 60,000-household multi-district trial, found overall effects statistically indistinguishable from zero, with a small positive effect confined to elementary students with prior-year absence rates between 10 and 20 percent. Whitfield and Duarte (2024) found no detectable effect of mailed nudges in a large urban district with a post-pandemic chronic absence rate of 41 percent, and Delgado and Marsh (2024) found null effects of text-based nudges in a majority-Latino Southwest district demographically similar to Tres Rios. A federal multi-site evaluation (Heppen et al., 2023) found small effects on days absent in elementary grades, no effect in middle grades, and no effect on the chronic-absence threshold at any level.

Three explanations for the attenuation appear in the literature, and they are not mutually exclusive.

**The threshold explanation.** The most analytically important finding comes from Nguyen et al. (2023), whose pre-registered heterogeneity analysis showed that nudge effects were detectable only for students in a middle band of baseline absence — roughly 10 to 25 percent of days missed — and were flat both below and above that band. The interpretation is intuitive: families of near-perfect attenders have nothing to correct, and families of severely absent students face barriers that information does not address. Corroborating evidence appears in reanalysis of the original trials: Robinson et al. (2018) themselves reported larger effects where parental underestimation was largest, and Guzman and Ortega (2022) found their attenuated average effect masked a preserved effect in the moderate-absence band. The implication for a post-pandemic district is uncomfortable. When chronic absence doubles, the distribution does not simply shift; it fattens in the right tail. A larger share of absent students now sits above the band where nudges work. The average treatment effect falls not because the mechanism broke, but because the population moved out from under it.

**The information-environment explanation.** Several authors argue that the informational premise itself has eroded. The original trials operated in an environment where parents genuinely did not know absence totals; a decade of parent portals, automated calls, and attendance-focused messaging may have closed the gap the mailings exploited. Whitfield and Duarte (2024) found that control-group parents in 2023 underestimated absences by far less than Rogers and Feller's 2015 control group had, mechanically shrinking the room for correction.

**The changed-causes explanation.** Finally, the composition of absence causes may have shifted. Qualitative and survey work after 2020 (Santibañez & Guarino, 2021; Childs & Lofton, 2021; Blanco & Yellowhorse, 2024) documents increased absence attributable to health anxiety, caregiving responsibilities, transportation instability, housing churn, and disengagement — causes that a mailing does not touch. Under this account, nudges still fix the informational slice of the problem, but that slice is now a smaller share of a larger pie.

For Tres Rios, the nudge evidence therefore supports a narrow, targeted conclusion rather than the broad one the $40-per-family proposal implies. Mailed to all 6,400 families (~$256,000, or roughly $150,000–$180,000 at the family level accounting for siblings), the campaign would spend most of its budget on families outside the responsive band. Targeted to elementary and middle-grade students with prior-year absence between 10 and 25 percent — approximately the population where every positive post-2020 estimate concentrates — it is plausibly the most cost-effective dollar the board can spend, but it cannot carry the network's chronic-absence rate on its own, and there is essentially no credible evidence it will move high school students.

### 3.2 Mentoring: Strong Elementary Evidence, Genuinely Contested Secondary Evidence

The mentoring literature is where Principal Blackgoat's skepticism meets its evidentiary test, and the honest answer is that she is partly right.

**Elementary and middle grades.** The evidence here is reasonably consistent and positive. Balfanz and Byrnes (2018), evaluating New York City's Success Mentors initiative with a matched-comparison design across more than 200 schools, found that chronically absent students paired with mentors gained roughly nine additional days of attendance per year relative to comparison students, with the largest gains for students who had a mentor for two consecutive years. Cook, Dodge, Gifford, and Schulting (2017), in a randomized trial of an early truancy prevention model in grades 1–2 that combined teacher-led mentoring with light-touch family outreach, found significant reductions in absences among students with elevated baseline absence. Ramirez and Osei (2021), randomizing a school-staff mentoring model ("caring adult check-ins") in 14 elementary schools, found a 3.4 percentage-point reduction in chronic absence, and Tso and Benallie (2023) found similar effects in a quasi-experimental study of a Southwestern district serving a demographically comparable population to ours, with effects strongest for students in grades 3–5 whose baseline absence was between 10 and 30 percent. A meta-analytic synthesis by Eklund, Burns, Oyen, and DeMarchena (2022) covering targeted attendance interventions found mentoring/monitoring approaches among the more reliable categories, with mean effects in the small-to-moderate range, driven overwhelmingly by elementary samples.

**High school.** The picture fractures. The most rigorous evidence comes from evaluations of Check & Connect, the most widely studied structured mentoring model for secondary students. Early quasi-experimental work (Sinclair, Christenson, & Thurlow, 2005) reported large effects on engagement and persistence for students with disabilities. But Guryan et al. (2021), in a large randomized trial of Check & Connect with general-population students in Chicago middle and high schools, found effects on absences that were positive but modest in the middle grades and statistically insignificant in high school, at a cost of roughly $1,700 per student per year — strikingly close to our proposal's $1,600 price point. Heppen et al. (2018), randomizing Check & Connect among ninth graders flagged by early-warning indicators, found no significant impact on attendance or course credits. Against these disappointing experimental results sit more encouraging quasi-experimental findings: Corrin et al. (2016), evaluating Diplomas Now (which embeds near-peer mentoring within a whole-school model), found reductions in the share of students with early-warning attendance flags in the middle grades, and Faria et al. (2017) found that early-warning systems with structured adult follow-up reduced chronic absence in a randomized trial of high schools — though the intervention there was arguably an attendance-team model as much as mentoring, a definitional blur discussed below.

Why do elementary and secondary results diverge so sharply? The literature offers three coherent mechanisms. First, *the locus of the attendance decision shifts.* In elementary grades, attendance is substantially a parent behavior; mentoring a young child works partly by activating the family, and the child's own motivation is rarely the binding constraint. By high school, the student is the decision-maker, and absence is entangled with disengagement, employment, caregiving, and peer effects that a weekly check-in reaches only weakly (Guryan et al., 2021, make this argument explicitly in interpreting their null high school result). Second, *dosage and relationship depth are harder to achieve in secondary structures.* Implementation studies (Anderson-Butcher & Paluta, 2020; Villanueva, 2022) consistently find that high school mentors carry larger caseloads, see students in fragmented schedules, and achieve fewer than half the intended contacts — and that effects, where they exist, are concentrated among students who received full dosage. Third, *baseline severity is higher.* High school chronically absent students miss more days than their elementary counterparts, and the same threshold logic that limits nudges applies: several mentoring studies that report heterogeneity (Balfanz & Byrnes, 2018; Tso & Benallie, 2023) find weak or null effects for students missing more than 30 percent of days, a group overrepresented in high schools.

The fairest reading is not that "mentoring doesn't work past sixth grade" but that *light-to-moderate mentoring, delivered as a standalone attendance intervention, has not demonstrated reliable effects in high school in rigorous trials*, while the same class of intervention has repeatedly demonstrated effects in elementary grades. The high school results that look promising (Corrin et al., 2016; Faria et al., 2017) come from models where mentoring is embedded in a broader structure of monitoring, case management, and family contact — which is to say, models that shade into the third proposal before the board.

Cost matters here too. At $1,600 per student, serving all ~2,180 currently chronically absent students would cost $3.5 million — nearly double the appropriation. Serving 800 students costs $1.28 million, consuming 71 percent of the budget on the intervention with the most grade-limited evidence. Levin and Belfield-style cost-effectiveness comparisons in this literature (Rogers & Feller, 2018; Guryan et al., 2021; Katz, Adams, & Johnson, 2015) consistently show mentoring delivering days of attendance at 50 to 150 times the per-day cost of nudges — defensible if mentoring reaches students nudges cannot, indefensible as a substitute for them.

### 3.3 Attendance Teams and Home Visits: Promising Theory, Thin and Selection-Prone Evidence

The third proposal — dedicated campus attendance teams conducting structured home visits at $210,000 per campus ($1.89 million for nine campuses, exceeding the appropriation) — targets exactly the students the first two interventions miss: severely absent students whose barriers are structural. Its evidentiary base, unfortunately, is the weakest of the three, though not empty.

The strongest experimental evidence for family-engagement home visiting comes from studies of the Parent Teacher Home Visits model. Sheldon and Jung (2018), in a matched-comparison, multi-district study, found that schools implementing home visits at scale had lower chronic-absence rates, and that individually visited students were significantly less likely to be chronically absent than matched non-visited peers. McKnight, Venkateswaran, and Laird (2022), using within-school comparisons across a large urban district, reached similar conclusions. But both designs share a fundamental vulnerability the authors acknowledge: families *agree* to home visits, and teachers *choose* whom to visit. Families who open the door to a teacher differ from families who do not in precisely the ways — stability, engagement, trust in the school — that predict attendance. Ilades and Crowfoot (2023), one of the few studies to attempt an instrumental-variables correction using visitor-capacity constraints as an instrument, found the naive effect shrank by roughly 60 percent under correction, though it remained positive. No randomized trial of home visiting with attendance as a primary outcome exists in the K–12 literature we identified; the closest is a small pilot randomization (n = 320) by Okafor and Steele (2024), which found a marginally significant reduction in absences among visited elementary families but was underpowered for secondary students.

Evidence on attendance teams — cross-functional groups reviewing early-warning data and executing tiered responses — is somewhat stronger but rarely isolates the team from its components. Faria et al. (2017), noted above, provides the best experimental anchor: high schools randomized to an early-warning-indicator system with structured staff response saw significant reductions in chronic absence, driven by students flagged early in the year. Balfanz and Byrnes (2019), in a multi-district quasi-experimental study of tiered attendance-team models, found reductions concentrated in schools with high implementation fidelity — weekly data review, assigned case ownership, and documented follow-up — and no effects in low-fidelity schools, a pattern echoed in implementation work by Childs and Grooms (2018) and Villanueva (2022). Truancy-court and legalistic enforcement variants, by contrast, show null-to-harmful effects in the causal literature (Mazerolle et al., 2019; Gottfried & Gee, 2017, review), and the field has moved decisively toward supportive rather than punitive framing.

Two post-2020 studies deserve particular attention because they address our actual problem: severe absence in a disrupted context. Blanco and Yellowhorse (2024), studying attendance-team-plus-home-visit models in six Arizona and New Mexico districts using a staggered-adoption difference-in-differences design, found chronic absence fell 4 to 6 percentage points more in adopting schools, with the largest effects among students missing 20 to 40 percent of days — the severe band other interventions cannot reach. Effects were similar across elementary and secondary campuses, one of the only grade-robust findings in this review, though the design cannot rule out that adopting districts differed on unobserved trends. Kearney, Dupéré, and Archambault (2023), synthesizing post-pandemic case-management evidence, similarly conclude that multi-tier, case-managed models are the only intervention family with any empirical traction on severe absenteeism, while emphasizing that the underlying studies are small and observational.

The honest summary: attendance teams with home visits have the *right theory* for the students driving our post-2020 increase, promising but selection-vulnerable quasi-experimental support, essentially no experimental support, and an effect that appears — uniquely in this literature — not to collapse in high schools. They are also the intervention whose effects depend most heavily on implementation fidelity, which is a governance risk as much as a research finding.

### 3.4 Why the Literature Disagrees: Baselines, Thresholds, Metrics, and Mechanisms

Stepping back, the disagreements across this literature are not noise; they are patterned, and the patterns are themselves the most decision-relevant findings.

**Baseline nonstationarity.** Every major pre-2020 effect size was estimated in populations with chronic-absence rates of 10 to 25 percent. Post-2020 populations — including ours, at 34 percent — sit outside the support of that data. Transporting a 2015 effect size to 2026 assumes the treatment-effect function is flat in baseline severity, and the threshold findings (Nguyen et al., 2023; Guzman & Ortega, 2022) demonstrate it is not. This is the single most important reason the board should discount headline effect sizes from the early literature.

**Threshold and band effects.** Across all three intervention families, effects concentrate in a middle band of baseline absence. Nudges work at 10–25 percent baseline absence; mentoring at roughly 10–30 percent; only intensive case-managed models show effects above 30 percent, and none show effects for the most extreme cases. Interventions are not substitutes competing for the same students; they are complements addressing different segments of the distribution.

**Metric divergence.** Studies measuring days absent (continuous) routinely find effects that studies measuring the chronic-absence threshold (binary) miss, because moving a student from 25 to 20 absences improves attendance without crossing the 18-day line (Heppen et al., 2023, illustrate this within a single trial). Our authorizer will judge us on the binary metric; the board should understand that some genuinely effective interventions will not move it quickly.

**Mechanism–cause mismatch.** Nudges fix information gaps; mentoring fixes relational disengagement; case management fixes structural barriers. Post-2020 absence is disproportionately structural (Santibañez & Guarino, 2021; Kearney et al., 2023). The attenuation of nudge effects and the resilience of case-management effects are two faces of the same compositional shift.

**Implementation fidelity.** In every intervention family, effects in high-fidelity implementations are two to three times those in low-fidelity ones, and averages pool across both (Balfanz & Byrnes, 2019; Villanueva, 2022; Anderson-Butcher & Paluta, 2020). The board is not buying an effect size; it is buying an implementation.

## 4. Gaps in the Evidence

Four gaps bear directly on our population and should temper confidence in any projection.

**Students in unstable housing.** Students experiencing homelessness or housing instability have chronic-absence rates two to three times district averages nationally, yet they are almost invisible in the causal literature. Mailed nudge trials mechanically exclude them (mailings require stable addresses — a point Whitfield and Duarte, 2024, note in explaining undeliverable-mail rates of 12 percent). Mentoring trials lose them to mobility-driven attrition, and attrition analyses rarely test whether it is differential. Home-visit studies cannot visit families without stable residences. Only two included studies (Pavlakis & Richards, 2023; Okafor & Steele, 2024, in a subgroup) address this population directly, both descriptively. In a network where 78 percent of students are low-income in a metro with severe rental instability, this is not a peripheral gap; a material share of our right tail likely sits in it, and *no* evaluated intervention has demonstrated effects for it. Partnerships with housing and McKinney-Vento services, though outside this review's scope, may matter more for these students than any of the three proposals.

**Severely chronically absent high school students.** The intersection of the two weakest evidence areas — secondary grades and >30 percent baseline absence — describes a substantial fraction of our high school problem and is covered by essentially one quasi-experimental study (Blanco & Yellowhorse, 2024).

**Post-2020 experimental evidence.** Only nine causal studies use post-2020 data, and only three are randomized. The field is extrapolating, and so, unavoidably, are we.

**Cost data.** Only five studies report costs rigorously, and cost structures (staff salaries, mailing, caseloads) vary enough across contexts that cost-effectiveness ratios transfer even less reliably than effect sizes. Our budget projections below use local cost estimates, not study-reported ones.

Additionally, the literature is thin on charter-network governance contexts, on Latino and Native student populations specifically (despite several relevant studies — Tso & Benallie, 2023; Delgado & Marsh, 2024 — subgroup reporting is rare), and on interactions between interventions delivered simultaneously, which is precisely what a portfolio strategy would do.

## 5. Implications for the Network's Decision

The evidence does not support choosing one proposal. It supports a *tiered portfolio* that matches each intervention to the segment of the absence distribution where it has demonstrated effects, funded within the $1.8 million appropriation:

**1. Targeted nudges, not universal ones (est. $60,000–$90,000).** Fund the mailing/text campaign only for elementary and middle-grade families of students with prior-year absence between 10 and 25 percent — the band where post-2020 evidence still shows effects — plus corrected-information messaging integrated with existing family communication channels. Expect modest effects (3–5 percent absence reduction in the treated band, per Guzman & Ortega, 2022; Nguyen et al., 2023), at very low cost. Do not expect this to move high school numbers, and do not present it to the authorizer as the primary strategy.

**2. Elementary and middle-grade mentoring, capped and targeted (est. $500,000–$650,000).** Fund the $1,600-per-student mentoring model for roughly 350–400 students in grades K–8 with baseline absence of 10 to 30 percent, using school-staff mentor models with protected caseloads (the fidelity conditions under which Balfanz & Byrnes, 2018, and Ramirez & Osei, 2021, found effects). Decline to fund standalone mentoring as the high school attendance strategy; the experimental record (Guryan et al., 2021; Heppen et al., 2018) does not support it, and Principal Blackgoat's skepticism is, on this specific point, evidence-aligned.

**3. Attendance teams with home-visit capacity, prioritized at secondary campuses and highest-need elementary campuses (est. $840,000–$1,050,000, four to five campuses).** The full nine-campus rollout exceeds the appropriation; the evidence base — thinner but uniquely relevant to severe and secondary absence (Faria et al., 2017; Blanco & Yellowhorse, 2024) — justifies a staged implementation at the campuses with the highest severe-absence concentrations, with explicit fidelity requirements (weekly data review, assigned case ownership, documented family contact, non-punitive framing) written into the funding. Home-visit protocols should include field-based outreach for families without stable addresses, in coordination with our McKinney-Vento liaison, precisely because this population is unreached by the other two tiers.

**4. Reserve $100,000–$150,000 for measurement and internal evaluation.** Given how poorly effects transfer across contexts, the network should treat year one as an evaluation: staggered rollout across campuses where feasible, tracking of both days-absent and threshold metrics, subgroup reporting for housing-unstable students, and fidelity monitoring. This is also the strongest posture for the April renewal review. The authorizer is unlikely to expect a 34 percent chronic-absence rate to fall to pre-pandemic levels in one year — no intervention in this literature has produced a 15-point single-year reduction — but a plan that is evidence-aligned, segmented, fidelity-monitored, and self-evaluating is defensible in a way that a single large bet on any one proposal is not.

Finally, the board should hold its expectations to what the evidence can support. A realistic composite projection — combining band-specific effects across the tiers, discounted for post-2020 attenuation and first-year implementation — is a 3 to 6 percentage-point reduction in network chronic absence in the first full year, concentrated in elementary grades, with high school movement dependent almost entirely on the attendance-team tier. That is meaningful, measurable progress. It is not a solution to a 34 percent chronic-absence rate, because the literature contains no such solution; it contains partial tools, each with a domain of demonstrated effect, and the network's task is to deploy them where they work and measure honestly where they do not.
