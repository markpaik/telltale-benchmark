## 1. Purpose and Approach of This Report

At the direction of the chief executive and the chief financial officer, this report presents the third quarter in full rather than by exception. Trustees expressed concern after the second-quarter report that problems reached the committee later than they should have. This report is written to correct that. Every program line is covered on its own terms — its results, its explanation, and its trajectory — whether the quarter went well or poorly. Financial figures are stated against budget at the three-quarter mark. Staffing, the state corrective action at the Petersburg site, and the outlook for the fourth quarter and fiscal 2027 are treated in dedicated sections.

The short version, stated plainly at the top so that nothing in this report comes as a surprise on page eight: this was a difficult quarter. Two of the five program lines missed performance targets by meaningful margins. One program line has overspent its budget with a quarter remaining. Medicaid revenue is running well behind projection. Agency turnover reached 29 percent annualized, which is both a cause of the program problems and a problem in its own right. One site remains under state corrective action. Against that, adult reentry held its placement rate despite losing its two most experienced case managers, early literacy met every target it carries, and the agency's overall financial position, while strained, is manageable through year-end if the committee endorses the mitigation steps described in Section 9.

The sections that follow take each program line in turn (Sections 2 through 6), then the consolidated financial position (Section 7), staffing (Section 8), the corrective action plan (Section 9 covers finances; Section 10 covers Petersburg), and the fourth-quarter and fiscal 2027 outlook (Section 11).

## 2. Youth Workforce Development

### 2.1 Results

Youth workforce is the agency's largest program by enrollment and had the weakest quarter of the five lines. The program served 1,340 participants year-to-date against a target of 1,800 — 74 percent of target with 75 percent of the year elapsed on the calendar, but the shortfall is not a timing artifact. Enrollment velocity slowed through the quarter, and the program will not close the gap by June 30. More concerning than the enrollment number is the placement rate: 47 percent of program completers were placed into employment, down from 58 percent in the same quarter last year. That is an eleven-point decline in the program's core outcome measure.

| Measure | Q3 FY2026 (YTD) | Target | Prior Year (Same Period) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Participants served | 1,340 | 1,800 | 1,710 |
| Percent of enrollment target | 74% | 100% | 96% |
| Job placement rate | 47% | 60% | 58% |
| Average wage at placement | $16.10/hr | $16.50/hr | $15.85/hr |
| 90-day retention (placed cohort) | 68% | 75% | 74% |

### 2.2 Explanation

Three factors explain the decline, and it is important that the committee understand them separately because they call for different responses.

**First, referral volume from the school divisions fell.** Norfolk and Portsmouth public schools both changed their alternative-pathways referral processes at the start of the calendar year, consolidating referrals through a single district coordinator rather than allowing school counselors to refer directly. Our intake staff report that referrals from those two divisions dropped roughly 30 percent between December and March. This is the largest single driver of the enrollment shortfall. Program leadership has met with both district coordinators; Norfolk has agreed to a streamlined referral memorandum effective in April, and we expect partial recovery in the fourth quarter, though not enough to reach the annual target.

**Second, staff turnover hit the job-development function directly.** The program lost three of its seven job developers during the quarter — the staff who cultivate employer relationships and match participants to openings. Job developer positions carry a starting salary of $41,000, and all three departing staff cited compensation in exit interviews; two left for similar roles at other organizations paying $6,000 to $9,000 more. Employer accounts went uncovered for weeks at a time, and several employers who had standing arrangements with departed staff stopped calling with openings. The placement rate decline tracks almost exactly with the timing of those departures.

**Third, the local entry-level labor market softened modestly.** Regional hospitality and logistics hiring, which absorb a large share of our placements, slowed over the winter. We estimate this accounts for perhaps three to four points of the placement decline, with the staffing disruption accounting for the balance. We flag this distinction because the market factor is outside our control and the staffing factor is not.

### 2.3 Trajectory

Program director Devon Halloran has filled one of the three job developer vacancies and has offers pending on the other two. The Norfolk referral memorandum should restore intake volume beginning in April. A realistic fourth-quarter outcome is enrollment finishing near 1,550 to 1,600 for the year — 86 to 89 percent of target — and a placement rate recovering to the low 50s for the fourth quarter, which would leave the annual figure near 49 to 50 percent. The committee should understand that the annual targets will be missed, and the relevant funder (the regional workforce board) has been notified informally; a formal variance explanation is due with the fourth-quarter grant report in July.

## 3. Adult Reentry Employment

### 3.1 Results

Adult reentry is the quarter's most instructive story: outcomes held, but the conditions underneath them deteriorated in ways the committee should not mistake for stability.

The program served 410 participants and placed 63 percent into employment, exactly on target and consistent with prior-year performance. On the outcome dashboard alone, this program had a clean quarter.

| Measure | Q3 FY2026 (YTD) | Target | Prior Year (Same Period) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Participants served | 410 | 400 | 395 |
| Job placement rate | 63% | 62% | 63% |
| Recidivism among completers (12-mo. tracked cohort) | 11% | Under 15% | 12% |
| Average caseload per case manager | 71 | 45 | 48 |

### 3.2 Explanation

In February the program lost both of its senior case managers — Alicia Fontaine after nine years and Marcus Webb after six. Both departures were compensation-driven; Ms. Fontaine moved to a state agency position paying approximately $14,000 more, and Mr. Webb left for a comparable nonprofit role in Richmond. These two staff carried the program's institutional relationships with probation and parole officers, the largest second-chance employers in the region, and the reentry councils in three cities. Their departures in the same month were, program director Jamal Okonjo reports, coincidental rather than coordinated, but the effect is the same.

The four remaining case managers absorbed the departed caseloads. Average caseload rose from 48 to 71 against a program design standard of 45. The 63 percent placement rate this quarter largely reflects participants whose employer matches and service plans were built before February. The staff carrying 71 cases each cannot sustain the relationship-intensive work — employer cultivation, probation coordination, crisis response — that produces that placement rate. Mr. Okonjo's candid assessment, which I share, is that fourth-quarter placement will fall unless caseloads come down, and that the greater risk is losing one of the four remaining case managers to burnout, which would push caseloads near 90 and force an intake freeze.

### 3.3 Trajectory

Two case manager positions are posted. The senior positions are hard to fill at the budgeted salary; both postings have been open eight weeks with a thin candidate pool. Human resources has recommended a $4,000 upward adjustment to the posted range, which the CFO has approved within existing program budget capacity created by the salary savings from the vacancies themselves. If hires are made by early June, caseloads return to the mid-50s by fiscal year-end — still above design standard but sustainable. The committee should read this program's on-target quarter as a lagging indicator, not a reassurance.

## 4. Emergency Housing

### 4.1 Results

Emergency housing is the quarter's most significant financial problem. The program has spent $4.9 million against a full-year budget of $4.2 million — that is, it has exceeded its annual budget by $700,000 with three months of the fiscal year remaining. Service demand did not fall to match; the program continues to serve every eligible household referred by the regional coordinated entry system, as its contracts require.

| Measure | Q3 FY2026 (YTD) | Budget / Target | Prior Year (Same Period) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total spending | $4,900,000 | $4,200,000 (full year) | $3,050,000 |
| Households served | 512 | 500 | 495 |
| Average length of stay | 66 nights | 45 nights | 41 nights |
| Average motel voucher cost per night | $118 | $88 budgeted | $86 |
| Exits to permanent housing | 44% | 55% | 53% |

### 4.2 Explanation

Two forces compounded each other, and the committee should understand that neither is a management failure in the ordinary sense — but the budget response to them was slower than it should have been, and that part is ours to own.

**Motel voucher costs rose 34 percent.** The program relies on motel vouchers for roughly 60 percent of its shelter capacity because the region lacks congregate shelter beds. Motel operators raised negotiated rates sharply beginning last summer, citing their own costs and stronger tourist demand, and two of our four contracted motels declined to renew at prior rates in October. The per-night cost moved from $86 to $118. The fiscal 2026 budget, built in spring 2025, assumed $88.

**Average length of stay rose from 41 nights to 66.** Households are staying longer because they cannot exit. Regional vacancy rates for units affordable at the incomes our households carry are below 2 percent, and the housing authority's voucher waitlist has been closed since November. Exits to permanent housing fell from 53 percent to 44 percent. Longer stays at higher nightly rates is a multiplicative problem: the cost per household served rose roughly 90 percent year over year.

Program director Consuelo Reyes-Marsh flagged the rate increases in the first quarter, and a budget amendment was discussed but deferred pending second-quarter data. In retrospect that deferral was a mistake; the overage was foreseeable by December. Management's process correction — quarterly reforecasting of the housing line rather than annual budgeting — is described in Section 9.

### 4.3 Trajectory

At current run rates, the program will spend approximately $6.3 to $6.5 million by June 30 against the $4.2 million budget, a full-year overage of $2.1 to $2.3 million absent intervention. Section 9 details the mitigation plan, which combines renegotiated block rates at two motels (signed in April, saving an estimated $14 per night on about half of voucher nights), an emergency allocation request to the city of Norfolk's homelessness services fund ($600,000 requested; decision expected in May), and a shift of eligible households to a shallower rapid-rehousing subsidy that costs roughly 40 percent of a motel stay. With these steps, the projected full-year overage narrows to approximately $1.3 to $1.5 million, absorbed as described in Section 7.

## 5. Behavioral Health Counseling

### 5.1 Results

Behavioral health's problem is on the revenue side. The program billed $2.1 million in Medicaid revenue year-to-date against $2.8 million projected — a $700,000 shortfall, or 25 percent below plan. The shortfall is not a billing or denials problem; the claims we submit are being paid at normal rates. The problem is that we are not generating enough billable sessions, because we do not have enough clinicians.

| Measure | Q3 FY2026 (YTD) | Projection / Target | Prior Year (Same Period) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medicaid revenue billed | $2,100,000 | $2,800,000 | $2,650,000 |
| Clinician positions filled (of 11) | 8 | 11 | 10 |
| Clients on waitlist (quarter-end) | 94 | 0–20 | 31 |
| Average wait for first appointment | 5.5 weeks | 2 weeks | 3 weeks |
| Client outcomes (reliable improvement, PHQ-9/GAD-7) | 61% | 60% | 62% |

### 5.2 Explanation

Three of eleven licensed clinician positions were vacant for the entire quarter. Each filled clinician position generates roughly $310,000 to $330,000 in annual Medicaid revenue at target productivity; three vacancies for a full year forgo roughly $950,000, and the year-to-date shortfall is consistent with those vacancies having opened in the first and second quarters.

The vacancies are a market problem. Licensed clinical social workers and licensed professional counselors command salaries in this region that have risen faster than our pay scale — health systems and private telehealth companies are recruiting the same pool at $12,000 to $20,000 above our budgeted range, often with remote flexibility we have not offered. Program director Dr. Evelyn Castellanos has had the positions posted continuously; the agency has made four offers this fiscal year and had three declined, all citing compensation.

The clinical quality of the program remains sound. Client outcomes among those served are at target. But the waitlist has tripled year over year, and a 5.5-week wait for a first behavioral health appointment carries real clinical risk for the population we serve, apart from the revenue consequence.

### 5.3 Trajectory

Two responses are underway. First, the CFO has approved a revised clinician salary band, funded in part by the vacancy savings the positions themselves generate — an unfilled clinician position saves salary but forgoes more revenue than it saves, so filling these positions at a higher salary is financially favorable, not merely programmatically necessary. Second, Dr. Castellanos is piloting supervised billing for two residents-in-counseling, which converts lower-cost pre-licensure staff into billable capacity under Medicaid rules. One resident started in March; a second starts in May. Realistic full-year Medicaid revenue is $2.9 to $3.0 million against the $3.75 million annual projection — a shortfall of roughly $750,000 to $850,000 that flows into the consolidated picture in Section 7.

## 6. Early Literacy

### 6.1 Results

Early literacy, the smallest program line at $1.6 million, met every target it carries this quarter, as it has each quarter this fiscal year. It merits the committee's attention anyway, for a reason covered in Section 6.3.

| Measure | Q3 FY2026 (YTD) | Target | Prior Year (Same Period) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Children enrolled | 620 | 600 | 585 |
| Average attendance rate | 87% | 85% | 84% |
| Children meeting benchmark literacy gains | 74% | 70% | 71% |
| Family engagement sessions held | 96 | 90 | 88 |
| Spending vs. budget (YTD) | $1,190,000 | $1,200,000 | On budget |

### 6.2 Explanation

The program's performance reflects stable staffing — early literacy is the only program line with zero turnover this fiscal year — a mature curriculum now in its fourth year, and strong partnerships with six child care centers and two library branches. Program director Simone Achterberg deserves specific recognition: the family engagement model she built two years ago is now the component most strongly correlated with children's benchmark gains in our internal analysis, and it has drawn interest from two peer organizations.

### 6.3 The grant reduction

The program's federal grant, which supplies $1.35 million of its $1.6 million budget, will be reduced by $600,000 effective October 1, 2026 — one quarter into fiscal 2027. The reduction is the result of a federal appropriations change affecting the entire grant program nationally, not a performance judgment on us; our most recent federal monitoring report, received in December, was clean.

A $600,000 cut against a $1.6 million program is a 37 percent reduction, and it cannot be absorbed by trimming. The realistic options, which management will bring to the board with the fiscal 2027 budget in June, are: (a) reduce enrollment capacity from roughly 620 children to roughly 400 and consolidate from eight sites to five; (b) replace the funding through a combination of state early childhood dollars (an application for $250,000 is being prepared for the state's June cycle), local philanthropy (two foundation conversations are active), and unrestricted funds; or most likely (c) a combination — partial replacement funding plus a smaller capacity reduction. The committee should understand that a decision framework, not a final decision, will accompany the fiscal 2027 budget, because the state application outcome will not be known until August. What the committee should *not* expect is that the strongest-performing program in the agency will pass through October 1 unchanged.

Cash on hand at March 31 was $4.7 million, equivalent to approximately 54 days of operating expenses, against a board policy floor of 45 days. The operating reserve stands at $2.9 million. A projected full-year deficit of $1.5 to $2.1 million before mitigation would, if drawn entirely from reserves, reduce the reserve to $0.8 to $1.4 million — a level the CFO regards as unacceptably thin entering a fiscal year that begins with a known grant reduction.

### 7.4 Mitigation and revised projection

Management's mitigation plan, portions of which are already executed, comprises:

1. **Housing cost actions** (Section 4.3): renegotiated motel rates, the $600,000 city allocation request, and the rapid-rehousing shift — combined estimated benefit of $800,000 to $1.0 million against the housing overage, mostly landing in the fourth quarter and in avoided fiscal 2027 exposure.
2. **Behavioral health revenue recovery** (Section 5.3): revised salary bands and resident billing — modest fourth-quarter benefit, larger fiscal 2027 benefit.
3. **A fourth-quarter discretionary spending hold** across administration and shared services: nonessential travel, conference attendance, equipment replacement, and consulting deferred — estimated benefit of $250,000 to $300,000.
4. **Vacancy discipline**: three administrative vacancies held open through year-end (program-facing positions are exempt) — estimated benefit of $110,000.

With mitigation, the projected full-year deficit narrows to a range of $700,000 to $1.1 million, funded from the operating reserve, leaving the reserve at $1.8 to $2.2 million at June 30. Management regards this as a manageable, though unwelcome, outcome, and the fiscal 2027 budget will include a reserve rebuilding plan. The CFO will present the reforecast in detail at the May 12 meeting and requests committee endorsement of the reserve draw at that time.

## 8. Staffing

### 8.1 The turnover picture

Agency-wide staff turnover ran 29 percent annualized in the third quarter, against a fiscal 2025 rate of 19 percent and a sector benchmark for comparable human services organizations of roughly 20 to 22 percent. Of 214 budgeted positions, 191 were filled at quarter-end; 23 were vacant, of which 14 are direct-service positions.

| Category | Budgeted | Filled (3/31) | Vacant | Annualized Turnover |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Direct service (case managers, clinicians, job developers, educators) | 148 | 134 | 14 | 33% |
| Program supervision and management | 31 | 28 | 3 | 21% |
| Administration and shared services | 35 | 29 | 6 | 17% |
| **Total** | **214** | **191** | **23** | **29%** |

### 8.2 Why staff are leaving

Human resources conducts exit interviews with all departing staff and achieved an 84 percent completion rate this fiscal year. The findings are consistent and the committee should hear them without softening: compensation is the dominant stated reason, cited by 71 percent of departing direct-service staff. The agency's last across-the-board salary adjustment was 3 percent in fiscal 2025; regional market movement for the same roles over the past two years has been on the order of 8 to 14 percent. Workload is the second most cited factor (44 percent), and the two compound — vacancies raise the workload of those who remain, which drives further departures. Adult reentry's caseload situation (Section 3) is the clearest instance, but the same dynamic operates in behavioral health and youth workforce.

Departures are not concentrated among weak performers. Of the 16 direct-service staff who left this quarter, 11 had most recent performance ratings of "exceeds expectations." The agency is losing the people it can least afford to lose, and they are largely going to state agencies, health systems, and better-funded nonprofits doing similar work.

### 8.3 Management response

Three actions are in motion. First, the targeted salary adjustments already described for clinicians and reentry case managers, funded within existing program budgets. Second, HR has commissioned a full compensation study, due in June, to inform a structural salary proposal in the fiscal 2027 budget; the committee should expect that proposal to carry a material price and should weigh it against the demonstrated cost of turnover — recruitment, onboarding, lost productivity, and lost revenue, which we conservatively estimate at $18,000 to $25,000 per direct-service departure, or roughly $900,000 to $1.2 million annualized at current turnover rates. Third, exit data on workload is being addressed program by program, principally through the hiring already described.

Management's honest assessment is that targeted adjustments will slow the bleeding in the most acute roles but that the agency faces a structural compensation problem that only the fiscal 2027 budget can address, and that budget must simultaneously absorb the early literacy grant cut. That tension is the central strategic question before the board this spring, and it is named explicitly in Section 11.

## 9. Financial Controls and Process Corrections

Two process failures contributed to this quarter's financial position, and management is correcting both.

**First, the housing budget was built on assumptions that were known to be stale by autumn and was not reforecast.** Motel rate increases were visible in October; the budget amendment discussion in the first quarter was deferred. Effective immediately, the emergency housing line moves to quarterly reforecasting, with variances above 5 percent reported to the finance committee within thirty days rather than at the next scheduled quarterly report.

**Second, the revenue consequences of clinical vacancies were tracked as a staffing issue rather than a financial one.** Vacancy reports went to HR; revenue projections were not adjusted until the second-quarter close. The CFO has instituted a monthly revenue-at-risk report that translates open billable positions into projected revenue impact, so that the finance function sees the earnings consequence of a vacancy in the same month it opens.

Both corrections respond directly to the trustees' second-quarter concern about late notice, and both will be visible in the materials the committee receives beginning with the fourth-quarter cycle.

## 10. State Corrective Action Plan — Petersburg Site

### 10.1 Background and status

The Petersburg site, which houses adult reentry and behavioral health services under site director Thuy Pham, remains under a corrective action plan imposed by the state Department of Social Services following a January monitoring visit that found 22 incomplete case files out of 140 reviewed. The deficiencies were documentation deficiencies — missing service plan signatures, incomplete quarterly progress notes, and unsigned consent updates — not findings of harm, ineligible service, or financial irregularity. The state's letter, received February 3, required a corrective action plan within 30 days, which was submitted February 27 and accepted March 6.

### 10.2 Progress against the plan

| Corrective Action Element | Required By | Status at March 31 |
|---|---|---|
| Remediate all 22 deficient files | April 30 | 19 of 22 complete; remaining 3 involve clients no longer in service and require documented good-faith contact attempts, underway |
| Implement monthly internal file audits (10% sample) | March 15 | Complete; March audit found 2 deficiencies in 14 files sampled, both corrected within a week |
| Retrain all Petersburg case-carrying staff on documentation standards | March 31 | Complete; 11 of 11 staff trained, with sign-off |
| Designate a quality assurance lead for the site | March 15 | Complete; role assigned to the Petersburg program supervisor with 20% dedicated time |
| Submit monthly progress reports to the state | Monthly through August | March and April reports submitted on time |

### 10.3 Context and assessment

The committee should understand the root cause candidly: the Petersburg site ran with two case manager vacancies for most of the fall, and documentation was what slipped when caseloads rose. This is the same turnover dynamic described in Section 8 expressing itself as a compliance failure rather than an outcome failure. Ms. Pham identified the file backlog internally in December and had begun remediation before the state visit; that head start is why the corrective plan is tracking well.

The state has indicated that if monthly reports remain satisfactory through August, the corrective action designation will be lifted at the scheduled follow-up visit in September. The practical risks in the interim are two: the designation must be disclosed on certain state grant applications, including one adult reentry renewal due in June, where we will disclose it alongside the progress record above; and a repeat finding at the September visit would escalate to a formal license condition. Management assesses the likelihood of a satisfactory September outcome as high, contingent on the site remaining fully staffed — one more reason the reentry hiring described in Section 3.3 matters.

## 11. Fourth Quarter and Fiscal 2027 Outlook

### 11.1 What the committee should watch in the fourth quarter

The committee should expect the following, stated as commitments against which management can be measured at the August meeting:

1. **Financial close.** A full-year deficit of $700,000 to $1.1 million after mitigation, funded from reserves, with the reserve ending between $1.8 and $2.2 million. The city allocation decision (May) and fourth-quarter housing costs are the largest remaining variables.
2. **Youth workforce.** Enrollment finishing at 1,550 to 1,600; fourth-quarter placement recovering to the low 50s; all three job developer positions filled by mid-May.
3. **Adult reentry.** Two case manager hires by early June; caseloads at or below 58 by June 30. If hiring fails, management will bring an intake-pause recommendation to the committee rather than allow caseloads to pass 80.
4. **Behavioral health.** At least two of three clinician vacancies filled at the revised salary band by June 30; waitlist below 60 by fiscal year-end.
5. **Petersburg.** Clean monthly reports to the state in May and June; full file remediation confirmed.
6. **Early literacy.** Continued on-target performance; the state grant application submitted in June.

### 11.2 The fiscal 2027 questions

Two questions will dominate the fiscal 2027 budget the board receives in June, and the committee should begin forming views now.

**First, the early literacy grant reduction.** A 37 percent cut to the agency's best-performing program takes effect October 1. The June budget will present the replacement-funding and capacity-reduction options described in Section 6.3, with a recommended path and a contingency depending on the August state grant decision. The board will be asked to decide how much unrestricted or reserve funding, if any, it is willing to commit to bridging the program while replacement funding is pursued.

**Second, compensation.** The evidence of this quarter is consistent across four program lines: the agency's pay scale has fallen behind the market, and the resulting turnover is now the proximate cause of missed performance targets, forgone Medicaid revenue, and a state compliance finding. The June compensation study will price a structural correction. It will be expensive, and it arrives in the same budget as the grant cut and a reserve rebuilding obligation. Management's view, offered plainly, is that continued underinvestment in compensation is the costlier path — the quarter just described is what that cost looks like — but the trade-offs belong to the board, and the June budget will present them with the same fullness as this report.

### 11.3 Closing note

This report was written to ensure the committee learns nothing about the third quarter later than today. The quarter's problems are real, their causes are identified, and the responses are specific, funded, and dated. Management welcomes the committee's questions on May 12, and program directors Halloran, Okonjo, Reyes-Marsh, Castellanos, and Achterberg, along with site director Pham, will be available for the discussion.

## Appendix C. Emergency Housing Cost Detail

Because the housing overage is the largest single financial variance in this report, the following detail supports the projections in Sections 4.3 and 7.4.

**Cost structure of the overage.** The $700,000 year-to-date overage at March 31 decomposes as follows: approximately $410,000 attributable to the motel rate increase ($118 versus the budgeted $88, applied across roughly 13,700 voucher nights year-to-date); approximately $250,000 attributable to extended lengths of stay beyond budget assumptions; and approximately $40,000 in associated costs (transportation, storage, and client supports that scale with stay length).

**Mitigation arithmetic.** The renegotiated block rates signed in April cover the Harborview Inn and the Coastal Suites properties at $104 per night, applicable to an estimated 55 percent of fourth-quarter voucher nights — a projected fourth-quarter saving of roughly $95,000. The rapid-rehousing shift moves an estimated 35 to 45 eligible households from motel stays (averaging $7,800 per household at current stay lengths) to shallow subsidies averaging $3,100 per household, for a projected fourth-quarter and early-FY2027 benefit of $165,000 to $210,000. The $600,000 city allocation, if granted in full, applies directly against the overage; the request has been formally acknowledged by the city's homelessness services office with a decision expected before Memorial Day. Management's mitigation range of $800,000 to $1.0 million assumes the city grants between $450,000 and $600,000; if the city declines entirely, the full-year deficit projection in Section 7.4 rises by that amount, and management would return to the committee in June with further options, including a temporary cap on new voucher placements, which would require funder consultation given contract obligations.

## Appendix D. Glossary of Measures

For trustees newer to the committee, the following defines the recurring measures in this report.

**Placement rate.** The percentage of program completers who obtained employment within 90 days of completing services, verified by employer confirmation or pay documentation.

**Annualized turnover.** Departures during the period, divided by average filled positions, scaled to a twelve-month rate. A quarter with 15 departures against 190 average filled positions annualizes to approximately 32 percent.

**Length of stay.** The average number of nights between a household's entry into emergency shelter (including motel vouchers) and exit, measured for households exiting during the period.

**Reliable improvement (PHQ-9/GAD-7).** The percentage of behavioral health clients whose scores on standardized depression and anxiety instruments improved by a statistically reliable margin between intake and most recent administration.

**Days of operating cash.** Unrestricted cash divided by average daily operating expense; the board policy floor is 45 days.

**Revenue-at-risk.** The new monthly measure defined in Section 9: projected revenue forgone from open billable positions, calculated at each position's target productivity.
