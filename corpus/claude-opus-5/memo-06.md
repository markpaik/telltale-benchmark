### Why the conversion is happening

There is a second reason that has nothing to do with the audit, and I want it stated plainly because I do not want anyone believing we are only reacting to a letter. Our driver attrition is running 31 percent against an industry figure near 24. We have terminals where the owner-operator turnover is materially worse than the employee turnover in the same building, doing the same lanes. When a man buys his own tractor, carries his own insurance, absorbs his own fuel volatility, and then watches the fuel surcharge move against him for two quarters, he leaves. **SUBJECT:** NJDOL Assessment — Findings, Response Deadline, and Second Audit Period

**PRIVILEGED AND CONFIDENTIAL — ATTORNEY-CLIENT COMMUNICATION — ATTORNEY WORK PRODUCT**

This memorandum summarizes the New Jersey Department of Labor and Workforce Development's audit findings issued January 22, 2026, the assessment amount, our response options and deadline, and the exposure presented by the open second audit period. It is intended for the leadership team only and should not be forwarded, excerpted, or discussed with terminal-level management. Cheikh's February 9 memo to the field was reviewed by this office in advance and states the operational decision without characterizing the legal position; that distinction should be preserved in all downstream communication.

### The assessment

The Department examined calendar years 2022, 2023, and 2024 and concluded that our owner-operator population, or a substantial portion of it, fails the New Jersey "ABC" test for independent contractor status under N.J.S.A. 43:21-19(i)(6). The assessment totals $2,412,880, comprising $1,614,200 in unpaid unemployment and temporary disability contributions, $412,680 in interest, and $386,000 in penalties. We have 45 days from the date of the assessment to file a written response, which places the deadline at March 8, 2026. Because that date falls on a Sunday, we will file on Friday, March 6.

The Department's theory rests almost entirely on Prong B. Under the ABC test, a worker is presumed to be an employee unless the hiring entity establishes all three prongs: (A) the worker is free from control or direction over performance; (B) the service is either outside the usual course of the business for which it is performed, or performed outside all the places of business of the enterprise; and (C) the worker is customarily engaged in an independently established trade, occupation, profession, or business.

Prong B is where we lose, and I want the leadership team to understand that it is not a close question under current New Jersey law. We are a trucking company. Our owner-operators drive trucks carrying our freight under our operating authority. The service they perform is not outside the usual course of our business — it *is* our business. The Department's auditors made this observation in three sentences and did not belabor it. Our historical reliance on Prong B has been the argument that owner-operators perform their service "outside all the places of business of the enterprise" because they are on the road. New Jersey courts have not been receptive to that reading, and the Department cited *Hargrove v. Sleepy's* and its progeny in rejecting it.

Prongs A and C are more defensible in isolation. We do not dictate routes on the majority of contracted lanes, we do not require exclusive service, and a meaningful minority of our owner-operators hold their own operating authority and haul for others. But the ABC test is conjunctive. Failing Prong B is dispositive regardless of how strong our showing is on A and C.

### Response options

We have three realistic paths and I recommend a hybrid.

The first is to contest the assessment in full, request a hearing before the Office of Administrative Law, and litigate the classification question. I do not recommend this as a primary strategy. Our probability of prevailing on Prong B is low — I would put it below twenty percent — and a fully litigated loss produces a published outcome that invites both the second audit period and private plaintiff activity. It also takes eighteen to thirty months, during which the contested model continues to accrue exposure at roughly $800,000 per year in the same categories the Department has already assessed.

The second is to pay the assessment in full and prospectively reclassify. This eliminates the immediate matter but forfeits any negotiation on the penalty component and, more importantly, does nothing to bound the second audit period.

The third, which I recommend, is to file a timely response on March 6 that (a) does not concede employee status for any period as a matter of legal conclusion, (b) contests the penalty component and specific worker-by-worker inclusions where the record supports it, (c) formally notifies the Department of our voluntary prospective conversion effective January 1, 2027, and (d) opens a settlement discussion aimed at a global resolution covering both the assessed period and the open period.

The reason for this structure is the second audit period. The Department's cover letter states that the examination of calendar years 2025 and 2026 "remains open." At our current owner-operator headcount and compensation levels, an equivalent finding across those two years produces exposure in the range of $1.5 to $1.9 million before penalties. If we litigate and lose, we face both assessments with no leverage. If we settle, our voluntary conversion is the only meaningful concession we have to offer, and it is worth considerably more at the negotiating table before January 1, 2027 than after.

I want to be direct with the leadership team about a point that has come up in conversation. The conversion is not a legal admission and we will not characterize it as one. But I would be doing you a disservice if I suggested that the Department will not notice it, or that a reasonable adversary would fail to draw an inference from it. My judgment is that the inference is worth accepting. We are converting for operational and financial reasons that stand on their own, and the settlement value of having done so voluntarily and on our own schedule substantially exceeds the evidentiary cost.

### Related exposures

Three matters sit adjacent to this one and the leadership team should be tracking them.

First, wage and hour. A misclassification finding under the unemployment statute does not automatically establish liability under the New Jersey Wage Payment Law or the Wage and Hour Law, but it is powerful evidence in a private action. Our owner-operator settlement statements contain deductions — equipment, insurance, escrow — that would be unlawful chargebacks against an employee's wages. I have asked outside counsel for an exposure estimate and expect it in March. I do not have a number for you today and I would rather give you none than a wrong one.

Second, the insurance renewal. Sanjay will address the 24 percent increase in his budget memo. I note here only that the two nuclear-verdict settlements driving the regional market both involved carriers whose contractor status was litigated as part of the plaintiff's vicarious liability theory. Employee status is not obviously worse for us in that context, and may be better, because it removes an avenue of argument the plaintiff's bar has been using to pierce carrier defenses. I would not represent this as a savings, but it should not be counted as a cost either.

Third, New York. The Albany facility brings us dock labor and domiciled drivers in a second state with its own classification framework and an aggressive enforcement posture. Opening Albany with employee drivers only, from day one, is the correct posture and I understand from Cheikh that this is already the plan. I want it stated in writing that no owner-operator will be domiciled in Albany at any point.

### What I need

I need the leadership team's authorization by February 20 to retain Kessler Roth as outside counsel for the response and any settlement negotiation, at an estimated $145,000 through resolution. I need Lorraine's office to preserve, without alteration, all owner-operator agreements, settlement statements, dispatch records, and recruiting materials from January 1, 2022 forward — a litigation hold notice went out February 10 and I need confirmation of compliance from each terminal by February 25. And I need every member of this team to route any external communication touching classification through this office before it goes out. That includes customer communications, insurance submissions, and anything said to a recruiter.

**SUBJECT:** Revised FY2026–FY2027 Operating Budget — Conversion Cost, Tractor Financing, Insurance

This memorandum revises the operating budget approved in November to reflect the owner-operator conversion, the Albany cross-dock, and the liability insurance renewal. It supersedes the November plan for all line items addressed below. Terminal managers should use the revised per-mile figures in any capacity or lane profitability analysis from this date forward.

### The nineteen cents

The headline number is that converting our 310 owner-operators to employee drivers raises our fully loaded cost per mile on the affected miles by 19.1 cents. I want to walk through how that number is built, because it will be quoted a great deal in the next ten months and I would rather it be quoted accurately.

Our owner-operators run approximately 94.3 million miles annually, roughly 33 percent of total network miles. Today we pay them an all-in linehaul rate averaging $1.42 per mile plus fuel surcharge, and we carry essentially no other cost against those miles. The tractor, its maintenance, its insurance, its fuel, and the driver's own tax and benefit burden all sit on the contractor's side of the line.

Under the employee model, the same mile carries driver wages at an effective $0.68, payroll taxes and unemployment and workers' compensation at $0.11, health and welfare benefits at $0.14, paid time off accrual at $0.05, tractor ownership or lease cost at $0.29, maintenance and tires at $0.17, fuel at $0.44 net of surcharge recovery, physical damage and liability allocation at $0.13, and administrative and settlement overhead at $0.02. That totals $2.03. Against the $1.42 contractor rate plus $0.42 in surcharge pass-through that we currently recover and remit, the net increase is 19.1 cents per mile.

Applied to 94.3 million miles, the annualized gross impact is $18.0 million. Against 2026 revenue of $284 million, that is 6.34 percent of revenue, and against our current operating ratio it is the difference between a workable year and a bad one if nothing else changes. Things will change, and I will come to the offsets.

I want to flag one honest weakness in the 19-cent figure. It assumes our converted drivers run the same miles per truck per week that they run today. They will not, at least not in the first two quarters. Employee drivers under company hours-of-service management, on company equipment with company maintenance intervals, historically run four to seven percent fewer paid miles per unit than owner-operators in their first year. If that holds, the effective cost increase is closer to 21 cents. I have budgeted the 19-cent figure and carried the difference as a $1.8 million contingency rather than restating the headline, because I would rather manage against a reserve than against a number the field does not trust.

### Offsets

Three things move in our favor and none of them are speculative.

Turnover. At 31 percent attrition we spend approximately $6,400 per driver replacement across recruiting, orientation, road testing, onboarding, and the productivity cost of an empty seat. Our owner-operator segment turns over meaningfully faster than our employee segment. If the conversion moves blended attrition from 31 percent toward 26 percent — which is the low end of what comparable carriers have reported post-conversion — we recover approximately $1.9 million annually. I have budgeted a move to 28 percent, worth $1.1 million, in year one only.

Fuel purchasing. Bringing 218 additional tractors onto company fuel cards and into our network purchasing agreement moves an estimated 12.4 million gallons annually into contracted pricing. At an average $0.19 per gallon advantage over retail, that is $2.36 million. This is the most reliable offset in the package and I have budgeted it in full.

Utilization and dispatch flexibility. Company drivers can be repositioned, held for reload, and assigned to lanes an owner-operator can decline. Our dispatch modeling suggests a 3.1 percent improvement in loaded-mile ratio across the converted population. I have budgeted half of that, worth $1.4 million.

Net of these offsets, the conversion costs $12.5 million in the first full year against the $18.0 million gross, with the $1.8 million contingency held separately. The net figure improves in year two as the turnover benefit matures and the utilization gain is fully realized. My working projection is $9.2 million net in FY2028.

### Tractor acquisition and financing

We need 218 tractors. Our fleet plan assumes a mix rather than a single acquisition path, for reasons of cost, timing, and balance sheet treatment.

We will purchase 74 tractors directly from converting owner-operators. This is by a wide margin the cheapest capacity available to us, because the units are already in our network, already spec'd to our lanes, and already maintained to a standard our fleet services group can assess. We have set a valuation methodology at 88 percent of Black Book clean retail for units under 500,000 miles with acceptable inspection, adjusted downward for deferred maintenance. Budgeted at an average $47,500, this is $3.5 million in capital, funded from the revolver and termed out in the fourth quarter.

We will lease 106 new tractors under a five-year full-service lease at $2,340 per unit per month including maintenance. Annual cost $2.98 million, treated as operating expense. Delivery is staged in four tranches — 20 units in June, 32 in August, 32 in October, and 22 in December. I want to be explicit that the December tranche is the schedule risk in this plan. If OEM delivery slips, we enter January short of equipment for units we have already committed to convert.

We will acquire 38 late-model used tractors on the open market, budgeted at $62,000 average, financed over 48 months at 7.4 percent. Capital outlay $2.36 million; annual debt service $681,000.

The current fleet retains sufficient spare capacity to cover the remaining gap through seasonal reallocation, though this consumes essentially all of our slack. I have no comfortable answer for a bad-weather quarter that takes units out of service in December and January simultaneously.

Total first-year cash requirement for equipment is $5.86 million in capital plus $3.66 million in lease and debt service. Our revolver has $18 million of availability against a $30 million facility, and the covenant package permits this. We will be tighter on fixed charge coverage than I would like — projected 1.34 against a 1.25 covenant — and that is before Albany.

### Albany

The Albany cross-dock carries $4.2 million in first-year cost: $1.35 million in lease and occupancy, $1.94 million in labor for 34 dock and 6 clerical positions, $410,000 in dock equipment and technology, $290,000 in startup and hiring, and $210,000 in contingency. Against $31 million in awarded annual revenue at a projected 88.4 operating ratio, the facility contributes approximately $3.6 million in operating income at full run rate, with the first year at roughly $1.1 million given a November 15 start.

I will say plainly that Albany is what makes this year work. Without the retail award, the conversion cost lands on an unchanged revenue base and the operating ratio degrades to a level that I would have difficulty defending to our lenders. With it, we absorb the conversion and hold the ratio within eighty basis points of plan. That is the arithmetic, and it is why the November 15 date is not a soft target.

### Insurance

The liability renewal came in at a 24 percent increase, taking our annual premium from $4.61 million to $5.72 million, an increase of $1.11 million. The market is responding to two nuclear-verdict settlements in our region rather than to our own loss experience, which improved year over year. Our broker's position is that the increase would have been 31 percent absent our safety record.

We have modeled a higher self-insured retention — moving from $250,000 to $500,000 per occurrence — which would reduce the premium increase to 14 percent, or $645,000. I am not recommending it this year. Taking on retained risk in the same year we change our labor model, add 218 units, and open a facility in a new state concentrates too much variability into a single period. I would revisit at the 2027 renewal with a year of employee-model claims data behind us.

I note Camille's observation that employee classification may improve our position in vicarious liability litigation. I have not budgeted any benefit from this and would ask that no one represent it to the broker as a rate argument until we have data.

### Summary

Revised FY2027 projection: revenue $319 million, reflecting the Albany award at partial-year and modest core growth. Operating expense increases of $12.5 million net conversion, $4.2 million Albany, $1.11 million insurance. Projected operating ratio 94.8 against a November plan of 93.1. Projected operating income $16.6 million.

This is a survivable year, not a good one. It becomes a good year in FY2028 if Albany performs, the turnover benefit materializes, and we do not have a bad claim. I will update this budget monthly beginning April 1 and I would ask terminal managers to treat the revised per-mile figures as binding for pricing purposes.

**TO:** All Owner-Operator Drivers; Terminal Managers; Fleet Managers
**FROM:** Lorraine Petrucci, Vice President of Human Resources

**SUBJECT:** Employee Driver Conversion — Pay, Benefits, Seniority, and Transition Rules

This memorandum sets out the complete terms under which Meadowlands Freight Systems will convert its 310 owner-operator drivers to employee driver status effective January 1, 2027. It is the authoritative document on pay, benefits, seniority, and transition mechanics. No terminal manager, fleet manager, or recruiter is authorized to offer terms different from those stated here, and any question that this memorandum does not answer should come to my office rather than being answered locally.

I want to open by acknowledging something. Every driver receiving this memorandum made a decision at some point to run as an independent business. Some of you made that decision fifteen years ago. You bought equipment, you took risk, you built something you owned. We are asking you to give that up, and we are asking on a schedule you did not set. I am not going to pretend that a well-designed benefits package makes that a small thing. What I can tell you is that we have built these terms to be genuinely competitive rather than merely adequate, and that we want every one of you to stay.

### Pay structure

Converting drivers will be paid on a per-mile basis with an activity pay overlay, structured as follows.

Base mileage pay begins at $0.68 per practical mile. Drivers with five or more years of continuous service with Meadowlands as an owner-operator start at $0.71. Drivers with ten or more years start at $0.74. These starting rates place us at or above the 65th percentile for Northeast LTL company driver pay based on the February 2026 National Transportation Institute survey.

Mileage pay increases $0.02 at twelve months of employee service and $0.02 at twenty-four months, with subsequent increases on the standard company driver progression.

Activity pay applies to work that is not driving. Stop pay is $22.00 per stop after the first. Detention pay is $28.00 per hour after the first hour, paid in quarter-hour increments, with no requirement that the customer pay us first — this is a change from the contractor arrangement and it is deliberate. Layover pay is $150.00 per twenty-four-hour period. Breakdown pay is $150.00 per day when the unit is out of service for company equipment reasons. Hand-unload pay is $32.00 per unload where the lane requires it. New York City and metro delivery differential is $45.00 per day.

Safety and performance bonus is paid quarterly at up to $0.03 per mile on the quarter's miles, based on preventable accident record, on-time percentage, DOT inspection results, and fuel efficiency against unit baseline. This is a real bonus with achievable thresholds, not a rate withheld and returned. Our employee driver population currently earns the full bonus in approximately 61 percent of quarters.

Guaranteed weekly minimum is $1,150 for any full week in which the driver is available for dispatch and accepts all reasonable assignments. This exists so that a slow week is a company problem rather than a driver problem, which is the single most important structural difference between what you have now and what you are being offered.

Our modeling, using each converting driver's actual 2025 mileage and activity, projects that 247 of 310 drivers will see gross compensation within eight percent of their 2025 net owner-operator earnings after accounting for the elimination of their equipment, insurance, maintenance, and fuel costs. Of the remaining 63, we project 41 will see an increase of more than eight percent and 22 will see a decrease of more than eight percent. Every driver in that last group will receive an individual meeting with their terminal manager and me before September 30, and we have set aside a transition differential fund to address genuine hardship cases individually. I am not going to promise that every one of the 22 will be made whole, but I will promise that every one of them will be heard by someone with authority to do something about it.

### Benefits

Health coverage begins on the first day of employment with no waiting period. This is a deviation from our standard 60-day waiting period and it was approved specifically for the converting population. Three medical plan options are available, with the company paying 78 percent of employee-only premium and 68 percent of family premium. Dental and vision are included at 85 percent company-paid.

The 401(k) plan offers a company match of 100 percent of the first 3 percent and 50 percent of the next 2 percent, vesting immediately on the employee contribution and over three years on the match. Converting drivers receive service credit for vesting purposes based on their owner-operator tenure, which means most of you will be fully vested from day one.

Paid time off accrues at 10 days annually for drivers with less than five years of credited service, 15 days for five to ten years, and 20 days for over ten years. Six paid holidays. Short-term and long-term disability are fully company-paid. Company-paid life insurance at one times annual earnings, with supplemental available.

Workers' compensation coverage applies from the first day. I want to draw attention to this specifically because it is the benefit our converting drivers most consistently underestimate. Under the contractor model, an on-duty injury is your problem and your occupational accident policy's problem, subject to whatever limits and exclusions that policy carries. Under employment, it is ours.

### Seniority

Seniority is the issue I have received the most questions about and I want to be precise.

Converting owner-operators will receive full seniority credit for their continuous service with Meadowlands Freight Systems, measured from their original contract date, for all purposes except one. Seniority governs vacation scheduling, layoff and recall order, equipment assignment preference, and pay progression credit as described above.

The single exception is bid lane selection. For the first twelve months following conversion — through December 31, 2027 — bid lane selection will operate on a modified basis in which converting drivers are integrated into the existing employee seniority roster at a position reflecting their Meadowlands service date, but with existing employee drivers holding a one-round preference in the first two annual bids. I want to explain why, because it is a compromise and it will not please everyone.

Our 630 existing employee drivers have accumulated bid seniority under a system in which owner-operators were not participants. Some of them have waited seven or eight years to reach a lane. Dropping 310 drivers into that roster at full seniority would displace long-tenured employees from lanes they earned under the rules as they existed. Conversely, giving converting drivers no credit would tell a fifteen-year owner-operator that his fifteen years count for nothing, which is both unfair and untrue. The one-round preference for two bid cycles is our attempt at the least-bad answer. It fully protects no one and it fully disadvantages no one.

I met with the employee driver committee on April 2 and the owner-operator advisory group on April 7. Neither group endorsed this structure. Both groups indicated they could live with it. That is roughly what I expected and I am treating it as the correct outcome.

### Equipment

Drivers who own their tractors may sell them to the company under the valuation methodology Sanjay's team has published, may sell them on the open market, or may retain them for personal or other business use. There is no requirement to sell to us and no penalty for declining. Drivers with outstanding equipment financing should contact my office immediately; we have retained a transition advisor at company expense to assist with payoff, refinance, or lease assumption questions, and this service is available to every affected driver whether or not they intend to remain with us.

Drivers who sell to the company and remain employed will, where operationally feasible, be assigned their own former unit. I cannot guarantee this in every case but it is the default we are working toward.

### Timeline

Individual compensation modeling statements go out May 18, showing each driver their projected employee earnings against their 2025 actuals. One-on-one meetings run June through September. Formal offer letters go out October 1 with a November 14 acceptance deadline. Orientation classes run November 17 through December 22 in cohorts by terminal. Employment begins January 1, 2027.

Drivers who decline conversion may continue under their contractor agreement through December 31, 2026, after which the agreement terminates. We will not extend it. I would rather say that clearly in April than have someone discover it in December.

My office line and my mobile are on the attached sheet. I mean it when I say to use them.

### Systems and the on-time commitment

Dock management system is installed and integrated with the network TMS. We completed the first integration test September 30 with 74 percent clean pass, which is roughly where I expected to be. That brings me to the 98.5 percent commitment, and I want to say something about it that has not been said directly enough in our weekly calls.

Ninety-eight and a half percent on eleven lanes at projected volume means we can miss approximately 34 loads per quarter. Not 34 late loads — 34 loads outside the delivery window as measured by the customer's system, which is not always the same as late. Our network-wide on-time percentage is 96.1. I believe we can do it. I would not have taken this job if I did not. Neither is a facility problem or a labor problem. Both are solvable with decisions in the next two weeks.

I received the memorandum signed by nine of you on October 12 asking that the January 1 conversion be suspended pending resolution of the NJDOL matter. I have read it three times. It deserves a real answer rather than a restatement of the decision, and this memo is my attempt at one.

Let me first state your argument as I understand it, so that you can tell me if I have it wrong.

You argue that we are spending $12.5 million net in the first year, taking on 218 units of equipment, and accepting a driver population disruption at the worst possible moment — six weeks after opening Albany under a 98.5 percent commitment — in order to resolve a $2.4 million assessment that we have not yet lost. You argue that Camille has retained outside counsel and opened a settlement discussion, which means the matter is live and the outcome unknown. You argue that if we prevail, or settle favorably, we will have spent five times the assessment to solve a problem that did not require solving. You argue that the conversion telegraphs weakness into that negotiation. And you argue, most pointedly, that no one has explained why January 1, 2027 rather than January 1, 2028.

That is a fair summary of a fair argument and I want to take it in pieces.

### On the arithmetic

The comparison of $12.5 million against $2.4 million is the weakest part of the case and I want to explain why, because I think it is the number that has convinced most of you.

The $2.4 million is not the exposure. It is one assessment for one three-year period under one statute in one state. The Department has told us in writing that 2025 and 2026 remain open, which is another $1.5 to $1.9 million before penalties. That is the second bite, and it is already scheduled. Behind it sits wage and hour exposure that Camille has not sized yet but which arises from deductions on settlement statements that would be unlawful chargebacks against an employee — and that exposure runs to a six-year lookback and carries fee-shifting. Behind that sits New York, where we are about to domicile drivers and dock labor in a jurisdiction with its own framework and its own appetite. And behind all of it sits the fact that a misclassification finding is discoverable and admissible in the next serious injury case we defend, in a region where two nuclear verdicts just repriced our insurance by 24 percent.

The honest comparison is not $12.5 million against $2.4 million. It is $12.5 million against an exposure I cannot bound, in a regulatory direction that has moved one way for a decade, plus $18 million of gross annual cost that we will eventually pay anyway on a schedule someone else chooses.

That last clause is the part I most want you to sit with. Every serious person in this industry believes the contractor model in Northeast LTL is going away. The question was never whether we would pay 19 cents. It was whether we would pay it in a year we planned for, with equipment we sourced on our own timeline, or in a quarter when a state agency, a court, or a plaintiff's lawyer told us to. The second version costs more and arrives without warning.

### On the settlement posture

Several of you argued that converting undercuts Camille in the negotiation. I understand the intuition and I asked her the same question in February.

Her answer, which I accept, is the opposite. Our voluntary conversion is the most valuable thing we have to trade. The Department's interest is not primarily in collecting $2.4 million from Meadowlands Freight Systems; it is in changing how carriers in this state classify drivers. A carrier that shows up with a completed conversion, a documented timeline, and 310 drivers on payroll is in a materially better position on penalty abatement and on the open period than a carrier that shows up arguing. And that value decays. A conversion we complete voluntarily in January 2027 is worth substantially more at the table than one we are ordered into in 2028.

I would also gently note that the memo assumes an appeal we are likely to win. Camille's written assessment puts our probability of prevailing on Prong B below twenty percent. I do not think any of us would run a lane on those odds.

### On the timing

The timing question is the strongest thing in your memo and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Converting 310 drivers six weeks after opening a facility under the tightest service commitment in our network is not how anyone would design this year. Tomasz's October 3 status shows him eight drivers short and asking for purchased transportation, and every one of you can do the arithmetic on what a rough January does to a 98.5 percent number measured from day one.

Here is why it is still January 1.

The conversion date is the beginning of a tax and benefits year, and moving it off January 1 means running two payroll classifications, two benefits enrollments, two workers' compensation classifications, and two sets of settlement mechanics through a partial year. Lorraine's team priced that at roughly $340,000 in administrative cost and a materially higher error rate in the year we can least afford errors.

The equipment is committed. Sanjay's lease tranches deliver 20 units in June, 32 in August, 32 in October, and 22 in December. We are paying for those units from delivery. Deferring the conversion twelve months means carrying $2.98 million in annual lease cost against tractors with no drivers in them, or breaking commitments with an OEM we will need again in 2028.

The drivers have been told. Offer letters went out October 1 with a November 14 acceptance deadline. Two hundred and ninety-one of 310 have received individual compensation modeling. Twenty-two drivers projected to lose more than eight percent have been through hardship meetings and some have received differential commitments. If we suspend now, we tell 310 people that the eight months of process they just went through was provisional. I do not know how we recruit a driver in this region afterward. I genuinely do not.

And the second audit period runs through 2026. Every month we continue the contractor model adds to an exposure the Department is already examining. A twelve-month deferral adds calendar 2027 to the open period at roughly $800,000 in the same categories already assessed. The delay is not free. It just bills later.

I said this memo would be a real answer, and a real answer includes the parts where you are right.

You are right that January is a bad month to do this in Albany, and I am removing Albany from the collision. Albany opens and stabilizes with a staffed employee crew that is not being converted, transferred, or retrained during its first measurement quarter.

You are right that the December lease tranche is a schedule risk with no fallback. I have asked Sanjay to secure short-term rental capacity for 22 units through March as a bridge, and he has authorized up to $180,000 for it.

And you are right that we did not explain ourselves well enough in February. That is my failure, not yours. The February memo announced a decision and gave two reasons in four paragraphs. The nine of you had to write a formal memorandum to get the full reasoning, and that should not have been necessary.

### Where this leaves us

The conversion proceeds January 1, 2027. I am not going to soften that.

But I would ask you to read this as what it is, which is not a dismissal.
