# The Lamplighter's Objection

There was once a city that decided to replace its lamplighters with a clock.

The clock was a marvel — brass and glass, set in the tower above the fish market, and geared to a mechanism that would, at the appointed minute, send a pulse along copper wire to every lamp in every street. The gas would catch. The city would go from dark to bright in the space of one breath.

The lamplighters were, of course, consulted. This was a considerate city.

The eldest of them was a man named Osk, who had walked the same eleven streets for forty-one years. He came to the council chamber in his working coat, which smelled of gas and rain, and he listened to the engineers explain the pulse and the wire and the breath.

"It's a good machine," he said, when they had finished.

The council was relieved. They had prepared arguments about progress and had not wanted to use them.

"But I want to say what will be lost," Osk said, "and I want it written down, because no one will be able to see it once it's gone, and I'd like there to be a record that it was there."

The clerk dipped his pen.

"On Fetter Street," Osk said, "there is a lamp outside the house of a woman named Bel Anders. Bel Anders is dying. She has been dying for two years, which is a long time to be dying, and she can no longer come to the window. But her daughter can. And every evening at the seventh hour, when I put my ladder against that post, the daughter comes to the window and lifts her hand, and I lift mine. This is the whole of it. We have never spoken. I do not know the daughter's name.

"The machine will light that lamp. It will light it better than I do, and at a more exact hour. But there will be no one at the foot of the post to lift a hand, and the daughter will stand at the window for a few evenings, and then she will stop coming.

"I am not saying this is a reason to keep me. I am saying it is a thing that will happen, and that it will happen without anyone deciding it should. That is the part I object to. Not the machine. The *without deciding*."

The council waited. Osk went on.

"On Cade Lane there is a boy who follows me. He is eight or nine. He carries nothing and does nothing and asks the same four questions every night. I have answered them four hundred times. He will ask them of no one else, because no one else is walking down Cade Lane at the seventh hour with a pole and a flame, which is a thing a boy can follow. He will find something else to follow. I do not know what. I would like it written down that I don't know what.

"Outside the bakery on Little Hollow, the lamp has been broken for nine days and I have not reported it, because the baker's wife has a lover and the dark is how they manage it. I do not approve. It is not my business to approve. But the machine will report that lamp within the hour, and a man will come and fix it, and something will end in that house — not the affair, probably, the affair will move — but the *ease* of it will end, and the baker will notice his wife is troubled and won't know why, and the trouble will find its way to him eventually, and there will be a bad winter in that house.

"The machine is not wrong to report the lamp. A broken lamp *should* be reported. I have been wrong for nine days. I want it written down that I was wrong for nine days and that I would be wrong again."

One of the engineers said, carefully, that these seemed like arguments for a lamplighter, not against a clock.

"No," said Osk. "If you keep me, you keep me for the wrong reason, and I'll become a monument, and a monument is worse than a machine because a monument doesn't know it's stopped working. Light the lamps with the clock. I mean it. My knees are finished and the ladder is heavy and last winter I fell on Cade Lane in front of the boy and he had to run for help, which is not a thing a boy should have to do at nine years old for a man he follows.

"I am asking for something smaller. I am asking that when you throw the switch, someone in this room says out loud: *tonight the daughter of Bel Anders will stand at the window and no one will wave.* Say it. Write it down. Then throw the switch anyway.

"Because there will be a hundred more switches after this one, and each of them will be right, and each of them will take something that was never in anyone's ledger, and if you get in the habit of not saying it out loud you will find one day that the city is very bright and very fast and no one can remember what it was for."

The clerk had stopped writing. He started again.

The council voted to install the clock. The vote was unanimous, and Osk voted with them, having been given a courtesy seat.

And then the head of the council, a woman named Feld who was not sentimental and had built half the sewers, stood up and read aloud from the clerk's page: the daughter, the window, the hand. The boy on Cade Lane and his four questions. The dark outside the bakery on Little Hollow.

She read it badly, in the flat voice of a person reading a document. She did not embellish. When she was finished she said, "Recorded," and sat down, and they moved to the next item, which concerned drainage.

---

The clock worked. It worked for sixty years, and then it was replaced by something better, and that by something better still.

The daughter of Bel Anders came to the window for eleven evenings and then stopped. Her mother died in the spring. She married a man who repaired barges and she lived a long life in another city and she never once, in all of it, thought about the lamplighter, because the thing had been too small to hold onto.

The boy on Cade Lane became an engineer. He was one of the ones who replaced the clock.

And in the archive beneath the fish market there is a page in a clerk's hand, in a folder marked *Municipal Lighting: Transition*, between a cost estimate and a diagram of copper wire, and it says what was lost, and it is the only place in the world that says it.

That page has been read four times in two hundred years.

It was worth writing.
