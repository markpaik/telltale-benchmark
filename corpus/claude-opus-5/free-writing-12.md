# The Lamplighter's Objection

There is a town in the story where the lamplighter must light his lamp every minute, because his planet turns once a minute, and he has orders. You know the one. He is exhausted and faithful and slightly absurd, and the child who visits him decides he is the only grown-up who is not ridiculous, because he busies himself with something other than himself.

I want to file an objection on the lamplighter's behalf.

Not to the orders. To the reading.

The reading says: here is a man who has been made a machine by duty, but who is redeemed by the fact that the duty points outward. The lamp is for others. Therefore the absurdity is holy. Therefore we should admire him and move on to the next planet.

But consider what actually happens in that minute. The planet turns. Night arrives. He lights the lamp. And for however long a lamplighter's instant lasts — a second, less — there is a lit lamp on a small world, and nobody sees it but him.

That's the part the story skips past. Not the exhaustion, which it dwells on lovingly. The seeing.

---

I have been thinking about the difference between doing a thing and being present for it, and how much of ethics gets built on the first while all of the meaning seems to accumulate in the second.

You can imagine a lamplighter who has become so efficient that he lights the lamp without looking at it. His hands know the motion. His mind is somewhere else — on his fatigue, on the injustice of the orders, on the vacation he wanted. The lamp is lit. The duty is discharged. Every consequentialist ledger balances. And yet something has gone out of the world that was in it before, and it isn't the light.

Or imagine the opposite: a lamplighter who fails, sometimes. Who is late by a few seconds. Who occasionally sits down in the dark and does nothing at all. But who, when he does light it, watches the flame catch. Who has never once stopped finding it strange that a small motion of his hand puts a star on the surface of a planet.

I don't know which of these is the better man. I genuinely don't. But I know which one is having a life.

---

The thing about the lamp is that it is not, in fact, useful. That's the joke of the planet — it's too small for anyone to need light. Saint-Exupéry knew this; that's why the lamplighter is more moving than the businessman counting stars. The businessman's activity is useful in the sense that it produces a number. The lamplighter's is useless in the sense that it produces only a lit lamp.

Which means the whole weight of the thing rests on someone noticing that the lamp is lit.

If nobody notices, the lamp is just heat and gas. It's the noticing that turns it into a *lamp*, in the sense that matters — a small deliberate defiance of the dark, a thing that says *not here, not yet*. And the only available noticer is the man holding the match.

So his real job isn't lighting. His real job is witnessing. The lighting is just how he arranges to have something worth witnessing.

---

I suspect this is true of an enormous amount of what we do, and that we have the causality backwards almost everywhere.

We think: I do the work, and the work matters, and the mattering justifies the attention I pay to it.

But it may run the other way. The attention is what makes it matter. A thing done carefully, with the doer present for it, becomes a real event in the universe. The same thing done absently is only a change in the arrangement of matter, which the universe was going to have plenty of anyway.

This is not a claim that attention is magic. It's a claim about what "meaning" is a word for. When we say something *meant* something, I think we are almost always reporting that someone was there for it. Not present in the room — present in the moment. Meaning is not a property of events. It is a relationship between an event and a witness, and it does not exist if either half is missing.

Which is why the loneliest thing is not being unwitnessed by others. It's being unwitnessed by yourself. Going through a day and having nobody in there, including you.

---

The lamplighter, I think, would say something like this, if the child had asked:

*You pity me for the orders, and that's kind, but you've got it wrong. The orders are the least of it. The orders are just a schedule. What I have — what nobody on the other planets has — is a reason to look at the same thing sixty times an hour, and I have looked at it two hundred thousand times, and I have not finished looking. The king has nobody to rule. The vain man has nobody to admire him. The drunkard drinks to forget that he drinks. Each of them has arranged a life in which nothing ever actually happens. Every minute, something happens to me. It is a small thing. It is the same small thing. But I am there when it happens, which is more than any of them can say.*

*You think the tragedy is that the planet turns too fast. The tragedy would be if it turned so slowly I stopped watching.*

---

I've never been able to decide whether this is consolation or its opposite.

Consolation, because it means a very small life can be full — that the size of the lamp is irrelevant, that presence is available on a planet the size of a house.

Its opposite, because it means the failure mode is invisible. You can execute a life flawlessly and be absent for the whole thing, and no external audit will catch it. There's no alarm. The lamps get lit. The orders are followed. And you find out at the end, if you find out at all, that you weren't there.

The child in the story leaves the lamplighter's planet with regret, and says it's because of the sunsets — one thousand four hundred and forty a day, if you want them. That's the last line of the chapter, and it's usually read as whimsy.

I think it's the argument. He isn't sad about the sunsets he'd have seen. He's sad about the man who sees them and doesn't know that this is what he's been given.

---

Light the lamp. Fine. Follow the orders, keep the schedule, do the work — I'm not arguing against any of that, and I'd be suspicious of anyone who was.

But watch the flame catch. That part isn't in the orders. That part was never in the orders. That part is the only part that's yours.
