## Abstract

Self-checkout has moved from novelty to default in much of the grocery industry, and now it is moving again — this time under scrutiny. Three national chains have publicly reduced or removed self-checkout lanes, at least one state legislature is considering a cap on lanes per attendant, and a scheduled minimum wage increase to $15.75 in January 2027 is forcing every independent operator to re-run the labor math. This paper draws on Bluestem Retail Research's dataset of 2.4 billion transactions from 1,240 independent grocery and convenience stores across eleven states (2021–2025), 340 audited physical inventories, and intercept surveys of 6,800 shoppers to answer a direct question: under what conditions does self-checkout pay, and under what conditions does it quietly cost more than it saves?

The headline findings are stark but not simple. Stores where self-checkout handles more than half of transactions show shrink at 1.68 percent of sales, against 1.02 percent at stores where self-checkout handles under 20 percent — a 66-basis-point gap that, at typical independent-grocery margins, can consume most of the labor savings. Yet labor hours per thousand transactions fall 31 percent at high-penetration stores, and attendant staffing ratios prove decisive: at one attendant per six lanes, shrink runs nearly double the rate observed at one attendant per four. Weighted produce and bulk items account for 44 percent of measured self-checkout loss, meaning the problem is concentrated and addressable rather than diffuse and inevitable.

We compare five deployment approaches — full conversion, hybrid with staffed express lanes, attendant-heavy deployment, exit receipt verification, and phone-based scan-and-go — and model the five-year economics for a representative thirty-store chain. The hybrid model with a one-to-four attendant ratio produces the strongest risk-adjusted return in stores above 12,000 square feet. Below that threshold, the case weakens sharply. We close by addressing the operational objections operators raise most often: labor relations, accessibility, benefit tender handling, alcohol age verification, and small-format viability.

## 1. The Problem: An Industry Re-Litigating a Settled Question

For most of the past decade, the self-checkout question in independent grocery was treated as *when*, not *whether*. The technology promised a straightforward trade: capital expense and modest shrink exposure in exchange for durable labor savings in an industry where labor is the largest controllable cost. Between 2021 and 2025, the share of Bluestem panel stores operating at least four self-checkout lanes rose from 38 percent to 61 percent.

Three developments have reopened the question.

**First, the national chains blinked.** Three major national retailers have publicly scaled back self-checkout — removing lanes from high-shrink locations, capping item counts, or converting stores back to staffed-only front ends. Whatever the internal analysis behind those decisions, the public signal to independents and their lenders was unmistakable: the largest, best-instrumented retailers in the country concluded that at least some of their self-checkout deployments were losing money.

**Second, regulation is arriving.** One state legislature within Bluestem's coverage area is weighing a bill that would cap the number of self-checkout lanes a single attendant may supervise. Whether or not that specific bill passes, it previews a regulatory environment in which attendant ratios may be set by statute rather than by store managers. Operators making seven-year capital commitments today should assume that the one-attendant-per-six-lanes staffing model — common in the panel — may not be legal everywhere by the end of the equipment's life.

**Third, the labor math is shifting again.** A minimum wage step to $15.75 takes effect in January 2027 across a substantial portion of the panel's footprint. Higher labor costs strengthen the case for automation in the abstract. But they also raise the cost of the attendant coverage that, as Section 2 shows, is the single most important variable determining whether self-checkout shrink stays controllable. The wage increase does not simply tilt the scale toward automation; it raises the stakes of getting the deployment model right.

The result is that operators, chain executives, and the lenders financing store remodels are all asking the same question at the same time, often with the same incomplete data. This paper is our attempt to answer it with the largest independent-store transaction dataset available.

### 1.1 What This Paper Does and Does Not Claim

Our data cover independent grocery and convenience stores between roughly 3,000 and 55,000 square feet, in eleven predominantly Midwestern and Plains states. We do not claim these findings generalize to urban supercenters, warehouse clubs, or coastal metro markets with materially different theft and labor dynamics. We also note a structural limitation up front: stores are not randomly assigned to self-checkout penetration levels. High-penetration stores differ from low-penetration stores in size, volume, and demographics. Section 2 describes the matching methodology we use to isolate the self-checkout effect, and Section 2.4 discusses residual confounding honestly. Readers should treat our shrink estimates as strong observational evidence, not experimental proof.

## 2. What the Data Show

### 2.1 The Dataset

Bluestem's analytical base comprises:

- **2.4 billion point-of-sale transactions** from 1,240 stores, January 2021 through December 2025, with lane-level identification distinguishing self-checkout from staffed transactions.
- **340 audited physical inventories** conducted by third-party inventory services, matched to book inventory to produce true shrink figures rather than estimates.
- **Intercept surveys of 6,800 shoppers** conducted in 2024 and 2025 at 74 panel stores, covering checkout preference, error self-reporting, and accessibility experience.

Shrink figures in this paper are total-store shrink expressed as a percentage of sales, from the audited inventories. Where we attribute loss to self-checkout specifically, we rely on lane-level exception data (voided items, weight mismatches, incomplete transactions) validated against audit results and, in 62 stores, against loss prevention case files supplied by participating operators.

### 2.2 The Core Shrink Finding

**Table 1. Shrink and labor metrics by self-checkout transaction penetration (2023–2025 audited stores, n = 340 inventories at 287 stores)**

| Self-checkout share of transactions | Stores (audits) | Shrink, % of sales | Labor hours per 1,000 transactions | Median store size (sq ft) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Under 20% | 96 | 1.02% | 14.6 | 11,400 |
| 20–50% | 118 | 1.31% | 12.1 | 16,800 |
| Over 50% | 73 | 1.68% | 10.1 | 21,300 |

The gradient is monotonic and large. Moving from under-20-percent penetration to over-50-percent penetration is associated with a 66-basis-point increase in total shrink. For a store doing $9 million in annual sales, that is roughly $59,000 a year — before considering the labor savings on the other side of the ledger.

The labor savings are real. High-penetration stores run 31 percent fewer labor hours per thousand transactions than low-penetration stores. At current wages, that difference is worth substantially more than $59,000 at most volumes, which is why so many operators made the investment. The question this paper addresses is whether the shrink cost is a fixed toll or a variable one — and the answer, decisively, is that it is variable.

### 2.3 The Attendant Ratio Is the Hinge Variable

When we segment high-penetration stores by attendant coverage, the shrink gap largely resolves into a staffing story.

**Table 2. Shrink at high-penetration stores (>50% SCO transactions) by attendant-to-lane ratio**

| Attendant ratio | Stores | Shrink, % of sales | SCO intervention rate* |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 attendant : 4 lanes or better | 31 | 1.19% | 8.4 per 100 transactions |
| 1 attendant : 5 lanes | 22 | 1.47% | 6.1 per 100 transactions |
| 1 attendant : 6 lanes or worse | 20 | 2.21% | 3.9 per 100 transactions |

*Interventions include weight-mismatch resolutions, age checks, assistance calls, and attendant-initiated audits.

At one attendant per six lanes, shrink runs nearly double the rate observed at one per four — 2.21 percent against 1.19 percent. The intervention-rate column explains the mechanism: an attendant covering six lanes physically cannot respond to exception flags, so the machines' deterrent and correction functions degrade. Shoppers learn quickly which stores resolve weight mismatches and which stores let them slide.

The practical implication is uncomfortable for operators who justified self-checkout on maximum labor removal: a properly attended self-checkout bank at 1:4 delivers shrink only 17 basis points above the low-penetration baseline while still capturing most of the labor savings. An understaffed bank at 1:6 delivers shrink 119 basis points above baseline and, as Section 4 shows, is frequently a net money-loser.

*Figure 1 caption: Weighted and bulk items — where the shopper selects the price by keying a PLU or product lookup — account for nearly half of measured self-checkout loss. Loss is concentrated where the shopper, not the barcode, determines the price.*

The 44-percent concentration in weighted produce and bulk items is the most actionable finding in this paper. The dominant pattern is not brazen theft; it is the systematic keying of expensive items as cheap ones — organic honeycrisp apples rung as conventional bananas, bulk pistachios rung as oats. Intercept survey data reinforce the point: 19 percent of self-checkout users admitted to at least one knowing mis-key in the prior year, but 31 percent reported *unintentional* errors on weighted items, most commonly PLU confusion. A meaningful share of "theft" is interface failure.

This concentration matters because it is fixable without abandoning the format. Produce recognition cameras, curated PLU screens with photographs, attendant-verified bulk purchases, and simply pre-packing and barcoding high-loss bulk SKUs all attack the 44 percent directly. Stores in our panel that barcoded their top-20 loss-ranked bulk items saw self-checkout exception losses on those items fall by more than half within two inventory cycles.

### 2.5 Selection Effects and Honest Caveats

High-penetration stores are larger, higher-volume, and more often located in markets with elevated baseline theft. When we match stores on size, volume, and market demographics, the shrink gap between high- and low-penetration stores narrows from 66 basis points to approximately 48 — still large, still economically decisive at thin margins, but a reminder that raw comparisons overstate the format's effect. All economic modeling in Section 4 uses the matched-sample estimates.

## 3. Five Deployment Approaches Compared

Operators in the field are not choosing between "self-checkout" and "no self-checkout." They are choosing among at least five distinct models, each with a different risk and cost profile.

### 3.1 Full Conversion

All or nearly all lanes are self-service; staffed checkout exists only as overflow. This model maximizes labor removal and is the configuration most associated with the 1:6-or-worse attendant ratios in Table 2. In our panel it produces the highest shrink, the highest rate of shopper complaints, and — notably for operators worried about regulation — it is precisely the model the pending state legislation targets. We observed no full-conversion store in the audited sample with shrink below 1.5 percent. We do not recommend this model for independent grocery at any format size.

### 3.2 Hybrid with Staffed Express Lanes

A self-checkout bank (typically four to six lanes) handles small-basket transactions, while two or more staffed lanes remain open at all trading hours, including at least one express lane. This is the modal configuration in the panel and, when paired with 1:4 attendant coverage, the best performer on risk-adjusted economics. The staffed lanes absorb the transaction types self-checkout handles worst — large baskets, heavy produce loads, WIC tenders, alcohol-heavy orders — which mechanically reduces self-checkout exception volume. Hybrid stores at 1:4 coverage in our sample averaged 1.16 percent shrink with labor hours 22 percent below staffed-only comparables.

### 3.3 Attendant-Heavy Deployment

Self-checkout with deliberately rich staffing — one attendant per three to four lanes, positioned in the bank rather than at a podium, actively bagging and assisting. This model sacrifices roughly a third of the theoretical labor savings but produces shrink statistically indistinguishable from staffed-only stores (1.08 percent in our sample). It is the right answer for high-theft locations and for operators whose primary concern is customer experience. It also happens to be compliant with any plausible version of the attendant-ratio legislation.

### 3.4 Exit Receipt Verification

Retaining self-checkout but adding receipt checks at the exit, either universally or on an exception-triggered basis. Our data on this model come from 41 stores, and the results are mixed. Exception-triggered verification (checking only flagged transactions) reduced measured self-checkout loss by roughly 20 percent with minimal shopper friction. Universal receipt checking reduced loss further but generated the highest complaint rates of any intervention we measured, and intercept surveys showed a 9-point drop in "likely to return" scores at universal-check stores. There is also legal exposure: detaining shoppers without individualized suspicion carries risk that varies by state, and independents lack the legal departments national chains use to manage it. We view exit verification as a supplement for high-loss stores, not a primary strategy.

### 3.5 Scan-and-Go on the Shopper's Phone

App-based scanning with payment on the device and, typically, randomized audit at exit. Only 28 panel stores run scan-and-go at meaningful volume, so our conclusions are provisional. The model shows the lowest capital cost (no lane hardware) and, counterintuitively, moderate loss rates — the audit lottery plus the identified, account-linked nature of every transaction appears to deter casual mis-keying. Its limitation is adoption: scan-and-go averaged just 6 percent of transactions even at mature deployments, concentrated among younger, higher-income shoppers. It is a complement to a front-end strategy, not a front-end strategy.

**Table 3. Deployment approach summary**

| Approach | Capital cost per store | Labor savings vs. staffed | Observed shrink | Regulatory exposure | Recommended use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full conversion | High ($200K+) | 30–35% | 1.5–2.2%+ | High | Not recommended |
| Hybrid, 1:4 attended | Moderate ($136–170K) | ~22% | ~1.16% | Low | Default for >12,000 sq ft |
| Attendant-heavy | Moderate | ~15% | ~1.08% | Minimal | High-theft locations |
| Exit verification | Low add-on | n/a | −20% loss (exception-based) | Moderate (legal) | Supplement only |
| Scan-and-go | Low | Minimal at current adoption | Low per transaction | Low | Complement only |

## 4. The Economics: A Thirty-Store Model

To make the trade-offs concrete, we model a representative thirty-store independent chain with the following assumptions, drawn from panel medians:

- Average store: 16,000 square feet, $8.8 million annual sales, 6,200 transactions per week.
- Baseline (staffed-only) shrink: 1.02 percent of sales.
- Front-end wage: $14.25 loaded today, stepping to $17.35 loaded in January 2027 (reflecting the $15.75 minimum plus taxes and benefits).
- Self-checkout lane: $34,000 installed, seven-year straight-line depreciation, $2,100 annual maintenance and license per lane.
- Reversion cost (removing a lane and restoring staffed capacity): $19,000 per lane.
- Five-lane self-checkout bank per store; matched-sample shrink estimates from Section 2.5.

### 4.1 Three Scenarios

**Scenario A — Hybrid, 1:4 attended (recommended).** Five SCO lanes, two staffed lanes at all hours, dedicated attendant coverage at 1:4 during all open hours.

**Scenario B — Full conversion, 1:6 attended.** Five SCO lanes, one staffed lane at peak only, thin attendant coverage.

**Scenario C — Staffed-only (status quo / reversion benchmark).**

**Table 4. Five-year per-store economics, 2027–2031 (post-wage-step dollars)**

| Line item (annual, per store) | A: Hybrid 1:4 | B: Full conv. 1:6 | C: Staffed only |
|---|---|---|---|
| Front-end labor cost | $318,000 | $264,000 | $407,000 |
| Labor savings vs. staffed | $89,000 | $143,000 | — |
| Depreciation + maintenance (5 lanes) | $34,800 | $34,800 | — |
| Shrink at scenario rate | 1.16% → $102,100 | 1.92% → $169,000 | 1.02% → $89,800 |
| Incremental shrink vs. staffed | $12,300 | $79,200 | — |
| **Net annual benefit vs. staffed** | **$41,900** | **$29,000** | **—** |
| Five-year net, per store | $209,500 | $145,000 | — |
| Five-year net, 30 stores | **$6.29M** | $4.35M | — |

Several features of Table 4 deserve emphasis.

**First, full conversion's larger labor savings are an illusion at the shrink rates it actually produces.** Scenario B removes $54,000 more labor per store per year than Scenario A but gives back $66,900 of it in incremental shrink — and Table 4 uses the matched-sample shrink estimate. At the raw observed rate of 2.21 percent for 1:6 stores, Scenario B's net annual benefit falls to approximately $3,500 per store, effectively zero, and turns negative in any store with above-average theft exposure. The chains that publicly retreated from self-checkout were, in our assessment, retreating from Scenario B, not from self-checkout as such.

**Second, the 2027 wage step strengthens Scenario A more than it strengthens Scenario B.** The wage increase raises the value of every labor hour removed — but it raises the cost of attendant coverage identically, and Scenario A's advantage is that its shrink control does not depend on removing the last attendant. Re-running the model at the pre-step wage, Scenario A's net annual benefit is $33,600; the wage step adds roughly $8,300 per store per year to the case for the hybrid model.

**Third, regulatory risk is asymmetric.** If attendant-ratio legislation passes at 1:4 or 1:5, Scenario A requires no change. Scenario B requires either additional attendant labor (eliminating most of its remaining advantage) or lane removal at $19,000 per lane. A thirty-store chain running Scenario B faces a contingent liability of roughly $570,000 to $1.7 million depending on how many lanes must come out. Lenders underwriting remodels should price this.

**Fourth, reversion is affordable but not free.** At $19,000 per lane, a chain that installs five lanes per store and later removes two per store spends $1.14 million across thirty stores to unwind a partial mistake. This is survivable; it is also two-thirds of a year's projected benefit under Scenario A. The cheapest reversion is the one you never need, which argues for piloting configurations in three to five stores for two full inventory cycles before chain-wide rollout.

### 4.2 Sensitivity: Where the Model Breaks

The hybrid model's net benefit goes to zero under any of the following conditions, holding the others at baseline:

- Store shrink under the hybrid model exceeds approximately **1.6 percent** (i.e., attendant discipline fails in practice).
- Weekly transactions fall below approximately **3,400**, at which point fixed lane costs and mandatory attendant coverage overwhelm the labor hours actually removed.
- Self-checkout adoption stalls below roughly **25 percent of transactions**, leaving staffed lanes fully staffed anyway.

The transaction-volume threshold is the empirical basis for our small-format guidance in Section 5.5.

## 5. Answering the Operator Objections

### 5.1 "This is a labor relations problem waiting to happen."

It can be, and the determining factor is whether self-checkout is framed — and genuinely operated — as redeployment or as headcount reduction. In our panel, stores that converted front-end hours into service-department, e-commerce fulfillment, and stocking hours reported no measurable increase in turnover following installation. Stores that executed straight headcount cuts saw front-end turnover rise 14 points in the following year, and turnover is expensive: panel operators report replacement costs of $2,800 to $4,100 per front-end associate. The attendant role itself matters here. A well-defined attendant position — with training in exception handling, age verification, and customer assistance — is a more skilled and typically better-paid job than traditional cashiering. Chains facing the 2027 wage step should note that they will be paying $15.75 regardless; the question is whether that wage buys a cashier or a trained attendant supervising four lanes. Fiona Brannigan's 62-store chain converted 80 percent of displaced cashier hours into attendant and fresh-department hours during its 2023–2024 rollout and recorded its lowest front-end turnover in six years in 2025.

### 5.2 "What about shoppers who can't use the machines?"

Accessibility is not an edge case. In our intercept surveys, 12 percent of shoppers reported difficulty using self-checkout due to vision, mobility, dexterity, or cognitive factors, and another 8 percent reported language-related difficulty with the interfaces. Two responses are non-negotiable. First, at least one staffed lane must be open at all trading hours — which the hybrid model provides by design and full conversion does not. Second, attendants must be trained and positioned to assist proactively, not merely to clear exception flags. ADA exposure is real for kiosk-only configurations; several national retailers have faced litigation over inaccessible self-checkout interfaces, and independents should specify accessible hardware (lowered scanners, tactile keypads, audio guidance, screen-reader compatibility) in any new purchase. The accessibility argument is, in practice, one more argument for the hybrid model over full conversion.

### 5.3 "WIC and SNAP transactions don't work at self-checkout."

Partially true, and consequential in many independent trade areas. SNAP EBT processes at self-checkout without difficulty in current-generation systems, and in our panel SNAP tenders appear at self-checkout at rates proportional to their overall share. WIC is different: item-level eligibility verification, mixed-tender splitting, and state-by-state eWIC rule variation make WIC transactions slow and error-prone at self-checkout, and several state WIC agencies have discouraged or restricted it. Stores with WIC shares above 8 percent of transactions should route WIC to staffed lanes explicitly — with signage that is factual rather than stigmatizing ("WIC transactions are fastest at lanes 1–2") — and staff accordingly. This is another structural argument for retaining staffed lanes: in high-WIC trade areas, full conversion is operationally unworkable, full stop.

### 5.4 "Alcohol age checks bottleneck the whole bank."

Age-restricted items are the single largest source of attendant interventions after weight mismatches — 2.1 interventions per 100 self-checkout transactions in stores with full alcohol assortments. At 1:6 attendant coverage, age-check delays averaged 74 seconds per occurrence in our observational sample; at 1:4, they averaged 28 seconds. Options in the field include routing all alcohol to staffed lanes (clean but forfeits convenience on a high-margin category), attendant-carried mobile approval devices (the best performer in our panel, cutting delay roughly in half), and emerging ID-scanning hardware at the kiosk (promising, but state alcohol regulators differ on whether it satisfies visual-inspection requirements, and operators must verify with their own state authority before relying on it). The compliance stakes are not symmetric with the convenience stakes: a failed sting can cost a license. Our recommendation is attendant-verified approval with mobile devices at 1:4 coverage, and staffed-lane routing during any hour when coverage falls below that.

### 5.5 "Does any of this work below 12,000 square feet?"

Usually not, in the configurations discussed above — and the reason is arithmetic, not technology. Small-format stores in our panel (under 12,000 square feet) average under 3,800 weekly transactions, close to the 3,400-transaction breakeven from Section 4.2. More fundamentally, a small store's staffed front end is often a single cashier who also stocks, faces, and answers the phone. Self-checkout cannot remove that person; someone must remain in the store. It can only *add* capital cost and shrink exposure on top of an irreducible labor floor. The exception is the convenience-format variant: one or two compact self-checkout units positioned as queue relief beside a single staffed register, with the cashier doubling as attendant. In the 47 small-format panel stores running this configuration, the units paid back in 3.5 to 5 years on queue-abandonment recapture rather than labor savings — a real but modest return that depends on peak-hour congestion actually existing. Small-format operators without a demonstrated peak-queue problem should keep their capital.

## 6. Conclusion and Recommendations

The self-checkout question, properly framed, is not "keep it or kill it." Our data support five conclusions:

**1. Self-checkout shrink is real, large, and — critically — variable.** The 66-basis-point raw gap (48 matched) between high- and low-penetration stores is an average across well-run and badly run deployments. The badly run ones drive it.

**2. The attendant ratio is the single most important operating decision.** At 1:4, self-checkout shrink is nearly indistinguishable from staffed baselines; at 1:6, it roughly doubles and frequently erases the format's entire economic case. Operators who cannot commit to 1:4 coverage during all operating hours should reduce lane counts until they can.

**3. Attack the 44 percent.** Weighted produce and bulk items are where the money leaves. Produce recognition, photographic PLU screens, barcoding high-loss bulk SKUs, and attendant verification of bulk purchases are cheap relative to the loss they prevent.

**4. The hybrid model at 1:4 is the default recommendation for stores above 12,000 square feet**, delivering roughly $42,000 in net annual benefit per store post-2027 in our thirty-store model, with low regulatory exposure and manageable accessibility and tender-handling profiles. Full conversion is not recommended at any size. Below 12,000 square feet, deploy compact units only against a demonstrated peak-queue problem, or not at all.

**5. Pilot before you commit, and price the exit.** Two full inventory cycles in three to five stores will reveal more than any consultant's projection, including this one. At $19,000 per lane, reversion is affordable — but a chain that pilots first rarely needs it.

The national chains' retreat from self-checkout is best read not as a verdict on the technology but as a verdict on a specific deployment model: maximum lanes, minimum attendants, and the assumption that shoppers would police themselves. They will not. The independents who prosper with self-checkout through the coming wage step and the coming regulation will be the ones who treat the attendant not as a cost to be engineered out, but as the component that makes the whole machine pay.

## Appendix A. Methodology Notes

### A.1 Panel Construction and Store Matching

The 1,240-store transaction panel is drawn from Bluestem's client base and is therefore not a random sample of independent grocery; it skews toward operators sophisticated enough to purchase transaction analytics. We believe this biases our shrink findings *downward* relative to the industry — panel stores are, on average, better managed than non-panel comparables — and readers should treat our loss figures as conservative.

For the matched-sample analysis referenced in Section 2.5, we paired each high-penetration store (over 50 percent SCO transactions) with the nearest available low-penetration store on four covariates: selling square footage (±15 percent), weekly transaction count (±20 percent), trade-area median household income (±$8,000, from census tract data), and a three-tier local theft-exposure index built from operator-reported incident data. Sixty-one of 73 high-penetration stores found acceptable matches; the 12 unmatched stores were disproportionately large-format and high-volume, and their exclusion narrows but does not eliminate the shrink gap. The matched estimate of 48 basis points carries a 90 percent confidence interval of 31 to 64 basis points.

### A.2 Shrink Attribution

Total-store shrink from audited inventories cannot, by itself, be attributed to the front end. Our attribution method triangulates three sources: (1) lane-level exception logs, priced at retail and aggregated by category; (2) category-level shrink deltas between high- and low-penetration matched pairs, on the logic that back-door and vendor loss should not vary systematically with front-end configuration; and (3) loss prevention case files at the 62 stores whose operators supplied them. The three methods agree within ±6 percentage points on the category composition shown in Figure 1. They agree less well on absolute magnitude — exception logs capture only detected anomalies, and we estimate they observe between 40 and 60 percent of true self-checkout loss. All dollar figures in Section 4 use the audit-derived totals, not the exception-log totals, precisely because the logs undercount.

### A.3 Labor Measurement

Labor hours per thousand transactions are computed from scheduling-system exports at the 412 panel stores that share labor data, restricted to front-end job codes (cashier, attendant, courtesy, front-end supervision). Hours redeployed to other departments are excluded from front-end totals but tracked separately for the redeployment analysis in Section 5.1. The 31 percent figure in Table 1 is a median, not a mean; the interquartile range runs from 24 to 37 percent.

### A.4 Limitations

Beyond the selection issues already discussed: (1) the 2021–2022 portion of the panel reflects pandemic-era shopping patterns, and we weight 2023–2025 data more heavily in all shrink models; (2) intercept surveys of self-reported theft and error behave as self-reports do, and the 19 percent knowing-mis-key figure is best read as a floor; (3) our scan-and-go sample of 28 stores is too small for the confidence we can offer elsewhere, which is why Section 3.5 is framed as provisional; and (4) the reversion cost of $19,000 per lane is a median of 14 observed reversions and varies from $11,000 to $31,000 depending on electrical work and whether the operator restores a full staffed lane or converts to merchandising space.

## Appendix B. Implementation Checklist for the Hybrid 1:4 Model

Operators adopting the Section 3.2 configuration should be able to answer yes to every item below before go-live. In our panel, stores that launched with all fourteen items in place reached target adoption (35 percent of transactions or better) a median of eleven weeks faster than stores that did not.

**Configuration and staffing**

1. Attendant coverage scheduled at 1:4 or better for every open hour, including early morning and closing shifts — not just daytime peaks.
2. At least one staffed lane, including an express option, open at all trading hours without exception.
3. Attendant position formally defined with a written job description, differentiated pay grade, and a certification checklist covering exceptions, age verification, tender handling, and accessibility assistance.
4. Attendant physically stationed inside the bank, not behind a podium, with a mobile approval device for age checks.

**Loss controls targeting the 44 percent**

5. Top-20 loss-ranked bulk SKUs pre-packed and barcoded, or removed from self-checkout eligibility.
6. PLU lookup screens configured with photographs, organic/conventional pairs displayed side by side, and the store's actual top-selling produce on the first screen.
7. Weight-mismatch tolerance set to the vendor's tight profile, with attendant response time to flags tracked weekly.
8. Produce recognition cameras enabled where hardware supports them, with the override log reviewed by the store manager weekly.

**Tender and compliance**

9. WIC routing decision made explicitly and signed off by the operator's WIC state agency contact; signage worded factually.
10. Age-restricted approval workflow verified against the state alcohol authority's current guidance in writing, not by vendor assurance.
11. At least one lane in the bank meeting accessible-hardware specifications, with attendants trained on proactive assistance.

**Measurement and governance**

12. Baseline inventory audited within 60 days before go-live, with follow-up audits scheduled at each of the next two cycles.
13. Weekly dashboard tracking SCO transaction share, intervention rate per 100 transactions, exception dollars by category, and attendant coverage attainment against the 1:4 standard.
14. Pre-agreed trigger points documented: the shrink level (we suggest 1.6 percent, per Section 4.2) and adoption floor (25 percent) at which the operator will change configuration rather than wait another cycle.

## Appendix D. Questions for Lenders Underwriting Front-End Remodels

Lenders financing self-checkout installations across the panel's footprint asked Bluestem for a short diligence framework. We suggest requiring the following from any borrower's remodel proposal:

1. **The attendant plan, in writing.** What ratio is scheduled, at what hours, and what happens to it when someone calls in sick? A capital plan without a staffing plan is the profile of every failed deployment in our data.
2. **Audited baseline shrink** within the past twelve months, not book-inventory estimates, for every store in the proposal.
3. **The pilot evidence.** For chains of ten stores or more, results from at least two inventory cycles at pilot stores before chain-wide draw-down. Structure the facility in tranches tied to pilot shrink outcomes if necessary.
4. **The regulatory contingency.** How does the borrower's model perform if a 1:4 or 1:5 attendant ratio becomes law? For hybrid proposals the answer is "no change"; for full-conversion proposals, the answer should be priced into the deal, including reversion costs at $19,000 per lane.
5. **The 2027 wage assumption.** Confirm the pro forma uses post-step loaded wages on both sides of the ledger — the labor removed *and* the attendant labor retained. Proposals that apply the wage increase only to the savings line overstate returns by 15 to 20 percent in our review of submitted models.
6. **The small-format screen.** Any store under 12,000 square feet or under 3,400 weekly transactions in the proposal should carry a store-specific justification, per Section 5.5, or be excluded from the equipment budget.

A borrower who can answer all six is, in our experience, a borrower whose front-end capital will perform. A borrower who cannot answer the first is asking the lender to finance Scenario B.
