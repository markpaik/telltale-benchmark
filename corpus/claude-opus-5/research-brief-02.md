# Did Medically Tailored Meals Reduce Hospital Use? Twelve-Month Utilization Findings from the Riverbend Community Table Meal Program

**A research brief prepared for the Ardenwald Health Foundation**

Prepared by Adaeze Okoro, Evaluation Manager, with Tomas Villalobos-Reyes, Graduate Evaluation Fellow
At the direction of Harriet Lindqvist-Moore, Executive Director
Riverbend Community Table, Cincinnati, Ohio
July 29, 2026

---

## The Question and What We Found

In April, program officer Renata Kowal asked Riverbend a direct question in advance of the foundation's September renewal decision: **did the medically tailored meal program reduce hospital use among the adults it served?** The program, launched in March 2024 with a $3.8 million three-year grant, delivers twenty-six weeks of medically tailored meals to adults in Hamilton County living with congestive heart failure or type 2 diabetes, alongside a dietitian assessment at intake and two check-in calls. The foundation is deciding whether to renew, at what level, and with what conditions attached.

This brief answers the question as far as our data allow, and is explicit about where they stop.

**What we found.** Among the 1,412 program participants whose hospital encounters we can observe, inpatient admissions in the twelve months after enrollment averaged **0.71 per person**, compared with **0.94** among 2,640 matched pantry clients with the same diagnoses who were referred to the program but never enrolled. The absolute difference is 0.23 fewer admissions per person-year (95% CI −0.31 to −0.15), a relative reduction of about 24 percent.

That headline number is real, but it is not evenly distributed, and it is not a causal estimate.

The difference is concentrated almost entirely among participants who actually received the meals. Participants who completed all twenty-six weeks of deliveries averaged 0.52 admissions; those who stopped before week ten — 38 percent of the cohort — averaged 0.98, statistically indistinguishable from the comparison group. The difference is also concentrated among younger participants: adults over seventy showed almost no separation from their comparison counterparts (0.88 versus 0.91), while adults under seventy showed a gap of 0.34 admissions. And it is concentrated among people living alone, whose estimated difference (−0.31) is roughly twice that observed among participants in households of three or more (−0.15).

**Three things constrain how far this can be pushed.** First, we observe admissions only at the partner health system's four hospitals; the county's two other acute-care hospitals are invisible to us, and we cannot directly measure how much of each group's utilization we are missing. Second, participants chose whether to enroll and whether to continue, so the dose–response pattern that looks most persuasive is also the pattern most vulnerable to reverse causation — people stop receiving meals *because* they are hospitalized. Third, a refrigerated van failure halted deliveries to two zip codes for eleven weeks in early 2025, affecting 187 participants and creating a block of "discontinuations" that reflect our logistics rather than participant behavior.

**Our judgment.** The evidence is consistent with a meaningful reduction in inpatient utilization among adults who receive a full course of meals, and it is more than the program had a year ago. It is not strong enough to support a claim that the program *causes* a 24 percent reduction in admissions, and any renewal materials that make that claim will not survive scrutiny. The more defensible reading is that the program appears to work when it is delivered completely, that it is currently delivered completely to fewer than half of enrollees, and that the largest available gain lies in retention rather than in enrollment volume. We recommend that renewal be framed around fixing delivery completion, closing the data gaps, and building a design capable of answering the causal question by the end of a second grant term.

## Background: The Program and the Grant

Riverbend Community Table operates a twelve-site pantry network serving roughly 41,000 individuals annually across Hamilton County and the near-river communities of northern Kentucky. The medically tailored meal program was a departure from that model: rather than distributing shelf-stable and fresh groceries to whoever arrives, it identifies a clinically defined population, enrolls them individually, and delivers a prescribed food intervention on a fixed schedule.

Program design has been stable since launch. Eligible adults are eighteen or older, live in the service area, carry a documented diagnosis of congestive heart failure or type 2 diabetes, and screen positive for food insecurity on the two-item Hunger Vital Sign. Enrolled participants receive ten prepared meals per week — sodium- and carbohydrate-controlled according to a dietitian-set protocol — plus a weekly produce supplement, delivered to the home by refrigerated van. The course runs twenty-six weeks. A registered dietitian conducts an intake assessment and two follow-up calls at weeks four and thirteen. All-in cost per completed course, including food production, delivery, dietitian time, and allocated overhead, is approximately $1,180.

Referrals arrive from three channels. Fifty-eight percent come from the partner health system's cardiology, endocrinology, and primary care clinics; 29 percent from Riverbend pantry site staff using an in-house screening protocol; and 13 percent from self-referral, community health workers, or other community organizations.

As of June 30, 2026, 2,341 adults have ever enrolled. The program's current active census is 494. The evaluation cohort described in this brief is restricted to the 1,903 adults who enrolled on or before March 31, 2025, since only these individuals have a complete twelve-month follow-up window within the encounter data available to us.

## Data and Methods

### Cohort definition and the follow-up window

The analytic window for each participant begins on the date of first delivery, not the date of enrollment. The two differ by a median of nine days and by more than thirty days for 7 percent of enrollees, generally where address verification or a scheduling conflict delayed the first drop. Using first delivery as the index date is the more conservative choice: it excludes from follow-up any hospitalization that occurred between enrollment and the first meal, a period during which the program cannot plausibly have had an effect but during which enrollees are, on average, at elevated risk.

Follow-up runs 365 days from the index date. Encounter data from the partner health system are current through May 31, 2026, and the system's own completeness audit indicates that inpatient records are substantially complete at sixty days after discharge. We therefore treat March 31, 2025 as the last enrollment date with reliable full follow-up.

### Linkage and who is missing from it

Utilization data are obtained under a data-use agreement executed in January 2024 with the partner health system, which permits Riverbend to receive a limited encounter data set — admission and discharge dates, facility, admission type, primary diagnosis category, and discharge disposition — for individuals who have signed a linkage authorization at program intake.

Of the 1,903 follow-up-eligible enrollees, **1,412 (74.2 percent) are linked**. The 491 unlinked enrollees fall into two groups. Roughly two-thirds (326) declined the linkage authorization at intake. The remaining 165 authorized linkage but produced no match in the partner system's master patient index, which in nearly all cases means they receive their care elsewhere — at the county's other hospital system, at the federally qualified health centers that do not share an index with the partner, or across the river in Kentucky.

The unlinked are not a random subset. Compared with the linked cohort, they are more likely to have come through a pantry-staff referral rather than a clinic referral (47 percent versus 24 percent), more likely to be under fifty (22 percent versus 14 percent), and more likely to live in the two westernmost zip codes in the service area, where the competitor system operates the nearest hospital. They are also somewhat more likely to have stopped deliveries early (43 percent versus 38 percent). We return to what this implies in the Limitations section; the short version is that our results describe a cohort tilted toward people already connected to the partner health system, and that this tilt is not incidental to the outcome we are measuring.

### Constructing the comparison group

There is no randomization in this program. Everyone who was eligible and wanted meals could have them, subject to capacity, and capacity constraints during the period studied were episodic rather than systematic. The comparison group is therefore constructed, not assigned, and its construction is the single most consequential methodological choice in this brief.

We began with the referral lists themselves. Between March 2024 and March 2025, 4,118 adults were referred to the meal program through the three channels described above, met the diagnostic and geographic criteria, and did not enroll. This is the right starting pool: these are people whom a clinician or a pantry screener identified as appropriate for the program, which controls — imperfectly but meaningfully — for the judgment call that precedes enrollment. Comparing meal recipients to the general pantry population would have produced a much larger and much less believable difference.

Of those 4,118, 3,057 had a linkage authorization on file through Riverbend's general intake consent and produced a match in the partner system's index. From these we selected 2,640 who met matching criteria.

Matching was exact on three dimensions and coarsened on two others:

- **Exact:** primary qualifying diagnosis (CHF, type 2 diabetes, or both); referral channel (clinic, pantry, community/self); and calendar quarter of referral, which serves as the index-date anchor.
- **Coarsened:** five-year age band, and a three-level band of baseline-year inpatient admissions (0, 1, 2 or more).

Sex, household size, and zip-code cluster were balanced by weighting rather than matching, since exact matching on all dimensions exhausted the available pool for several strata.

Comparison-group index dates are set to the referral date plus nine days, the median enrollment-to-first-delivery interval among participants. This preserves seasonal alignment, which matters: heart failure admissions in this county peak in January and February, and any comparison that let index dates drift across seasons would introduce a spurious difference.

**What we could not match on.** We have no clinical severity data. Ejection fraction, NYHA functional class, HbA1c, medication regimens, renal function, and comorbidity burden are all absent from the limited data set our agreement permits. We also have no direct measure of social support, functional status, transportation access, or housing stability. These are precisely the variables that plausibly drive both the decision to enroll in a meal program and the risk of hospitalization. Their absence is not a footnote; it is the reason this brief cannot report a causal estimate.

### Outcome definitions

The primary outcome is **inpatient admissions per person in the twelve months following the index date**, counting each acute inpatient stay at any of the partner system's four hospitals. Observation stays are excluded from the primary count and reported separately. Transfers between partner facilities within twenty-four hours are collapsed into a single admission.

Secondary outcomes are emergency department visits not resulting in admission, thirty-day all-cause readmission among those with at least one index admission, and total inpatient days.

Because 63 participants and 96 comparison-group members died during follow-up, and death truncates the opportunity to be admitted, we computed all rates two ways: per person over the full 365-day window (the "per-person" rate, which treats decedents as having a full window) and per person-year of observation, censoring at death. The two produce nearly identical estimates — the per-person-year participant rate is 0.73 against a comparison rate of 0.97, a difference of 0.24 rather than 0.23 — and we report per-person figures throughout for readability, noting that the substantive conclusions do not depend on the choice.

### Analytic approach

Our primary analysis is a simple comparison of adjusted means, estimated from a negative binomial regression with the participant indicator as the exposure of interest and covariates for age band, sex, qualifying diagnosis, referral channel, baseline admissions band, household size band, and zip-code cluster. Confidence intervals are computed with robust standard errors clustered at the household level, because 218 participants and 341 comparison members share an address with at least one other person in the study.

We also report a difference-in-differences estimate using each individual's baseline-year admissions, and we report a set of pre-specified sensitivity analyses described in the Results.

We did not conduct formal mediation analysis, cost-effectiveness modeling, or instrumental-variable estimation. We considered an instrumental-variable approach using distance from the delivery hub as an instrument for enrollment, and rejected it: distance is not plausibly excludable, since it correlates with the neighborhood factors that drive both hospital access and food access.

### A note on multiplicity

This brief reports one primary comparison and eleven subgroup or secondary comparisons. We have not adjusted p-values for multiplicity, and readers should treat the subgroup estimates as descriptive rather than confirmatory. The age and household-size splits were pre-specified in the evaluation plan filed with the foundation in June 2024; the dose analysis was not, and was added in February 2026 after program staff observed the retention pattern.

## Results

### Overall utilization

In the twelve months following the index date, the 1,412 linked participants averaged 0.71 inpatient admissions, against 0.94 among the 2,640 comparison-group members. The adjusted difference is −0.23 admissions per person (95% CI −0.31 to −0.15).

The distribution matters as much as the mean. Fifty-four percent of participants had no observed admission at all, compared with 47 percent of comparison members. At the other tail, 9 percent of participants had three or more admissions, compared with 14 percent of comparison members. The reduction is thus not a uniform downward shift; it comes disproportionately from fewer people in the high-utilization tail, which is where inpatient cost concentrates.

Secondary outcomes move in the same direction with weaker signal. Emergency department visits not resulting in admission averaged 1.36 among participants and 1.51 in the comparison group (difference −0.15, 95% CI −0.29 to −0.01). Among those with at least one admission, thirty-day all-cause readmission occurred for 18.2 percent of participants and 22.7 percent of comparison members (difference −4.5 percentage points, 95% CI −9.8 to +0.8). Total inpatient days averaged 3.4 versus 4.6.

### Baseline utilization and the difference-in-differences problem

Participants entered the program with higher baseline utilization than the comparison group despite our coarsened matching on that dimension: 1.04 admissions in the year before the index date, against 0.89. The coarsened bands (0, 1, 2+) permitted substantial residual imbalance within the top band, where participants averaged 2.9 baseline admissions and comparison members 2.4.

Running the comparison as a difference-in-differences produces a *larger* apparent effect. Participants fell from 1.04 to 0.71, a change of −0.33; the comparison group rose from 0.89 to 0.94, a change of +0.05. The difference-in-differences estimate is therefore −0.38 admissions.

**We do not recommend using that number.** The participant group's higher baseline is exactly the condition under which regression to the mean inflates a difference-in-differences estimate. People are often referred to a meal program in the weeks after a hospitalization; that hospitalization is in their baseline window; and their subsequent year would show fewer admissions whether or not they received meals. The comparison group, drawn from the same referral lists, is subject to the same dynamic, but to a lesser degree given its lower baseline. The honest reading is that the raw difference (−0.23) is probably somewhat conservative relative to a fully adjusted estimate and the difference-in-differences (−0.38) is almost certainly too large, and that the true value, if the groups were otherwise comparable, sits somewhere between. We use the raw adjusted difference throughout.

### Results by delivery dose

The clearest pattern in the data is the gradient by how many weeks of meals a participant actually received.

| Delivery dose | Participants | Admissions per person | Difference vs. comparison (0.94) | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Completed all 26 weeks | 579 (41%) | 0.52 | −0.42 | −0.52 to −0.32 |
| Stopped weeks 10–25 | 296 (21%) | 0.59 | −0.35 | −0.48 to −0.21 |
| Stopped before week 10 | 537 (38%) | 0.98 | +0.04 | −0.09 to +0.17 |
| **All linked participants** | **1,412** | **0.71** | **−0.23** | **−0.31 to −0.15** |

Read at face value, this is the most persuasive result in the brief: the more meals people received, the fewer admissions they had, and those who received almost none looked exactly like people who received none at all. Read carefully, it is also the result most likely to be an artifact.

The problem is that dose is not assigned. Riverbend's discontinuation logs record a reason for 61 percent of the 833 participants who stopped early. Among those with a recorded reason, 19 percent stopped because they were hospitalized or entered a skilled nursing facility. That is direct reverse causation: the hospitalization causes the low dose, not the other way around. Another 24 percent moved or became unreachable, 16 percent reported disliking the food, 11 percent said they no longer needed the service, 8 percent cited access or scheduling problems, and 22 percent gave other or unclassifiable reasons.

We ran a partial correction. Excluding all early-stoppers whose first discontinuation followed within fourteen days of an observed admission (n = 118) raises the early-stop group's rate from 0.98 to 0.84 and lowers the difference for that group to −0.10 (95% CI −0.25 to +0.05). The gradient survives but flattens materially. We would not describe the remaining gradient as clean; it simply means reverse causation does not explain all of it.

A second correction is worth stating. If we assign every early-stopper the twelve-month outcome regardless of when they stopped — which we do, and which is the correct intention-to-treat handling — then the overall 0.71 figure is the honest program-level number, and 0.52 is not. The foundation should understand that 0.52 describes what happens among people who complete, and that completing is itself a marker of stability, mobility, and probably health.

**Figure 1 (described).** A cumulative-incidence plot would make this legible. On a horizontal axis of days since index (0 to 365) and a vertical axis of cumulative admissions per person (0 to 1.1), four curves are plotted: the comparison group, and the three dose strata. All four rise steeply in the first sixty days and then diverge. The completed-course curve flattens noticeably after roughly day 90 and ends at 0.52. The early-stop curve tracks the comparison curve closely throughout, briefly exceeding it between days 30 and 120 — the interval in which hospitalization-driven discontinuations cluster — before converging to 0.98. The visual point is that the early-stop group's excess is front-loaded, which is what reverse causation looks like.

### Results by age

| Age at index | Participants (rate) | Comparison (rate) | Difference | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Under 70 | 932 (0.62) | 1,637 (0.96) | −0.34 | −0.44 to −0.24 |
| 70 and over | 480 (0.88) | 1,003 (0.91) | −0.03 | −0.18 to +0.11 |

Among participants aged seventy and over, the program shows essentially no association with inpatient utilization. The confidence interval is wide enough to accommodate a modest benefit, but it also comfortably accommodates zero and a small harm.

We do not know why. Three explanations are consistent with what we can see, and we cannot distinguish among them.

The first is clinical. Older participants in this cohort have higher baseline admission rates (1.19 versus 0.96 for those under seventy) and, in the CHF subgroup, a higher share of admissions coded to acute decompensation with renal involvement. It is plausible that dietary sodium and carbohydrate management has less marginal effect on admission risk in a population whose admissions are driven by advanced disease progression and polypharmacy.

The second is behavioral. Dietitian call notes, which we reviewed qualitatively for a sample of 80 participants, disproportionately record concerns among older participants about meal portion size, texture, and unfamiliarity — and, in eleven cases, that meals were being shared with or given away to family members. If a meaningful share of delivered meals are not eaten by the intended recipient, effective dose is lower than delivered dose, and we cannot measure it.

The third is measurement. Adults over seventy in this county are overwhelmingly Medicare-enrolled and have longer-standing relationships with specific health systems. If older participants and older comparison members are both well captured in the partner system data, while younger people in both groups scatter across facilities more, then the older stratum is measured more accurately — and the smaller apparent effect could reflect the absence of noise rather than the absence of benefit. We regard this as the least likely of the three but cannot rule it out.

### Results by household size

| Household size | Participants (rate) | Comparison (rate) | Difference | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lives alone | 621 (0.65) | 1,082 (0.96) | −0.31 | −0.43 to −0.19 |
| Two-person household | 466 (0.76) | 898 (0.94) | −0.18 | −0.32 to −0.04 |
| Three or more | 325 (0.75) | 660 (0.90) | −0.15 | −0.32 to +0.02 |

The gradient is monotone from living alone to two-person households and then flattens. The estimated difference among people living alone is roughly twice that among people in households of three or more, and the estimate for the largest households is not distinguishable from zero.

This is the subgroup result we find most substantively interpretable, and it is consistent with the program's own theory of change and with the meal-sharing observation above. A person living alone who receives ten prepared meals a week has most of their food problem solved. A person in a household of four who receives ten prepared meals a week has received roughly one-sixth of the household's weekly food need, in a form that is not designed for the household and that competes with the household's shared cooking. Under those conditions, meals are likely to be redistributed, and the therapeutic specificity — the sodium ceiling, the carbohydrate distribution — is lost.

It is also possible that the pattern reflects confounding in the opposite direction: people living alone may have fewer informal supports, and the comparison group of people living alone may therefore be at genuinely higher risk than the comparison group in larger households, inflating the apparent effect. Comparison-group rates by household size (0.96, 0.94, 0.90) offer weak support for this. We report both readings.

### The delivery interruption

Between January 14 and April 1, 2025, deliveries to zip codes 45204 and 45232 stopped for eleven consecutive weeks after the failure of one of the program's two refrigerated vans and a procurement delay in securing a replacement. Riverbend substituted shelf-stable food boxes for four of those eleven weeks; for the remaining seven weeks, affected participants received nothing from the program.

One hundred eighty-seven linked participants lived in the affected zip codes, of whom 118 had an active course during the outage. Of those 118, 71 never resumed deliveries after service was restored, and are recorded in our data as discontinuations. Forty-four of the 71 had received fewer than ten weeks of deliveries at the time of the interruption, and therefore sit in the early-stop stratum of the dose table above.

This matters in two ways. It contaminates the dose analysis, since 44 of the 537 early-stoppers (8 percent) stopped for reasons entirely attributable to Riverbend's logistics rather than to their own health, mobility, or preferences. And it means that the program as delivered during the evaluation period was not the program as designed for a nontrivial minority of participants.

We ran the primary analysis excluding all 187 participants from the affected zip codes and the 341 comparison members matched to those zip-code clusters. The participant rate falls slightly to 0.69 and the comparison rate is unchanged at 0.94, producing a difference of −0.25 (95% CI −0.34 to −0.16). The overall conclusion is not sensitive to the interruption. The dose gradient is modestly affected: excluding the affected zip codes raises the early-stop stratum's rate from 0.98 to 1.01 and leaves the completed-course stratum at 0.53.

## Limitations

We list these in descending order of how much they threaten the finding.

### 1. Selection into enrollment

Participants and comparison members were drawn from the same referral lists, matched on diagnosis, referral channel, age band, quarter, and coarse baseline utilization, and balanced on sex, household size, and geography. None of that addresses why one referred person enrolled and another did not.

The plausible drivers of that choice — perceived need, mobility, competing demands, trust in institutions, cognitive capacity to navigate an intake call, having a working phone — are also plausible drivers of hospitalization risk. We can tell a story in which enrollees are sicker (they enrolled because they needed help), which would make our estimate conservative. We can tell an equally plausible story in which enrollees are more organized and better resourced within a low-income population, which would make our estimate inflated. Our baseline data weakly support the first story; our qualitative sense of the intake process weakly supports the second.

This is the limitation that prevents a causal claim. No amount of additional analysis of the existing data will resolve it.

### 2. Incomplete hospital capture

We see admissions at four of the county's six acute-care hospitals. Based on Ohio Hospital Association discharge summaries for the service-area zip codes, we estimate the partner system accounts for approximately 62 percent of adult medical admissions among residents of our catchment, with the remaining 38 percent split between the competitor system's two facilities and out-of-county facilities.

If capture is equal between the two groups, incomplete capture attenuates absolute differences but leaves the relative comparison intact: the true rates would be roughly 1.15 and 1.52, a difference of 0.37 rather than 0.23, and the same 24 percent relative reduction. The direction of our conclusion would be unchanged and its magnitude understated.

If capture is unequal, the picture changes. We can state the tipping point precisely. Our observed finding would be entirely explained by measurement error if participants' admissions were captured about 32 percent less completely, in relative terms, than comparison members' — for instance, 47 percent capture for participants against 62 percent for comparison members.

We think unequal capture in that direction is unlikely. Participants are *more* likely than comparison members to have been referred through partner-system clinics (68 percent versus 54 percent, after matching, because matching on referral channel was exact only within available strata) and more likely to have had a partner-system encounter in the baseline year. Both suggest participants are captured *better*, which would mean our estimate understates the true difference. The sensitivity analysis restricting to those with a baseline partner-system encounter — a crude proxy for equal capture — produced an identical difference of −0.23, which is modestly reassuring.

But we want to be plain: we are reasoning about the size of an unmeasured quantity from indirect evidence. The only real fix is all-payer or health-information-exchange data, discussed below.

### 3. Non-random dose

Discussed at length above. The dose gradient is the most rhetorically powerful result and the least defensible one. At minimum, 19 percent of early discontinuations with recorded reasons were caused by hospitalization or institutional placement, and 8 percent of early-stoppers stopped because of our own van failure.

### 4. Linkage attrition and its non-randomness

Twenty-six percent of follow-up-eligible enrollees are absent from the analysis. They differ systematically from the linked cohort: more pantry-referred, younger, more concentrated in the western zip codes, and more likely to have stopped early. If early-stopping predicts higher utilization — which our own dose table suggests — then the unlinked cohort likely has higher utilization than the linked cohort, and our participant rate of 0.71 understates the program-wide rate.

The comparison group faces a parallel problem: only 3,057 of 4,118 referred non-enrollees had usable linkage, and the same forces operate. Whether the two attritions offset is unknowable.

### 5. Absence of clinical severity data

We cannot adjust for ejection fraction, NYHA class, HbA1c, renal function, comorbidity count, or medication regimen. In a study of hospitalization among people with CHF and diabetes, these are not minor covariates; they are the dominant predictors. Our matching is a poor substitute.

Our data-use agreement permits only a limited encounter data set. Expanding it is negotiable and should be negotiated.

### 6. Subgroup fragility and multiplicity

The household-size and age analyses were pre-specified; the dose analysis was not. We report twelve comparisons without multiplicity adjustment. The smallest subgroup (three-or-more-person households, n = 325) yields an interval spanning −0.32 to +0.02. These estimates are hypotheses about where the program works, not conclusions.

### 7. Unmeasured effective dose

We measure meals delivered, not meals eaten. The dietitian-call evidence of meal-sharing, particularly in larger households and among older participants, means delivered dose and effective dose diverge in an unmeasured and probably non-random way — and in exactly the subgroups where we observe the weakest effects.

### 8. Generalizability and program drift

The cohort enrolled between March 2024 and March 2025, during the program's first year, when it was smaller, more heavily staffed relative to census, and disproportionately serving clinic-referred participants near the delivery hub. The program today is larger, more pantry-referred, and geographically wider. Results from the first year may not describe the program's second year, and certainly do not describe a scaled version.

### 9. What we did not measure at all

We report no outcomes on glycemic control, blood pressure, weight, medication adherence, diet quality, food security status at exit, quality of life, or participant experience beyond the qualitative call-note review. We report no mortality comparison, because the death counts are small and the ascertainment differs between groups. We report no costs beyond the per-course figure. Any of these might matter as much as admissions to a foundation deciding what it funded.

## What This Evidence Can and Cannot Support

**It can support:** a statement that participants in Riverbend's medically tailored meal program had meaningfully fewer observed inpatient admissions than a matched group of similarly diagnosed, similarly referred pantry clients who did not enroll; that the difference is concentrated among those who received a substantial share of the intended course; and that the pattern is stable across every sensitivity analysis we ran.

**It can support:** a statement that the program's own delivery completion is the dominant operational problem, that 38 percent of enrollees receive less than ten weeks of a twenty-six-week course, and that Riverbend's ability to demonstrate impact is currently limited more by retention than by enrollment.

**It cannot support:** a claim that the meal program caused a 24 percent reduction in hospital admissions. It cannot support a claim that a completed course causes a 45 percent reduction. It cannot support a return-on-investment or cost-savings figure — and while it is arithmetically true that 0.23 avoided admissions at a local average net inpatient cost of roughly $13,500 would represent about $3,100 against a $1,180 per-course cost, that arithmetic rests on a causal premise the data do not establish, accrues to payers rather than to Riverbend or the foundation, and should not appear in renewal materials without the surrounding caveats.

**It cannot support:** any claim about the 491 unlinked enrollees, about admissions at the two unobserved hospitals, or about clinical outcomes of any kind.

## Implications for the Foundation's Decision

The foundation is deciding in September whether to renew a $3.8 million, three-year commitment. We offer five observations bearing on that decision.

**1. The retention problem is the program's central finding, and it is actionable.** Whatever the causal status of the dose gradient, the operational fact stands: fewer than half of enrollees complete the course. If the program's benefit is concentrated in completion — and both our data and the broader literature suggest it is — then moving completion from 41 percent to 60 percent would do more for outcomes than enrolling additional participants. This is a specific, fundable, measurable objective. We would propose a retention target as an explicit renewal condition, with quarterly reporting against it.

**2. Cold-chain redundancy is not an overhead expense.** An eleven-week service failure affecting 187 participants was caused by a single-point-of-failure vehicle fleet. Seventy-one of those participants never returned. The program lost them, and the evaluation lost the ability to interpret their data cleanly. A second backup vehicle and a service contract are modest costs against that.

**3. The evaluation design should change in a second term, and the foundation is the party with the leverage to require it.** Riverbend cannot randomize away a program people want. But the program has had waitlist periods, and a stepped-wedge or waitlist-randomized design across pantry sites or zip-code clusters is both ethically defensible and operationally feasible: everyone eventually receives meals, and the order of receipt is randomized. Implemented at the start of a renewal term, this would produce a defensible causal estimate by month thirty. We recommend it as a renewal condition and note that Riverbend cannot credibly impose it on itself.

**4. The data infrastructure needs three specific fixes.** First, expand the data-use agreement to include a limited clinical severity set — ejection fraction, HbA1c, comorbidity index, discharge disposition detail. Second, pursue participation in the regional health information exchange, which would capture admissions at the county's other facilities and largely dissolve the capture problem. Third, move linkage authorization from an optional intake form to a standard, well-explained element of enrollment, with a target of 90 percent authorization. The current 74 percent is a design choice, not a fact of nature.

**5. The subgroup findings should shape program design, not just reporting.** If the effect is genuinely weaker in larger households, the ten-meals-per-week single-participant model is mismatched to a substantial share of the caseload, and a household-scaled variant — or a hybrid of tailored meals plus household grocery support — deserves a pilot. If the effect is genuinely weaker among adults over seventy, then either the clinical protocol or the enrollment criteria warrant review. Both are testable within a renewal term.

**Our recommendation to the board**, which Ms. Lindqvist-Moore will present alongside this brief, is that Riverbend seek renewal at approximately current levels with a modest capital addition for fleet redundancy, and that it propose — rather than resist — the evaluation conditions described above. The program has produced a result worth taking seriously. It has not yet produced a result that would survive a skeptical reader, and the fastest route to one runs through admitting that plainly.
