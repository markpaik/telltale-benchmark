## Statement of Need

Merced County has approximately 3,900 children living in kinship care — children being raised by grandparents, aunts, uncles, adult siblings, and other relatives after a parent has died, been incarcerated, entered treatment, or otherwise become unable to parent. This is a large number for a county of roughly 290,000 people. It reflects a set of pressures that have converged here: agricultural labor economies that fracture households across seasons and borders, an opioid and methamphetamine burden that has fallen heavily on the San Joaquin Valley, incarceration rates above the state average, and immigration enforcement actions that have removed parents from homes with little warning. What remains, in most cases, is a relative who says yes.

Saying yes carries a cost that the public systems around these families are not built to absorb. A licensed foster parent in California receives a basic rate, a social worker, a placement agency, and a defined set of services. A grandmother who takes in three grandchildren after a Friday-night phone call from a hospital or a jail receives, in the typical case, none of these things. She may qualify for a CalWORKs child-only grant of a few hundred dollars per month per child. She may not know it exists. If the children were never formally removed by the child welfare agency — and the majority in Merced County were not — she has no legal authority to enroll them in school, consent to medical care, or access their records, and no agency assigned to help her obtain it. She is, in the language of the field, an informal or "voluntary" kinship caregiver, and she is largely invisible to the systems that count children.

Los Robles Family Resource Network exists to make these families visible and to give them what the formal system gives foster families. We are the only organization in Merced County whose primary mission is kinship support. Last year we served 810 households. The demographic profile of those families explains a great deal about what the work requires. Fifty-eight percent are Hispanic or Latino, and a substantial share of those households are mixed-status, meaning that a caregiver's willingness to approach a courthouse, a county office, or a school district is mediated by fear of exposure. Fourteen percent are Hmong, a community with deep roots in Merced going back to the resettlement of the 1980s, in which kinship caregiving is culturally normative but formal guardianship is culturally unfamiliar and often actively distrusted, and in which the older caregivers who do most of the raising frequently have limited English and limited literacy in any written language. Nine percent are Black, a group that in Merced County as elsewhere is overrepresented in child welfare involvement relative to population share and underrepresented in the receipt of voluntary support services.

Sixty-four percent of the caregivers we serve are over 60. This single figure drives much of what follows in this proposal. A caregiver over 60 is more likely to be managing chronic illness while managing a toddler. She is more likely to be on a fixed income that was calculated for one person and is now stretched across four. She is more likely to be caring for the children of a child she is also grieving — because grief, not abstraction, is the emotional substrate of most kinship care. And she is more likely to face the question that no program has ever answered well: what happens to these children if something happens to me. Forty-three percent of the households we serve live below the federal poverty line, and among caregiver-headed households over 65 that figure is higher still.

The consequences of unsupported kinship care are measurable, and they are expensive. When a kinship placement fails, a child typically enters formal foster care, at a public cost that dwarfs the cost of prevention, and with outcomes that research has consistently found to be worse than those of children who remain with relatives. Merced County's kinship placement stability rate at twelve months averages 71 percent. Los Robles families sustained placement at 88 percent over the same period. That seventeen-point gap represents, applied across the county's kinship population, several hundred children per year whose living situation holds or does not hold — and it is the clearest available evidence that what we do works.

We also know where our work falls short of the need, and this proposal is built around those gaps.

**Hmong families are underserved relative to their share of the kinship population.** Fourteen percent of our caseload is Hmong, but community estimates and school district enrollment data suggest the Hmong share of Merced County's kinship-raised children is meaningfully higher. Our support groups run in English and Spanish. A Hmong grandmother who does not read and does not speak English has, in practice, no group to attend. Interpretation at a group is not a solution; it makes a caregiver a guest at her own support meeting. What is required is a group facilitated in Hmong, by a Hmong facilitator, structured around Hmong understandings of family obligation, elder authority, and shame — a structure in which the concept of "asking for help outside the clan" is itself part of what the group has to work through.

**Caregivers have no access to relief.** Respite — a few hours or a weekend in which someone else is responsible — is the single most requested and least available service in kinship care. We have no respite fund. When a 71-year-old caregiver with congestive heart failure tells our case manager that she has not slept through a night in eight months and has not seen a doctor since the children arrived, we can offer her a support group and a referral. We cannot offer her Saturday.

**Our legal clinic is rationing.** Last year we completed 210 guardianship petitions. This is a substantial number for a clinic staffed at our level, and each completed petition represents a child who can be enrolled in school, insured, treated, and legally protected, and a caregiver who can act as a parent without asking permission. But our waitlist at any given moment runs three to five months. Families in crisis — a caregiver facing a school enrollment deadline, a parent threatening to retrieve children from a home that has become their only stable one — cannot wait three to five months. We turn away or defer families whose cases we could win.

To this picture we must add candor about our own circumstances, because the Foundation is entitled to it and because it bears on what this grant would accomplish. On September 30 an $850,000 federal earmark that funded approximately one-third of our kinship program lapsed. It was a single-year appropriation with no reauthorization pathway, and its loss was not the result of any performance finding. In January our founding executive director retired after nineteen years. Our board has contracted from thirteen members to eight over roughly two years, through a combination of term expirations, relocations, and the ordinary attrition that follows a founder's departure. We are, at this moment, an organization with strong outcomes, a proven model, a diminished balance sheet, an interim executive, and a board that must rebuild. We are asking the Foundation to invest in the outcomes while we repair the infrastructure, and the balance of this proposal explains specifically how both will happen.

## Project Description

The project is the stabilization and expansion of the Los Robles kinship program along three lines of service, delivered by a staff of 38 within a total organizational budget of $6.1 million, and grounded in a partnership structure that predates this request.

### The existing platform

Los Robles operates a kinship program with four established components. **Kinship navigation and case management** assigns each enrolled household a case manager who conducts a structured intake, builds a family plan, and maintains contact at a frequency set by acuity — weekly for households in crisis, monthly for stable households, quarterly for households in maintenance. Case managers handle benefits enrollment, school liaison work, medical and dental access, and referral to behavioral health. **Caregiver support groups** run in English and Spanish at three sites, weekly and biweekly, with concurrent children's programming so that attendance does not require finding childcare. **The legal clinic** provides guardianship representation, consent-authorization assistance, benefits appeals, and limited school-rights advocacy. **Children's services** provide tutoring, a summer program, and group counseling for children processing parental loss.

This platform is not hypothetical and does not require construction. It requires funding at a level that matches demand, and it requires three additions.

### Component one: Hmong-language caregiver support groups

We will establish two Hmong-language caregiver support groups, one in the city of Merced and one in Livingston, meeting biweekly. Each will be co-facilitated by a Hmong-speaking Los Robles staff member and a facilitator identified through the Hmong community center, our partner in this component. The center is not a referral source in this arrangement; it is a design partner. Its staff and elders will help us determine meeting locations that carry no stigma, times that accommodate multi-generational households and farm work schedules, and a curriculum that engages rather than overrides cultural understandings of family obligation.

Curriculum development will occupy the first four months of Year One. We will not adapt an English-language curriculum by translation. Concepts central to Western kinship support — self-care, boundary-setting, individual counseling, the disclosure of family difficulty to non-relatives — do not map cleanly onto the frameworks these caregivers bring, and a translated curriculum communicates that the program is for someone else. We will build content around the questions Hmong caregivers actually raise: how to honor obligation to an absent adult child while protecting grandchildren from that child's instability; how to navigate a school system that communicates only in writing and only in English; how to think about legal guardianship when the clan already knows who is responsible and the court's involvement feels like an accusation.

Groups will launch in Month Five of Year One. We project twelve to eighteen participants per group per cycle, three cycles per year, reaching approximately 90 to 100 unduplicated Hmong caregivers annually by Year Two.

### Component two: The respite fund

We will establish a flexible respite fund providing direct relief to caregivers, structured in three tiers.

**Tier one — hourly respite.** Vouchers for four to eight hours of supervised care, redeemable through vetted providers including licensed family childcare homes, the Hmong community center's youth program, and trained relative-respite providers recruited and background-checked by Los Robles. Intended for medical appointments, court dates, and the ordinary errands that become impossible when a caregiver cannot leave children unattended.

**Tier two — extended respite.** Weekend or multi-day care, typically two to three times per year per household, for caregivers experiencing acute strain, medical procedures, or bereavement.

**Tier three — emergency stabilization.** Direct cash assistance for expenses that threaten a placement: a car repair that determines whether children reach school, a rent shortfall in a month when a caregiver's hours were cut, a bed for a child sleeping on a floor, a deposit for utilities in a caregiver's name. Capped per household per year, disbursed on case manager authorization within 72 hours, with documentation completed after payment rather than before.

Tier three deserves explanation because it is the component most likely to draw scrutiny. Our experience is that placements do not usually fail because a caregiver stops loving a child. They fail because an accumulation of unmet material needs reaches a point at which the caregiver concludes she cannot do it. Interrupting that accumulation at the $400 mark is dramatically cheaper, for the family and for the county, than managing its consequences at the point of placement disruption. We will track every tier-three disbursement against subsequent placement status, and we will report what we find, including if what we find is that the relationship is weaker than we expect.

The fund will serve an estimated 240 households in Year One, rising to 320 by Year Three.

### Component three: Two staff attorneys

We will hire two full-time staff attorneys, expanding legal clinic capacity from its current level toward a target of 340 completed guardianship petitions annually by Year Three — a 62 percent increase over last year's 210. Both attorneys will carry guardianship caseloads. In addition, one will develop capacity in school-rights advocacy and special education, an area where kinship caregivers are systematically disadvantaged because they frequently lack the educational rights holder status that districts require. The second will focus on benefits appeals and on the specific legal complications that arise in mixed-status households, where a caregiver's immigration status, a child's status, and the whereabouts of a parent interact in ways that generalist practitioners often mishandle.

Our legal aid partner will provide malpractice coverage under an existing memorandum, supervision for the first year of each attorney's tenure, conflict screening, and backup representation in matters requiring contested litigation beyond our clinic's scope. This arrangement is in place and has governed our clinic's operation to date; it is not a new relationship requiring negotiation.

### Partnership structure

**The county human services agency** provides referrals and, under a data-sharing agreement executed prior to this application, placement-status data that permits us to track outcomes for enrolled families beyond the point of our own contact. This agreement is the foundation of our evaluation design and is the reason we can report twelve-month stability figures at all. The agency also participates in a quarterly case conference on shared high-acuity households.

**The legal aid organization** provides the supervision, insurance, and backup litigation described above.

**The Hmong community center** co-designs and co-facilitates the Hmong-language groups, hosts one group site, and provides respite capacity through its youth programming.

**UC Merced** conducts independent evaluation under a subcontract described in the following sections. The relationship is with faculty in the social sciences who have worked on Central Valley family and child welfare questions and who bring both methodological rigor and local knowledge.

### Leadership and staffing

Chue Vang Lor serves as Interim Executive Director. He came to the role from within the organization and has direct working knowledge of the kinship program, the county partnership, and the Hmong community relationships on which component one depends. The board, under Ravi Chandrasekaran, has initiated a national executive search with a target of a permanent appointment by the second quarter of Year One. Antoinette Boudreaux, Program Director, has led the kinship program through the earmark loss and the leadership transition and will hold day-to-day authority over all three new components regardless of the search outcome. The Foundation should understand program continuity as resting on Boudreaux, and the board's transition plan is explicitly designed so that no component of this grant depends on the identity of the permanent executive.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

The Foundation evaluates on five dimensions: placement stability at twelve and 24 months, caregiver depression screening scores, school stability, guardianship completion, and cost per family. Our goals and objectives are organized to those dimensions.

### Goal 1: Sustain and extend kinship placement stability

**Objective 1.1.** Maintain twelve-month placement stability at or above 88 percent for enrolled households in each year of the grant, against a county baseline of 71 percent. Measured through county data-sharing and case management records; reported annually with confidence intervals.

**Objective 1.2.** Establish and report 24-month placement stability, a measure we have not previously tracked. Year One establishes the baseline through retrospective analysis of the Year-Minus-Two cohort. Years Two and Three target 82 percent or above. We set this figure below our twelve-month rate deliberately; attrition compounds, and we would rather commit to a number we can meet than to one that flatters the proposal.

**Objective 1.3.** Among households receiving tier-three emergency stabilization, achieve twelve-month placement stability at or above 85 percent. This is the test of whether crisis-triggered cash assistance protects placements or merely delays their failure.

### Goal 2: Improve caregiver mental health

**Objective 2.1.** Administer the PHQ-9 at intake and at six-month intervals to all enrolled caregivers, achieving 80 percent screening coverage of the active caseload. Coverage, not merely score improvement, is the first-year objective, because our current screening is inconsistent and an improvement statistic drawn from a self-selected subset is worth little.

**Objective 2.2.** Among caregivers with baseline PHQ-9 scores of 10 or above, achieve a mean reduction of four points or greater at twelve months, with 50 percent or more moving below the clinical threshold of 10.

**Objective 2.3.** Achieve 70 percent connection to behavioral health services within 60 days for caregivers scoring 15 or above, and 100 percent same-day safety response for any caregiver endorsing item nine.

**Objective 2.4.** Establish Hmong-language screening capacity, including validated translation and administration protocols developed with the Hmong community center and UC Merced. We note that PHQ-9 performance in Hmong populations is imperfectly established and that somatic presentation of distress is common; our evaluation partner will assess instrument validity for this subgroup rather than assuming it.

### Goal 3: Protect school stability

**Objective 3.1.** Achieve 85 percent school-year stability — no non-promotional school change — among enrolled school-age children, and establish the county comparison figure through the data-sharing agreement.

**Objective 3.2.** Achieve 90 percent average daily attendance among enrolled children, with chronic absenteeism below 12 percent.

**Objective 3.3.** Complete educational rights holder designation for 90 percent of enrolled children lacking one, within 90 days of intake.

**Objective 3.4.** Provide special education advocacy to 60 children annually by Year Two, with 75 percent of cases resulting in an added or improved service.

### Goal 4: Expand guardianship completion

**Objective 4.1.** Complete 260 petitions in Year One, 300 in Year Two, 340 in Year Three — a cumulative 900 against a three-year baseline projection of 630.

**Objective 4.2.** Reduce median time from intake to petition filing from the current 94 days to 60 days by Year Two.

**Objective 4.3.** Reduce the waitlist from three to five months to under six weeks by the end of Year Two.

**Objective 4.4.** Achieve Hmong household representation among guardianship clients at or above 14 percent, matching their share of our caseload. Hmong families are currently underrepresented in the clinic relative to their enrollment, and closing that gap is a specific objective rather than an expected byproduct.

### Goal 5: Demonstrate cost-effectiveness

**Objective 5.1.** Establish a validated cost-per-family-served figure using full program cost allocation, reported annually and disaggregated by service intensity tier.

**Objective 5.2.** Maintain or reduce cost per family across the grant period while increasing service volume, with Year Three cost per family at or below Year One in nominal terms.

**Objective 5.3.** Publish a cost-avoidance analysis in Year Three comparing program cost per stable placement against Merced County foster care cost per child-year, using county-verified figures.

### Goal 6: Reach Hmong families at population parity

**Objective 6.1.** Launch two Hmong-language support groups by Month Five of Year One with a combined enrollment of 24 or more caregivers.

**Objective 6.2.** Increase Hmong households from 14 percent to 20 percent of total enrollment by Year Three.

**Objective 6.3.** Achieve 65 percent or greater retention through a full group cycle, and equivalent satisfaction scores between Hmong-language and English/Spanish groups.

## Evaluation Plan

Evaluation is conducted by UC Merced under subcontract, with an independent scope that includes the authority to report findings unfavorable to Los Robles. We have structured it this way because the Foundation's investment thesis rests on whether kinship support actually produces the outcomes we claim, and an evaluation controlled by the grantee cannot answer that question credibly.

### Design

The evaluation uses a quasi-experimental comparison-group design. Random assignment is not ethically available to us; we will not withhold service from a family in crisis to preserve a control condition. Instead we will construct a matched comparison group from county administrative data, drawn from kinship households known to the county that did not enroll with Los Robles, matched on child age, sibling group size, caregiver age, caregiver relationship to child, household poverty indicator, race and ethnicity, primary language, and formal versus informal placement status. Propensity score methods will be used to construct weights, and sensitivity analysis will assess how large an unobserved confounder would need to be to overturn any observed effect.

We are explicit about the principal threat to validity: families who seek out and enroll in kinship support may differ systematically from those who do not, in motivation, in social connectedness, and in awareness of resources, in ways that also predict placement stability. Selection bias could account for some portion of the seventeen-point gap between our stability rate and the county average. The evaluation will estimate the magnitude of this threat rather than ignoring it, and if the adjusted effect is smaller than the raw comparison suggests, that is what will be reported.

### Data sources

Five sources feed the evaluation. **County administrative data** provides placement status, placement changes, re-entry into formal care, and comparison group construction. **Los Robles program data**, housed in a case management system with structured fields for every objective in the preceding section, provides service dosage, screening scores, legal case status, and disbursement records. **School district data**, accessed through consent-based release, provides enrollment continuity, attendance, and where available academic indicators. **Court records** provide petition filing dates, disposition, and time-to-completion, which allows verification of guardianship figures against an external source. **Primary collection** — caregiver surveys at intake, six, twelve, and 24 months; qualitative interviews with 40 caregivers annually including a purposive Hmong subsample; focus groups with each support group cohort; and interviews with partner staff — provides the mechanism and experience data that administrative sources cannot.

### Instruments

The **PHQ-9** measures caregiver depression, with the Hmong-language validity assessment described above. The **Caregiver Strain Questionnaire** measures the objective and subjective burden dimensions that depression screening misses. A brief **social support measure** captures whether the program is building durable network connections or only substituting for them. **Placement stability** is defined operationally as continuous residence with the enrolling caregiver, with sub-codes distinguishing planned transitions such as reunification or adoption from disruptions, so that a successful reunification is not miscounted as a failure. **School stability** is defined as absence of non-promotional school change within an academic year.

### Analysis

Primary analysis compares outcomes between the enrolled and matched comparison groups using difference-in-differences where pre-period data exists and adjusted regression otherwise. Dose-response analysis examines whether outcomes vary with service intensity, which is our best internal check on causal claims: if families receiving more contact do better than families receiving less, holding baseline acuity constant, the case for program effect strengthens. Subgroup analysis is prespecified for the three principal racial and ethnic groups, for caregivers over and under 60, for formal versus informal placements, and for households receiving versus not receiving respite. Cost analysis allocates full program cost including administrative overhead across families served, disaggregated by intensity tier, and compares against county foster care costs.

### Reporting

UC Merced delivers a Year One baseline and implementation report at month fourteen, an interim outcomes report at month 26, and a final report at month 38. The interim report includes a formative component with recommendations for course correction, and Los Robles commits in advance to a documented written response to each recommendation. Quarterly dashboards go to the Foundation, the board, and program staff. Findings will be shared with the county, the partners, and participating families in accessible formats, including oral presentation in Hmong and Spanish.

The evaluation subcontract is budgeted at seven percent of total grant funds. UC Merced retains publication rights subject only to a review period for factual accuracy, not for editorial approval.

## Organizational Capacity

### What we have built

Los Robles employs 38 staff against a $6.1 million total budget and has operated kinship services in Merced County for nineteen years. Our capacity claim rests on four demonstrated capabilities.

**We produce outcomes at scale.** Eight hundred ten households served, 88 percent twelve-month stability against a 71 percent county average, 210 completed guardianship petitions. These are not pilot numbers. They represent a mature operation with established referral flow, functioning case management infrastructure, and a legal clinic that has moved hundreds of families through a court process most nonprofits do not attempt.

**We hold institutional relationships that took years to build.** The county data-sharing agreement is unusual; public child welfare agencies do not routinely give community organizations placement-level data. It exists because two decades of accurate reporting and discreet handling built the trust it requires. The legal aid supervision and malpractice arrangement similarly reflects a partner's willingness to extend professional liability to our staff. The Hmong community center relationship rests on staff and board members from that community and on years of showing up without asking for anything.

**We are culturally and linguistically staffed.** We deliver services in English, Spanish, and Hmong. Our staff includes Spanish-dominant and Hmong-speaking case managers, and our leadership reflects the communities we serve. This is not a diversity statement; it is an operational precondition. A monolingual English organization cannot serve this caseload at all.

**We measure honestly.** The figures in this proposal are drawn from a functioning data system and are verifiable against county and court records. We report our waitlist, our underrepresentation of Hmong families in the legal clinic, and our absence of 24-month tracking because a proposal that reports only strengths is not a management document.

### What we are repairing

**The earmark loss.** The $850,000 federal earmark that lapsed September 30 funded approximately one-third of the kinship program. We responded with a sequence of decisions the board reviewed and approved: we did not reduce direct service staff in the kinship program; we held four vacant positions open across the organization rather than filling them; we reduced administrative and non-personnel spending; and we drew on operating reserves at a rate the board set as a hard ceiling. The result is that program capacity has been preserved and the organization's runway has shortened. We are not concealing that this is a temporary posture. Reserves are finite and the board has set a floor below which they will not be drawn.

This grant does not simply replace the earmark. At $1.25 million over three years — roughly $417,000 annually — it covers less than half of what lapsed. Diversified revenue replacement, described in the following section, must do the rest.

**The executive transition.** The founding executive director retired in January after nineteen years. Founder transitions are the most predictable failure point in nonprofit organizations, and the board treated it as such. A succession committee formed before the retirement date. Chue Vang Lor was appointed interim from within, preserving continuity in county, community, and funder relationships. The board retained a search firm and has set a second-quarter target for permanent appointment. Critically, the board resolved that program leadership authority sits with Antoinette Boudreaux independent of the executive search outcome, so that no component of this grant is contingent on who occupies the executive office.

Interim leadership carries real risk. An interim executive has less authority to make binding long-term commitments and may face reduced funder confidence. We mitigate the first through explicit board delegation of authority for this grant's obligations, ratified by resolution. We address the second by disclosing the situation rather than obscuring it.

**Board contraction.** Eight members is below our bylaws' target range and below the size required to staff committees adequately while maintaining the fundraising capacity a $6.1 million organization needs. Board chair Ravi Chandrasekaran has led a governance response with four elements. First, a skills audit identifying gaps in finance, legal, philanthropy, and lived kinship experience. Second, a recruitment target of six new members over eighteen months, with at least two being current or former kinship caregivers — a constituency currently absent from our board and one whose absence we regard as a governance deficiency, not merely a diversity gap. Third, a committee restructure consolidating five committees into three so that eight members can staff them functionally during the rebuild. Fourth, a board development budget line supporting orientation, training, and a facilitated annual retreat.

Two new members have been identified and are in the vetting process. We expect ten members by the end of the current fiscal year and thirteen by the end of Year Two of this grant.

### Assessment

Los Robles is an organization with strong programs and weakened infrastructure. Both statements are true and neither cancels the other. The programmatic risk of this grant is low: the model works, the staff exists, the partnerships hold. The institutional risk is real and concentrated in the next twelve months, during which an executive must be hired, a board rebuilt, and revenue diversified. We are asking the Foundation to fund the programs while we do the institutional work, and we have structured our reporting so that the Foundation can watch that work happen. We commit to quarterly governance and financial updates alongside program reporting for the full grant term, and to immediate notification of any material change in leadership, board composition, or financial position.

## Sustainability

The Foundation asks how the work survives past year three. We take the question seriously enough to answer it component by component rather than gesturing at diversification.

### The legal clinic

The clinic is the component with the clearest sustainability pathway and the most demanding one, and we address it first because the Foundation has asked specifically.

**Court-appointed counsel compensation.** California superior courts appoint counsel in probate guardianship matters under specified circumstances, with compensation available from county funds or party assets. Our clinic has not systematically pursued appointment or claimed compensation. With two additional attorneys and appropriate administrative infrastructure, we project that appointment-based revenue could cover fifteen to twenty percent of clinic cost by Year Three. This requires a formal agreement with the Merced County Superior Court, and we have initiated that conversation. It is not concluded, and we present it as a pathway, not a commitment.

**Legal services funding streams.** The clinic's work qualifies for support from the State Bar's Legal Services Trust Fund Program, from the Equal Access Fund administered through the Judicial Council, and from Interest on Lawyers' Trust Accounts distributions. Our legal aid partner receives funding from these sources and has indicated willingness to support a joint application in which Los Robles operates as a subgrantee, an arrangement that leverages their established eligibility and reporting infrastructure. We target Year Two application and Year Three revenue.

**County contract.** The county human services agency currently refers families to the clinic and derives direct benefit: every completed guardianship reduces the probability of formal foster care entry, at a cost per case far below the cost of a single child-year in care. We will pursue a fee-for-service or performance-based contract in which the county compensates Los Robles per completed guardianship for county-referred families. The cost-avoidance analysis in Year Three of our evaluation is designed in part to make this case with verified figures. We target contract negotiation in Year Three for Year Four implementation.

**Cross-subsidy.** The clinic's fixed costs — supervision, malpractice, case management infrastructure — are partially absorbed by the broader organization and by our legal aid partner's in-kind supervision. This is not a growth strategy but it lowers the marginal revenue required.

Our realistic assessment: with all four pathways developed, the clinic reaches sixty to seventy percent non-Foundation funding by the end of Year Three, requiring continued philanthropic support for the balance. We will not claim full self-sufficiency for a legal services program serving indigent clients, because that claim would not be credible and no legal services program in California achieves it.

### The Hmong-language groups

Support groups are the lowest-cost component, requiring facilitator time and space rather than professional licensure or litigation infrastructure. The community center partnership provides space in kind. Once the curriculum is developed — a one-time Year One cost — ongoing delivery cost is modest and absorbable within general program funding. We also anticipate that this component will develop peer facilitators from within the first two cohorts, a common trajectory in culturally grounded group work that further reduces cost while improving fidelity. We regard this component as sustainable within our operating base after the grant period.

### The respite fund

Respite is the hardest component to sustain because it is direct assistance with no reimbursement stream. Three pathways apply. California's Kinship Navigator programs receive federal Title IV-E funding under the Family First Prevention Services Act at a fifty percent match rate for evidence-based navigator models; documenting our model through the UC Merced evaluation is a prerequisite for pursuing this, and the evaluation is designed with the federal evidence review criteria in mind. Second, local and regional funders have shown interest in flexible-assistance funds as a discrete, comprehensible ask, and we will build a respite-specific donor base. Third, the county's own prevention services allocation under Family First is an appropriate source, and our data-sharing relationship positions us to make that case.

We are candid that respite is the component most at risk after Year Three. If the Foundation's investment produces the evidence base we expect, the Title IV-E pathway becomes viable and the component becomes durable. If it does not, respite will require continued philanthropic support or will contract.

### Organizational sustainability

Underneath the component-level answers sits a revenue diversification plan led by Siobhan McAllister. The earmark's loss exposed a concentration risk we should have addressed earlier. The plan targets: growth in individual giving from a base that has been underdeveloped, with a major-donor program and a planned-giving track appropriate to our donor demographics; foundation revenue expanded beyond current funders through a systematic prospect pipeline; government revenue diversified across county, state, and federal sources with no single source exceeding twenty percent of total revenue; and earned revenue explored through training and technical assistance, given that our kinship model has drawn inquiry from other Central Valley counties.

The board has adopted a reserve policy targeting six months of operating expenses, currently unmet, with a rebuilding schedule tied to the diversification plan. Board recruitment includes explicit fundraising capacity criteria.

We will report on revenue diversification quarterly alongside program metrics. If diversification does not proceed on schedule, the Foundation will know it early.

## Budget Narrative

The request is $1,250,000 over three years, structured to fund the three new components and a proportional share of the infrastructure required to deliver them, with evaluation independently funded.

### Personnel — approximately 62 percent

The largest allocation supports the two staff attorneys, at salaries competitive with public interest legal employers in the Central Valley, together with a legal assistant whose absence is currently the binding constraint on clinic throughput — attorneys spend hours on filing preparation and document assembly that paraprofessional support handles more efficiently. Component one requires a Hmong-language program coordinator at 1.0 FTE and facilitator time. Component two requires a respite coordinator at 1.0 FTE, responsible for provider recruitment, vetting, voucher administration, and disbursement documentation. Partial allocation of the Program Director's time reflects Antoinette Boudreaux's direct supervisory responsibility for all three components. Benefits are calculated at our standard rate covering health, dental, retirement match, and required payroll taxes. Modest annual increases are budgeted at three percent to maintain real compensation.

### Direct assistance — approximately 16 percent

The respite fund itself: hourly voucher purchases, extended respite payments, and tier-three emergency stabilization. This allocation grows across the three years as the fund reaches full utilization. Per-household caps are set at levels we will report against, and disbursement is authorized by case managers within a documented protocol with supervisory review of aggregate patterns rather than individual pre-approval, because pre-approval defeats the purpose of emergency assistance.

### Evaluation — 7 percent

The UC Merced subcontract, covering faculty and graduate researcher time, data acquisition and management, instrument development including the Hmong-language validity work, survey and interview administration, incentives for research participants, analysis, and report production.

### Program operations — approximately 8 percent

Curriculum development for the Hmong-language groups, including community consultation and materials production; group supplies and refreshments, which matter more than their cost suggests in cultures where meeting without food communicates disrespect; interpretation and translation; case management system enhancements required to capture the new data fields our objectives specify; court filing fees for families unable to pay and ineligible for waiver; legal research subscriptions; mileage for staff serving a geographically dispersed rural caseload; and professional development including required continuing legal education.

### Indirect — approximately 7 percent

Allocated administrative cost covering finance, human resources, information technology, insurance, facilities, and executive oversight. This rate is below our federally negotiated rate and below the actual cost of supporting this program. We absorb the difference and disclose that we are doing so, because understating true program cost has been a durable pathology in this sector and we would rather be accurate than appear efficient.

### What this budget does not include

The grant does not fund the existing case management operation, the English and Spanish support groups, children's services, or the current legal clinic baseline. Those continue under other funding, and their continuation is the responsibility of the diversification plan, not of this grant. We say this plainly because we want the Foundation to understand that its investment is additive rather than substitutive, and because we do not want a future conversation in which the Foundation discovers it has become the sole support of a program it believed it was expanding.

### Cost per family

Applying the grant across projected new families served — approximately 240 respite households and 90 Hmong-language group participants in Year One, rising through Year Three, with expanded legal clinic capacity serving families across all groups — yields a marginal cost per additional family in the range the Foundation's cost-per-family criterion contemplates. The evaluation's cost analysis will produce a validated figure using full allocation rather than the estimate we offer here, and we will report it whether or not it flatters us. For context, Merced County's cost per child-year in formal foster care exceeds our projected cost per family served by a wide margin, and every placement this program holds together is a cost the county does not incur.

### Financial management

Los Robles maintains segregated fund accounting with grant-specific cost centers, receives an annual independent audit, and has had no material findings. Financial statements go to the board finance committee monthly and the full board quarterly. We will provide the Foundation with audited statements annually and grant-specific financial reports quarterly. Given the earmark loss, we will additionally provide quarterly cash position and reserve reporting for the full grant term, unrequested, so that the Foundation is never surprised by our financial condition.

## Closing

Three thousand nine hundred children in Merced County are being raised by relatives. Most of those relatives are older than sixty, many are poor, and a substantial share are navigating systems that do not speak their language and were not built with them in mind. What they have chosen to do is the least expensive and most humane response available to a family in crisis, and the public systems that benefit from that choice have never adequately supported it.

Los Robles has narrowed that gap for nineteen years, and last year did it for 810 households at an eighty-eight percent stability rate. We are asking the Wendell Ruthers Foundation to fund three specific additions — support in Hmong for caregivers who currently have none, relief for caregivers who currently get none, and legal capacity for a clinic that currently turns families away — at a moment when our own institutional footing is less certain than it has been in two decades. We have not concealed that circumstance. We have described what we are doing about it, committed to reporting on it quarterly, and structured this proposal so the Foundation can judge both the program and the repair.

The families will still be there in three years. We intend to be there with them, and this grant is a substantial part of how.
