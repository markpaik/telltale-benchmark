## 1. Purpose and Approach

This postmortem examines the undeclared milk allergen incident that originated during the April 4, 2026 weekend shutdown and culminated in a Class I recall filed May 2, 2026. The purpose of this document is to establish a shared, factual account of what happened; to identify the root cause and contributing factors; to evaluate the effectiveness of the detection, investigation, and response phases; and to commit the organization to specific corrective actions with named owners and due dates.

This review follows a blameless postmortem methodology. The individuals named in this document are identified for factual clarity and accountability for forward-looking actions — not to assign fault. The premise of this review is that the people involved acted reasonably given the information, tools, training, and systems available to them at the time. Where individual decisions are examined — for example, the April 24 decision to attribute the contamination to a supplier and restart the line — the analysis focuses on why the decision was reasonable within the system as it existed, and what systemic conditions allowed a reasonable person to reach a wrong conclusion. Fixing those conditions, not correcting individuals, is how we prevent recurrence.

Sources for this document include the maintenance work order record, production and shipping records for Line 3, the consumer complaint log, laboratory results (internal and third-party), the FDA Establishment Inspection Report and Form 483 observations, recall effectiveness data, and structured interviews with 23 employees across maintenance, sanitation, production, quality, supply chain, and regulatory affairs.

## 2. Summary

During a planned weekend shutdown on April 4, 2026, a maintenance technician reconfigured a diverter valve on the dry ingredient pneumatic conveyance system. The reconfiguration caused Line 3 — the plant's certified dairy-free line, which produces a nationally distributed plant-based entree — to draw dry ingredients through a pneumatic header dedicated to Line 2, which conveys a cheese powder blend. The work order was closed the same day without the required post-work verification step and was never routed to allergen review, because the change control system did not flag the valve as allergen-critical.

Line 3 ran nine production days between April 6 and April 24, producing 218,000 cases shipped to 14 states, each carrying milk protein (casein) contamination not declared on the label. The contamination went undetected by the plant's environmental monitoring program: the two allergen swab sites that would most plausibly have caught it had been eliminated when the program was reduced from 20 sites to six during a 2025 headcount reduction.

The first external signal arrived April 17 as a consumer complaint coded "flavor — off-taste," a category with no allergen escalation trigger. Two more complaints arrived the week of April 20, including a report of a hospitalization in Rhode Island following an allergic reaction. Amit Vaidya, Director of Food Safety and Quality, opened a formal investigation April 24. Initial testing confirmed casein in finished product. The investigation focused on the most recent process change visible in quality records — a new dry blend supplier onboarded in late March — and concluded the supplier's material was the likely source. The suspect raw material was placed on hold, and Line 3 was cleared to restart. An additional 26,000 cases were produced and shipped before April 29, when finished-product testing found casein in a lot made entirely from the original (incumbent) supplier's material, disproving the supplier hypothesis.

Plant Manager Rosalie Thibodeau shut down Line 3 on April 29. On April 30, Maintenance Superintendent Curtis Mbeki traced the contamination path to the April 4 diverter valve reconfiguration. Sylvie Charbonneau, Vice President of Supply Chain, activated the retrieval effort April 30, and Regulatory Affairs Manager Grant Halloran filed the Class I recall with FDA on May 2.

The recall covered 244,000 cases, recovered 71 percent of shipped product, cost $4.7 million, and followed three reported allergic reactions, one requiring hospitalization. An FDA inspection conducted May 6–15 closed with six observations. At the time of the incident, a revised food safety plan addressing allergen cross-contact hazards in shared utilities had been pending approval since March 2026.

The root cause was a change control failure: a physical modification to allergen-critical infrastructure was executed and closed without verification or allergen review. Five contributing factors — an incomplete allergen hazard map for shared utilities, a degraded environmental monitoring program, complaint coding without allergen triggers, an investigation process without a structured hypothesis discipline, and a restart decision gate that did not require confirmed root cause — turned a single maintenance error into a 25-day, multi-state incident.

## 3. Impact

### 3.1 Consumer Impact

- **3 reported allergic reactions** in consumers, all linked to Line 3 product produced during the contamination window.
- **1 hospitalization** (Rhode Island, reported week of April 20).
- **14 states** received affected product through retail and foodservice distribution.

### 3.2 Product and Recall Impact

- **244,000 cases** produced and shipped during the contamination window:
  - 218,000 cases from the initial production run (April 6–24).
  - 26,000 cases from the post-misdiagnosis restart (April 25–29).
- **71 percent recovery rate** (approximately 173,000 cases retrieved or verified destroyed at the point of distribution); approximately 71,000 cases presumed consumed or otherwise unrecoverable.
- **9 production days** of contaminated output before the first line shutdown; **4 additional production days** after the erroneous restart.
- **25 days** from the origin of the contamination (April 4) to the confirmed root cause (April 30); **13 days** from first consumer signal (April 17) to line shutdown (April 29).

### 3.3 Financial Impact

- **$4.7 million total recall cost**, comprising product retrieval and destruction, customer credits and fees, third-party laboratory and consulting costs, freight, disposal, and lost production on Line 3 during the shutdown and re-validation period.
- Line 3 was out of production from April 29 through re-validation (production loss included in the figure above).
- Potential downstream costs not included in this figure: customer delisting risk, litigation exposure related to the reported reactions, and dairy-free certification review costs.

### 3.4 Regulatory and Certification Impact

- **Class I recall** filed with FDA on May 2, 2026 — the most serious recall classification, reserved for situations with a reasonable probability of serious adverse health consequences.
- **FDA inspection May 6–15, 2026**, closing with **six observations**, covering allergen preventive controls, change management, environmental monitoring adequacy, complaint handling, corrective action verification, and the status of the pending food safety plan revision.
- Line 3's **dairy-free certification** placed under review by the certifying body pending demonstration of corrective actions.

### 3.5 Organizational Impact

- Line 3 workforce reassigned or on reduced schedules during the shutdown and re-validation period.
- Significant unplanned workload across Quality, Maintenance, Supply Chain, and Regulatory Affairs during the response, displacing planned work including the food safety plan revision approval.
- Reputational impact with the plant's largest plant-based retail customers, two of which conducted their own supplier audits in May and June 2026.

## 5. Root Cause

### 5.1 Root Cause Statement

**A physical modification to allergen-critical infrastructure — the reconfiguration of a dry ingredient diverter valve creating a cross-contact pathway between the Line 2 cheese powder header and Line 3's certified dairy-free intake — was executed, closed, and returned to service through the routine maintenance process, without the required post-work verification and without allergen review, because the change control system did not identify the valve as allergen-critical and did not enforce the verification step before closure.**

### 5.2 Analysis

The technician's reconfiguration on April 4 was the initiating event, but the initiating event is not the root cause. Maintenance work on complex conveyance systems will occasionally produce incorrect as-left configurations; that is precisely why verification and review controls exist. The root cause is that the control system designed to catch such an error had three simultaneous gaps at the point where this work order passed through it:

**First, the asset registry did not classify the diverter valve as allergen-critical.** The allergen hazard analysis on file mapped allergen cross-contact risks at the line level — shared equipment, shared tools, personnel flow, air handling in production rooms — but did not extend to shared upstream utilities, including the pneumatic conveyance headers. Because the valve carried no allergen-critical designation, WO-26-1187 was categorized as routine mechanical work. It was never eligible for allergen review, and no rule existed that could have routed it there. The revised food safety plan pending since March 2026 addressed exactly this gap: it identified shared dry ingredient conveyance as an allergen cross-contact hazard requiring preventive controls. It had not been approved or implemented.

**Second, the post-work verification requirement was procedurally required but not systemically enforced.** The maintenance procedure required independent verification of as-left condition for work affecting product-contact or product-adjacent systems. The work order system, however, allowed the verification field to be marked complete by the same person closing the order, with no independent sign-off, no as-left documentation requirement, and no hold preventing closure. Interviews indicate this shortcut was common practice during compressed weekend shutdown windows, when verification personnel were not always available and the priority was returning lines to service by Monday. The April 4 closure at 16:42 with an unperformed verification was not an anomaly; it was the normalized condition of the system.

**Third, closed maintenance work was invisible to quality.** Once WO-26-1187 closed, it existed only in the maintenance system. It did not appear in the quality change log, was not summarized for the food safety team, and was not surfaced during the April 24 investigation's change review. The investigation team looked at every change it could see — and the change that mattered was not in its field of view. This invisibility is why the misdiagnosis on April 24 was not an individual failure of rigor but a predictable outcome: the team performed a change-based investigation using a change record that was structurally incomplete.

Had any one of these three gaps been closed — the valve flagged allergen-critical, the verification enforced, or maintenance changes visible to quality — the incident would likely have been prevented on April 4 or identified on April 24. All three existed simultaneously, and the error passed through.

## 6. Contributing Factors

The root cause explains how the contamination pathway was created and why it was not caught at the point of change. Five contributing factors explain why the contamination then ran undetected for 11 days, why the first investigation reached the wrong conclusion, and why contaminated product continued shipping for four days after the problem was known to be real.

### 6.1 Degraded Environmental Monitoring: The Swab Program Reduction

In 2025, as part of a plant-wide headcount reduction, the allergen environmental swab program was reduced from **20 sample sites to six**. The reduction was made to fit the program to available quality technician hours; the site-selection rationale at the time prioritized finished-product zones and the highest-traffic shared equipment surfaces. Two of the eliminated sites were located on Line 3's dry ingredient receiving and transfer equipment — downstream of the diverter valve and upstream of the mixing stage. Retrospective analysis indicates that either site would very likely have returned a casein positive within the first week of contaminated production, converting an 11-day detection lag into a two-to-four-day lag and preventing the majority of shipped volume.

The contributing failure here is not the headcount decision itself but the absence of a **risk-based review** when the program was cut. No documented hazard-based analysis supported the selection of the six retained sites versus the fourteen eliminated ones, and the food safety team was not required to sign off that the reduced program still provided adequate coverage of allergen cross-contact pathways. The program was resized to fit resources rather than redesigned to fit risk. Additionally, the reduced program's continued pattern of all-negative results created unwarranted confidence during the April investigation: negative environmental swabs on the retained sites were read as evidence that the plant environment was clean, reinforcing the supplier hypothesis.

### 6.2 Complaint Handling: No Allergen Escalation Trigger

The April 17 complaint — a "cheesy" off-taste in a dairy-free product — was the earliest external signal of the contamination, arriving 12 days before the line was shut down. It was coded "flavor — off-taste" and entered the monthly trending queue with no escalation. The complaint coding system had **no allergen-specific codes, no keyword triggers, and no escalation rule** tying complaint content to product allergen claims. A "cheesy" flavor note on a certified dairy-free product is a high-signal event; the system had no way to recognize it as such. The second complaint (April 21, alleged allergic reaction) was routed to the weekly quality review rather than triggering immediate action. Only the third complaint — a hospitalization, transmitted through a retailer — produced same-day escalation, and even that escalation depended on the severity of the outcome rather than on any systemic trigger.

Net effect: the complaint system converted a 12-day early warning into a 7-day delay. If the April 17 complaint had triggered same-week allergen testing of retained samples, the contamination would likely have been confirmed around April 20–21, preventing roughly one week of shipped production and the entire restart episode.

### 6.3 Investigation Discipline: Single-Hypothesis Convergence

The April 24 investigation moved quickly and reached a conclusion the same day. Given three consumer reports including a hospitalization, speed was appropriate. But the investigation converged on the first plausible hypothesis — the new dry blend supplier — without a structured process requiring the team to enumerate and test alternative contamination pathways (shared utilities, sanitation failures, rework errors, packaging/label errors, upstream conveyance). The supplier hypothesis was attractive for understandable reasons: it was the most recent change *visible in the records available to the team* (see Section 5.2), supplier-introduced contamination is a common failure mode, and it offered a containable corrective action. Confirmation followed: the hold on the new supplier's material and negative post-clean line swabs were both read as supporting evidence, though neither actually tested the hypothesis.

Critically, the investigation did not require **testing of the suspect raw material itself before restart**. Had the new supplier's dry blend been tested for casein on April 24–25, the negative result would have disproven the hypothesis before the restart, not after 26,000 additional cases shipped. The verification test that ultimately disproved the hypothesis on April 29 was run as due diligence, not as a gate.

### 6.4 Restart Decision Gate: Confirmed Root Cause Not Required

Line 3 was cleared to restart on April 24 on the basis of a *probable* root cause, a raw material hold, and a completed allergen clean with negative line-surface swabs. No plant procedure required that root cause be **confirmed by evidence** — for example, positive allergen findings in the suspect material, or a verified physical pathway — before restarting a line implicated in an allergen event with reported consumer reactions. The restart decision was made within existing authority and followed existing practice; the practice itself was the gap. The consequence was direct and quantifiable: 26,000 cases, four production days, and a materially larger recall.

### 6.5 Pending Food Safety Plan Revision

The revised food safety plan — which identified shared dry ingredient conveyance as an allergen cross-contact hazard and specified preventive controls including allergen-critical asset designation for conveyance routing components — had been drafted and pending approval since **March 2026**. Interviews attribute the delay to competing priorities and an approval workflow with no deadline enforcement. Had the revision been approved and implemented before April 4, the diverter valve would have carried an allergen-critical designation and WO-26-1187 would have required allergen review before closure. The organization had already diagnosed the vulnerability; it had not yet acted on the diagnosis. This factor also appeared among the FDA's six observations.

### 6.6 Interaction of Factors

No single contributing factor caused this incident. Their interaction did. The change control gap created the pathway; the swab program cut removed the environmental safety net; the complaint coding gap silenced the earliest external signal; the investigation and restart gaps converted a contained event into an extended one; and the pending plan revision represents the organization's known-but-unimplemented fix for the originating gap. This layered pattern — multiple independent barriers each degraded for locally reasonable reasons — is the central lesson of this incident.

## 7. What Worked and What Did Not

### 7.1 What Worked

**Escalation and shutdown, once the hypothesis was disproven.** The interval from the April 29 disproving lab result to line shutdown was approximately 75 minutes. Thibodeau's decision to shut Line 3 and hold all warehouse and interceptable in-transit product was immediate and unambiguous, with no observed pressure to keep the line running while the investigation continued.

**Root cause tracing.** Mbeki's team confirmed the physical contamination pathway within roughly 24 hours of being engaged, by physically walking the conveyance system rather than relying solely on drawings or system records. The valve was isolated and locked out within two hours of identification. The as-found condition was photographed and preserved, which materially strengthened both the internal investigation and the FDA interaction.

**Lot tracing and retrieval execution.** Charbonneau's team completed distribution-level tracing of all 244,000 cases across 14 states within approximately 36 hours of activation. The 71 percent recovery rate exceeds typical Class I recall effectiveness benchmarks for retail-distributed frozen product, reflecting strong lot coding discipline, distributor cooperation, and a well-executed customer notification cadence.

**Regulatory engagement.** Halloran opened voluntary dialogue with the FDA district recall coordinator on April 30 — before classification was finalized — and filed a complete Class I recall within 48 hours of confirmed root cause. The proactive posture was noted favorably during the subsequent inspection and likely contributed to a for-cause inspection closing with observations rather than escalated enforcement.

**Retained sample program.** The availability of retained finished-product samples spanning the full production window enabled both the initial April 24 confirmation and the April 29 lot-by-lot testing that disproved the supplier hypothesis and precisely bounded the contamination window. Without complete retains, the recall scope would have been broader and the root cause slower to confirm.

**Cross-functional response cohesion.** From April 29 forward, the response operated as a unified incident team with daily stand-ups, a single decision log, and clear ownership. Interviewed employees consistently described the April 29 – May 2 period as well-coordinated.

### 7.2 What Did Not Work

**Detection.** The contamination ran for 11 production days with no internal signal. Every internal barrier that could have detected it — change verification, allergen review, environmental swabbing, pre-op checks — was either bypassed, out of scope, or degraded. Detection ultimately came from consumers, which is the failure mode an allergen control program exists to prevent.

**Complaint signal processing.** A "cheesy taste" complaint on a certified dairy-free product sat in a monthly trending queue for a week. The system as designed could not recognize the single most diagnostic complaint the plant received. Escalation occurred only when harm severity forced it.

**The first investigation and restart.** The April 24 investigation was fast but structurally blind (incomplete change visibility) and procedurally unguarded (no requirement to test the suspect material, no confirmed-root-cause gate before restart). The result was four days of additional contaminated shipments during a period when the plant knew it had a confirmed allergen problem. This is the single costliest response failure: the 26,000 restart cases were entirely preventable with the information already in hand on April 24, had the suspect material simply been tested before restart.

**Change control as practiced.** The verification bypass on WO-26-1187 reflected normalized practice under weekend shutdown time pressure, not an isolated shortcut. The system permitted what the procedure prohibited.

**Organizational follow-through on known risks.** The food safety plan revision identifying this exact hazard sat unapproved for over a month. The swab program reduction proceeded without risk-based review. In both cases, the organization's formal risk knowledge outran its implementation.

**Early-phase communication.** Between April 24 and April 29, the plant leadership team, corporate quality, and key customers were not informed that an allergen investigation with a confirmed positive was underway, because the event was believed contained. When the hypothesis collapsed on April 29, external communication began from a standing start, compressing the notification timeline and complicating early customer conversations.

## 9. Lessons Learned

**1. Allergen hazards live in utilities, not just on lines.** The plant's allergen controls were strongest where allergen risk was most visible — production surfaces, changeovers, personnel flow — and weakest in the shared infrastructure connecting lines. A dairy-free certification is only as strong as the least-controlled pathway into the line. Hazard analysis must follow the physical routing of every material stream, not the organizational boundaries of each line.

**2. A control that can be bypassed under time pressure will be bypassed under time pressure.** The verification requirement on WO-26-1187 existed on paper and failed in practice because the system permitted closure without it and shutdown schedules rewarded speed. Critical controls must be enforced by the system, not by discipline alone.

**3. Monitoring programs must be sized to risk, not to headcount.** The swab program cut was a resource decision made without a risk decision attached to it. Any future reduction in detection capability must carry an explicit, signed assessment of what the organization is choosing not to see.

**4. Complaints are a detection system — if they are coded to detect.** The most diagnostic signal in this incident arrived 12 days before shutdown and was invisible because the coding taxonomy had no concept of an allergen-inconsistent complaint. Consumer feedback channels should be engineered with the same rigor as laboratory monitoring.

**5. Speed without hypothesis discipline produces fast wrong answers.** The April 24 investigation's error was not slowness or carelessness; it was convergence on the first visible explanation, unprotected by any requirement to test it. A confirmed-root-cause gate before restart is the structural remedy — it makes the cost of an untested hypothesis a delayed restart rather than a larger recall.

**6. Known risks age badly.** The organization had already written down the fix for this incident's root cause in March. The gap between identifying a risk and implementing its control is itself a risk, and it needs an owner, a clock, and an escalation path.

**7. What went right must be protected.** The retained sample program, lot traceability, decisive shutdown authority, and proactive regulatory posture limited a serious incident from becoming a worse one. These capabilities were resourced decisions too, and they should be recognized as such when future resource decisions are made.
