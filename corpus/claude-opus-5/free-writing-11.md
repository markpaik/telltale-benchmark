# The Lamplighter's Objection

There is a class of argument I keep circling back to, and I want to set it down properly, because I think it is more interesting than the uses it usually gets put to.

The argument runs like this. Suppose you are a lamplighter in 1890. Every evening at dusk you walk your route with a long brass pole and a wick, and one by one the gas lamps come up along the street, and the street becomes a place where people can walk. You know the route in your feet. You know which lamp on Cathcart Street has a temperamental valve and needs a moment's patience. You know the widow at number 12 waits for your lamp before she draws her curtains, and you have come, without ever discussing it, to light hers first.

Then the electric arc lights arrive. They are brighter, cheaper, safer, and they come on all at once at the throw of a switch. Nobody has to walk anywhere.

The standard telling of this story is a story about progress, and the lamplighter is a minor casualty of it — sympathetic, obsolete, a man whose skill evaporated. The moral is usually addressed to the lamplighter's descendants: learn to service the dynamo.

But I have never been able to shake the sense that the standard telling drops something on the floor and then walks briskly away from the spot.

---

Here is what it drops. The lamplighter was not, in fact, in the business of producing photons. That is what he *did*, but it is not what he *was for*. He was a recurring human event in the life of a street. He was the thing that happened at dusk. The light was the occasion of his presence, not its purpose, and when the light was decoupled from the presence, something disappeared that nobody had thought to name, because it had never before been possible to have the one without the other.

This is the shape of the argument I care about. Not "the old way was better" — it usually wasn't, and the arc lights really were better lights. The shape is: *when a bundled thing comes apart, the component nobody was tracking goes missing, and its absence is very difficult to notice, because absence has no advocate.*

The widow at number 12 did not write a letter to the Corporation complaining that the new lights, while excellent, had removed the one reliable moment in her day when a person she recognized passed beneath her window. She probably could not have articulated it. She may not have known it herself until years later, if ever. What she would have said, if asked, is that the new lights were very good and very bright.

---

I want to be careful here, because this argument has a bad neighborhood it can wander into.

The bad neighborhood is nostalgia dressed as analysis. There is a version of the lamplighter's objection that gets deployed against every change whatsoever, and it always sounds profound, because it is unfalsifiable. *Something ineffable has been lost.* Well — perhaps. Something ineffable is always being lost, and also always being made, and the ledger cannot be audited because both columns are written in invisible ink. A person who runs this argument reflexively is not thinking; they are performing a mood.

The historical record is fairly brutal to reflexive lamplighters. The people who mourned the loss of the manuscript scriptorium were mourning, in large part, the loss of a bottleneck. The people who found the railway an assault on the proper human relationship to distance were describing motion sickness and calling it metaphysics. The letter-writing culture that the telephone supposedly destroyed was, for most people, not a culture at all; most people did not write letters, because most people could not write.

So the objection needs a discipline. It needs some way of distinguishing the widow at number 12 from a man who simply dislikes electricity.

---

The discipline I have arrived at is this: **ask whether the lost component was doing structural work.**

Not whether it was pleasant. Not whether people were fond of it. Whether something else was resting on it.

The lamplighter's rounds were load-bearing in a specific way: they were an involuntary, unchosen, recurring contact between a household and the wider world. The widow did not opt into them. She did not schedule them. She could not have cancelled them without cancelling her own light. And that quality — the *unchosenness* — is the thing doing the structural work, because a person's sense of being situated in a place is built almost entirely out of contacts they did not choose.

You can test this. Take away all the unchosen contacts from a life and see what happens. The person still has friends, perhaps more of them, better selected, more congenial. What they no longer have is a *street*. They have a network, which is a different object, and which has the peculiar property that it can be maintained perfectly while the physical space around the person becomes, functionally, weather.

So the disciplined version of the objection is not "the old thing was warm." It is: "the old thing was a hidden load-bearing member, and you have removed it in order to improve the wall's appearance, and the wall does look better, and the ceiling will come down in eleven years, and when it does nobody will connect it to the wall."

---

The eleven-year lag is the crux of the whole problem, and it is why I think the objection deserves more respect than it gets.

Improvements arrive fast and legibly. The arc lights went in over a season, and everyone could see they were brighter. Structural consequences arrive slowly and illegibly, and by the time they arrive, the counterfactual is unrecoverable. Nobody in 1930 could run the experiment where the lamplighter kept walking. The comparison that would settle the question is precisely the comparison that history refuses to provide.

Which means the objection can never be *proven* in the case where it is true. It can only be proven in the case where it is false — where the change happens and nothing goes wrong and the objector is revealed as a sentimentalist. The error is asymmetric. False alarms are exposed; true alarms are absorbed into the ambient condition of the world and become "how things are."

This is a genuinely bad epistemic situation. It means that the reflexive lamplighters — the ones who are wrong most of the time — are the only ones generating the signal that would ever catch the case where the objection is right. They are wrong, wrong, wrong, wrong, and then once they are right, and the once cannot be distinguished from the wrongs in advance, and afterward it cannot be distinguished at all, because afterward there is no *before* to compare it to.

---

I do not have a resolution. I want to say that clearly rather than manufacture one, because manufacturing one is the essay-writer's version of the same error — smoothing a real structural gap because the shape of the piece calls for a beam there.

What I have instead is a practice, which is smaller than a resolution but not nothing.

The practice is: **when a bundle comes apart, write down what was in it.** Not to prevent the unbundling — usually you shouldn't and usually you can't. Just to have the list. Because the specific harm of the lamplighter case is not that the lamplighter went away. It is that nobody wrote down that he had been doing two jobs, so when the second job stopped getting done, there was no record that it had ever been a job at all. The loss became invisible not at the moment of loss but at the moment of forgetting that a thing had been bundled.

A list is a weak instrument. It does not tell you which items matter. It does not stop anything. But it converts an invisible absence into a visible one, and an absence with a name can at least be argued about. Somebody in 1935 could have said: *we used to have a thing where a recognized person passed every house at dusk; we do not have that now; is anything else doing that work?* And the answer might have been yes, plenty, the milkman, the newspaper boy, the parish visitor. Fine. Good. The question was still worth asking, and the asking was cheap, and it is the *cheapness* of the asking against the *unrecoverability* of the answer that makes the asymmetry worth respecting.

---

There is a last thing, which is the reason I actually wanted to write this down.

The lamplighter himself knew. Not the whole argument — he did not have the vocabulary of load-bearing members and unbundled components. But he knew about the widow at number 12. He knew he lit her lamp first, and he knew why, and he knew it was not part of his job.

That knowledge existed nowhere else. It was not in the Corporation's records. It was not in the specification of the route. It was held in one man's head as a kind of unofficial supplement to his employment, and when the position was eliminated, the knowledge was eliminated with it — not because anyone destroyed it but because there was no receptacle for it. It was never written down because it was never *sayable* in the register in which such things get written down. What would he have written? *Lamp 12 first, on account of she waits.* On what form? Filed where?

So the deepest version of the objection is not about lamplighters or lamps. It is about the enormous quantity of load-bearing knowledge that exists only inside roles, that is generated as a byproduct of doing a thing repeatedly in a place among people, that has no institutional form and no place to go, and that evaporates the instant the role does. Every unbundling is also a small burning of an unindexed library, and the reason we underrate this is that unindexed libraries produce no catalogue of what they held.

I think about this more than I probably should. It is not an argument against anything in particular. It is closer to a request: that when we are pleased with ourselves for having separated the light from the man who carried it — and we should be pleased, the light is better — we spend one minute, before moving on, trying to say out loud what else he was carrying.

Most of the time the answer will be *nothing much.* That is fine. That is the expected result. The minute is cheap.

It is the eleven years that are expensive.
