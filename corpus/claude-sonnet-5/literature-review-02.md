# Twelve-Hour Shifts and Injury Risk in Heavy Manufacturing: A Review for Southwest Advanced Manufacturing Institute Member Plants

**Prepared by Dr. Sofia Kuznetsova, Research Director, and Bernard Achebe-Lomax, CIH**
**Southwest Advanced Manufacturing Institute, Chandler, Arizona**
**July 2026**

---

## 1. Introduction

In April, a worker at a member foundry died during the eleventh hour of a night shift. The Southwest Advanced Manufacturing Institute board commissioned this review in response, but the question it raises predates the fatality and will outlast it. Three member plants are converting from eight-hour to twelve-hour rotating shifts. A fourth converted and then reversed course last year, after its recordable injury rate rose from 2.9 to 4.6 per 100 workers in the eighteen months following the change. Operations council chair Doyle Ferris, whose 700-person plant has run twelve-hour schedules for over a decade, argues that his workforce would not tolerate a return to eight-hour shifts, and that the retention and morale benefits of the compressed schedule are not adequately weighed against injury statistics drawn from unrelated industries and eras.

Both positions can point to evidence. That is the central difficulty this review confronts, and it is worth stating plainly at the outset: the scientific literature on extended-hour shift schedules does not converge on a single answer to the question "are twelve-hour shifts more dangerous than eight-hour shifts?" It converges instead on a set of conditional answers, dependent on rotation direction, napping policy, overtime practice, ambient heat, task type, and how "risk" is measured — injuries, near-misses, quality escapes, vigilance decrement, or subjective fatigue. Two large meta-analyses conducted a generation apart, using substantially overlapping primary literatures, reach different conclusions about the same schedule types. This is not evidence of bad science on either side; it reflects genuine heterogeneity in how twelve-hour schedules are implemented and in what workplaces they are studied.

This review does not attempt to resolve that heterogeneity into a single recommendation applicable to all 38 member plants. Member facilities differ in process (continuous casting and foundry work versus discrete assembly), climate exposure, overtime culture, and workforce age structure, and the evidence base differs in its coverage of these variables. What this review offers is a structured account of where the literature agrees, where it disagrees and why, what has not been adequately studied, and what a plant considering a schedule change should therefore examine locally rather than assume from the literature.

The scope is bounded to production and manufacturing settings, or settings judged close enough in task demand (heavy industry, process manufacturing, foundry and casting operations, and comparably physically demanding continuous-operations work) that findings are likely to transfer. We excluded studies confined to healthcare nursing schedules and long-haul transportation except where they were used as comparison groups within included meta-analyses, since the shift-length literature in those sectors is large enough to dominate a pooled analysis without necessarily reflecting manufacturing task demand, thermal exposure, or machinery risk.

## 2. Search Methods

We searched Scopus, PsycINFO, MEDLINE and a federal occupational safety and health database covering the period 1985 through 2025. Search terms combined shift-length descriptors (twelve-hour, 12-hr, compressed workweek, extended shift, rotating shift) with outcome terms (injury, accident, vigilance, fatigue, error, quality defect, turnover, retention, sleep) and setting terms (manufacturing, industrial, production, plant, foundry, process industry). The 1985 start date reflects the point at which twelve-hour schedules began appearing with any frequency in North American and European manufacturing, following earlier adoption in continuous-process industries such as petrochemicals.

The search returned 1,932 records after de-duplication. Title and abstract screening against inclusion criteria (production or comparably demanding industrial setting; injury, error, vigilance, sleep, or retention outcome; twelve-hour schedule as an exposure or comparison arm) reduced this to 310 records read in full. Ninety-four studies met full inclusion criteria: 39 field studies (injury records, incident reporting, or observational cohort designs conducted in operating plants), 21 laboratory studies of sleep, vigilance, or simulated task performance under controlled shift-length manipulation, 18 surveys addressing worker preference, satisfaction, or retention intention, and ten meta-analyses or systematic reviews pooling subsets of the above.

Effect sizes, shift pattern details (rotation direction, shift start time, number of consecutive shifts, break structure), and exposure measures for all 94 included studies are archived in the consortium research database and are cited here by author and year; readers seeking pooled effect estimates or study-level detail should consult that database rather than this narrative synthesis, which is organized thematically rather than study by study.

Two limitations of the search are worth flagging before the synthesis. First, non-English-language literature was not systematically searched, which likely underrepresents relevant European continuous-process industry findings, particularly from German and Scandinavian sources known to have produced early twelve-hour schedule research. Second, gray literature — internal company safety reports, insurer loss data, and unpublished consultant studies — was not searched, meaning the review likely under-samples null or negative findings that companies had no incentive to publish, a form of publication bias whose direction is difficult to predict since both proponents and opponents of schedule change have incentive to publicize favorable results.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Injury outcomes: the central disagreement

The foundational disagreement in this literature is between laboratory-demonstrated performance decrement and field-measured injury rates, and it recurs at every level of the synthesis below.

Laboratory studies are close to unanimous that sustained vigilance and psychomotor performance degrade measurably after roughly the ninth hour of continuous wakefulness combined with time-on-task, with decrements steepening further after the eleventh hour (Halloran & Ferreira, 1994; Okonkwo, 2001; Lindqvist & Brannigan, 2003; Petrossian, 2009; Dubois & Tran, 2014). These findings are highly consistent across laboratories, tasks (reaction time, simulated inspection, tracking tasks), and decades, and they were the basis for the widely cited "fatigue risk" models adopted by several regulatory bodies in transportation and used informally by safety officers arguing against twelve-hour manufacturing shifts.

Yet field studies measuring actual injury rates in operating plants running twelve-hour schedules do not consistently reproduce this pattern. Of the 39 field studies included, 17 found no statistically significant difference in injury rate between eight-hour and twelve-hour schedules after controlling for total hours worked and job type (Vasquez-Herrera, 1997; Okafor, 2002; Lindgren, 2006; Petráková, 2011; Whitfield & Osei, 2015; Choudhary, 2018, among others), and two found lower injury rates under fixed (non-rotating) twelve-hour schedules than under the eight-hour schedules they replaced (Almeida-Souza, 2008; Reyes-Kaplan, 2016). Fourteen studies found elevated injury risk associated with twelve-hour schedules, though the elevation was frequently concentrated in the last two hours of shift and in specific task categories — material handling and mobile equipment operation, in particular — rather than uniformly distributed across the shift (Marchetti, 1999; Boyko, 2004; Hutchings-Adeyemi, 2010; Sasaki & Colón, 2017). The remaining eight field studies had mixed or schedule-specific findings not easily classified either way (e.g., Dzhaparidze, 2013, which found elevated risk only in summer months, discussed in Section 3.5).

This is the pattern member plants should understand clearly before assuming the laboratory literature settles the question: laboratory vigilance decrement is a robust and repeated finding, but it does not translate uniformly into elevated injury rates measured in the field. The most plausible reconciliation, discussed across several of the included studies (Petráková, 2011; Whitfield & Osei, 2015), is that injury outcomes depend heavily on the interaction between vigilance decrement and task exposure — a fatigued worker performing a low-hazard, well-guarded task may show measurable vigilance decrement without a corresponding rise in injury, while the same decrement in a worker operating mobile equipment or handling hot metal carries materially different consequence. Foundry and casting operations, the setting of the April fatality, sit at the higher-consequence end of this spectrum, and the review team was unable to find field studies specific to foundry twelve-hour scheduling with sample sizes adequate to detect a moderate effect; this gap is addressed further in Section 4.

### 3.2 The dueling meta-analyses

Ten meta-analyses were included, but two dominate the debate because they pool substantially overlapping primary study sets and reach different headline conclusions. A 1996 meta-analysis (Reinholt, 1996), pooling 31 primary studies then available, concluded that twelve-hour shifts carried a modestly elevated injury risk (pooled relative risk approximately 1.13) relative to eight-hour shifts, an effect the authors characterized as small but consistent enough to warrant caution. This finding was widely cited through the following two decades and shaped much of the early opposition to twelve-hour conversion in manufacturing settings.

A 2019 meta-analysis (Castellano-Nakamura, 2019), incorporating 58 primary studies including many published after 1996 and applying updated methods for handling heterogeneous exposure measures, found no significant pooled effect of shift length on injury risk (pooled relative risk 1.02, confidence interval crossing 1.0), while finding a significant effect of consecutive-shift count and of rotation direction independent of raw shift length. The 2019 authors argued explicitly that the 1996 finding was likely driven by studies conducted during the earliest and least-refined period of twelve-hour schedule implementation, before plants had developed the nap policies, staffing buffers, and rotation practices that later research suggests moderate the risk (see Sections 3.3 and 3.4).

Three smaller meta-analyses conducted in the intervening years (Abubakar, 2004; Lindqvist, 2010; Moreau-Whitcombe, 2015) fall between these poles, generally finding small, schedule-dependent effects that reach significance for rotating but not fixed twelve-hour schedules. The remaining five meta-analyses in the included set addressed narrower outcomes — sleep quality (Federov, 2008), retention (Achterberg, 2012), and quality/error rates rather than injury (Osei-Bonsu, 2017; Nakagawa, 2020; Villareal-Kim, 2021) — and are discussed in the relevant thematic sections below.

The practical implication for the board is that citing "the meta-analysis" as though a single authoritative pooled estimate exists is not defensible; the field has produced two conflicting authoritative pooled estimates roughly twenty years apart, and the more recent, larger, and methodologically more careful of the two finds no significant raw shift-length effect once rotation direction and consecutive-shift count are accounted for separately. This does not mean twelve-hour shifts are safe; it means shift length alone is the wrong variable to argue about. The variables that consistently predict risk within the more recent literature are addressed next.

### 3.3 Rotation direction

A relatively consistent finding across both laboratory and field literatures is that the direction of shift rotation matters more than the raw duration of any single shift. Forward-rotating schedules (day to evening to night) are associated with better sleep quality, faster circadian adaptation, and lower injury risk than backward-rotating schedules (night to evening to day) across both twelve-hour and eight-hour comparisons (Lindqvist & Brannigan, 2003; Federov, 2008; Castellano-Nakamura, 2019). This finding held in 11 of the 13 field studies that reported rotation direction explicitly, and in all eight of the laboratory studies that manipulated rotation direction as a variable.

The one significant point of disagreement concerns the interaction between rotation direction and shift length: two field studies found that backward rotation on a twelve-hour schedule produced injury rates markedly worse than backward rotation on an eight-hour schedule (Boyko, 2004; Sasaki & Colón, 2017), suggesting the twelve-hour format may specifically amplify the harm of poor rotation design rather than being independently harmful. Three other studies found no such interaction, with backward rotation carrying similar elevated risk regardless of shift length (Marchetti, 1999; Abubakar, 2004; Petráková, 2011). This disagreement has not been resolved and appears related to differences in how quickly each schedule cycled through rotation (a fast-rotating backward twelve-hour schedule behaves differently from a slow-rotating one), a design detail not consistently reported across the primary literature, which itself is a measurement gap discussed in Section 4.

For the consortium, this is among the more actionable findings: any plant that has not already confirmed its rotation direction and cycle speed, and that continues to run backward-rotating schedules, is very likely operating under the schedule design most consistently associated with elevated risk in this literature, independent of the twelve-versus-eight-hour question entirely.

### 3.4 Napping and recovery policy

Sixteen of the included studies addressed on-shift napping or structured recovery breaks as a moderating variable, and here the literature is more consistent than in most other areas: permitted or scheduled naps during the latter portion of a twelve-hour night shift are associated with reduced vigilance decrement in laboratory settings (Okonkwo, 2001; Dubois & Tran, 2014) and with reduced injury rates in the field studies that examined nap policy directly (Almeida-Souza, 2008; Whitfield & Osei, 2015; Reyes-Kaplan, 2016). The magnitude of benefit reported varies considerably — from a modest reduction in near-miss reporting to the substantially lower injury rate reported by Almeida-Souza (2008) in a facility that had introduced a mandatory twenty-minute nap break in hour nine of night shifts — and no study in the included set used a randomized design, so causal interpretation should be cautious; plants that adopt nap policies may differ from those that do not in other safety-relevant ways.

Disagreement in this area centers less on whether naps help and more on whether they are operationally acceptable, a question the literature is not well positioned to answer since it concerns implementation rather than physiology. Two survey studies found strong worker resistance to formal nap policy on grounds of stigma and supervisor mistrust, even among workers who privately reported napping informally during breaks (Ferreira-Osman, 2013; Choudhary, 2018), suggesting that the gap between what the physiological literature recommends and what is culturally adoptable on a shop floor is itself an unstudied barrier worth the board's attention independent of the underlying evidence for nap efficacy.

### 3.5 Stacked overtime

Overtime stacked onto a base twelve-hour schedule — additional shifts or hours beyond the base rotation, common during demand surges or short-staffing — is one of the more clearly hazardous practices identified in this literature, and one of the few areas where field and laboratory findings agree without significant qualification. Field studies consistently find that injury risk rises sharply with cumulative hours across a rolling seven-day period, independent of base shift length, with the sharpest rise occurring when workers exceed roughly 60 cumulative hours in a week (Marchetti, 1999; Hutchings-Adeyemi, 2010; Moreau-Whitcombe, 2015 meta-analysis). Laboratory work correspondingly shows cumulative sleep debt effects on vigilance that compound across consecutive shortened-recovery days in a manner consistent with the field pattern (Petrossian, 2009; Dubois & Tran, 2014).

Where disagreement exists, it concerns threshold: some studies place the inflection point for meaningfully elevated risk closer to 48 cumulative hours (Okafor, 2002), others closer to 60 (Marchetti, 1999), and this variation is plausibly attributable to differences in recovery quality (a worker getting eight hours of sleep between stacked shifts differs from one getting five) that most field studies did not measure directly, relying instead on scheduled rather than actual hours. This is a case where the underlying disagreement is more a measurement limitation than a genuine empirical conflict, and it bears directly on the fourth member plant's experience: a facility moving to twelve-hour rotating shifts without simultaneously constraining overtime stacking is, per this literature, changing two variables at once and may attribute to "twelve-hour shifts" an injury increase substantially or entirely attributable to overtime practice layered on top of the new base schedule.

### 3.6 Heat exposure

Ambient heat interacts with shift length and timing in ways the literature has only unevenly captured, which is a significant concern for member plants operating in Arizona and the broader Southwest. Of the 39 field studies, only six reported thermal exposure data adequate to examine heat as a moderating variable, and these produce a genuinely inconsistent picture. One study found injury risk on twelve-hour day shifts during summer months roughly 40 percent higher than on equivalent winter-month shifts, an effect not observed on eight-hour day shifts in the same facility over the same period, suggesting a shift-length-by-heat interaction specific to extended exposure duration (Dzhaparidze, 2013). Two other studies found no significant heat interaction with shift length, though both were conducted in temperate-climate facilities where peak ambient temperatures were considerably lower than typical Arizona summer shop-floor conditions, limiting their relevance to member plants regardless of statistical outcome (Lindgren, 2006; Petráková, 2011). The remaining three studies addressed heat only as a control variable rather than a variable of primary interest and did not report effect estimates in a form usable for this synthesis.

This is one of the clearest gaps in the literature relative to the consortium's specific operating conditions, discussed further below, and it should carry particular weight in board deliberations given that Chandler-area member plants operate under thermal conditions more extreme than nearly all facilities studied in the included literature.

### 3.7 Quality escapes as distinct from injuries

A recurring theme across several field studies and two dedicated meta-analyses (Osei-Bonsu, 2017; Nakagawa, 2020) is that quality defect rates and injury rates do not move together under extended shift schedules, and treating them as proxies for the same underlying fatigue construct is not supported by the data. Several studies find quality escape rates rising measurably in the final two hours of twelve-hour shifts even where injury rates in the same facility show no corresponding rise (Osei-Bonsu, 2017; Villareal-Kim, 2021), consistent with a model in which fatigue affects fine-grained attentional tasks (inspection, precision assembly) before it affects the grosser motor and situational awareness demands more directly tied to injury causation. Conversely, one study found the opposite pattern — stable quality metrics alongside elevated injury rates — in a facility with heavy machinery exposure and comparatively little precision-inspection work (Boyko, 2004), reinforcing that the relationship between fatigue, task type, and outcome measure is not fixed across settings but depends on what a given job actually demands in its final hours.

The practical implication is that a plant's own quality data, where available, may be a more sensitive and faster-arriving indicator of accumulating fatigue-related risk than injury data, which is comparatively rare and therefore slow to reveal a trend statistically. This point is developed further in the implications section.

### 3.8 Preference and retention

The 18 survey studies included in this review present the most consistent findings of any thematic area, and they run in a direction that complicates rather than resolves the safety debate: workers overwhelmingly prefer twelve-hour schedules to eight-hour schedules, with preference driven predominantly by the additional consecutive days off rather than by any belief that the longer shift itself is preferable (Ferreira-Osman, 2013; Choudhary, 2018; Achterberg, 2012 meta-analysis). Preference margins in individual surveys range from roughly 65 percent to over 85 percent favoring twelve-hour schedules where workers had experience with both formats, and retention intention is consistently reported as higher under twelve-hour schedules, a finding directly relevant to Doyle Ferris's contention that his workforce would resist reversion.

Disagreement in this literature is narrower than elsewhere but not absent: two studies found that stated preference for twelve-hour schedules declined measurably among workers over a multi-year tenure on the schedule, suggesting either adaptation of expressed preference to circumstance, cumulative fatigue effects not captured in point-in-time surveys, or cohort attrition in which workers most bothered by the schedule had already left the sampled workforce by the time of survey (Lindgren, 2006; Federov, 2008). None of the included survey studies were able to distinguish among these explanations, and this represents a further gap: preference data are almost entirely cross-sectional, and the few longitudinal exceptions do not agree on direction of change over tenure.

## 4. Gaps

Several gaps in the evidence base limit how confidently this literature can be applied to the consortium's specific decision.

**Age.** Workers over 55 are substantially underrepresented in the included studies; where age is reported at all, the over-55 category typically constitutes under 8 percent of pooled samples, and no included study had a subsample of older workers large enough to support a separately powered analysis of age-by-shift-length interaction. This matters because circadian adaptation and recovery from sleep restriction are both known from broader physiological literature to change with age, and several consortium member plants have workforce age profiles skewing considerably older than the study samples reviewed here. The literature simply does not tell us how a 58-year-old worker's injury risk under a rotating twelve-hour schedule compares to a 30-year-old's, because too few older workers have been studied on these schedules to say.

**Sex.** Women constitute under one-fifth of pooled samples across the included studies, and no included study reported a sex-stratified injury or vigilance analysis with adequate power. Given that several member plants have workforces with women representing a considerably larger share than in the reviewed literature, this is a second population for which the evidence base offers limited direct guidance.

**Foundry-specific field data.** As noted in Section 3.1, field studies with adequate sample size specific to foundry and casting operations under twelve-hour schedules were not identified in this search. Given that the triggering event for this review was a foundry fatality, this is a significant limitation: much of the injury literature synthesized above comes from discrete manufacturing, assembly, and general process-industry settings whose hazard profile (molten metal handling, extreme localized heat, specialized mobile equipment) differs materially from foundry work. Extrapolation from the general manufacturing literature to foundry operations specifically should be treated as provisional.

**Heat exposure at Southwest-relevant intensity.** As discussed in Section 3.6, only six field studies reported thermal exposure data, none from a climate as consistently hot as central Arizona summer conditions. This is arguably the single most consequential gap for this consortium specifically, since it means the literature's treatment of one of the variables most likely to be operative at member plants is thin even by the standards of an already heterogeneous evidence base.

**Longitudinal and cumulative-tenure data.** Most field and laboratory studies capture outcomes over weeks or single seasons rather than years; the two survey studies noted in Section 3.8 that found declining preference over tenure are exceptions, and no included study tracked injury risk longitudinally across a worker's multi-year tenure on a twelve-hour schedule. Cumulative long-term health and injury-risk effects, as distinct from short-term vigilance and immediate injury effects, remain essentially unstudied in this literature.

**Rotation cycle speed and detail.** As noted in Section 3.3, primary studies inconsistently report rotation cycle speed, direction, and the specific interaction between direction and consecutive-shift count. This is less an unstudied population gap than a reporting-standard gap, but it materially limits the precision with which the more nuanced, actionable findings in this review (rotation direction, cumulative hours) can be applied without a plant examining its own specific schedule design in detail.

## 5. Implications for Member Plants

Given the above, several implications follow for member plants evaluating or operating twelve-hour schedules, offered as considerations for local evaluation rather than a uniform prescription, since the evidence base does not support a uniform prescription.

First, the board should resist framing this decision as a binary choice between eight-hour and twelve-hour schedules considered as monolithic categories. The literature's more consistent and more actionable findings concern design details within the twelve-hour format — rotation direction, cumulative overtime hours, and nap policy — rather than shift length per se. A plant converting to twelve-hour rotating shifts while maintaining forward rotation, capping cumulative weekly hours below roughly 48–50, and permitting structured recovery breaks during night shifts is, per this literature, operating under materially different risk than a plant converting to backward-rotating twelve-hour shifts with unconstrained overtime stacking. The fourth member plant's reversal, following a rise in recordable injury rate from 2.9 to 4.6, should be examined specifically for which of these design variables were present, since the literature suggests the schedule-length conversion itself may not have been the operative cause.

Second, plants should treat quality escape data, where systematically collected, as a potentially earlier and more statistically tractable warning signal than injury data. Injuries are comparatively rare events, and detecting a true change in injury rate against normal statistical noise takes time and volume that a single plant may not accumulate quickly; quality metrics, where fine-grained and time-stamped within shift, may reveal a fatigue-related trend sooner, per the pattern described in Section 3.7 — with the caveat that this relationship is task-dependent and plants with primarily gross-motor or machinery-operation work rather than precision inspection may not see the same leading-indicator relationship.

Third, given the heat-exposure gap identified in Section 4, member plants operating in high ambient-temperature conditions, which describes most of the consortium's Arizona facilities, should not assume that field findings from temperate-climate studies transfer directly. Local monitoring of injury and near-miss timing relative to shift hour during summer months, compared to winter months, would generate consortium-specific evidence the existing literature cannot supply, and is a comparatively low-cost step available to any member plant already collecting incident timestamps.

Fourth, given the foundry-data gap, the board should treat the general manufacturing literature synthesized here as informative but not conclusive for foundry and casting operations specifically, and should weigh the April fatality accordingly: the literature offers reasonable confidence about rotation direction and overtime effects in general manufacturing settings, and considerably less confidence about how those findings apply to foundry-specific hazard exposure in the critical late-shift hours.

Fifth, the age and sex composition gaps mean that any plant with a workforce age or sex profile substantially different from the pooled study samples described in Section 4 — which is likely true of several member plants — should treat the aggregate injury-risk findings as approximations rather than precise estimates for its specific workforce, and should consider whether its own injury and near-miss records can be examined by age band, even informally, to check whether the general pattern holds locally.

Sixth, worker preference for twelve-hour schedules is genuine, consistent, and should not be dismissed as a secondary consideration; it is among the most robust findings in this literature. But the literature is equally clear that preference is not evidence of safety, and the two considerations should be weighed as separate questions rather than allowing strong preference evidence to substitute for weak or absent safety evidence in facility-specific decisions, particularly at foundry and other high-consequence sites.

Finally, the board may wish to consider commissioning a consortium-wide standardized data-collection protocol — capturing rotation direction, cumulative weekly hours, nap policy, ambient temperature, and shift-hour-stamped injury, near-miss, and quality data uniformly across member plants — given that several of the gaps identified above are gaps the consortium itself is well positioned to close, at modest incremental cost, given that 24,000 production workers across 38 plants already generate this data in fragmented and inconsistently structured form.
