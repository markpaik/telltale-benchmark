## 1. Executive Summary

The first half of fiscal 2026 tested the network in ways that the fiscal 2025 planning cycle did not fully anticipate. Volume across the five distribution centers held broadly to plan, and four of five sites remained profitable on a fully loaded basis, but the half was defined by three problems that the executive committee must treat as connected rather than separate: cost discipline eroding at our highest-volume site in Indianapolis, service quality deteriorating at Groveport and Carlisle, and a startup at Winchester that is absorbing cash faster than contracted volume is arriving to cover it.

The most consequential item remains Carlisle. The site shipped on time at 91.3 percent against a 98 percent contractual commitment to Beaumont Home Brands, and in April Beaumont placed the building on a formal performance improvement plan. Beaumont represents 31 percent of company revenue. The improvement plan carries a review gate in October, and the warehouse management system upgrade that was central to Carlisle's recovery path has slipped from March to September — meaning the site must demonstrate performance recovery through the plan period largely on process discipline and labor stability rather than on new tooling, and must then absorb a system cutover in the same month it begins peak-season ramp. Section 7 treats this risk in detail. It is the single largest operational and commercial exposure in the company.

The second structural item is Winchester. The building opened in August 2025 with a contracted volume commitment that has not materialized; the site is receiving 68 percent of contracted volume and losing approximately $180,000 per month, an annualized drag of roughly $2.2 million. The shortfall is a commercial problem before it is an operational one, and Section 8 lays out the options: enforcement of minimum-volume provisions, backfill with new business, cost restructuring of the building, or some combination.

Elsewhere, Indianapolis delivered the network's best quality performance at 99.4 percent order accuracy while missing its unit cost budget by three cents — a variance that at 41.2 million units is worth roughly $1.24 million, driven principally by a 62 percent annualized warehouse turnover rate and the training and productivity costs that follow it. Groveport, our second-largest building by throughput, allowed order accuracy to fall to 98.1 percent against a 99.5 percent service level and absorbed $214,000 in customer chargebacks. Louisville is the opposite problem: a small, full building running at 96 percent of storage capacity, 18 percent overtime, and turning away inbound freight — two refused trailers in June — because there is no dock capacity to receive it.

The network enters the second half with adequate but not comfortable margins for error. Peak season planning must assume Carlisle executes a system cutover under customer scrutiny, that Louisville has no surge capacity, and that Winchester's cost base is not yet matched to its volume. The recommendations in Section 11 are structured around those three constraints.

## 3. Indianapolis, Indiana

**Profile.** 610,000 square feet; the network's highest-throughput building at 41.2 million units shipped in the half; multi-client with a concentration in consumer packaged goods and a growing e-commerce fulfillment segment.

**Volume and cost.** Indianapolis shipped 41.2 million units, roughly four percent above plan, and did so at $0.42 per unit against a $0.39 budget. The three-cent unfavorable variance across 41.2 million units represents approximately $1.24 million in unbudgeted cost in the half. The variance decomposes into three drivers of roughly equal weight: (1) new-hire training hours and the productivity ramp of a workforce that is turning over at 62 percent annualized; (2) temporary agency labor used to bridge staffing gaps, which carries a markup of 28 to 34 percent over direct wages; and (3) overtime deployed in weeks where absenteeism spiked, running between 9 and 12 percent of hours through the second quarter.

**Quality.** Order accuracy of 99.4 percent is the best in the network and above the building's 99.2 percent commitment. This is a genuine achievement given the labor churn, and it reflects the quality-gate scanning process the site implemented in fiscal 2025. The site recorded no material customer chargebacks in the half.

**Labor.** The 62 percent annualized turnover rate is the central story of this building. It compares to a network average of 46 percent and a regional market benchmark, per our third-quarter wage survey, of approximately 51 percent for comparable warehouse work in the Indianapolis metro. Exit-interview data collected by site HR points to two dominant causes: a starting wage that has fallen 90 cents to $1.40 below three named competitors within a ten-mile radius, and dissatisfaction with mandatory weekend scheduling introduced in February to cover the volume upside. Each departing associate costs an estimated $3,800 to $4,600 in recruiting, onboarding, training, and reduced-productivity ramp; at the current rate of separations, that is $2.6 to $3.2 million in annualized friction cost — the true source of the unit-cost miss.

**Assessment.** Indianapolis is a well-run building with a wage-competitiveness problem. The site leadership team has held quality through the churn, but the cost line will not recover until turnover does. A wage adjustment of $1.00 at the entry tier for the Indianapolis building would cost approximately $1.7 million annualized; against $2.6 to $3.2 million in turnover friction, the arithmetic favors the adjustment, and Section 11 recommends it be approved before peak hiring begins in September.

## 4. Groveport, Ohio

**Profile.** 900,000 square feet; the network's second-largest building; 28.7 million units shipped in the half; anchor client mix in housewares and general merchandise, including secondary Beaumont Home Brands volume.

**Volume and cost.** Throughput of 28.7 million units was essentially on plan. Unit cost of $0.44 against a $0.43 budget is a modest miss, and would be unremarkable were it not for the composition: approximately $214,000 of the variance is customer chargebacks, which is to say the site paid for its quality miss twice — once in rework and once in penalties.

**Quality.** This is the site's problem. Order accuracy fell to 98.1 percent against a contracted service level of 99.5 percent, a gap of 140 basis points on a building shipping 28.7 million units — roughly 400,000 more defective or inaccurate order lines than the contract permits. The $214,000 in chargebacks breaks down as follows.

**Table 2. Groveport Chargeback Composition, First Half FY2026**

| Category | Amount | Share | Primary Root Cause |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mis-picks / wrong item shipped | $96,000 | 45% | Slotting errors after March re-profile; look-alike SKU adjacency |
| Short shipments | $61,000 | 29% | Inventory record inaccuracy; cycle count program understaffed |
| Labeling / compliance violations | $38,000 | 18% | Routing-guide changes by two retail customers not loaded timely |
| Damage in transit attributable to packing | $19,000 | 9% | Packing standard drift on third shift |
| **Total** | **$214,000** | **100%** | — |

The root-cause pattern is instructive. Nearly half the chargeback exposure traces to a March slotting re-profile that placed look-alike SKUs in adjacent pick locations without a corresponding update to pick-verification rules. The site identified this in May and completed a corrective re-slot of the 340 highest-risk locations by June 20; accuracy in the final two weeks of June recovered to 99.0 percent, which suggests the fix is working but has not yet closed the gap to the 99.5 percent commitment.

**Labor.** Turnover at Groveport ran 44 percent annualized — near network average — and overtime at 8 percent of hours. Staffing is not the constraint here; process control is. The cycle count program has been running with four counters against a staffing model that calls for seven, and inventory record accuracy of 97.2 percent (against a 99.0 percent internal standard) is the upstream cause of the short-shipment chargebacks.

**Assessment.** Groveport's issues are correctable and largely self-inflicted. The recovery plan requires three commitments in the third quarter: full staffing of the cycle count team, completion of the pick-verification rule update for the remaining re-slotted locations, and a standing routing-guide change-management process with a named owner, since two of the compliance chargebacks resulted from customer routing changes that sat in a shared inbox for over three weeks. Given Groveport carries Beaumont volume, its accuracy miss will be visible in the joint session, and we should present the June recovery trend proactively rather than defensively.

## 5. Louisville, Kentucky

**Profile.** 450,000 square feet; the network's smallest legacy building; 14.9 million units shipped; regional distribution for two food-adjacent consumer clients with high inventory turns and appointment-driven inbound.

**Volume and cost.** Louisville shipped 14.9 million units at $0.47 per unit against a $0.44 budget. The variance is almost entirely overtime: the site ran 18 percent of total hours on overtime through the half, more than double the network norm and the highest figure in the company. Overtime at that sustained level is not a scheduling choice; it is a symptom of a building that has run out of room.

**Capacity.** The site operated at 96 percent of storage capacity for most of the half, with pallet positions above 98 percent occupancy in four separate weeks. Industry practice treats 85 percent as the threshold above which productivity begins to degrade measurably — travel paths lengthen, honeycombing increases, product gets double-handled into overflow locations and handled again coming out. Louisville has been above that threshold for at least nine consecutive months. The consequences arrived concretely in June, when the site refused two inbound trailers for lack of dock and staging space. Both refusals generated detention and diversion costs (approximately $6,400 combined, absorbed by us) and, more importantly, a client escalation from the affected shipper, who has asked for a written capacity assurance before committing fourth-quarter promotional volume.

**Quality and labor.** To the site's credit, order accuracy held at 99.1 percent and on-time shipping at 97.9 percent despite the congestion. Turnover ran 38 percent — the lowest in the network — reflecting a tenured workforce, though supervisors report fatigue-related concerns given the sustained overtime, and recordable-injury data (Section 10) shows Louisville's incident rate ticking up in the second quarter, with two of its three recordables occurring during overtime hours.

**Assessment.** Louisville does not have a management problem; it has a physics problem. There are three paths, not mutually exclusive: (1) an off-site overflow arrangement — approximately 60,000 to 80,000 square feet of short-term space is available in the Riverport submarket at an estimated $310,000 to $390,000 annualized, which is less than the current annualized overtime premium of roughly $470,000; (2) a client-level inventory rationalization, since aging analysis shows about 11 percent of on-hand pallets have not moved in over 180 days and could be candidates for return, disposal, or client-funded outside storage; and (3) a longer-term decision about whether Louisville's client base has outgrown the building, which belongs in the fiscal 2027 network strategy discussion. Peak season makes options (1) and (2) urgent — the building cannot absorb a seasonal surge in its current state, and a repeat of the June trailer refusals during a client's promotional window would be a commercial event, not just an operational one.

## 6. Carlisle, Pennsylvania

**Profile.** 1.1 million square feet; the network's largest building; general manager Tanya Broussard; dedicated primarily to Beaumont Home Brands, the customer representing 31 percent of company revenue; 22.4 million units shipped in the half.

**Volume and cost.** Carlisle shipped 22.4 million units, roughly on plan, at $0.49 per unit against a $0.45 budget. The four-cent miss — approximately $900,000 in the half — is dominated by the costs of poor service: expedited outbound freight to recover late orders, rework labor, and premium staffing deployed in April and May to stabilize the operation after the improvement plan was issued.

**Service performance.** The site shipped on time at 91.3 percent against a 98 percent contractual commitment. This is a severe miss on any terms, and it is the direct cause of the performance improvement plan Beaumont issued in April. The monthly trend matters more than the half-year average:

**Table 3. Carlisle On-Time Ship Performance by Month, First Half FY2026**

| Month | On-Time Ship % | Commitment | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| January | 94.1% | 98.0% | Post-holiday inventory reconciliation backlog |
| February | 92.6% | 98.0% | Wave-planning failures; two weather closure days |
| March | 88.4% | 98.0% | WMS upgrade postponed; workaround processes strained |
| April | 87.9% | 98.0% | Low point; Beaumont issues PIP April 14 |
| May | 92.8% | 98.0% | Stabilization actions take hold |
| June | 95.9% | 98.0% | Improving trend; still below commitment |

The trajectory shows a building that deteriorated through the first quarter, bottomed in April, and has recovered meaningfully — but not sufficiently — since. Order accuracy at 98.8 percent has been less of an issue than timeliness; the failure mode is orders shipping late, not orders shipping wrong.

**Root causes.** Ms. Broussard's own diagnostic, which the analytics team has validated, identifies four interacting causes. First, the site's legacy warehouse management system cannot support dynamic wave planning at Beaumont's current order profile, which has shifted over eighteen months toward smaller, more frequent orders with tighter ship windows; the site is running a batch-wave process designed for a case-pick profile that no longer exists. Second, the WMS upgrade meant to fix this — originally committed for March — slipped to September (Section 7). Third, the site entered the year understaffed in its outbound shipping department by approximately 22 heads after fourth-quarter attrition was not backfilled on schedule. Fourth, dock-door scheduling conflicts with Beaumont's own carrier network caused late trailer availability on roughly 15 percent of scheduled outbound loads in the first quarter — a shared-cause item that we have documented carefully because it is relevant to the improvement plan discussions.

**Labor.** Carlisle's turnover ran 49 percent annualized. The outbound staffing gap has been closed as of June 1, and the site added a second wave-planning analyst in May.

**Assessment.** Carlisle is recovering, but the recovery is running on manual discipline — earlier wave releases, a daily order-aging review chaired personally by Ms. Broussard, and premium staffing — rather than on structural fixes. That posture is sustainable through the summer but is expensive and fragile. The full improvement plan status and the system-upgrade risk are treated in the next section because they are, at this point, a single intertwined problem.

## 7. The Carlisle Improvement Plan and the Delayed WMS Upgrade

**Terms of the plan.** Beaumont Home Brands issued the performance improvement plan on April 14, 2026, following the March and April service misses. Its material terms: on-time ship performance of 96.0 percent or better for the July–September measurement quarter; 97.5 percent or better for October–December; a formal review gate on October 15 at which Beaumont may, per the master agreement, invoke volume-reallocation rights covering up to 25 percent of Carlisle throughput; monthly joint operating reviews, which have been held on schedule since April; and a corrective-action plan with named owners and dates, which was delivered May 1 and accepted by Beaumont May 9.

**Commercial exposure.** Beaumont is 31 percent of company revenue — approximately $96 million annualized. Carlisle carries the majority of that relationship. A 25 percent volume reallocation from Carlisle would represent roughly $14 to $17 million in annualized revenue at risk directly, and the relationship risk beyond the contractual mechanics is larger: a customer that has exercised reallocation rights once has established both the precedent and the alternative supply arrangements to do it again. The October 15 gate is, in practical terms, the most important date on the company's calendar this year.

**The WMS upgrade delay.** The warehouse management system upgrade was the structural centerpiece of the recovery plan and was originally scheduled to go live in March 2026. It has slipped to September. The delay traces to three causes: integration testing failures between the new WMS and Beaumont's order-management system discovered in January (four interface defects, all now resolved); the departure of the implementation vendor's lead architect in February, which cost approximately six weeks of schedule; and a decision — correct, in my assessment — by Ms. Broussard and Mr. Villanueva in March that cutting over a struggling building onto new software mid-crisis would compound rather than resolve the service problem.

The consequence, however, is a difficult sequencing problem. The upgrade is now scheduled to go live the week of September 14, which places the cutover: (a) inside the July–September measurement quarter of the improvement plan, (b) approximately four weeks before the October 15 review gate, and (c) at the front edge of peak-season ramp. WMS cutovers, even well-executed ones, typically depress throughput and service for two to four weeks. A cutover that goes poorly in September could single-handedly cause a failure at the October gate.

**Risk mitigation in place.** The program has adopted the following controls: a phased go-live beginning with inbound and inventory functions only, deferring the outbound and wave-planning modules to a second phase in early October if September readiness criteria are not met in full; a parallel-run period of ten operating days with the legacy system available for fallback; a go/no-go decision on September 4 chaired by Mr. Villanueva with defined readiness criteria (interface stability across a two-week test window, 100 percent of supervisors trained, cycle count accuracy above 99 percent entering conversion); and proactive disclosure to Beaumont — the customer has been briefed on the cutover plan and, in the June joint review, indicated qualified support for the September date provided the phased approach and fallback plan hold.

**Decision required.** The committee should be aware of an alternative under active discussion: deferring the full cutover to January 2027, after both the October gate and peak. The case for September is that the site's manual workarounds are expensive, fragile, and themselves a service risk through peak. The case for January is that it removes cutover risk from the two highest-stakes periods of the year at the cost of one more peak on the legacy system and continued premium operating cost of roughly $150,000 per month. My recommendation, detailed in Section 11, is to hold the September date for the inbound/inventory phase and make the outbound-phase timing decision at the September 4 gate based on demonstrated readiness — but this is properly an executive committee decision and should be settled before the joint session with Beaumont.

## 8. Winchester, Virginia

**Profile.** Approximately 300,000 square feet; opened August 2025 as a dedicated startup for a mid-Atlantic e-commerce and retail client under a five-year agreement with contracted minimum volumes; 6.1 million units shipped in the half.

**Volume shortfall.** Winchester is receiving 68 percent of contracted volume. The client's own sell-through has underperformed its launch forecast, and two product lines that were to route through Winchester have been delayed at the client's sourcing end. The shortfall has been consistent, not episodic — monthly receipts have run between 63 and 73 percent of contract since November — which indicates a structural demand gap rather than a ramp timing issue that will self-correct.

**Financial position.** The site is losing approximately $180,000 per month, an annualized drag of roughly $2.2 million. The loss is a fixed-cost absorption problem: the building was staffed, racked, and equipped for contract volume, and its cost base cannot flex down proportionally. At 68 percent of contracted volume, actual unit cost is running $0.61 against a $0.46 budget built on full contract volume. It is worth stating clearly that Winchester's operational execution is good — 99.0 percent order accuracy, 98.8 percent on-time shipping, the best safety record in the network, and turnover of 41 percent. The building is performing; the contract is not.

**Contractual position.** The agreement contains a minimum-volume commitment with a shortfall payment mechanism. We have not yet invoked it. Through the first half, the accrued shortfall payment entitlement is approximately $640,000. The commercial team's judgment to date has been that invoking the clause in the first year of a five-year relationship risks souring a client whose long-term forecast remains attractive. That judgment was defensible through the ramp period; twelve months in, with the shortfall structural, it should be revisited.

**Options.** Four paths, which can be combined:

1. **Invoke the shortfall mechanism**, in full or as a negotiated partial settlement, recovering some or all of the $640,000 accrued and establishing that the minimums are real. Recommended in at least partial form.
2. **Backfill capacity with new business.** Approximately 90,000 square feet is effectively idle. The Winchester/I-81 submarket has active 3PL demand, and business development has two prospects in early discussion. A backfill tenant at market rates could offset $60,000 to $90,000 of the monthly loss but requires client consent under the dedication provisions — consent we should trade for shortfall-payment relief.
3. **Restructure the cost base**: reduce to a single shift in two of four operating departments, defer the second phase of racking investment, and flex 15 to 20 heads to Carlisle for peak. Estimated monthly savings of $45,000 to $55,000 without service impact at current volume.
4. **Renegotiate the agreement** to reset minimums against realistic client forecasts in exchange for term extension or rate adjustment.

**Assessment.** Recommended course: pursue option 3 immediately (it is within our own control), open the option 1 / option 2 conversation with the client as a package in the third quarter, and hold option 4 in reserve for the annual contract review in November. Left unaddressed, Winchester will cost the company $2.2 million in fiscal 2026 for a building that is executing well.

## 11. Second-Half Outlook, Peak Season Readiness, and Recommendations

**Demand outlook.** Client forecasts indicate second-half network volume of approximately 128 to 134 million units, with peak weekly volume in November running 35 to 45 percent above baseline at Indianapolis and Groveport and 25 to 30 percent above baseline at Carlisle. Beaumont's peak forecast at Carlisle is up 6 percent year over year, which means the site must deliver its improvement-plan targets while handling more volume than it handled last peak.

**Peak readiness constraints.** Three constraints define the second-half plan: Carlisle must pass through a WMS cutover and the October review gate before peak arrives; Louisville has zero surge capacity as configured; and peak hiring at Indianapolis will fail at current wage positioning. Each is addressed below.

**Recommendations for executive committee action:**

1. **Carlisle / Beaumont.** Hold the September 14 date for the inbound/inventory phase of the WMS cutover; decide outbound-phase timing at the September 4 go/no-go gate against the defined readiness criteria, with January 2027 as the fallback. Maintain the daily order-aging review and premium staffing through October 15 regardless of cutover outcome. Present the May–June recovery trend, the phased cutover plan, and the documented shared-cause carrier scheduling data at the joint session. Target: 96.0 percent or better on-time for July–September, clearing the first plan gate. *Owner: Villanueva / Broussard.*
2. **Indianapolis wage action.** Approve a $1.00 entry-tier wage adjustment effective September 1, ahead of peak hiring, at an annualized cost of approximately $1.7 million against a modeled turnover-friction recovery of $2.6 to $3.2 million. *Owner: Villanueva with HR.*
3. **Louisville capacity.** Execute a short-term overflow lease of 60,000 to 80,000 square feet in the Riverport submarket before October 1, and complete the aged-inventory rationalization with the two anchor clients by September 30, targeting removal of at least 8 percent of on-hand pallets. Issue the requested written capacity assurance to the affected client only after the overflow lease is signed. *Owner: Villanueva.*
4. **Groveport quality recovery.** Fully staff the cycle count team by August 31, complete pick-verification rule updates for all re-slotted locations, and stand up the routing-guide change-management process with a named owner. Target: 99.5 percent order accuracy by October 31 and zero preventable chargebacks in the fourth quarter. *Owner: Groveport site GM, reporting monthly to Villanueva.*
5. **Winchester.** Implement the cost restructuring (single-shifting two departments, deferring phase-two racking, flexing 15–20 heads to Carlisle for peak) by September 15, reducing the monthly loss to approximately $125,000–$135,000. Open the packaged shortfall-payment and backfill-consent negotiation with the client in the third quarter. Bring a renegotiation recommendation to the committee before the November contract review. *Owner: Piotrowski with commercial team.*
6. **Financial planning.** Build the fiscal 2027 budget with a Beaumont partial-reallocation sensitivity case, and reforecast fiscal 2026 operating income in September reflecting the actions above; current modeling indicates a full-year operating income range of $34 to $38 million against the original $46 million budget, with the outcome heavily dependent on Carlisle's October gate and peak execution. *Owner: Bhatt with finance.*

**Closing assessment.** The first half exposed real weaknesses, but every one of them has a defined owner, a defined action, and a defined date. The network's quality fundamentals are intact — four of five sites are at or near their accuracy commitments, and the fifth is recovering. The second half will be decided at Carlisle in September and October. Everything in this plan is sequenced to make sure that when the October 15 review arrives, the site walks in with a passing quarter behind it and a working system underneath it.
