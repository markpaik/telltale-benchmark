# Self-Checkout at the Crossroads: A Data-Driven Framework for Grocery and Convenience Operators

**Bluestem Retail Research | Des Moines, Iowa**

**Authors:** Dr. Anneke Vermeulen, Chief Research Officer; Jamal Whitcomb-Reyes, Director, Loss Prevention Practice; Xiomara Delacruz, Senior Analyst; Fiona Brannigan, Vice President of Store Operations, guest contributor (62-store chain, Nebraska)

**July 2026**

---

## Abstract

Self-checkout adoption across independent and regional grocery has plateaued after a decade of expansion, and a visible minority of national chains have begun reversing course. Independent operators and the regional executives who oversee them are now asking a narrower and more urgent question: not whether self-checkout works, but how much of it, staffed by whom, and under what conditions it pays for itself before a January 2027 minimum wage increase to \$15.75 changes the labor math again. This white paper draws on Bluestem Retail Research's database of 2.4 billion transactions from 1,240 independent grocery and convenience stores across eleven states between 2021 and 2025, 340 audited physical inventories, and intercept surveys of 6,800 shoppers. We find that stores where self-checkout carries more than half of transaction volume run shrink at 1.68 percent of sales, compared with 1.02 percent at stores where self-checkout handles less than a fifth — a 65 percent relative gap that is not explained by store size, format, or region alone. Attendant staffing ratio is the single strongest lever available to operators: shrink at one attendant per six lanes runs nearly double the rate observed at one per four. We model five deployment approaches — full conversion, hybrid with staffed express lanes, attendant-heavy deployment, exit receipt verification, and phone-based scan-and-go — against a representative thirty-store regional chain, and we answer the five objections operators raise most often: labor relations, accessibility, benefit tender handling, alcohol age verification, and feasibility in stores under 12,000 square feet. Our conclusion is not a single recommendation but a decision framework: the right configuration depends on format, labor market, and a chain's tolerance for shrink risk against labor savings, and that calculus shifts meaningfully once the wage floor rises.

---

## 1. Defining the Problem

Self-checkout entered independent and regional grocery a decade ago as a labor-cost solution. It has become, for a growing number of operators, a shrink-cost problem wrapped in a customer-experience question, a labor-relations question, and now a legislative question. Three developments have brought the issue to a head in 2026.

First, three national chains — a large-format grocer, a drugstore chain, and a big-box operator with grocery departments — have publicly reduced or removed self-checkout lanes in a portion of their stores over the past eighteen months, citing shrink and customer complaints. Independent operators who followed the national chains into self-checkout are now watching those same chains retreat and asking whether they should follow again, or whether their format and customer base make the comparison irrelevant.

Second, at least one state legislature is weighing a bill that would cap the number of self-checkout lanes a single employee may attendant simultaneously. Whatever the bill's final language, the debate itself signals that staffing ratios operators once treated as an internal optimization problem may soon be a compliance floor.

Third, and most concretely, a step increase in the minimum wage to \$15.75 takes effect in January 2027 across several of the states in our footprint. That increase changes the denominator in every labor-savings calculation self-checkout vendors and internal finance teams have run for a decade. A lane that pays for itself against a \$12.00 wage may take considerably longer against a \$15.75 wage — but it may also make attendant-heavy and hybrid models, which depend on fewer, better-paid staff per lane, relatively more attractive than full conversion.

Operators are not asking whether self-checkout "works" in the abstract. Two thousand independent and regional stores are already running it, in eleven states, in formats from 6,000-square-foot convenience stores to 55,000-square-foot supermarkets. The question on the table is narrower and more practical: given a specific store's format, shrink exposure, and labor market, which of the available deployment models produces the best economic outcome, and what does an operator need to do operationally to hit the numbers the model assumes rather than the numbers a poorly staffed deployment actually produces.

This paper answers that question using transaction-level and audit-level data rather than vendor case studies or national survey averages, which tend to blend store types and mask the variation that matters most to a thirty-store regional chain deciding what to do with its 2027 capital budget.

## 2. The Evidence Base

### 2.1 Data and methodology

Bluestem's dataset spans five years (2021–2025) and 1,240 independent grocery and convenience stores across eleven Midwestern and Plains states, ranging from single-store operators to regional chains of up to 80 locations. We draw on three sources:

- **Transaction data:** 2.4 billion point-of-sale transactions, including lane type (staffed, self-checkout, scan-and-go), item counts, tender type, and time stamps, allowing us to reconstruct labor hours, lane utilization, and attendant staffing ratios at the store-day level.
- **Physical inventory audits:** 340 full physical inventories conducted by independent audit firms between 2022 and 2025, matched to store-level self-checkout share and staffing ratio at the time of audit, giving us a shrink measure that is not self-reported by store management.
- **Shopper intercept surveys:** 6,800 in-store and exit interviews conducted across 96 stores, covering checkout preference, perceived wait time, accessibility experience, and reasons for tender or lane choice.

Shrink figures throughout this paper are drawn from the audited inventory subset, not from point-of-sale exception reporting, which under-counts shrink relative to physical count by a margin that varies by store and is therefore unreliable for cross-store comparison.

### 2.2 Shrink by self-checkout share

The central finding of our audit analysis is a clear, monotonic relationship between the share of transactions run through self-checkout and the shrink rate recorded at physical inventory.

**Table 1. Audited shrink rate by self-checkout share of store transactions (2022–2025 audits, n = 340)**

| Self-checkout share of transactions | Number of audited stores | Average shrink (% of sales) |
|---|---|---|
| Under 20% | 71 | 1.02% |
| 20–39% | 96 | 1.19% |
| 40–49% | 68 | 1.34% |
| 50–69% | 63 | 1.55% |
| 70% and above | 42 | 1.68% |

The gap between the lowest and highest bands — 1.02 percent versus 1.68 percent of sales — is a 65 percent relative increase and, at the average annual sales volume of stores in our sample, translates to roughly \$180,000 to \$210,000 in additional annual shrink for a mid-size supermarket at full self-checkout adoption relative to a store that keeps self-checkout below a fifth of volume. This is not a marginal effect. It is large enough that, for many operators, it should be treated as the primary variable in any self-checkout economic model, not a footnote to the labor-savings case.

We tested for confounding by store size, format, region, and produce/bulk assortment depth. The relationship holds within format bands (convenience, small-format grocery, full-service supermarket) and within region, though the absolute shrink levels differ by format. It is not simply that larger stores with more self-checkout also happen to carry more high-shrink categories; self-checkout share itself is independently associated with shrink after controlling for assortment mix.

### 2.3 Labor hours and the attendant-ratio finding

Self-checkout does reduce labor hours, and the reduction is real and measurable. Across the panel, labor hours per thousand transactions fell 31 percent in stores that moved from predominantly staffed lanes to majority self-checkout over the study period. That is the number vendors lead with, and it is accurate as far as it goes.

What the transaction data adds is that the labor savings and the shrink cost are not independent — they move together, governed by a single operational variable: the ratio of attendants to lanes.

**Table 2. Audited shrink rate by attendant-to-lane staffing ratio (self-checkout stores only, n = 174)**

| Attendant-to-lane ratio | Average shrink (% of sales, self-checkout stores) |
|---|---|
| 1 attendant per 4 lanes | 1.21% |
| 1 attendant per 5 lanes | 1.44% |
| 1 attendant per 6 lanes | 2.11% |

At one attendant per six lanes, audited shrink runs nearly double the rate observed at one per four. This is the most actionable finding in our dataset, because staffing ratio, unlike self-checkout share, is a decision an operator can make week to week without a capital project. It also means that the labor-hour savings self-checkout advertises are partly illusory if they are achieved by thinning attendant coverage below the ratio at which shrink accelerates — the store is not saving labor cost so much as converting it into shrink cost, which is harder to see on a weekly P&L and shows up only at the next physical inventory.

**Figure 1. Labor hours per 1,000 transactions and shrink rate, by attendant-to-lane ratio**

| Attendant-to-lane ratio | Labor hours / 1,000 transactions | Shrink (% of sales) |
|---|---|---|
| Fully staffed lanes (no SCO) | 18.4 | 0.94% |
| 1:4 | 13.6 | 1.21% |
| 1:5 | 11.2 | 1.44% |
| 1:6 | 9.8 | 2.11% |

Read across Figure 1: the labor-hour curve declines smoothly as attendant ratio thins, but the shrink curve is flat through 1:4 and 1:5 and then steepens sharply at 1:6. The inflection point matters more than the average. An operator staffing at 1:5 gives up relatively little shrink protection for a meaningful labor saving; an operator staffing at 1:6 is trading a small additional labor saving for a shrink increase that, in most stores, exceeds the value of the labor saved.

### 2.4 Where the loss concentrates

Not all merchandise is equally exposed. Weighted produce and bulk items — anything requiring the shopper to look up a PLU code, weigh the item, or select from an open bin — account for 44 percent of measured loss at self-checkout stores in our audit sample, despite representing a much smaller share of total sales dollars in most formats.

**Table 3. Share of self-checkout shrink dollars by department (audited stores, self-checkout share ≥ 40%)**

| Department | Share of self-checkout shrink |
|---|---|
| Weighted produce and bulk | 44% |
| Deli, bakery, prepared foods | 17% |
| General grocery (packaged) | 16% |
| Health, beauty, and OTC | 12% |
| Alcohol and age-restricted | 6% |
| Other | 5% |

This concentration has direct implications for both attendant deployment and technology investment. An attendant whose attention is evenly spread across six lanes provides little friction against PLU mis-keying at a produce scale; an attendant stationed near the weighted-item scanners, or a system that requires attendant verification for any weighed transaction, addresses the largest single loss category directly rather than diffusing coverage across categories that contribute comparatively little.

### 2.5 What shoppers say

Our intercept surveys of 6,800 shoppers add a demand-side dimension the audit and transaction data cannot provide alone. Sixty-one percent of shoppers say they prefer self-checkout for small baskets of ten items or fewer; that preference reverses for baskets over twenty items, where 58 percent prefer a staffed lane. Twenty-two percent of respondents report having left a store, on at least one occasion in the prior year, because of a self-checkout-only or self-checkout-dominant configuration with no adequate staffed alternative — a figure that rises to 34 percent among shoppers over 65 and 29 percent among shoppers who identified as using SNAP or WIC benefits. These figures reappear in Section 5, where we address accessibility and benefit-tender objections directly.

## 3. Comparing the Five Deployment Approaches

Operators in our footprint have converged, in practice, on five distinct models. Few stores are pure examples of any one model — most blend elements — but the five represent meaningfully different points on the labor-shrink-experience tradeoff, and each has a distinct cost structure.

**Full conversion.** All or nearly all lanes are self-checkout, with minimal staffed alternative (typically one staffed lane reserved for large baskets, age-restricted purchases, or customers who decline self-checkout). This is the model three national chains have been retreating from, and it is the model that produces the highest shrink rates in Table 1.

**Hybrid with staffed express lanes.** A majority of lanes are self-checkout, but the store maintains two or more fully staffed express lanes (typically 15-items-or-fewer) in addition to self-checkout, rather than a single overflow lane. This is the most common configuration among stores in our 40–69 percent self-checkout share band and, notably, is the configuration associated with the smallest gap between labor savings and shrink cost in our modeling.

**Attendant-heavy deployment.** Self-checkout lanes are present but staffed at or better than a 1:4 ratio, often with attendants assigned to specific lane clusters rather than roaming the full bank. This model sacrifices most of the labor-hour saving of full conversion but captures nearly all of the shrink protection of staffed checkout, per Table 2.

**Exit receipt verification.** Self-checkout lanes operate at a thinner attendant ratio, but every transaction (or a randomized, weighted sample of transactions) is checked against a receipt at an exit point staffed by a dedicated associate. This model shifts loss-prevention labor from the lane bank to a single checkpoint and is common in convenience and small-format grocery, where a single exit lends itself to verification in a way a large-format store's multiple exits do not.

**Scan-and-go on the shopper's phone.** Shoppers scan items with a store app as they shop and pay via the app, bypassing a fixed lane entirely; a small number of stores validate a percentage of baskets at exit. This model has the lowest fixed capital cost of the five, since it requires no lane hardware, but the lowest adoption among shoppers in our survey (14 percent of respondents had used a store's scan-and-go app in the prior year, concentrated among shoppers under 40) and, where audited, the least mature shrink data — our sample of scan-and-go audits (n = 11) is too small to report a reliable rate and is presented here descriptively rather than in Table 1.

**Table 4. Deployment approach comparison matrix**

| Approach | Relative shrink risk | Relative labor savings | Capital intensity | Best-suited format |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full conversion | Highest | Highest | High | Large-format, high-volume, lower-shrink categories |
| Hybrid with staffed express | Moderate | Moderate-high | High | Full-service supermarket, mixed basket sizes |
| Attendant-heavy | Low | Low-moderate | High | Stores with high weighted-produce mix, benefit-tender share |
| Exit receipt verification | Low-moderate | Moderate | Moderate (single checkpoint) | Convenience, small-format, single-exit stores |
| Scan-and-go (phone) | Unknown/emerging | Highest per labor dollar spent | Lowest | Urban, younger demographic, smaller basket stores |

No single approach dominates across every column, which is the point: a chain's format mix should drive its deployment mix rather than a single chain-wide standard. Fiona Brannigan's 62-store Nebraska chain, discussed further in Section 4, runs three of these five models across its footprint, segmented by store size and local labor market, and reports that the segmentation — not a single preferred model — is what improved its blended shrink-to-labor ratio over the past two years.

## 4. Economic Model: A Thirty-Store Regional Chain

To make the tradeoffs concrete, we model a representative thirty-store regional chain: mixed full-service supermarket and small-format grocery, average store size 22,000 square feet, average annual sales of \$11.2 million per store, consistent with the mid-size chains most represented in our dataset. We model five scenarios corresponding to the deployment approaches in Section 3, holding store count, sales volume, and category mix constant, and we run each scenario against both the current minimum wage and the \$15.75 wage step effective January 2027.

**Capital assumptions.** A self-checkout lane costs approximately \$34,000 installed on a seven-year useful life (roughly \$4,860 per lane per year in straight-line capital cost). Reverting a self-checkout lane to a staffed lane configuration costs approximately \$19,000. We assume a chain-wide average of six self-checkout lanes per store in the full-conversion and hybrid scenarios, four in the attendant-heavy scenario, three in the exit-verification scenario (paired with a staffed exit checkpoint), and zero fixed lanes in the scan-and-go scenario (offset by a one-time app and infrastructure cost we estimate at \$210,000 chain-wide, amortized over five years).

**Table 5. Thirty-store chain: annual economic comparison by deployment approach (current wage vs. 2027 wage step)**

| Scenario | Annual capital cost (chain-wide) | Annual labor cost, current wage | Annual labor cost, \$15.75 wage | Annual shrink cost (at chain avg. sales) | Net annual cost, current wage | Net annual cost, 2027 wage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full conversion (6 lanes/store, 1:6) | \$874,800 | \$3.10M | \$3.42M | \$5.65M | \$9.62M | \$9.94M |
| Hybrid, staffed express (6 lanes/store, 1:5) | \$874,800 | \$3.55M | \$3.92M | \$4.51M | \$8.93M | \$9.30M |
| Attendant-heavy (4 lanes/store, 1:4) | \$583,200 | \$4.05M | \$4.47M | \$4.06M | \$8.69M | \$9.11M |
| Exit receipt verification (3 lanes/store + checkpoint) | \$437,400 | \$3.90M | \$4.31M | \$3.94M | \$8.27M | \$8.68M |
| Scan-and-go (phone, minimal fixed lanes) | \$126,000 | \$3.30M | \$3.64M | Not reliably modeled | Not fully comparable | Not fully comparable |

*Shrink cost figures are calculated by applying the audited shrink rates in Tables 1 and 2 to chain-wide sales of \$336 million (30 stores × \$11.2M average). Labor cost figures reflect front-end staffing hours only, not full store payroll, and are derived from Bluestem's transaction-panel labor-hour estimates applied to a blended wage.*

Three observations follow directly from Table 5.

First, **the shrink line, not the labor line, is what separates the scenarios.** Labor cost is lowest under full conversion and rises steadily as staffing thickens across the scenarios, exactly as expected. But shrink cost runs in the opposite direction and moves by a wider dollar margin — the \$1.59 million shrink gap between full conversion and exit-receipt verification is larger than the \$820,000 labor gap between the same two scenarios. On a pure net-cost basis at the chain's sales volume, exit receipt verification and attendant-heavy deployment outperform full conversion, despite the higher labor spend, because the shrink savings more than offset it.

Second, **the 2027 wage step compresses but does not reverse the ranking.** Labor cost rises by roughly 10–11 percent across every staffed scenario, since all scenarios retain some front-end labor. Because the wage increase applies to every scenario's labor line, it does not change which scenario has the lowest net cost — it raises the absolute cost of all five by roughly \$320,000 to \$410,000 chain-wide — but it does narrow the dollar gap between full conversion and the higher-shrink-protection scenarios, since full conversion's labor line, while smaller, still grows in dollar terms. Operators should not read the wage step as an argument for reducing attendant coverage further; the data suggests the opposite, since the wage increase makes each unit of shrink relatively more expensive to offset with labor cuts, not less.

Third, **capital intensity does not track with net cost.** Full conversion and hybrid carry the highest capital cost in our model (both assume six lanes per store) but do not produce the lowest net cost; exit receipt verification, with roughly half the lane count, produces a lower net cost through most of the model. For a thirty-store chain evaluating a remodel capital budget, this argues for right-sizing lane count to format and shrink exposure rather than defaulting to a lane count set by footprint or vendor recommendation.

**Break-even and reversion.** For chains that adopted full conversion in the past three to five years and are now evaluating a partial reversion — the path three national chains have taken publicly — the reversion cost of \$19,000 per lane is a meaningful but not prohibitive number against the shrink gap in Table 1. A chain converting two of six lanes per store back to staffed configuration, at \$19,000 per lane, spends \$38,000 per store, or \$1.14 million chain-wide for a thirty-store chain — a cost that our model suggests is recovered within roughly fourteen to eighteen months through the shrink reduction associated with moving from a 1:6 to a 1:4 attendant ratio, before accounting for any labor-relations or customer-experience benefit. This is the calculation Bluestem recommends operators run before treating a reversion as a sunk-cost write-off; in most cases it is closer to a fast-payback reinvestment.

## 5. Answering the Operator's Questions

Five objections recur in every operator conversation Bluestem has had on this topic over the past year. We address each with data rather than reassurance.

### 5.1 Labor relations

Operators in unionized markets and in tight local labor markets alike raise concern that self-checkout expansion damages morale, invites organizing activity, or simply makes front-end jobs undesirable enough that turnover rises and hiring gets harder. Our panel does not include union status as a variable, so we cannot speak to organizing activity directly. We can report that front-end turnover in our transaction-panel stores is meaningfully lower in attendant-heavy and hybrid configurations than in full-conversion stores — 38 percent annualized turnover in full-conversion front-end roles versus 24 percent in attendant-heavy roles, a gap consistent with what store managers describe anecdotally as a difference between a "watch six lanes and get yelled at about coupons" job and a "help customers and stop theft" job with clearer purpose and more customer interaction. Fiona Brannigan's Nebraska chain moved two of its higher-turnover stores from full conversion to attendant-heavy deployment in 2024 specifically to address hiring difficulty in tight rural labor markets, and reports front-end applicant volume improved within two quarters. This is not a controlled experiment, but it is directionally consistent with the turnover data.

### 5.2 Accessibility

Twenty-two percent of survey respondents overall, and 34 percent of respondents over 65, report having left a store because of inadequate staffed-lane alternatives to self-checkout. Self-checkout terminals present specific accessibility barriers — screen height, touch-target size, weight-scale interaction, and the cognitive load of PLU lookups — that are well documented outside our own research and that our survey data corroborates from the shopper side. The clearest mitigation in our data is not a technology fix but a staffing and signage one: stores that maintain a clearly marked, consistently staffed lane (not an overflow lane activated only during rushes) show survey-reported accessibility complaints roughly half those of stores where the staffed lane is intermittently staffed. Operators evaluating any of the five deployment models should treat a permanently staffed lane, not a contingently staffed one, as a fixed cost of doing business rather than a variable one, both for accessibility and for the benefit-tender reasons below.

### 5.3 Benefit tender handling

SNAP, WIC, and other benefit tenders carry restrictions — eligible item verification, WIC's category-specific rules, in some states a prohibition on hot or prepared foods — that self-checkout systems handle with varying degrees of reliability. Twenty-nine percent of survey respondents who identified as benefit users reported leaving a store due to self-checkout difficulty, the second-highest rate of any subgroup we measured after shoppers over 65. This is not a minor edge case for many of the independent grocers in Bluestem's footprint, where benefit tender can represent 15 to 30 percent of transaction volume in some communities. Exit receipt verification and attendant-heavy models perform better here for a structural reason: an attendant physically present at or near the point of transaction can resolve a WIC category mismatch in seconds, where a fully self-service system either blocks the transaction and calls for help anyway or, worse, allows an ineligible purchase that creates compliance exposure for the store. Operators with meaningful benefit-tender volume should weight this factor more heavily than the average shrink and labor figures in Section 4 suggest, since the cost of a benefit-tender failure is not just lost sales but potential program compliance risk.

### 5.4 Alcohol and age-restricted purchases

Age-restricted categories represent only 6 percent of self-checkout shrink dollars in our audit data (Table 3), which understates their operational importance: the risk in this category is not shrink but compliance — a failed or bypassed age check carries licensing consequences that dwarf the value of the transaction itself. Every deployment model in Section 3 that Bluestem has reviewed handles alcohol through some form of attendant intervention (an ID check triggered at the terminal, requiring attendant approval before the sale completes), which means age-restricted sales are never fully self-service in practice, regardless of the nominal deployment model. The operational question is not whether to require attendant intervention — every viable model already does — but whether attendant staffing is thick enough that the intervention happens promptly rather than becoming a bottleneck that pushes shoppers to abandon the purchase or, worse, trains attendants to wave through age checks to clear a queue. This is another argument for the 1:4 to 1:5 ratio identified in Table 2 over the 1:6 ratio common in full-conversion stores.

### 5.5 Feasibility below 12,000 square feet

Small-format grocery and convenience operators ask whether any of this applies to them, given that a full self-checkout bank rarely makes sense below 12,000 square feet, where two or three lanes are more typical than six. Our data suggests the framework applies, but the model shifts. Convenience and small-format stores in our panel show the same directional shrink relationship (self-checkout share and attendant ratio both matter) but with smaller absolute dollar exposure, given lower overall sales volume and, in convenience formats, a lower weighted-produce mix that reduces exposure to the single largest shrink category in Table 3. Exit receipt verification is disproportionately effective in this format specifically because a small-format store typically has one exit rather than several, making a single checkpoint model operationally simple in a way it is not in a 40,000-square-foot supermarket with multiple exits. For operators below 12,000 square feet, Bluestem's recommendation is exit receipt verification or attendant-heavy deployment over full conversion or hybrid, both because the labor savings from a large lane bank are unavailable at that scale and because the shrink exposure per lane is harder to absorb against a smaller sales base.

## Conclusion

The data does not support a single answer to "should independent and regional grocery operators keep self-checkout." It supports a more useful and more specific answer: self-checkout's economics are governed less by whether a store has it than by how thickly the store staffs it and how much of its assortment sits in the weighted-produce and bulk categories that account for 44 percent of measured self-checkout loss. The attendant-to-lane ratio is the most consequential and most controllable variable in an operator's hands — the difference between 1:4 and 1:6 is nearly a doubling of shrink — and it is a variable that can be adjusted without a capital project, while the choice of deployment model and lane count is a variable that should be set at the next remodel or refresh cycle based on format, benefit-tender share, and local labor market rather than on a chain-wide default.

For the representative thirty-store chain modeled in Section 4, full conversion is not the lowest-cost approach once shrink is priced in alongside labor, and the January 2027 wage step, while it raises costs across every model, does not change that ranking — it raises the cost of understaffing self-checkout more than it raises the cost of staffing it adequately. Operators who adopted full conversion in the past several years and are now watching national chains retreat should treat reversion not as an admission of failure but as a fourteen-to-eighteen-month payback investment, and operators still deciding on their first deployment should segment by store format rather than adopt a single chain-wide model, following the pattern Fiona Brannigan's Nebraska chain has already put into practice.

The legislative attention self-checkout is now drawing — the proposed cap on lanes per attendant among it — should not be read by operators as an external threat to a working system. Our data suggests the ratio such legislation would likely target is close to the ratio operators should already be running for their own shrink protection. The chains that get ahead of that requirement operationally, rather than waiting for it to become mandatory, are the ones our model suggests will carry the lowest net cost through the wage increase and whatever regulatory change follows it.

## Appendix A: Methodology Notes

**Sample construction.** The 1,240-store panel was constructed from Bluestem's syndicated point-of-sale data-sharing program, which independent and regional grocery and convenience operators join on a subscription basis in exchange for benchmarking access. Participation is not randomly assigned, and the panel therefore skews toward operators already investing in data infrastructure, which may understate shrink and overstate operational maturity relative to the full universe of independent grocery in the eleven-state region. We flag this as a limitation rather than correct for it statistically, since no reliable population-level shrink benchmark exists against which to reweight the sample.

**Shrink measurement.** Audited shrink figures in Tables 1 through 3 are calculated as the dollar variance between perpetual inventory value and physical count value at the time of audit, expressed as a percentage of trailing-twelve-month sales for the department or store in question. Physical inventories were conducted by one of four independent audit firms under contract to individual operators, not by Bluestem directly; Bluestem's role was limited to matching audit results to the corresponding transaction-panel records for self-checkout share and staffing ratio at the audit date. Audits conducted within 30 days of a known inventory disruption (fire, flood, extended system outage) were excluded from the compendium, removing 14 audits from an initial pool of 354.

**Attendant ratio calculation.** Attendant-to-lane ratio was calculated from labor scheduling data matched to self-checkout lane counts by store and shift, then weighted by transaction volume during the shift to produce a store-level average. Ratios in Table 2 reflect the ratio in effect during the 90 days preceding each physical inventory, on the assumption that shrink accumulates over the audit period rather than reflecting staffing conditions on the audit date alone. Stores with materially inconsistent staffing ratios across the 90-day window (a standard deviation exceeding one full attendant per lane) were excluded from Table 2 to avoid attributing a single shrink outcome to an average of two very different staffing regimes; this removed 9 stores from the 183-store self-checkout audit subsample.

**Survey administration.** Intercept surveys were conducted in person at 96 stores selected to represent the panel's format and regional distribution, with a target of 70 completed interviews per store. Interviews were conducted across weekday and weekend shifts and across morning, midday, and evening dayparts to reduce time-of-day bias in basket size and shopper demographic. Respondents were not compensated. The 22 percent figure for self-checkout-driven store abandonment, and its subgroup variants, reflect self-reported recall of behavior in the prior twelve months and are subject to the recall and social-desirability limitations inherent in that method; Bluestem did not independently verify abandonment against loyalty-program visit-frequency data, which is a planned extension of this research.

**Economic model assumptions.** The thirty-store model in Section 4 uses a synthetic chain constructed from panel averages rather than any single operator's actual financials, in order to avoid disclosing any subscriber's proprietary data. Labor cost figures apply a blended wage of \$13.85 per hour (current) and \$15.75 per hour (2027 step) to front-end labor hours only, excluding scheduling premiums, benefits load, and overtime, which vary too widely across the panel's operators to model reliably at a chain-wide level. Capital cost figures for lane installation and reversion were supplied by two self-checkout equipment vendors active in the eleven-state footprint and cross-checked against capital project records from four panel operators who completed lane installations or reversions between 2023 and 2025; the \$34,000 and \$19,000 figures represent the median of those sources, not a vendor list price.

**Scan-and-go limitations.** The scan-and-go audit subsample (n = 11) is too small to support a reliable shrink estimate and is not included in Table 1 or in the net-cost comparison in Table 5. Bluestem is expanding data collection in this category and expects to publish an updated estimate in 2027 as more operators in the panel adopt phone-based scanning at sufficient scale to audit.

**Dr. Anneke Vermeulen** is Chief Research Officer at Bluestem Retail Research, where she oversees the transaction-panel program and the firm's audited-shrink research agenda. She holds a doctorate in operations research and has published on inventory variance measurement in multi-format retail.
