# Hapax

In the thirteenth chapter of the first book of Samuel there is a word that occurs nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible, and for roughly two and a half thousand years nobody knew what it meant.

The passage is about a monopoly. The Philistines controlled ironworking, and so the Israelites had to go down to the Philistine smiths to have their plowshares and mattocks and axes sharpened, and the price of the sharpening was — here the text gives a word, *pim*, and then goes on about something else. The King James translators, working in 1611 with no lexicon that contained the word and no cognate to lean on, did what honest people do when they cannot read a sentence: they produced a plausible sentence of their own. *Yet they had a file for the mattocks, and for the coulters, and for the forks, and for the axes, and to sharpen the goads.* It is not quite English and it is not quite anything. It is the sound of a good craftsman covering a hole in the floor with a rug.

Then, early in the twentieth century, archaeologists digging in the hill country began turning up small polished stones, dome-shaped, the size of a large olive pit, weighing about seven and a half grams. Scratched into them were three Hebrew letters: *pe*, *yod*, *mem*. They were weights. A pim was two-thirds of a shekel, the standard fee a farmer paid to have his blade put right. The word had been lying in the ground the whole time, and the sentence closed over it like water.

---

The technical term is *hapax legomenon*: said once. A word that appears exactly one time in a given body of text, and therefore has, in that body, no second instance to be compared against. It is a word without a control group.

The first surprising thing about hapaxes is how common they are. In almost any corpus you care to count — a novel, a newspaper year, the collected letters of a dead statesman — something on the order of half of all distinct words occur exactly once. This is not a quirk of any particular writer. It falls out of the shape of language itself, the long tail that Zipf described: a few words doing enormous work (*the*, *of*, *and*, the load-bearing walls) and then a vast thinning population of words that show up, do their one job, and are never called again. *Bergamot.* *Sluice.* *Pearled.* You could read every word a person ever wrote and still, in the final page, meet a stranger.

The second surprising thing is that this is not a defect. It is information.

---

Beowulf is about three thousand lines long. It contains a startling number of words attested nowhere else in the surviving language, which sounds like a statement about the poem but is really a statement about the survival. The entire corpus of Old English — every charter, homily, riddle, medical recipe, and marginal scribble that made it through fire and damp and the Reformation and the ordinary indifference of centuries — comes to around three million words. That is less than a year of a mid-sized newspaper. An entire language, four hundred years of people saying things to each other, and what reaches us would fit in a single filing cabinet.

So when a word in Beowulf occurs once, the poem is not being obscure. The cabinet is small. Somewhere out beyond it there was a whole ordinary vocabulary of insults and cooking and weather that nobody happened to write down, or wrote down on something that burned. The hapax is where the edge of the record becomes visible, the way a coastline becomes visible only where the water stops.

Homer has something like eleven hundred of them. Classicists have argued for centuries over particular epithets: whether a word means *swift* or *shining* or refers to some detail of horse tack that everyone in the eighth century BC could have identified at a glance and no one has been able to identify since. The scholarship on some of these words is longer than the poems. There is a specific, slightly comic melancholy in a hundred pages of argument about a word that a bard used once, casually, expecting to be understood.

---

Here is where it turns.

During the war, at Bletchley Park, Alan Turing was working on a problem that looks nothing like Beowulf and is exactly like Beowulf. Given a sample of intercepted material, how do you estimate the probability of encountering something you have not yet encountered? The naive answer is zero: you have never seen it, so it has probability zero, so it will never happen. This is obviously wrong, and being wrong about it in 1941 was expensive.

Turing's insight, later written up by I. J. Good, was this: to estimate how much of the world you haven't seen, count the things you have seen exactly once.

The intuition is almost physical. If you draw a hundred marbles from an urn and ninety of them are duplicates of marbles you already have, the urn is probably nearly exhausted; you are seeing the same few colors again and again. But if forty of your hundred draws are colors you've never seen before, the urn is deep. The singletons are the frontier. They are the population of things that were *just barely* caught, and their abundance is a direct measure of the population of things that were *just barely* missed. Good–Turing estimation says, roughly: the probability that your next draw is something entirely new is approximately the fraction of your sample that occurred exactly once.

Ecologists found this independently, counting butterflies. Fisher, Corbet and Williams in 1943, working from a collection made in Malaya, asked how many species were in the forest given how many were in the net, and the answer came from the species represented by a single specimen. The lonely butterfly is not an anomaly to be discarded. It is the estimator.

And in 1976 two statisticians, Efron and Thisted, ran the method on Shakespeare. They counted the words he used once, twice, three times, and asked how many words he knew and never happened to use. The answer came back around thirty-five thousand — a second Shakespeare, entirely silent, the same size as the one we have. Nine years later, when a disputed poem surfaced, they tested it against the shape of that silence, and found that it fit.

---

I keep returning to that. The unsaid is measurable, and it is measurable *only* through the barely-said. Not through what was repeated and insisted upon and made central. Through the offhand, the once, the thing that slipped out.

Every person is running this estimate constantly and without noticing. You know someone for a year and you learn their repertoire: the reliable opinions, the anecdotes with worn grooves, the way they say goodbye on the phone. And then one evening they mention, in passing, a summer they spent somewhere you'd never heard them name, and do not elaborate, and never mention it again. That is a hapax. And what it tells you is not primarily about that summer. It tells you the size of the urn. It tells you that the person you have been talking to for a year has a vocabulary you will never exhaust, and that the ratio of what you have heard to what there is remains, after all this time, uncomfortably clear.

Households generate whole private languages this way — a word a child mispronounced at four that became the family term for the thing, a name for a particular quality of Sunday afternoon light. Corpus of one household, duration of a few decades, no external attestation. When the last speaker dies the word does not become obscure. It becomes unreadable, in the strict philological sense: there is no second instance to compare it against, and no stone in the ground with the letters scratched on it.

---

There is one more.

In Genesis, God gives Noah the specifications for the ark: the wood, the pitch, the three decks, the dimensions in cubits. And then a word, *tzohar*, which occurs there and nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible, followed by the instruction to finish it to a cubit from the top.

Nobody knows what it is. The old translators said *window*, and that has stuck in most English Bibles. But the word looks related to the root for *noon*, for brightness, and so there is an ancient line of interpretation — it turns up in the rabbinic commentaries — that it was not an opening at all but a light source. A stone that shone. Something that gave off its own illumination for a year in a sealed wooden box on a drowned world, because there was nothing outside worth opening a window onto.

Both readings are defensible and neither is provable, and there will never be more evidence, because there is only one instance and the language that surrounded it is gone. What remains, at the very top of the ark, one cubit from the roof, is a gap in the text.

It is a hole in the record precisely the shape of a source of light.
