# Executive Summary: Street Resurfacing Program

**Audit Period:** July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025, with cost and output comparisons to the FY2022 contracted delivery model
**Program Sponsor:** Guadalupe Renteria, Director of Public Works
**Report Date:** October 21, 2025
**Prepared for:** Mayor Andre Toussaint and the City Council Budget and Finance Committee, Chair Sylvia Rocha
**Auditor:** Priscilla Vandermeer, City Auditor

## Finding and Recommendation

The Public Works Department's 2023 decision to bring street resurfacing back in-house has produced materially better cost and output performance than the contracted model it replaced, and the volume of work completed has significantly outpaced the department's own annual plan. That success, however, has not been reflected in the city's planning documents: the five-year pavement management plan and the underlying budget assumptions still reference production levels and unit costs from the contracted era. Absent an update, future budget requests risk understating what the in-house crew can deliver, and the committee will lack a reliable baseline against which to measure the program going forward, whether performance continues to improve or begins to erode.

The auditor recommends that the Budget and Finance Committee direct Public Works to formalize the in-house delivery model as the basis for the FY2027 budget submission, to revise the five-year pavement management plan to reflect demonstrated production capacity rather than the superseded contracted baseline, and to adopt a standing cost-per-lane-mile benchmark that the department reports to the committee annually. This benchmark should be paired with a corresponding capital and staffing plan so that the committee can distinguish sustainable production gains from one-year anomalies before locking in higher annual targets.

## Cost and Performance Evidence

In fiscal 2025, the resurfacing crew completed 128 lane-miles of resurfacing against a departmental plan of 90 lane-miles, delivering 142 percent of the planned volume. Unit cost came in at $61,000 per lane-mile, compared with $84,000 per lane-mile under the prior contract, a reduction of $23,000, or approximately 27 percent, per lane-mile. Applied across the 128 lane-miles actually completed, that unit-cost differential represents roughly $2.9 million in avoided cost relative to what the same volume of work would have cost under the former contracted rate. Put differently, the department resurfaced 42 percent more lane-miles than planned while spending well below what the old model would have charged for even the planned 90 lane-miles.

The audit also examined whether the increase in volume came at the expense of quality or of complaint resolution, a common risk when production targets are exceeded substantially in a single year. The evidence points the other way: citywide pavement-condition complaints fell 44 percent over the audit period, a result consistent with more lane-miles receiving attention and with the department's shift to in-house crews, who the audit found have more flexibility to sequence work around problem corridors identified through 311 complaint data rather than around a contractor's mobilization schedule. The audit did not identify material safety incidents, warranty claims, or rework associated with the higher production volume, though the office notes that its fieldwork focused on cost, complaint, and volume data rather than a full materials-quality review, and recommends that Public Works' own quality-assurance testing continue at current or increased frequency as production scales.

The audit did identify one planning gap worth flagging for the committee's budget deliberations: because the FY2025 plan assumed a 90 lane-mile target carried over from the contracted era, the department's original budget request understated both the labor and materials funding that would ultimately be needed to support 128 lane-miles of actual work. The department covered the gap through mid-year reallocation of capital funds originally earmarked for a stormwater project that experienced its own delays, which is not a sustainable basis for annual planning. The recommendation to update the five-year plan and adopt a standing benchmark is intended to close this gap prospectively, so that future budget requests are built on the program's demonstrated capacity rather than requiring after-the-fact reallocation.

## Management Response

Director Renteria concurs with the finding and the recommendation. In written comments provided to the audit team, the department states that it has already begun drafting an updated five-year pavement management plan reflecting the in-house model's demonstrated capacity, with a target completion date of December 2025, ahead of the FY2027 budget cycle. The department further commits to submitting an annual cost-per-lane-mile report to the Budget and Finance Committee beginning with the FY2026 fourth-quarter reporting cycle, and to including that benchmark, along with year-over-year complaint data, in future capital budget narratives. Renteria's response notes that the department views the FY2025 results as validation of the 2023 in-sourcing decision but cautions that continued materials-cost inflation could narrow the cost advantage over the prior contracted rate in future years, and recommends the committee treat the $61,000 per lane-mile figure as a current benchmark subject to annual revision rather than a permanent target.

# Executive Summary: Downtown Outreach Program

**Audit Period:** July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025, including contract compliance review through September 2025
**Vendor Contract Value:** $2.8 million (annual)
**Report Date:** October 21, 2025
**Prepared for:** Mayor Andre Toussaint and the City Council Budget and Finance Committee, Chair Sylvia Rocha
**Auditor:** Priscilla Vandermeer, City Auditor

## Finding and Recommendation

The vendor operating the city's downtown outreach program is substantially underperforming against the housing placement outcome the contract was designed to produce, and it has not filed the quarterly outcome reports the contract requires since December 2024, leaving the city without the documentation it needs to evaluate performance or enforce the agreement in the interim. The volume of street contacts logged by the vendor is high, but contact volume is an activity measure, not the outcome the city is purchasing, and the gap between contacts made and people actually housed is wide enough, and the reporting lapse long enough, that the audit treats this as a contract compliance failure rather than a program that is merely falling short of an ambitious target.

The auditor recommends that the Budget and Finance Committee direct the contract administrator to issue a formal notice of noncompliance for the missing quarterly reports, to withhold the next scheduled payment installment pending submission of all outstanding reports, and to require the vendor to submit a corrective action plan with specific, dated placement targets before any contract renewal or extension is considered. The auditor further recommends that the city revise the contract's performance metrics, before any renewal, to weight housing placements and other verified outcomes more heavily relative to contact counts, so that future reporting reflects results rather than activity, and that the committee direct staff to prepare a rebid analysis in parallel, so the city is not negotiating a renewal from a position where the incumbent is effectively the only option under consideration.

## Cost and Performance Evidence

Under the $2.8 million annual contract, the vendor logged 1,900 street contacts with individuals experiencing homelessness or housing insecurity in the downtown area during the audit period. Against a contractually specified target of 300 permanent housing placements, the vendor achieved 47, or approximately 15.7 percent of target. On a per-contact basis, this represents one housing placement for every 40 street contacts logged, a conversion rate the audit was unable to benchmark against the vendor's own historical performance or against comparable programs in peer cities, because the vendor's required quarterly outcome reports, which would normally contain that data, have not been filed since the fourth quarter of calendar year 2024. That gap spans three full reporting quarters as of the close of audit fieldwork.

The absence of quarterly reporting is itself a material finding independent of the placement shortfall. The contract obligates the vendor to submit quarterly reports detailing contacts made, services provided, referrals issued, and housing outcomes achieved, along with supporting case-level documentation sufficient for the city to verify the figures. Without those reports, the city's contract administrator has been unable to confirm whether the 1,900 contacts and 47 placements reported informally to program staff are consistent with the vendor's internal case records, whether placements represent permanent housing as defined in the contract or shorter-term transitional arrangements, or whether the vendor is tracking outcomes at all in the manner the contract requires. The audit obtained the 1,900 and 47 figures from the vendor's monthly invoice narratives, which are not subject to the same documentation standard as the contractually required quarterly reports and should not be treated by the committee as verified performance data.

On a cost basis, the $2.8 million contract, if evaluated solely against the 47 verified placements reported to date, implies a cost of approximately $59,600 per housing placement achieved, more than four times what the city's separately administered rapid rehousing program spends per placement, according to figures the audit obtained from the Department of Housing and Community Development for comparison purposes. The auditor notes this comparison is illustrative rather than definitive, given the outreach program's broader mandate beyond housing placement alone, but it underscores why verified quarterly reporting matters: without it, the committee cannot determine whether the program's cost is justified by results the city cannot currently confirm.

## Management Response

The Department of Housing and Community Development, which administers the contract, concurs with the finding regarding missing quarterly reports and states that it issued an informal written request to the vendor for the outstanding reports in June 2025, which the vendor has not fulfilled. The department's written response attributes the reporting lapse in part to staff turnover at the vendor organization, including the departure of the vendor's data manager in November 2024, and states that a formal notice of noncompliance had not been issued prior to the audit because department staff were "working informally with the vendor toward resolution." The department states it will issue the formal notice of noncompliance recommended by the auditor within 30 days and will withhold the next quarterly payment pending report submission. On the placement shortfall itself, the department's response characterizes the 300-placement target as having been set at contract inception in 2022 "without adequate consideration of the constrained inventory of affordable and supportive housing units available downtown," and states it intends to propose a revised target as part of any renewal negotiation. The department did not, in its response, commit to the rebid analysis the auditor recommends, stating instead that it prefers to evaluate the vendor's corrective action plan before determining whether to pursue alternative vendors.

# Executive Summary: Curbside Composting Pilot

**Audit Period:** Program launch through June 30, 2025
**Report Date:** October 21, 2025
**Prepared for:** Mayor Andre Toussaint and the City Council Budget and Finance Committee, Chair Sylvia Rocha
**Auditor:** Priscilla Vandermeer, City Auditor

## Finding and Recommendation

The curbside composting pilot has enrolled a meaningful number of households and diverted a real quantity of material from the landfill, but its performance on the two metrics that matter most for a decision about citywide expansion, participation and contamination, has not moved to a level that would support scaling the program without design changes. Participation among enrolled households has held flat at 22 percent, well below the range typically needed to make curbside organics collection cost-competitive with landfill disposal at scale, and the contamination rate on collected loads, at 18 percent, has twice been high enough to trigger load rejection fees from the composting facility, adding cost to a program that is already running below the participation level its business case assumed.

The auditor recommends that the Budget and Finance Committee decline to fund citywide expansion of curbside composting in the FY2027 budget cycle and instead direct the Department of Solid Waste Services to spend a second pilot year testing specific interventions aimed at participation and contamination, including expanded household education, contamination-triggered outreach to individual addresses, and possible changes to container design or collection frequency, with a revised cost-benefit analysis brought back to the committee before any expansion decision is made. The auditor further recommends that the department report contamination and participation data to the committee quarterly during any extended pilot period, rather than only at year-end, so that corrective measures can be evaluated and adjusted in-cycle rather than after a full year has elapsed.

## Cost and Performance Evidence

The pilot currently serves 3,400 households and has diverted 610 tons of organic material from the landfill waste stream since launch, a result the audit does not dispute represents genuine diversion. Participation among the 3,400 enrolled households has held at 22 percent throughout the audit period, meaning that on any given collection day, fewer than one in four eligible households actually sets out material for collection. The department's original pilot business case, provided to the audit team, assumed participation would climb to 35 percent by the end of year one as households became accustomed to the program; that assumption has not materialized, and the flat trend line across multiple collection cycles suggests the shortfall reflects sustained behavior rather than a slow ramp-up that will self-correct.

Contamination, meaning non-compostable material collected in organics bins, has run at 18 percent of collected volume, compared with a 10 percent threshold specified in the city's processing agreement with the regional composting facility above which the facility may assess load rejection fees or additional sorting charges. That threshold has been breached twice during the audit period, and each rejection resulted in the load being redirected to the landfill at disposal cost, meaning the city paid both the cost of curbside collection and a rejection fee for material that ultimately received no diversion benefit at all. The audit was unable to isolate a single cause for the contamination rate but notes that the department's public education budget for the pilot, $18,000 across the audit period, is a small fraction of what peer-city programs the audit reviewed spent on household education during comparable pilot phases, several of which reported contamination rates in the 8 to 12 percent range.

On cost, the pilot's per-ton diversion cost, calculated by dividing total program spending during the audit period by tons diverted, came to approximately $412 per ton, more than double the disposal cost per ton at the city's landfill contract rate. The auditor notes that per-ton cost is expected to be elevated during a pilot phase, given fixed costs spread across a smaller volume, and that the comparison is not by itself a reason to end the pilot. It is, however, a reason the auditor believes expansion should not proceed on the current trajectory, since scaling a program with flat participation and above-threshold contamination would scale the cost disadvantage along with the diversion benefit, rather than resolving it.

## Management Response

The Department of Solid Waste Services concurs with the recommendation to extend the pilot rather than proceed directly to citywide expansion. The department's written response states that it had independently identified contamination as a priority concern prior to the audit and had already begun designing a targeted outreach protocol under which households whose bins are flagged for contamination on two consecutive collections receive a doorstep visit from department staff, a measure the department states will launch in the next collection cycle. On participation, the department's response attributes the flat 22 percent rate in part to the pilot's geographic footprint, which it says includes a higher proportion of renter-occupied households than the citywide average, and states it is evaluating whether renter-specific education materials, distributed through property managers, might improve enrollment behavior. The department agrees to provide quarterly participation and contamination data to the committee during any extended pilot period and states it will bring a revised cost-benefit analysis to the committee no later than the FY2027 budget submission, consistent with the auditor's recommended timeline. The department's response does not concur with characterizing the $412 per-ton cost as a primary decision factor, stating that landfill capacity constraints the city expects to face within five years justify continued investment in diversion infrastructure even at a near-term cost premium, though it agrees this argument should be made explicitly to the committee rather than left implicit in the pilot's budget request.

# Executive Summary: Permitting System Replacement

**Audit Period:** Project inception (FY2022) through June 30, 2025
**Program Sponsor:** Kwame Boateng, Chief Information Officer
**Approved Budget:** $4.6 million | **Expenditures to Date:** $7.9 million
**Report Date:** October 21, 2025
**Prepared for:** Mayor Andre Toussaint and the City Council Budget and Finance Committee, Chair Sylvia Rocha
**Auditor:** Priscilla Vandermeer, City Auditor

## Finding and Recommendation

The permitting system replacement project has spent 72 percent more than its approved budget across two vendors, following the termination of the original contractor in August 2024, and the system's core performance measure, residential permit turnaround time, has moved in the wrong direction over the life of the project rather than improving. Residential turnaround has lengthened from 21 days before the project began to 34 days currently, a 62 percent increase, meaning that after nearly four years of investment and $7.9 million in spending, the public-facing outcome the project was undertaken to improve has instead gotten measurably worse. The audit finds that the root causes include inadequate requirements definition prior to the initial contract award, insufficient stage-gate review before the first vendor was allowed to proceed through multiple phases without demonstrated deliverables, and a compressed timeline imposed on the second vendor that has introduced new configuration issues layered on top of unresolved problems from the first implementation.

The auditor recommends that the Budget and Finance Committee withhold approval of any further funding for the permitting system project beyond currently obligated amounts until an independent post-implementation review, conducted by a party with no financial relationship to either vendor, assesses whether the current system architecture is a viable foundation to build on or should be substantially reworked. The auditor further recommends that the committee require the Information Technology Department to adopt a formal stage-gate governance framework for all technology capital projects exceeding $1 million, under which funding for each subsequent project phase is contingent on independent verification that the prior phase's deliverables were met, and that the department report project status, spending against budget, and the turnaround-time metric to the committee quarterly for the duration of the permitting project's remediation.

## Cost and Performance Evidence

The project was approved by Council at $4.6 million. Cumulative expenditures through the close of the audit period total $7.9 million, an overrun of $3.3 million, or 72 percent above the approved budget. That spending is split across two vendors: the original contractor, engaged at project inception, and a replacement vendor brought on after the city terminated the first contract in August 2024. The audit reviewed the termination file and found that the first vendor had been paid for two of four contracted implementation phases despite the department's own acceptance testing records showing that deliverables for the first phase did not meet the contract's specified functional requirements, a gap that was not identified or acted upon until nearly a year after the first phase was accepted and paid. This delay in catching the deficiency is a significant driver of the eventual overrun, since work performed on top of a deficient foundation by the first vendor required partial rework by the second.

Turnaround time for residential permits, the performance measure the City Council was told the project would improve, stood at 21 days before the project began and has since risen to 34 days, an increase of 13 days, or 62 percent. The audit examined whether this deterioration might be attributable to factors outside the project itself, such as a surge in permit application volume, and found that application volume during the audit period was within 4 percent of the prior three-year average, not materially higher. The audit instead attributes the turnaround increase to the operational disruption of running parallel legacy and replacement systems during the vendor transition, data migration errors that the second vendor's team is still resolving as of the close of fieldwork, and staff time diverted from permit processing to system troubleshooting and duplicate data entry across the two platforms.

The audit was not able to determine, based on documentation currently available, a firm date by which the second vendor's implementation is expected to be complete or a firm total cost to completion, because the current contract with the second vendor is structured on a time-and-materials basis without a fixed completion milestone or maximum price. The auditor considers this contract structure, combined with the absence of an independent go/no-go review before the transition to the second vendor, to be a governance gap that the recommended stage-gate framework is intended to close for this and future technology projects.

## Management Response

Chief Information Officer Boateng's written response acknowledges the cost overrun and the turnaround deterioration and states that the department does not dispute the audit's figures. The response attributes the bulk of the overrun to the first vendor's non-performance, stating that the department terminated the contract "as soon as the pattern of deficient deliverables became undeniable" and that the department has since referred the matter to the City Attorney's office to evaluate recovery of amounts paid for unmet deliverables under the terminated contract. On turnaround time, the response states that the department expects performance to improve once the second vendor completes data migration remediation, which it projects, without committing to a firm date, will occur "within two to three quarters," and that turnaround has continued to worsen in the interim because staff are currently operating both systems simultaneously. Boateng's response concurs with the recommendation for an independent post-implementation review and states the department will issue a solicitation for that review within 60 days. The response does not fully concur with converting the second vendor's contract to a fixed-price, milestone-based structure, stating that doing so at this stage of the project could trigger a change-order dispute and further delay, but commits to bringing a recommendation on contract restructuring to the committee once the independent review is complete.

The audit further notes that the department's current staffing model for the permitting project, which relies on two full-time equivalent positions reassigned from the broader IT applications team rather than dedicated project staff, has left the department without dedicated capacity to manage vendor oversight and business-process documentation in parallel, a gap the auditor believes contributed to the delayed detection of the first vendor's deficient deliverables. The response states that the department has requested one additional dedicated project manager position in its FY2027 budget submission specifically to address this gap, contingent on the committee's separate action on the broader technology capital request.

## Additional Risk to Future Budget Requests

The auditor flags for the committee's attention that the Information Technology Department's FY2027 capital request, submitted concurrently with this audit, includes $2.1 million in additional funding described as necessary to "complete remediation and stabilization" of the permitting system. Because this request was prepared before the independent post-implementation review recommended above has been conducted, the audit was unable to verify whether $2.1 million represents a reasonable estimate of the work remaining or whether it reflects the same absence of firm milestones and fixed pricing that characterized the second vendor's current time-and-materials contract. The auditor recommends that the committee treat the $2.1 million request as provisional pending the independent review, and that any approval be structured to release funds against the stage-gate milestones described above rather than as a single lump-sum appropriation, consistent with the governance framework recommended for this and future technology capital projects generally.

The audit also compared the permitting project's cost and timeline trajectory against two peer-city permitting system replacements identified by the Government Finance Officers Association's technology project database, both of which were completed within 15 percent of original approved budget and within the original approved timeline. Neither peer project involved a mid-project vendor termination. While the sample is small and the audit does not treat it as a rigorous benchmark, the comparison is consistent with the audit's broader finding that this project's cost and schedule performance reflects specific governance and requirements-definition failures at the outset, rather than a level of overrun that should be regarded as typical or unavoidable for a project of this type and scale.

## Auditor's Overall Assessment

Taken together, the four fiscal 2025 performance audits present a mixed picture for the committee heading into budget deliberations. The street resurfacing program demonstrates that an operational change, in this case in-sourcing, can produce verifiable cost savings and output gains when the underlying delivery model is sound, and the auditor's recommendation there is oriented toward capturing and formalizing that success rather than correcting a deficiency. The downtown outreach program and the permitting system replacement, by contrast, both present the committee with active contract and governance risk: a vendor that is not meeting its contractual reporting obligations in one case, and a capital project that has significantly exceeded its approved budget while its core performance metric has moved in the wrong direction in the other. The curbside composting pilot falls between these poles, showing genuine but currently insufficient results that the auditor believes warrant a second pilot year with tighter measurement rather than either termination or expansion at this time.

The auditor recommends that the committee sequence its FY2027 budget deliberations accordingly, treating the resurfacing program's demonstrated capacity as a basis for a more confident multi-year commitment, while withholding further discretionary funding increases for the outreach contract and the permitting project until the specific corrective actions identified in each summary, quarterly vendor reporting in one case and an independent post-implementation review in the other, have been completed and reported back to the committee. The auditor's office stands ready to brief the committee in closed session on any of the four findings in greater detail ahead of or during the October 21 hearing.

## Appendix A: Scope, Methodology, and Standards Compliance

This report presents the results of four separate performance audits conducted by the Office of the City Auditor during the fiscal 2025 audit cycle, covering the Street Resurfacing Program, the Downtown Outreach Program, the Curbside Composting Pilot, and the Permitting System Replacement project. Each audit was scoped independently based on risk factors identified during the office's annual risk assessment, including budget variance, prior audit history, and issues raised by Council members or the public during the FY2025 budget cycle. Fieldwork for all four audits was conducted between March 2025 and September 2025.

The audits were conducted in accordance with Generally Accepted Government Auditing Standards (GAGAS), also known as the Yellow Book, as promulgated by the U.S. Government Accountability Office. Those standards require that the auditor plan and perform each audit to obtain sufficient, appropriate evidence to provide a reasonable basis for findings and conclusions based on the audit objectives, and the auditor believes the evidence obtained provides a reasonable basis for the findings and recommendations presented in this report. Evidence gathering for each audit included review of departmental financial records, contract files, invoices, and correspondence; analysis of program performance data maintained by the responsible department or, where applicable, provided by the contracted vendor; site observations of program operations, including ride-alongs with resurfacing crews and observation of curbside composting collection routes; and structured interviews with program managers, department directors, and, for the outreach and composting programs, a sample of program participants and enrolled households. Where a program's own performance data could not be independently verified, as with the downtown outreach vendor's contact and placement figures, this report states that limitation explicitly rather than presenting the figures as audited or confirmed.

The auditor notes one scope limitation applicable to the Downtown Outreach Program audit specifically: because the vendor did not produce the quarterly outcome reports and supporting case documentation that its contract requires, the audit was unable to independently verify the 1,900 contact figure or the 47 placement figure against case-level records, and instead relied on the vendor's monthly invoice narratives, a lower standard of documentation than the contract specifies. The audit's finding regarding the reporting failure itself is based on the city's own contract compliance file and is not subject to this limitation.

Each department and program sponsor named in this report was provided a draft of the relevant audit finding, recommendation, and evidence no fewer than 15 business days prior to the finalization of this report and was invited to submit a written response for inclusion. The management responses presented in each executive summary are reproduced substantially as submitted, subject only to editing for length and consistency with this report's format; no response was altered in a manner that changes its substantive content or the department's stated position.
