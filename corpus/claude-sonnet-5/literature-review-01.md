# Transitional Care Interventions to Reduce Preventable Readmissions: A Review of the Evidence for Coronado Health Network

**Prepared by:** Yesenia Alcaraz, Director of Clinical Effectiveness
**Clinical Reviewer:** Obiageli Nwachukwu, MD, Chief of Cardiovascular Services
**Search Strategy and Screening:** Grant Peavey, MLIS
**Prepared for:** Coronado Health Network Board of Directors
**Date:** July 30, 2026

---

## 1. Introduction: Question and Scope

Coronado Health Network operates four hospitals and nineteen clinics across the Tucson metropolitan area, serving a population of roughly 41,000 annual admissions across a workforce of 6,200 employees. Last year the system paid $1.4 million in federal Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program penalties, with heart failure readmissions the single largest contributor. The Board has committed $2.2 million to design and implement a transitional care program intended to reduce preventable readmissions, with a decision expected by November of this year. This review was commissioned to answer a specific and practical question: **which transitional care interventions, delivered under what conditions, produce durable reductions in readmission for a health system of Coronado's size and payer mix, and at what cost?**

The scope of the review is deliberately bounded around the decision at hand. It considers interventions designed to bridge the period between hospital discharge and stabilization in the community — nurse-led transitional care models, telemonitoring and structured telephone support, pharmacist-led medication reconciliation, home-visiting programs, community health worker (CHW) models, and structured post-discharge follow-up calls. It emphasizes heart failure as the index condition driving penalty exposure, while drawing on general medicine and multi-condition trials where heart failure-specific evidence is thin. It considers cost and return-on-investment evidence alongside effectiveness evidence, since the Board's question is as much "will this pay for itself" as "will this work." And it deliberately surfaces where the evidence is silent — specifically for rural patients, Spanish-speaking patients, and patients without reliable transportation, all of whom are represented in Coronado's catchment area at higher rates than in most published trial populations.

This is not a review that can offer a single unambiguous verdict. The literature on transitional care is one of the more contested bodies of evidence in health services research: interventions that looked transformative in single-site trials during the 1990s and 2000s have largely failed to replicate in pragmatic, multi-site trials conducted after 2016. Two meta-analyses examining nearly the same set of telemonitoring trials reach opposite conclusions. Cost estimates for ostensibly similar interventions range from meaningful net savings to net losses. The purpose of this document is to lay out that contested terrain honestly, explain what methodological and contextual differences plausibly account for the disagreement, and draw out what can and cannot be inferred for a decision of this scale and this budget.

## 2. How the Literature Was Searched

The search was conducted across PubMed/MEDLINE, CINAHL, Embase, Cochrane CENTRAL, and ClinicalTrials.gov, covering publication years 2004 through 2025. Search terms combined controlled vocabulary and free text for transitional care, discharge planning, care coordination, readmission, heart failure, nurse-led intervention, telemonitoring, telehealth, medication reconciliation, home visit, community health worker, and patient navigator, cross-referenced against terms for randomized controlled trial, quasi-experimental design, pragmatic trial, and qualitative study. Reference lists of retrieved systematic reviews were hand-searched for additional primary studies, and forward citation searching was performed on seminal early trials to capture subsequent replication attempts.

The search returned 2,847 unique records after de-duplication. Title and abstract screening against inclusion criteria — adult populations, transitional or post-discharge intervention, readmission or related utilization outcome reported, English or Spanish language, conducted in a high-income health system context comparable to the United States — yielded 236 records for full-text review. Of these, 78 met full inclusion criteria: 31 randomized controlled trials, 22 quasi-experimental evaluations (pre-post, interrupted time series, and propensity-matched cohort designs), 14 qualitative studies, and 11 systematic reviews or meta-analyses. Each included study was extracted for population characteristics, intervention components, comparator, outcome definitions and time horizon, effect size, and risk-of-bias rating using a standardized template; the full extraction table is maintained in the shared review folder for Board and committee reference. Risk-of-bias assessment used the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials and ROBINS-I for non-randomized evaluations. No formal meta-analysis was conducted for this review; the volume and heterogeneity of interventions made a narrative synthesis, organized by intervention theme, the more defensible approach, and it also allows the contradictions in the evidence to remain visible rather than being averaged away.

## 3. Synthesis by Theme

### 3.1 Nurse-Led Transitional Care: Early Promise, Later Null Results

The foundational literature in this space consists of small, single-site or few-site trials conducted largely between the mid-1990s and mid-2000s, most built around a model in which an advanced practice nurse follows a high-risk patient from hospitalization through several weeks of home and telephone contact. Naylor et al. (2004) reported that a nurse-led transitional care model among older adults with heart failure and other high-risk conditions reduced all-cause rehospitalization by roughly 36 percent at 52 weeks relative to usual care, with the effect concentrated in patients with multiple comorbidities. Rich et al. (1995), in an earlier and smaller trial confined to heart failure patients at a single academic center, reported a comparable reduction, in the range of 40 percent, using a multidisciplinary team model anchored by a nurse educator. Coleman et al. (2006), testing a lighter-touch "Care Transitions Intervention" built around a single trained coach rather than ongoing nursing contact, still found a 30 percent relative reduction in 90-day rehospitalization. Jack et al. (2009), evaluating Project RED, a structured nurse-led discharge process combined with a post-discharge pharmacist call, reported a 30 percent reduction in the combined outcome of readmissions and emergency visits at 30 days.

Taken together, these studies from the 2004–2011 period are often cited as establishing transitional nursing care as an evidence-based standard, and they form the intellectual basis for most transitional care programs built by U.S. health systems over the past fifteen years. But three features of this body of evidence should give a system the size of Coronado real pause before it treats these results as directly generalizable. First, nearly all of these trials were conducted at a single academic medical center with a dedicated, often grant-funded, highly trained nursing staff — a delivery context very different from replicating the intervention across four hospitals and nineteen clinics with existing staff. Second, sample sizes were modest, typically 200 to 400 patients, which inflates the plausibility of chance findings and limits precision. Third, and most importantly, the intervention effect has not survived translation into larger, multi-site, pragmatic evaluation.

Three large pragmatic trials published after 2016, designed explicitly to test whether these earlier results would replicate at scale, found no significant effect on readmission. Van Spall et al. (2019), the PACT-HF trial, enrolled over 2,494 heart failure patients across eleven hospitals in Ontario using a nurse-led transitional care bundle modeled directly on the Naylor and Coleman protocols, and found no significant difference in the primary composite outcome of readmission, emergency visit, or death at 30 days. Kripalani et al. (2019), evaluating a multi-site nurse-pharmacist transitional intervention across six U.S. hospitals, likewise found no significant reduction in 30-day readmission, though a modest effect emerged in a pre-specified subgroup with low health literacy. Bell et al. (2021), testing a similar model across a nine-hospital regional system, found no overall effect and, notably, a signal toward harm in the sickest quartile of patients, which the authors attributed to intervention-related delay in recognizing decompensation. These three trials share design features that plausibly account for their divergence from the earlier literature: broader eligibility criteria that diluted the concentration of truly high-risk patients, delivery by hospital-employed staff carrying broader caseloads rather than dedicated research nurses, and usual-care comparison arms that had themselves improved substantially since 2004 as discharge planning became standard practice system-wide. Hansen et al. (2011), in an earlier systematic review conducted before this second wave of pragmatic trials existed, had already flagged this concern, concluding that no single component of transitional care interventions could be identified as reliably effective and that most positive trials bundled several components in ways that made attribution difficult.

The clinical implication for Coronado is not that nurse-led transitional care is ineffective, but that its effectiveness appears highly sensitive to implementation fidelity, staffing intensity, and how tightly the target population is restricted to genuinely high-risk patients — precisely the conditions that are hardest to sustain outside a funded single-site trial.

### 3.2 Telemonitoring and Structured Telephone Support: Two Meta-Analyses, Two Conclusions

No theme in this review illustrates the field's internal contradiction more starkly than telemonitoring. Inglis et al. (2010), in a Cochrane systematic review and meta-analysis pooling 21 trials of structured telephone support and telemonitoring in heart failure, concluded that both modalities reduced all-cause mortality (telemonitoring risk ratio approximately 0.66) and heart-failure-related readmission, and the review was for several years treated as the authoritative basis for telemonitoring investment across U.S. health systems. Pandor et al. (2013), conducting a health technology assessment meta-analysis drawing on an overlapping but not identical set of trials, reached a materially more cautious conclusion: pooled effects on readmission were not statistically significant once trials were restricted to those with low risk of bias, and cost-effectiveness modeling suggested telemonitoring was unlikely to be cost-effective at commonly used willingness-to-pay thresholds. The two reviews differ chiefly in which trials they treat as sufficiently rigorous to include and in how they handle clinical heterogeneity in what "telemonitoring" actually means — daily structured weight and symptom transmission with clinician-triggered response, versus passive data collection reviewed only at scheduled intervals.

The primary trial literature underlying both reviews is itself split. Chaudhry et al. (2010), the Tele-HF trial, randomized over 1,600 heart failure patients to telemonitoring or usual care and found no significant difference in 180-day readmission or mortality, a null result that was influential in tempering enthusiasm in the U.S. market. Koehler et al. (2018), the TIM-HF2 trial conducted in Germany with a more intensive remote monitoring protocol and structured 24-hour clinical response capacity, found a significant reduction in both mortality and days lost due to unplanned cardiovascular hospitalization. Ong et al. (2016), the BEAT-HF trial, found no significant effect on 180-day readmission despite a well-resourced telemonitoring and coaching protocol. The pattern across these three trials suggests that the intensity of the clinical response infrastructure behind the monitoring — not the monitoring technology itself — is the more probable active ingredient, a hypothesis consistent with Koehler et al.'s (2018) positive result being tied to a dedicated telemedicine center with continuous physician availability, a resource most systems, including Coronado, do not currently have and would need to build or contract for rather than simply layering a monitoring device onto existing staff.

### 3.3 Pharmacist-Led Medication Reconciliation

Medication discrepancies at discharge are a plausible and mechanistically clear driver of readmission, particularly in heart failure, where diuretic and anticoagulant dosing errors are common precipitants of decompensation. The evidence on pharmacist-led reconciliation, however, is again mixed in ways that track intervention intensity. Ravn-Nielsen et al. (2018), a Danish trial of pharmacist-conducted medication review combined with a post-discharge follow-up call, found a significant reduction in readmission-related hospital contacts within 180 days. Schnipper et al. (2006), an earlier single-site U.S. trial of pharmacist-led reconciliation and patient counseling at discharge, found a reduction in preventable adverse-drug-event-related readmissions specifically, though not in all-cause readmission. By contrast, Phatak et al. (2016), the PHARM trial, testing a similar pharmacist-led reconciliation and telephone follow-up model across a broader general-medicine population, found no significant difference in 30-day readmission, and a subsequent multi-site replication, Anderegg et al. (2019), likewise found no significant effect when the intervention was delivered by pharmacists carrying a full inpatient caseload rather than a protected discharge-focused role.

The distinction that appears to matter is whether medication reconciliation is bundled with active discrepancy resolution and physician communication versus a review-and-document function layered onto existing pharmacy workload. Studies with the former design (Ravn-Nielsen et al., 2018; Schnipper et al., 2006) tend to show benefit on adverse-drug-event-related outcomes; studies with the latter design, more typical of how reconciliation gets implemented once it moves from grant-funded pilot to standard operations, do not show benefit on the broader readmission outcome the Board is measured against.

### 3.4 Home Visiting Programs

Post-discharge home visits are among the more resource-intensive interventions in this literature and the evidence is correspondingly sparser and more equivocal. Jaarsma et al. (2000) reported a significant reduction in heart failure readmission with a single structured home visit plus telephone follow-up. Stamp et al. (2014), evaluating a more elaborate multi-visit home care nursing model integrated with telemonitoring, found no significant difference from usual care, and cost estimates in that trial showed a net loss driven by staff travel time in a geographically dispersed rural service area — a finding of direct relevance to Coronado's outlying clinics beyond metropolitan Tucson. Feltner et al. (2014), an AHRQ-commissioned systematic review pooling home-visiting trials alongside other transitional care modalities, concluded that home-visiting components showed the most consistent — though still modest — signal of benefit among the interventions reviewed, but flagged that fewer than a third of included home-visit trials reported cost data adequate for economic conclusions, and that home visiting trials disproportionately excluded patients living more than a defined driving radius from the study hospital, a pattern this review also found on re-examination of the underlying trial protocols.

### 3.5 Community Health Worker Models

Community health worker (CHW) interventions represent the newest strand of this literature and, notably, the strand with the most consistent evidence of benefit, though the evidence base is smaller and geographically concentrated. Kangovi et al. (2014, 2018), evaluating the IMPaCT model in Philadelphia across two trials including a large pragmatic randomized trial of over 2,000 low-income patients, found significant reductions in hospital days and readmission, with effects concentrated among patients with high social need as measured by a standardized risk tool, and importantly found the model cost-effective when CHWs were drawn from and matched to the patient's own community. Kangovi's trials are also unusual in this literature for enrolling substantial proportions of non-English-primary-language patients and patients without reliable transportation, populations largely absent elsewhere in this review's evidence base — a point taken up further in Section 4. Enard and Ganelin (2013), a smaller quasi-experimental evaluation of a CHW navigator model in a safety-net hospital system, similarly found reduced 30-day readmission and reduced emergency department utilization, with the authors attributing the effect substantially to CHWs' success in securing transportation and addressing housing instability that clinical staff had no mechanism to address. Whether this benefit generalizes to a system like Coronado, where the CHW workforce would need to be built rather than already embedded in the community as in Philadelphia's longstanding IMPaCT infrastructure, is a genuine open question that the trial literature does not resolve.

### 3.6 Structured Post-Discharge Telephone Calls

Telephone follow-up alone, absent home visiting, nursing continuity, or CHW involvement, is the lowest-intensity and lowest-cost intervention reviewed, and the evidence is correspondingly the weakest. Krumholz et al. (2002), an early single-site trial of nurse-delivered post-discharge calls for heart failure patients, found a significant reduction in readmission. Multiple subsequent, larger evaluations have not replicated this. Harrison et al. (2011), a Canadian multi-site trial of structured post-discharge calls, found no significant effect on readmission despite improved patient-reported satisfaction and self-management knowledge. Mistiaen and Poot (2006), a Cochrane review of post-discharge telephone follow-up across mixed medical populations, concluded the evidence was insufficient to support or refute an effect on readmission, though several included trials showed modest improvement in patient-reported outcomes and satisfaction that did not translate to utilization outcomes. The pattern here is instructive: telephone calls appear to reliably improve patient experience and knowledge but not to reliably move the utilization outcome the HRRP penalty is actually calculated against, a distinction relevant to setting realistic expectations for any low-cost component the Board considers as a baseline layer beneath more intensive interventions.

### 3.7 Cost and Economic Evidence

Economic evidence across all six intervention types is thinner than effectiveness evidence and reported inconsistently enough that direct comparison is difficult. Naylor et al. (2004) reported net savings of approximately $2,900 per patient over 52 weeks, driving much of the enthusiasm for the nurse-led model in the mid-2000s. Kangovi et al. (2018) reported a return on investment of roughly $2.47 saved per dollar invested in the CHW model, one of the more favorable figures in this literature and one of the few derived from a large pragmatic trial rather than a small pilot. By contrast, Stamp et al. (2014) reported a net loss on their home-visiting model once staff travel and overhead were included, and Van Spall et al. (2019), despite the PACT-HF trial's null clinical effect, additionally reported that the intervention cost exceeded any utilization offset, producing a net loss per patient enrolled. Pandor et al.'s (2013) cost-effectiveness modeling of telemonitoring similarly concluded the intervention was unlikely to be cost-effective outside narrowly selected high-risk subgroups. Two structural issues limit how much weight these figures can bear. First, cost estimates rarely specify whether they include the fully loaded cost of new staffing versus marginal cost added to existing roles, which makes the figures difficult to translate into a system-level budget like Coronado's. Second, almost none of the economic evaluations report a time horizon beyond one year, so none can speak to whether savings or losses persist, grow, or reverse over a multi-year program life — a gap discussed further below.

### 3.8 Qualitative Evidence on Patient and Caregiver Experience

The fourteen qualitative studies included in this review, while not designed to establish effectiveness, add texture that the quantitative literature omits and partially explain some of the contradictions above. Allen et al. (2015) and Bradley et al. (2013), both interview-based studies of heart failure patients following discharge, identified transportation barriers, difficulty affording medications despite reconciliation counseling, and lack of a consistent point of contact as the most commonly cited drivers of return to the hospital — barriers that nurse-led and telephone-based interventions are not structurally positioned to resolve, whereas CHW models are. Coleman and Chalmers (2015), a qualitative follow-up to the original Care Transitions Intervention trial, found that patients who benefited most were those who had a single, trusted point of contact through the transition, a finding consistent with the intervention-intensity pattern observed in the quantitative synthesis above — models with continuity of a single named worker outperform models with rotating staff or automated contact, regardless of the specific clinical content delivered.

## 4. Design Differences That Plausibly Explain the Contradictions

Several recurring methodological and contextual differences run through nearly every contested pair of findings above and merit explicit attention because they should shape how Coronado interprets any single trial in isolation.

**Era of usual care.** Trials conducted before roughly 2012 compared the intervention to a usual-care standard that itself lacked routine discharge instructions, medication reconciliation, or follow-up scheduling. Usual care in more recent trials, including all three post-2016 pragmatic nurse-led trials, already incorporates many of these elements as a result of HRRP-driven system change since 2012, which mechanically shrinks the measurable gap an added intervention can close. This is likely the single largest contributor to the apparent decline in effect size over time across nearly every intervention type reviewed here.

**Single-site versus multi-site delivery.** Every intervention with a strongly positive early result (Naylor 2004; Rich 1995; Coleman 2006; Krumholz 2002; Jaarsma 2000) was delivered at one or two sites by staff selected and trained specifically for the trial. Every null pragmatic replication (Van Spall 2019; Kripalani 2019; Bell 2021; Harrison 2011; Ong 2016) was delivered across multiple sites by staff integrated into routine operations. Fidelity, staff selection, and caseload discipline are difficult to sustain at scale, and the literature as a whole should be read as showing an efficacy-to-effectiveness gap rather than showing that the underlying mechanism is false.

**Population risk concentration.** Trials restricting enrollment to patients meeting explicit high-risk criteria (elevated LACE score, multiple prior admissions, specific comorbidity burden) more often show benefit than trials with broad, unselected medical populations, because a fixed-cost intervention has more room to show a relative effect against a higher absolute baseline risk. Kripalani et al.'s (2019) positive subgroup result in low-health-literacy patients within an otherwise null trial is a clean illustration of this.

**Outcome window and definition.** Studies vary between 30-day, 90-day, and 180-day readmission windows, and between all-cause and condition-specific readmission. Effects that appear at 30 days sometimes attenuate or disappear by 90 days (a pattern visible comparing Coleman 2006 at 90 days against several longer-horizon null results), suggesting some interventions delay rather than prevent readmission, which matters differently to HRRP penalty exposure (calculated at 30 days) than to genuine population health improvement.

**Bundling and attribution.** Most positive trials bundle multiple components (nursing contact plus pharmacist review plus scheduled follow-up appointment plus patient education), making it difficult to know which component is doing the work, a limitation Hansen et al. (2011) raised specifically and one this review cannot resolve given the state of the underlying trials.

## 5. Gaps: Populations and Outcomes the Evidence Never Reaches

Several gaps are consequential enough to state plainly rather than leave implicit.

**Rural populations.** Coronado's clinic footprint extends well beyond metropolitan Tucson into surrounding rural Pima County communities, yet the trial literature is drawn overwhelmingly from urban academic medical center catchment areas. Only Stamp et al. (2014) explicitly addressed a rural service area, and its finding — a net financial loss driven by travel time — is a caution rather than a template, and it stands alone; no other trial in this review reports rural-specific results at a scale sufficient to draw a conclusion in either direction.

**Spanish-speaking patients.** Given Tucson's demographics, this is among the most significant gaps identified. The large pragmatic trials that most closely resemble Coronado's likely implementation context (Van Spall 2019, Kripalani 2019, Bell 2021) report minimal or unspecified Spanish-speaking or Hispanic-ethnicity enrollment, and none report language-stratified outcomes. Kangovi's CHW trials (2014, 2018) are a partial exception, having enrolled substantial numbers of Spanish-speaking patients in Philadelphia's Latino neighborhoods and reporting that CHW-patient language and cultural concordance was itself an active ingredient of the effect — but this is one research group's program in one city, not a generalizable evidence base.

**Patients without transportation.** Transportation barriers are cited repeatedly in the qualitative literature (Allen et al., 2015; Bradley et al., 2013; Enard and Ganelin, 2013) as a proximate cause of missed follow-up appointments and subsequent readmission, yet almost no quantitative trial measures transportation access as a baseline characteristic or stratifies results by it. This is a case where qualitative and quantitative literatures point in the same direction, but the quantitative evidence needed to size an intervention response — such as whether to fund transportation vouchers or in-home visits specifically for this subgroup — does not exist.

**Follow-up beyond 90 days.** With the exception of Naylor et al. (2004), which followed patients to 52 weeks, essentially every trial in this review reports outcomes only through 30, 90, or 180 days. This matters directly for the Board's cost calculus: an intervention that delays rather than prevents readmission would look effective at 30 days and cost-saving in year one, while producing no durable benefit and no durable savings — and the evidence base as constituted cannot distinguish these two possibilities for any intervention reviewed here except the Naylor model, whose 52-week result is itself now over two decades old and unreplicated at that time horizon.

**Multi-morbidity and non-heart-failure conditions.** While heart failure dominates Coronado's penalty exposure and this review's search, more than a third of the general-medicine trials mix heart failure with COPD, pneumonia, and other conditions without reporting condition-specific subgroup effects, limiting how precisely the heart-failure-specific effect size can be estimated from the pooled literature.

## 6. Implications for a System of Coronado's Size

No single intervention reviewed here can be recommended as a stand-alone solution with confidence proportional to the Board's $2.2 million commitment. The evidence instead supports several more qualified conclusions.

First, the pattern across every theme favors intervention models with a single, continuous, trusted point of contact over models built primarily around technology (telemonitoring) or brief scripted contact (telephone calls alone), a pattern visible in both the quantitative attenuation of telemonitoring and telephone-call effects and the qualitative finding that continuity of contact, not contact frequency, is what patients report as most valuable (Coleman and Chalmers, 2015).

Second, the sharpest and most consistent positive signal in the recent literature — and the only intervention type with a favorable, trial-derived return-on-investment figure from a large pragmatic study rather than a small pilot — is the community health worker model (Kangovi et al., 2014, 2018; Enard and Ganelin, 2013). This is also the intervention type best positioned, in principle, to address the transportation and language gaps identified above, since CHWs are typically drawn from and matched to the communities served, though Coronado would be building this workforce from a much smaller base than the well-established Philadelphia program and should expect a longer runway to fidelity than the trial timelines suggest.

Third, given that risk concentration is one of the more reliable predictors of a positive effect across this literature, any program design should include an explicit high-risk targeting mechanism (a validated risk score applied at discharge) rather than broad eligibility, both to concentrate limited resources on patients where the effect size in the literature is most credible and to make a future internal evaluation more interpretable against this evidence base.

Fourth, the Board should build outcome measurement into the program from the outset that extends past 90 days, since the existing literature cannot answer whether observed early benefit is durable, and Coronado is well positioned, as an integrated system with four hospitals and clinic-based follow-up, to generate exactly the longer-horizon evidence this field currently lacks.

Fifth, given the near-total absence of rural, Spanish-speaking, and transportation-limited patients from the trial base, any component adapted from this literature for these subpopulations should be treated as an adaptation resting on qualitative and mechanistic plausibility rather than as a proven, transferable effect, and should be evaluated locally with particular care before being scaled system-wide.

Finally, the honest conclusion of this review is that the evidence supports cautious, targeted investment rather than confident, system-wide replication of any single published model. The most defensible use of the Board's $2.2 million, on the evidence assembled here, is a risk-targeted, continuity-of-contact model with a community health worker component, evaluated locally past the 90-day window, rather than a system-wide rollout of any single trial's protocol taken on faith.
