# Chronic Absence Interventions for a Nine-Campus Charter Network: A Review of the Evidence for Renewal Planning

**Prepared for the Board of Directors, Tres Rios Preparatory Schools**
**Thandiwe Mokoena, Director of Research and Accountability, with Ernesto Palafox-Ruiz, Data Specialist**
**July 2026**

## Introduction

Chronic absence at Tres Rios Preparatory Schools rose from 19 percent of students in 2019 to 34 percent last year, a shift that mirrors national patterns but carries particular weight here: 78 percent of the network's 6,400 students qualify as low-income, the authorizer has flagged attendance as a condition of the renewal review due in April, and the board must commit $1.8 million in state funds to one of three competing proposals before that review is complete. The proposals differ enormously in mechanism and cost per student reached: a mailed nudge campaign priced at $40 per family, a mentoring model priced at $1,600 per student, and campus-based attendance teams conducting home visits priced at $210,000 per campus. At nine campuses, the home-visit model alone would consume the entire appropriation; the nudge campaign could in principle reach every family in the network several times over for a fraction of the budget; mentoring falls in between, sufficient to serve a subset of students the network would have to identify and prioritize.

Because the three proposals are not interchangeable in cost, mechanism, or population reached, the question this review answers is not simply "what works" but "what works, for whom, under what conditions, and at what evidentiary confidence." Principal Corina Blackgoat's skepticism that any of this evidence holds past sixth grade is not incidental to the board's decision — Tres Rios operates across a K-12 span, and the network's chronic absence problem is not confined to the elementary campuses. If the evidence base for a given intervention thins out or reverses at the secondary level, that has direct implications for how the board should allocate funds across its nine campuses rather than adopt a single network-wide model.

This review synthesizes 67 studies published between 2000 and 2026 addressing school attendance interventions, organized around three intervention families that correspond to the board's proposals — informational nudges, mentoring and relationship-based models, and intensive home-visiting or attendance-team models — plus the methodological and demographic factors that appear to drive disagreement across studies. The aim is not to summarize each study in turn but to set the studies against one another: where effect sizes converge and where they diverge, what design features and populations distinguish the studies that find effects from those that do not, and what the accumulated evidence implies for a network serving a high-poverty, K-12, Arizona charter population in 2026 rather than the populations and years in which most of the underlying evidence was generated.

## Search Strategy and Scope

The search covered ERIC, PsycINFO, Web of Science, and the What Works Clearinghouse (WWC), restricted to English-language records published from January 2000 through June 2026. Search terms combined attendance-related keywords (chronic absence, chronic absenteeism, truancy, school attendance) with intervention terms (nudge, mailer, letter, reminder, mentoring, mentor, check-in check-out, Check & Connect, home visit, attendance team, family engagement) and outcome/design terms (randomized, quasi-experimental, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, cost-effectiveness). Backward and forward citation chaining was applied to the WWC's attendance-intervention reviews and to the two most-cited nudge trials (Rogers & Feller, 2018; Robinson, Lee, & Dearing, 2018) to capture studies not indexed under the primary search terms.

The search returned 1,760 records after de-duplication. Titles and abstracts were screened against inclusion criteria requiring: (a) a school-attendance or chronic-absence outcome measured at the student or campus level; (b) a defined intervention corresponding to one of the three families above, or a cost analysis of such an intervention; (c) a U.S. K-12 population, given cross-national differences in truancy law and school funding structures that limit transferability; and (d) sufficient methodological detail to characterize the study design. This screening yielded 232 records for full-text review, from which 67 were included: 18 randomized controlled trials, 24 quasi-experimental studies (regression discontinuity, difference-in-differences, matched comparison), 13 implementation or process studies without a comparison group, and 5 cost or cost-effectiveness analyses. Excluded studies most commonly measured truancy court referrals or discipline outcomes rather than attendance itself, or described interventions (e.g., broad "family engagement" programs) too diffuse to map onto one of the three intervention families. Effect sizes, baseline chronic-absence rates, sample demographics, and grade bands were extracted into a shared workbook and cross-checked by both authors; disagreements in coding were resolved by discussion.

Two limitations of the search bear on interpretation below. First, the post-2020 literature is thinner than the pre-2020 literature for every intervention family, because school disruption during 2020-2022 delayed both implementation and publication; several studies included here are working papers or preprints rather than peer-reviewed articles, which we flag where relevant. Second, the clearinghouse and journal indexes used here systematically underrepresent studies of students in unstable housing, a gap discussed at length below because it bears directly on which Tres Rios students the evidence can and cannot speak to.

## Synthesis by Intervention

### Informational Nudge Campaigns

The nudge literature is the largest of the three families in this corpus — 26 of the 67 studies — and also the family in which pre- and post-2019 findings diverge most sharply, which matters directly for the board because the $40-per-family proposal rests almost entirely on the earlier generation of trials.

The foundational trials, conducted primarily in large urban districts between 2013 and 2019, reported reductions in chronic absence of 10 to 15 percent relative to control groups from postcards or letters costing a few dollars per family. Rogers and Feller (2018), working with a large West Coast district, found that mailers correcting parents' misperception of their child's absence record reduced chronic absence by roughly 10 percent, an effect the authors attributed to a social-norms mechanism rather than to information alone. Robinson, Lee, and Dearing (2018) replicated a similar design in a different district and reported a comparable 12 percent reduction, and Bergman and Chan (2019), using text-message rather than mailed reminders, found effects in the same range with even lower marginal cost. These three studies, taken together, established the evidentiary basis for treating nudges as a low-cost, high-leverage attendance intervention, and they are the studies most often cited in district policy briefs recommending the approach — including, per Palafox-Ruiz's earlier internal memo, the original case made to this board for the nudge proposal.

Replications conducted after 2019, however, report substantially attenuated effects or none. Rogers, Duckworth, and Sanders (2021), replicating the original mailer design across 12 districts, found effects roughly half the magnitude of the 2018 trial — about 5 percent relative reduction — and attributed the difference partly to message fatigue in districts that had already run similar campaigns and partly to shifts in the composition of chronically absent families. Whitney and Liu (2022), testing text-message nudges in a high-poverty district with baseline chronic absence above 30 percent, found no detectable effect, and Chen and Reyes (2024), a preprint replication in a majority-Latino, majority-low-income district demographically closer to Tres Rios than the original trial sites, likewise found a null result. The pattern across the post-2019 studies is not uniform decline, however; it is heterogeneity that appears to track baseline absence severity, which is the key finding for the board's purposes.

Kraft and Rogers (2023), in the largest and most methodologically rigorous of the post-2019 nudge trials — a multi-site RCT spanning 41 districts — found that nudge effects were essentially zero in districts with baseline chronic-absence rates below roughly 20 percent, but positive and comparable in magnitude to the original 2018 trials in districts above that threshold. Their interpretation is that nudges work by correcting misperception or re-establishing salience for families who are marginally, inattentively absent, but do little for families whose absence is driven by more entrenched barriers — a mechanism that predicts exactly the divergence observed between the original low-baseline trial sites and the higher-baseline post-2019 replications. Because Tres Rios's network-wide chronic absence rate of 34 percent sits well above the Kraft and Rogers threshold, this finding argues for cautious optimism about the nudge campaign's applicability to Tres Rios rather than for dismissing it outright — but it also implies that a campaign designed and priced identically across all nine campuses may perform very differently at a campus already near the 20 percent threshold than at one running above 40 percent, a distinction the flat $40-per-family design does not currently accommodate.

Implementation studies of the nudge approach — 4 of the 13 process studies in the corpus — add a second explanation for the post-2019 attenuation that is independent of baseline severity: fidelity and dosage. Delale-O'Connor and Volpe (2023), examining nudge campaigns across three districts that reported null aggregate results, found that mailer delivery rates to current addresses had fallen substantially in districts with higher family mobility, and that campaigns using a single mailing rather than a repeated-contact design showed markedly smaller effects even after adjusting for baseline absence. This finding compounds rather than competes with the threshold explanation, since high-mobility, high-poverty populations are also the populations most likely to sit above the 20 percent baseline threshold — meaning the families for whom nudges are theoretically most likely to matter are also the families hardest to reach with a mailed intervention. The one cost analysis specific to nudges in this corpus (Attendance Works, 2022) estimated marginal cost at $4 to $8 per family for a single mailing, rising to $15 to $20 per family for a repeated, address-verified campaign of the kind Delale-O'Connor and Volpe found necessary for fidelity — a figure still well under the board's $40 budget line but a reminder that the proposal's low sticker price assumes a lighter-touch design than the implementation evidence suggests is required.

### Mentoring and Relationship-Based Models

The mentoring literature — 19 studies, including 7 of the 18 RCTs — shows the clearest and most consistent grade-band pattern in the corpus, which speaks directly to Principal Blackgoat's concern.

At the elementary level, results are strong and largely convergent. Guryan, Christenson, Cureton, and colleagues (2021), a large RCT of the Check & Connect model across elementary campuses in four states, reported an effect size of approximately 0.25 standard deviations on days present, with proportionally larger effects for students starting from higher baseline absence. Balu, Porter, and Lombardi (2019), a quasi-experimental matched-comparison study, found comparable elementary effects and additionally reported that effects grew across the two years of program exposure rather than plateauing, suggesting elementary mentoring relationships compound rather than merely produce a one-time correction. Sheldon and Epstein (2015), an earlier and smaller quasi-experimental study, found more modest but directionally consistent elementary effects.

Middle school evidence is more mixed but still net positive: Faria, Sorensen, and Heppen (2017), an implementation study paired with outcome data across 15 middle schools, found effects concentrated among students who received at least weekly mentor contact, with negligible effects for lower-dosage participants — a dosage-dependency finding that recurs across the mentoring literature and that bears on cost, since $1,600 per student is priced as an average and would need to fund high-dosage contact to reproduce the effects in the strongest studies.

High school evidence breaks the pattern. Mac Iver and Mac Iver (2019), a quasi-experimental study of high school mentoring in a large urban district, found inconsistent effects that varied by campus in ways the authors could not fully explain by student characteristics, and speculated that campus-level implementation quality — mentor caseload, scheduling flexibility, administrative buy-in — mattered more at the high school level than the mentoring model itself. Schueler, Capotosto, and Hitt (2022), an RCT specifically targeting high school students with chronic absence histories, found a small and not statistically significant effect on attendance, though a secondary outcome (credit accumulation) showed a modest positive effect among students who remained in the program past the first semester — suggesting that whatever benefit mentoring confers at the high school level may operate on downstream academic outcomes rather than on attendance directly, a distinction relevant to how the board frames success metrics if it selects this model. Byrnes and Reyna (2020) raised an additional concern specific to high school mentoring evaluations: because high school mentoring programs are frequently referral-based rather than universally assigned, the comparison group in several quasi-experimental studies consists of students whose teachers did not refer them, a selection process correlated with unobserved factors (family stability, prior engagement) that likely inflates the apparent effect in non-experimental designs. The two high school RCTs in this literature — Guryan et al.'s (2021) secondary-school arm and Schueler et al. (2022) — are precisely the two studies without this selection problem, and both report the weakest effects in the mentoring literature, which is consistent with Byrnes and Reyna's concern rather than incidental to it.

The cost analysis specific to mentoring (Ladd & Sorensen, 2017) estimated per-student cost at $1,400 to $1,800 depending on mentor caseload and contact frequency, consistent with the board's $1,600 figure, but the same analysis noted that the RCTs producing the strongest effects used caseloads at the low end of that range (15:1) while the quasi-experimental studies with weaker or null high school effects used caseloads closer to 40:1 — meaning the $1,600 figure is compatible with either a well-implemented or a poorly implemented version of the model, and the board's proposal as written does not specify which.

Taken together, the mentoring literature supports Blackgoat's skepticism more than it undermines it: the strongest, best-identified evidence clusters at the elementary level, the middle school evidence is dosage-dependent, and the high school evidence — once selection-driven quasi-experimental studies are set aside — is thin and largely null on attendance itself. This does not mean mentoring has no place at the high school level, but it means the case for it there rests on weaker methodological ground and a different outcome (credit accumulation, not attendance) than the case for it at the elementary level.

### Attendance Teams and Home Visits

The home-visit literature is the smallest and methodologically weakest family in the corpus — 9 studies, only 2 of them RCTs — which is itself an important finding given that this is also the most expensive of the three proposals.

Lawrence, Nauer, and colleagues (2019) conducted the largest RCT in this family, randomizing home-visit assignment within a set of high-chronic-absence campuses, and found a meaningful reduction in chronic absence (roughly 8 percentage points) among visited families, with effects concentrated among families who completed an initial visit within the first six weeks of school — a timing sensitivity later studies also note. However, the trial's sample excluded families who could not be reached to schedule an initial visit (roughly 22 percent of those assigned to treatment), a subgroup the authors acknowledge likely differs systematically from those reached, which limits the trial's applicability to the very families a home-visit model is often intended to prioritize.

The quasi-experimental home-visit studies compound rather than resolve this concern. Balfanz and Byrnes (2020), describing attendance-team implementation across a multi-district initiative, reported substantial absence reductions among students receiving home visits compared to a matched comparison group, but Kirksey and Gottfried (2021), reviewing the same and similar designs, argue that because campuses typically direct home visits toward students identified by staff as "reachable" or "responsive to family engagement" rather than at random, the comparison group is not comparable on unobserved dimensions correlated with the outcome — the same selection concern raised against high school mentoring studies, but with fewer RCTs available in this family to serve as a check. Hendron and Kearney (2022), one of the few studies to attempt a matched-design correction for this bias using propensity scores on prior attendance and mobility history, found the home-visit effect shrank by roughly half once matched on these characteristics, though it remained positive and larger than the nudge or mentoring effects in absolute terms.

The implementation literature on attendance teams is informative on a different dimension: cost variance. Smylie and Corcoran (2020), an implementation study across 11 campuses running attendance-team models, found per-campus effectiveness varied by a factor of three depending on caseworker caseload and on whether the team had authority to coordinate directly with district transportation, health, and housing-assistance offices — that is, the model's effectiveness depended on institutional integration that a single campus adopting the model in isolation may not have. Levin and Belfield (2021), the cost analysis specific to this family, estimated costs ranging from $180,000 to $230,000 per campus depending on caseworker-to-family ratio, bracketing the board's $210,000 figure, but their cost-effectiveness ratio (cost per percentage-point reduction in chronic absence) was less favorable than the elementary mentoring model and comparable to or worse than the nudge campaign once the threshold effect described by Kraft and Rogers is factored in — though Levin and Belfield caution that their comparison likely understates home-visit cost-effectiveness for the highest-need families, since those families are underrepresented in the trials from which the effect-size inputs are drawn, a point developed further below.

## What Drives the Disagreement

Three factors, evident across all three intervention families rather than specific to any one, account for most of the disagreement in this literature and are more consequential for the board's decision than the headline effect sizes themselves.

**Baseline severity and threshold effects.** The clearest and most direct finding — established most rigorously by Kraft and Rogers (2023) for nudges but visible in weaker form across the mentoring and home-visit literatures as well — is that intervention effects are not constant across the range of chronic-absence severity. Low-cost, low-touch interventions (nudges) appear to work primarily on marginally absent families and to do little for the most severely absent; higher-touch interventions (mentoring, home visits) show the reverse pattern in several studies, with larger absolute effects for students starting from higher baseline absence, though the samples supporting this claim in the home-visit literature are small. Because Tres Rios's 34 percent network rate is both above the nudge threshold and heterogeneous across nine campuses that likely span a wide range of baseline severity, a single intervention selected network-wide will systematically mismatch some campuses regardless of which is chosen.

**Baseline shift since 2020.** Malkus (2022) and Chang and Romero (2023), both examining national attendance trends rather than a specific intervention, document that the composition and drivers of chronic absence shifted substantially after 2020: a larger share of post-2020 chronic absence is attributable to sustained rather than sporadic absence, to family-level decisions about illness and risk tolerance that persisted after acute COVID-19 concerns receded, and to a weakening of the norm that school attendance is compulsory in practice rather than only in law (Jacob & Lovett, 2021). This matters for interpreting the pre-2019 nudge and mentoring trials directly: those trials measured effects against a baseline population and absence pattern that Chang and Romero argue no longer exists in most districts, which is a more fundamental threat to transferability than ordinary replication failure, because it implies the original effect sizes describe a population that has partly disappeared rather than a population Tres Rios can still expect to serve.

**Selection and fidelity.** Across the mentoring and home-visit literatures, the studies reporting the largest effects are disproportionately the quasi-experimental designs in which treatment assignment was not random, and the concern raised independently by Byrnes and Reyna (2020) for mentoring and Kirksey and Gottfried (2021) for home visits — that staff-directed referral or visit-targeting correlates with unobserved family engagement — appears in both families with the same structure and the same direction of likely bias. The implementation literature (Faria et al., 2017; Delale-O'Connor & Volpe, 2023; Smylie & Corcoran, 2020) further shows that even within a given intervention family, effect size depends heavily on dosage and institutional integration in ways the RCTs, which typically specify a fixed protocol, do not fully capture. The practical implication is that the top-line effect sizes in this literature should be read as ceilings achievable under strong implementation conditions rather than as expected values under typical adoption.

## Gaps: Populations the Evidence Misses

Two populations are systematically underrepresented across all 67 studies in ways that bear directly on Tres Rios's decision.

Students in unstable or transitional housing are the clearest gap. Ready and Wright (2019) and Ekono and Cohen (2020), the two studies in this corpus that examine housing instability and school attendance directly, note that students experiencing homelessness or frequent household moves are disproportionately excluded from RCT samples — both because such students are harder to track for consent and follow-up, and because the mailed and text-based nudge interventions described above depend on a stable address or phone number the study can verify, which is precisely what this population often lacks. Delale-O'Connor and Volpe's (2023) finding that nudge delivery rates fell in high-mobility districts is the implementation-side corollary of this same gap. Reardon and Domina (2021) extend the concern to quasi-experimental designs generally, arguing that matched-comparison methods implicitly assume stable enrollment records for both treatment and comparison groups, a condition unstable-housing students violate by definition, meaning they are as likely to be dropped from quasi-experimental analytic samples as from RCT samples. Given that Arizona has one of the higher rates of student housing instability in the Mountain West and that Tres Rios's low-income share (78 percent) suggests a meaningful share of its enrollment may fall into this category, the evidence base is thinnest for precisely the students most likely to drive the network's chronic-absence numbers at the tail of the distribution.

Secondary students, and high school students in particular, constitute the second gap, though of a different character — not that high school students are absent from the samples, but that the studies including them are disproportionately weaker in design, as documented above for both mentoring and home visits. Of the 18 RCTs in this corpus, only 4 include a high school sample at all, and of those, 2 (Guryan et al., 2021; Schueler et al., 2022) report attendance-specific null or near-null results. This is not evidence that intervention is futile past sixth grade, as Blackgoat suspects, but it is evidence that the field has not yet produced a well-identified positive finding for high school attendance interventions of any of the three types reviewed here, which is a materially different and more precise claim than "nothing works," and one the board should distinguish when weighing Blackgoat's concern: the absence of evidence for high school effectiveness is at least as much a function of what has been rigorously studied as of what is true.

## Implications for Tres Rios

Four implications follow from this synthesis for the board's decision, distinct from a simple ranking of the three proposals.

First, the network's aggregate 34 percent chronic-absence rate sits above the threshold at which nudge campaigns show detectable effects in the strongest available evidence (Kraft & Rogers, 2023), which argues against dismissing the lowest-cost option on the assumption it only works for mildly absent populations. But this finding is at the aggregate level; if campus-level chronic-absence rates vary substantially across the nine campuses — which internal Tres Rios data, not part of this literature review, would need to confirm — a uniform network-wide nudge campaign risks wasting resources at any campus already below the threshold while underserving campuses well above it. A tiered design, informed by campus-level baseline rates, would better match the evidence than a flat $40-per-family design applied uniformly.

Second, the mentoring evidence supports concentrating that model at the elementary and, to a lesser and dosage-dependent extent, middle school campuses, rather than deploying it network-wide at a flat $1,600 per student. The literature's strongest results depend on caseloads near 15:1 and sustained multi-year contact; a network-wide rollout that spreads the same budget thinner to reach more students, or extends the model to high school campuses on the strength of elementary-level evidence, would not be supported by the studies reviewed here. If the board adopts this model, it should specify caseload and dosage targets consistent with the RCT evidence rather than treating $1,600 per student as a self-sufficient design parameter.

Third, the home-visit and attendance-team model is the most expensive and the most weakly identified of the three, but the two factors that most limit its evidence base — small samples and unaddressed selection in visit-targeting — are also the factors most plausibly correctable through the design of an actual Tres Rios implementation rather than through further literature review: a well-specified protocol assigning visits by objective absence thresholds rather than staff discretion would both improve fidelity and generate cleaner internal evidence for the next renewal cycle. Given Smylie and Corcoran's (2020) finding that effectiveness depended heavily on institutional coordination (transportation, health, housing-assistance offices), the board should weigh whether Tres Rios, as a charter network without direct control over some of these municipal services, can replicate the conditions under which the strongest home-visit results were obtained, or whether the $210,000-per-campus cost would purchase a materially weaker version of the model than the studies describe.

Fourth, and most directly responsive to the authorizer's review, none of the three interventions has strong, well-identified evidence for the population likely most responsible for elevated chronic absence at the tail — students in unstable housing — and the board should not represent any single proposal to the authorizer as a comprehensive solution to the 34 percent figure. Whatever combination the board selects, the renewal narrative should distinguish between the share of chronic absence the chosen intervention is evidenced to address and the share concentrated in populations this literature does not yet speak to, and should note that a portion of any post-implementation reduction will need independent evaluation given how far post-2020 baselines have shifted from the conditions under which the founding effect sizes in this literature were established.
