# One County, One Line: The Consolidation of Emergency Dispatch in Cahaba Bend County, Alabama

## Overview

In the spring of 2023, the Cahaba Bend County Commission voted 3-2 to consolidate four independent emergency dispatch centers into a single county-wide operation. The vote followed nearly two years of internal argument over cost, control, and the county sheriff's authority over her own communications staff. What followed was an eighteen-month construction and procurement period, a botched February 2025 cutover that put 812 emergency calls on paper for nearly five hours, a $900,000 penalty assessed against the systems vendor, an eleven-week period of running two dispatch systems in parallel, and a slower, three-wave migration that eventually delivered the operational improvements the county had promised its residents and its bond holders. By June 2025, the county had a single dispatch center answering calls faster, losing fewer of them, paying less overtime, and costing $21.4 million instead of the $18.6 million originally budgeted.

This case study follows that sequence in order: the condition of dispatch services before consolidation, the case made for change and to whom it was made, the failure of the initial cutover and the political consequences that followed, the recovery effort the county mounted, the resulting numbers, and the lessons the county's own leadership now says they would apply if asked to do this again.

## The Baseline Problem

Cahaba Bend County sits in west-central Alabama with a population of roughly 264,000 spread across a county seat, three incorporated municipalities, and a large unincorporated area served directly by the sheriff's office. Through 2022, the county operated four separate emergency dispatch centers: one run by the Sheriff's Office for unincorporated areas and county-wide fire mutual aid, and three municipal centers, one for each incorporated city, each independently staffed, independently funded, and independently governed by its respective city council or public safety board.

These four centers ran on four different computer-aided dispatch (CAD) platforms, none of which could exchange data with the others. A caller in the unincorporated county reporting a structure fire near a municipal boundary might be answered by a sheriff's telecommunicator who had no way to see whether a municipal fire unit was already closer to the address than the county's mutual-aid unit. Address data was maintained separately in each system, occasionally producing map disagreements at jurisdictional boundaries — a problem that would resurface later in a much more damaging form. Radio interoperability existed only through a patchwork of manual channel-patching that depended on a dispatcher remembering to do it.

Staffing across the four centers totaled 88 telecommunicators, but the four centers scheduled, trained, and certified staff independently, which meant that a telecommunicator hired by the county could not simply be moved to cover a shift at a municipal center during a staffing crunch, and vice versa. This inflexibility, combined with chronic understaffing at all four centers, drove heavy reliance on mandatory overtime. Across the four centers combined, the county and its municipalities spent $2.1 million annually on dispatch overtime, a figure County Administrator Delphine Roussard would later describe to the commission as "four centers competing for the same shrinking pool of certified telecommunicators, at overtime rates, indefinitely."

Turnover across the four centers reached 34 percent annually by 2022, a rate emergency communications professionals in the county considered unsustainable even by the standards of an industry that nationally struggles with retention. Exit interviews compiled by Emergency Communications Director Ramon Escalante's office, drawing on records from three of the four centers that agreed to share them, pointed to a familiar cluster of complaints: mandatory overtime, outdated equipment, limited career advancement in centers too small to offer supervisory tracks to more than one or two people, and the psychological toll of high call volume without adequate staffing to absorb it.

The performance consequences of this structure showed up most starkly in two numbers the county tracked closely. Average time from call answer to unit assignment across the four centers stood at three minutes and 41 seconds, a figure well above regional benchmarks for comparable-sized jurisdictions, which typically ran closer to two minutes. And 7.9 percent of incoming 911 calls were abandoned before being answered — callers who hung up, sometimes after being placed in queue during simultaneous high-volume events at short-staffed centers, sometimes redialing and reaching a different center than the one that would ultimately dispatch a unit to their location.

By late 2022, these numbers had become politically visible. A structure fire in the unincorporated county in September of that year took nine minutes from initial call to fire unit dispatch, a delay traced afterward to a shift-change gap at the sheriff's center compounded by confusion over which of the four systems had jurisdiction over the address, which sat near a disputed boundary line. The incident, covered by the regional newspaper, gave Roussard and Escalante the concrete example they needed to move a long-simmering internal planning conversation into public view.

## Building the Case for Consolidation

Roussard and Escalante had discussed consolidation informally since 2021, but it was the September 2022 incident that gave them the opening to bring a formal proposal to the County Commission. Their pitch, delivered in a series of commission work sessions between November 2022 and April 2023, rested on three linked arguments.

The first was operational: a single center, on a single CAD platform, with a single address database and a single radio interoperability plan, would eliminate the boundary confusion that had contributed to the September fire delay and would allow supervisors to deploy telecommunicators flexibly across what had previously been four separate staffing pools, reducing the structural overtime problem.

The second was financial: Escalante's office produced a comparison showing that the four centers, taken together, were spending more per year on overtime, redundant software licensing, and duplicated supervisory and technical staff than a single consolidated center would cost to operate on an ongoing basis, once the up-front capital cost of consolidation was excluded. Roussard framed the capital cost — projected at $18.6 million — as a one-time investment that would pay down the $2.1 million annual overtime bill within roughly nine years, before accounting for the harder-to-quantify savings from lower turnover and reduced retraining costs.

The third argument was regional and reputational: Alabama's state emergency management agency had begun encouraging consolidation among the state's smaller and mid-sized counties, and had made grant funding available to jurisdictions that pursued it. Escalante and Roussard secured a commitment of $5.2 million in state grant funding contingent on the county completing consolidation to defined performance standards, a commitment that became central to the financing package and, later, to the political stakes of the February 2025 outage.

The proposed funding structure divided the $18.6 million project cost three ways: $11 million in county general obligation bonds, the $5.2 million state grant, and $2.4 million assessed proportionally against the county's four municipalities based on call volume originating in each jurisdiction. This last piece proved the most politically delicate, since it required each of the three incorporated cities to agree to close their own dispatch centers, lay off or transfer their own dispatch staff into a new county-run structure, and pay an assessment for a facility they would not independently control.

Opposition centered on Sheriff Wanda Pruitt, who ran the largest of the four existing centers and who argued, across two commission votes, that consolidation would dilute her operational control over dispatch for unincorporated areas and county-wide law enforcement, hand day-to-day authority over a core public safety function to a civilian county administrator's office, and expose the county to exactly the kind of cutover risk that in fact later materialized. Pruitt's position commanded consistent support from two of the five commissioners through 2022 and early 2023, producing two failed votes before the matter reached a third and decisive vote in May 2023.

That May vote came down to Council Chair Elias Mwangi, who had previously withheld his support pending firmer commitments on staffing protections for existing telecommunicators and a clearer governance structure defining the sheriff's ongoing role in the consolidated center's operational policy, even though administrative and budgetary authority would sit with the county administrator's office. With those commitments added to the final proposal — including a memorandum of understanding giving the sheriff a permanent seat on a newly created Emergency Communications Oversight Board — Mwangi voted with Roussard's side, producing a 3-2 majority and formal authorization to proceed.

The county issued a request for proposals in the summer of 2023 and, after a competitive process, selected Lattimore Public Safety Systems as the CAD, records management, and mapping vendor for the consolidated center, with a contracted go-live date of October 2024.

## Procurement, Construction, and the First Delay

The eighteen months between the May 2023 authorization vote and the original October 2024 go-live date were consumed by three parallel workstreams: construction of a new consolidated dispatch facility, procurement and configuration of the Lattimore CAD and mapping platform, and negotiation of transfer terms for the 88 existing telecommunicators moving from four employers into one.

Construction proceeded largely on schedule, with the new facility substantially complete by mid-2024. The technology implementation did not. Lattimore's contract required migration and reconciliation of address and mapping data from all four legacy systems into a single unified geographic information system, a task complicated by the fact that the four legacy centers had, over years of independent operation, developed inconsistent address conventions, overlapping and sometimes contradictory boundary designations, and — in a detail that would prove critical seven months later — different conventions for how each system's mapping service handled a loss of connection to its primary data source.

By August 2024, it was clear to Vikram Chaudhary, the county's Information Technology Director, that Lattimore's data reconciliation and system testing were behind schedule. Chaudhary flagged the risk to Escalante and Roussard, and the county negotiated a delay of the go-live date from October 2024 to February 2025, with Lattimore agreeing to additional testing milestones ahead of the revised date. Those milestones were formally reported as met in January 2025. In particular, Lattimore represented to the county that failover testing — verifying that the mapping service would continue to function correctly if it lost its primary connection during a live cutover — had been completed successfully.

That representation would turn out to be incorrect in a specific and consequential way: the testing had verified failover for the CAD system's call-handling functions but had not verified failover for the mapping service specifically under the conditions the system would actually encounter during a live, county-wide cutover, when call volume and system load differed meaningfully from the vendor's test environment.

## The Cutover

The county proceeded with a full cutover to the new consolidated system on February 18, 2025, bringing all four legacy centers' functions online simultaneously in the new facility, retiring all four legacy CAD systems in the same operation. This all-at-once approach — as opposed to the phased, wave-based migration the county would adopt on its second attempt — reflected both Lattimore's recommended implementation method and the county's desire to avoid extending the disruption of running old and new systems side by side for an extended period, given that the county was already seven months past its original schedule and facing pressure from the state grant administrator to complete the transition.

The cutover began in the early morning hours of February 18. Within the first several hours of live operation, the mapping service began intermittently failing to resolve incoming call locations to the correct jurisdiction and unit assignment, a fault that escalated into a full service interruption. The untested failover path Chaudhary would later identify as the root cause did not activate as designed when the mapping service's primary connection degraded under live call volume; instead of failing over cleanly to a backup routing, the system produced incorrect or blank location data for incoming calls, and telecommunicators lost the ability to reliably dispatch units to correct addresses through the CAD system.

The outage lasted four hours and 40 minutes. During that window, the consolidated center's telecommunicators — newly relocated, still adjusting to shared physical space and unified call queues for the first time — reverted to a paper-based dispatch protocol that had been retained as a contingency procedure but had not been used at this scale by this combined staff before. Over the course of the outage, 812 calls were handled entirely on paper: call-takers wrote down caller information and locations by hand, walked or ran printed slips to dispatch positions, and radioed units using manually cross-referenced address information rather than automated mapping.

No fatalities were attributed to the outage in the county's after-action review, but the incident produced documented delays in unit assignment relative to the county's own performance targets, and it generated immediate and severe reputational damage. Two of the three municipalities that had agreed to fold their dispatch operations into the consolidated center, and that were contributing to the $2.4 million municipal assessment, formally notified the county within the following two weeks that they were considering withdrawing from the consolidated arrangement and reconstituting independent dispatch operations, citing the outage as evidence that consolidation had compromised rather than improved public safety.

The state agency administering the $5.2 million grant that had helped fund the project opened a formal review of the county's compliance with the performance conditions attached to that funding, a review that placed the disbursement of remaining grant funds in question at exactly the moment the county needed additional capital to fund the remediation effort the outage had made necessary.

## Political Fallout

The four months following the February 18 outage were, by Roussard's later account, the most politically difficult period of the entire consolidation effort, more difficult than the original authorization fight. Sheriff Pruitt, who had opposed consolidation through both earlier commission votes, used the outage as direct vindication of her original position, appearing before the commission to argue that the county should reconstitute an independent dispatch function for unincorporated areas and law enforcement calls, separate from the consolidated center, rather than attempt to fix the existing arrangement.

Council Chair Mwangi, whose deciding vote had authorized consolidation, faced pressure from constituents in his district, which included one of the two municipalities threatening withdrawal, but did not reverse his position; instead, in commission sessions in late February and March 2025, he pushed for a formal accountability process directed at the vendor rather than a reversal of the underlying consolidation decision, a position that ultimately shaped the county's response.

Chaudhary's root-cause investigation, completed over the three weeks following the outage, identified the untested failover path in the mapping service as the direct technical cause, and identified as a contributing factor the county and vendor's shared decision to proceed with a single, all-at-once cutover rather than a phased migration that would have limited the operational blast radius of any single point of failure. This finding became central to both the county's negotiating position with Lattimore and to the redesign of the remediation plan.

Negotiations with Lattimore over accountability for the outage concluded with the county assessing a $900,000 penalty against the vendor under the contract's service-level and implementation-failure provisions, a figure the county's legal counsel and Escalante's office regarded as a meaningful but partial offset against the roughly $1.8 million in additional direct remediation costs the county estimated it would incur to complete the fix properly, and one that did not include the harder-to-quantify reputational and political costs of the outage.

The state grant administrator's review concluded in April 2025 without withdrawal of funding, following the county's submission of the root-cause findings, the vendor penalty agreement, and a revised remediation and completion plan; the state agency's final determination credited the county's response for demonstrating a credible path to the performance standards attached to the grant, though it attached additional reporting requirements through the completion of the remediation.

Of the two municipalities that had threatened withdrawal, neither formally exited the consolidated arrangement, though both extracted commitments from the county — reflected in an amended interlocal agreement finalized in April 2025 — for enhanced local representation on the Emergency Communications Oversight Board and for compensation credits against their share of the municipal assessment tied to future service-level performance.

## The Recovery Plan

With the root cause identified, the county and Lattimore developed a remediation plan that departed substantially from the original all-at-once cutover approach in three specific ways.

First, the county held the vendor to the $900,000 penalty as a condition of continuing the remediation work under the existing contract rather than rebidding the project to a new vendor, a decision Roussard defended to the commission on the grounds that switching vendors at that stage would have required restarting data reconciliation work from a point further back than continuing with Lattimore under stricter oversight, at a delay and cost the county judged greater than the risk of continuing with the incumbent.

Second, rather than attempting a second all-at-once cutover once the mapping service failover issue was fixed, the county required an eleven-week period during which the new consolidated system and elements of the legacy systems were run in parallel, with call-handling responsibility split so that the new system's performance could be validated under live conditions without full reliance on it, and with the legacy systems retained as an active fallback rather than a paper-based contingency. This parallel-run period allowed Chaudhary's team and Lattimore's engineers to observe the mapping service's behavior under genuine live call volume across a range of conditions — including simulated and, eventually, real instances of connection degradation — before the county removed the legacy systems as a safety net.

Third, all 88 telecommunicators — the full combined staff from the four original centers — underwent a formal retraining program on the corrected system before the final cutover, addressing both the technical corrections to the mapping service and the operational lessons of the February 18 outage, including revised paper-contingency protocols that this time were rehearsed under supervision rather than relied upon only in the event of an actual failure.

Finally, and most significantly as a departure from the original implementation strategy, the county moved police, fire, and medical dispatch functions across to the corrected system in three separate waves rather than attempting a single simultaneous transition. Each wave was completed, monitored, and stabilized before the next began, an approach that limited the operational consequences of any residual defect to a single discipline's call volume rather than the full combined load that had overwhelmed the mapping service in February.

This three-wave, parallel-run, fully retrained approach took considerably longer than the original single-night cutover plan, but it produced no outage of comparable severity during the remainder of the transition, and by the county's own account restored confidence among the municipalities, the state grant administrator, and the commission that had been badly shaken by the February incident.

## Results: Before and After

By June 2025, roughly sixteen months after the original planned go-live and four months after the corrected cutover process was complete, the consolidated center's operational metrics showed substantial improvement over the pre-consolidation baseline across every measure the county had originally used to justify the project.

Average call-to-assignment time fell from three minutes and 41 seconds across the four legacy centers to two minutes and 14 seconds under the consolidated system, an improvement of roughly 87 seconds, or just under 40 percent, and a figure that brought the county's dispatch performance in line with the regional benchmarks Escalante's office had cited during the original 2022-2023 planning process.

The abandoned-call rate fell from 7.9 percent to 2.1 percent, a reduction that Escalante attributed principally to the elimination of the four-way jurisdictional confusion that had previously caused some callers to be transferred or misrouted between centers, combined with the staffing flexibility the consolidated model allowed once the three-wave migration was fully complete and normal staffing patterns could be established.

Annual dispatch overtime fell from $2.1 million to $1.3 million, a reduction of roughly 38 percent, reflecting the ability of supervisors in the consolidated center to draw on a single combined pool of 88 cross-trained telecommunicators rather than four separate, smaller staffing pools each subject to its own coverage gaps.

Turnover figures for the consolidated center's first full year of stable operation were not yet finalized as of the June 2025 reporting period the county used for its public accounting, but Escalante's office reported preliminary indications of improvement consistent with the reduced overtime burden and the career-advancement opportunities a single larger center could offer that the four smaller centers could not, including a formal supervisory career ladder introduced as part of the consolidated center's staffing plan.

## The True Cost

The original 1project budget, authorized in 2023, projected total cost of $18.6 million, funded through $11 million in county bonds, $5.2 million in state grant funding, and $2.4 million assessed against the four municipalities. The final project cost, as reported by the county in mid-2025, came to $21.4 million, an overrun of $2.8 million, or roughly 15 percent above the original projection.

That overrun reflected several distinct cost components layered on top of the original scope. The extended timeline itself carried holding and administrative costs not contemplated in the original budget. The eleven-week parallel-run period required maintaining both new and legacy system infrastructure and, in some cases, legacy staffing arrangements, simultaneously, rather than the clean single-transition cost the original budget had assumed. The full retraining of all 88 telecommunicators added a training cost the original implementation plan had budgeted at a smaller scale, sufficient for a single cutover rather than a full recertification effort undertaken after a major operational failure. And the three-wave migration approach, while limiting operational risk, extended the vendor engagement and county project-management staffing commitment well beyond the original schedule.

Against these additional costs, the county recovered $900,000 through the penalty assessed against Lattimore, an amount that offset roughly one-third of the $2.8 million overrun but left the remaining two-thirds — approximately $1.9 million — borne directly by the county and, through the amended interlocal agreement, in modest part by the municipalities, whose final assessment included the service-level compensation credits negotiated in April 2025.

Roussard's public accounting of the final cost to the commission in June 2025 characterized the $21.4 million final figure as within the range the county could sustain within its existing bond authorization and operating budget, without requiring additional borrowing or additional municipal assessment beyond the credits already negotiated, but she did not describe the overrun as costless, noting to the commission that the additional $2.8 million represented resources that could otherwise have funded other capital priorities, and that the reputational cost of the February outage — measured in the two municipalities' near-withdrawal and the state grant administrator's review — did not appear in the dollar figure at all.

## What the County Would Sequence Differently

In an internal after-action review Escalante's office prepared for the commission in the months following the completion of the remediation effort, county leadership identified a specific sequencing change they said they would apply if undertaking a similar consolidation again: testing the failover behavior of critical subsystems — specifically the mapping service, but by extension any subsystem whose failure mode had not been validated under conditions matching actual live deployment — under load conditions that matched the full combined call volume of the consolidated center, rather than accepting vendor-reported test results from a smaller-scale or non-representative test environment as sufficient grounds to proceed with a full simultaneous cutover.

Tied to that lesson, the county's review concluded that the phased, wave-based migration approach used in the second attempt — moving police, fire, and medical dispatch across in three separate stages rather than simultaneously — should have been the original implementation plan rather than a response adopted only after a major failure. Chaudhary's assessment, reflected in the after-action document, was that a wave-based approach would have limited the scope of the February 18 failure to a single discipline's call volume, likely avoiding the full four-hour-40-minute, 812-call outage that resulted from bringing all functions online simultaneously against an unvalidated failover path.

The county's review also identified the eleven-week parallel-run period — treating the legacy systems as an active fallback during a validation window rather than as a contingency retired immediately upon cutover — as a practice that should have preceded the original go-live rather than followed the outage as a remediation measure. Running old and new systems in parallel before fully retiring the legacy centers, the review concluded, would have allowed the county to observe the mapping service's behavior under live conditions and identify the untested failover path before it caused an operational failure, at a cost in extended timeline that the county judged, in retrospect, to have been considerably smaller than the cost of the outage and its political and financial aftermath.

Finally, the review noted that the original $18.6 million budget had not included a meaningfully sized contingency reserve for implementation risk on a project of this technical complexity, spanning the consolidation of four independent legacy systems with inconsistent underlying data. County leadership indicated that future capital projects of comparable technical complexity would carry an explicit contingency line closer to 15 percent of projected cost, rather than the smaller margin built into the original 2023 budget, on the reasoning that the actual overrun experienced — $2.8 million against an $18.6 million base, almost exactly 15 percent — validated that such a reserve would not have been excessive.

## Takeaways for Similar Efforts

For public agencies considering consolidation of dispatch or other safety-critical systems across previously independent operating units, the Cahaba Bend County experience offers several concrete lessons beyond the county's own after-action findings.

The case illustrates that the financial and operational argument for consolidation — lower overtime, faster response, fewer abandoned calls, reduced turnover — can be sound and ultimately vindicated by results, while the implementation risk of the transition itself remains a separate and independently serious problem that a compelling business case does not resolve on its own. Cahaba Bend County's final numbers validated the original 2022-2023 case for consolidation almost precisely: overtime fell by close to the margin projected, response times improved to the benchmark levels cited in the original planning documents, and the abandoned-call rate improvement exceeded what the original proposal had promised. Yet none of that ultimate vindication prevented the February 2025 outage, which arose entirely from implementation execution — an untested failover path — rather than any flaw in the underlying rationale for consolidation.

The case also demonstrates the particular danger of accepting vendor assurances about testing completeness without independent verification calibrated to actual deployment conditions, especially for subsystems, like mapping services, whose failure modes may not manifest until subjected to load and complexity that a vendor's standard test environment does not replicate. The gap between Lattimore's reported successful failover testing in January 2025 and the system's actual behavior on February 18 was not a matter of the vendor concealing a known problem, based on the county's own root-cause findings, but a matter of the test conditions themselves being insufficiently representative — a distinction that matters for any agency deciding how much independent validation to require before accepting a vendor's completion certification.

Finally, the case suggests that the sequencing choice between an all-at-once cutover and a phased, wave-based migration is not merely a matter of project management preference but a direct risk-management decision with consequences that, in this instance, played out in a public and costly way. The county's own conclusion — that the phased approach it adopted only after the outage should have been the original plan — is offered here not as a universal rule for every consolidation project, since faster single-transition approaches carry their own advantages in cost and disruption where the underlying systems are less complex or less safety-critical, but as a specific lesson this county drew from a specific, costly failure, and one likely to be relevant to any agency contemplating a comparably complex, safety-critical systems consolidation across previously independent operating units with inconsistent underlying data.
