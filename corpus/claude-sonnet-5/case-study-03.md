# Blue Ridge Hardware and Supply: The Nine-Minute Promise That Took Eighteen Months to Keep

## A Case Study in Retail Technology Transformation

---

## Executive Summary

In January 2023, Blue Ridge Hardware and Supply committed $6.8 million to replace its inventory management system and launch buy-online-pickup-in-store (BOPIS) service across its 38-store footprint in eastern Tennessee and southwest Virginia. The company promised customers nine-minute pickup times, partnering with Cardinal Retail Systems to deliver the technology backbone. When the pilot launched at twelve stores during the November 2023 holiday season, it exposed a foundational gap the company had not reckoned with: the cycle-counting discipline the new system assumed had simply never existed in Blue Ridge's stores. Pick accuracy ran at 62 percent. Actual pickup times averaged 41 minutes — more than four times the promise. Customers cancelled 2,300 orders in six weeks, and store managers, unable to trust the system, reverted to paper counts kept in binders under the register.

Rather than push forward or cancel outright, CEO Loretta Ashby-Ng and her leadership team paused the rollout in spring 2024, funded a dedicated picker role in every store, ran a six-month cycle count remediation program, reset the customer promise from nine minutes to two hours, and restarted the rollout store by store rather than as a single event. By March 2025, inventory record accuracy had climbed from 71 percent to 94 percent, out-of-stocks on the top 200 items had fallen from 18 percent to 6 percent, and the pickup channel accounted for 11 percent of total sales, running at $34 million annualized. Comparable store sales, negative for six straight quarters entering the program, turned positive at 3.8 percent. The total cost of the program, including the pause, the remediation, and the added store labor, reached $10.5 million — roughly 54 percent above the original $6.8 million authorization.

This case traces the arc from the pressures that drove Blue Ridge into the bet, through the pilot's collapse, the diagnostic that found the true cause, the reset that followed, and the results that eventually justified the investment. It closes with what the leadership team says, in hindsight, they would have done differently.

---

## Part One: The State of the Business Going In

Blue Ridge Hardware and Supply traces its roots to a single feed-and-seed store opened in Knoxville in 1961. By 2022 it had grown into a regional hardware and home improvement chain with 38 locations spread across a roughly 200-mile radius touching Knoxville, Chattanooga's northern exurbs, the Tri-Cities region of upper east Tennessee, and a cluster of stores in southwest Virginia around Abingdon and Bristol. The company employed about 1,900 people and generated $310 million in annual revenue, putting individual store volumes in the $7 to $9 million range — respectable for the format, but not enough to buy the company much slack against larger regional and national competitors.

That competitive pressure had been building for years by the time this story begins. A national home improvement chain had opened three big-box stores within Blue Ridge's trading area between 2019 and 2021, each within fifteen miles of an existing Blue Ridge location. A regional farm-and-ranch competitor out of the Carolinas had been quietly rolling up independent hardware stores in the same counties, absorbing several accounts that had bought lumber and fasteners from Blue Ridge for decades. And the broader shift of home improvement spend online, accelerated by the pandemic years, had left Blue Ridge's leadership uneasy about a customer base that skewed rural, older, and loyal — but not infinitely so.

The financial signals by late 2022 were not subtle. Comparable store sales had been negative for six consecutive quarters, averaging minus 4.2 percent. That is a meaningful, sustained erosion for a specialty retailer with a fixed real estate footprint and largely fixed labor costs; it is the kind of number that, left unaddressed, turns into store closures within a few years. Operating margin had compressed by roughly 140 basis points over the same period, driven partly by the comp decline and partly by wage inflation the company had not been able to pass through given competitive pricing pressure from the new big-box entrants.

Underneath the top-line numbers sat an operational problem that would turn out to be the real story of this case: inventory. Blue Ridge's inventory management system, a modestly customized instance of a mid-2000s-era retail ERP product, had never been paired with a disciplined cycle-count program. Store-level inventory record accuracy — the percentage of SKUs where the system's on-hand count matched a physical count — sat at 71 percent. In an industry where 90 to 95 percent is considered functional and anything above 97 percent is considered excellent, 71 percent meant that roughly three in ten items in the store were wrong in the system at any given time, in either direction. Some of that was theft and damage never written off. Much of it was simply drift: receiving errors, miscounted cycle counts that were never actually performed, and price-and-quantity overrides made at the register that never made it back into the perpetual inventory record.

The consequence customers felt most directly was stockouts. Blue Ridge tracked out-of-stock rates on its top 200 SKUs — the fast-moving items like fasteners, PVC fittings, batteries, and seasonal lawn-and-garden staples that drove disproportionate store traffic — and found those items were out of stock 18 percent of the time. Nearly one in five visits for a top-selling item ended in disappointment. Store managers had adapted by hoarding backroom stock off the books, padding orders, and generally not trusting what the system told them, which of course made the system's accuracy worse, not better, because those informal workarounds were never reconciled into the official record. It was a slow-motion, self-reinforcing failure that had been normalized inside the company long enough that new store managers were trained, implicitly, to work around the system rather than through it.

Loretta Ashby-Ng had become CEO in 2020, coming from a regional grocery chain where she had overseen a successful e-commerce and curbside pickup rollout. Ellis Trumbo, the Chief Information Officer, had joined Blue Ridge in 2018 from a logistics technology background and had spent much of his tenure arguing, with increasing urgency, that the company's core systems were aging out of vendor support and needed replacement regardless of what else the company chose to do. By late 2022, the two of them — along with the board — had converged on a view that the company's core problem was not really pricing or assortment or store count. It was that customers increasingly expected to know, with confidence, whether an item was in the store before they drove there, and increasingly expected the option to buy online and pick up quickly rather than shop the aisles at all. Blue Ridge could not offer either with any credibility, because its inventory data could not be trusted by the company itself, let alone exposed to customers.

## Part Two: Why This Bet

The board approved a $6.8 million program in January 2023, structured around two linked initiatives presented as a single program: a wholesale replacement of the inventory management system, and the launch of buy-online-pickup-in-store service across the chain.

The logic, as Ashby-Ng and Trumbo presented it to the board, was straightforward and, in isolation, hard to argue with. The existing inventory system was both inaccurate and technically obsolete; the vendor had announced end-of-life support timelines that gave Blue Ridge perhaps three more years before the platform became a genuine operational risk. Replacing it was going to happen regardless. Given that a replacement was unavoidable, the argument went, the company should choose a platform capable of supporting the customer-facing capability — real-time inventory visibility and BOPIS — that competitors already offered and that Blue Ridge's own customer research said shoppers wanted. Doing the systems replacement and the BOPIS launch as one program, rather than sequentially, was expected to save on integration cost and, more importantly, to compress the multi-year timeline the company felt it did not have given six straight quarters of negative comps.

Blue Ridge selected Cardinal Retail Systems as its vendor following a roughly four-month evaluation that included two other finalists, one a large enterprise retail platform and one a smaller regional player already used by two of Blue Ridge's direct competitors. Cardinal was chosen for a combination of price — its bid came in about 20 percent below the enterprise competitor — and for what the selection committee's internal memo described as "strong reference calls from comparable-format specialty retailers," specifically two hardware chains in the Midwest that had gone live on Cardinal's platform in the prior two years. Cardinal's sales team, in the proposal and in subsequent planning sessions, used a nine-minute pickup benchmark drawn from those reference accounts — the average time, per Cardinal's account, between a customer's arrival notification and an associate handing over the order. Blue Ridge's marketing team, eager for a headline commitment that would differentiate the company from local competitors who offered no comparable service, built the external launch campaign around that nine-minute figure before the pilot had even begun. Internal planning documents show the promise was finalized for external marketing use in August 2023, three months before the pilot store go-live and roughly nine months before the company would have any operating data of its own to validate it.

It's worth being precise about what the $6.8 million was intended to cover, because the eventual cost overrun is easier to understand against this baseline. The budget included the Cardinal software licensing and implementation fees (roughly $3.1 million), integration work connecting the new system to Blue Ridge's point-of-sale, e-commerce site, and accounting systems (about $1.4 million), in-store hardware including handheld scanners and pickup lockers or designated pickup counters at each location (approximately $1.2 million), and a marketing and launch budget for the BOPIS promise (about $600,000), with the remainder allocated to project management, training, and contingency. Notably, and this becomes important later, the budget included no line item for additional store labor. The working assumption, carried through planning without serious challenge according to later internal review, was that BOPIS picking could be absorbed within existing store labor hours, augmented modestly by efficiency gains from the new system itself. Cycle counting — the physical, recurring process of counting inventory to keep system records accurate — was similarly assumed to be a function of the new software rather than a function of dedicated labor hours and management discipline. Cardinal's system included automated cycle-count scheduling and mobile-scanner workflows; the implicit assumption inside Blue Ridge was that turning on that software feature was equivalent to having a cycle-count program, when in fact the software could only schedule and record counts that people actually performed.

VP of Merchandising Sofia Karagiannis, who inherited responsibility for the inventory-accuracy side of the program roughly midway through implementation, would later describe this gap plainly in the post-mortem she co-authored: "We bought a system that assumed a discipline we had never built. Nobody asked, in the room where we approved this, who is going to walk the aisles and count the screws. We asked what the software could do. We didn't ask who was going to do the thing the software needed done."

## Part Three: Building the Program, January to November 2023

The implementation ran roughly on the schedule Cardinal's team had proposed, if not exactly on the terms Blue Ridge had budgeted. Systems integration and data migration ran from February through July 2023. A significant complication emerged in April, when the data migration team discovered that the existing system's SKU master file — the record of every item Blue Ridge sold, its attributes, and its store-level assignments — was itself compromised by years of the same drift that had produced the 71 percent accuracy figure. Roughly 40,000 SKUs across the chain's approximately 65,000-item assortment required manual reconciliation before they could be migrated cleanly, a task that consumed an additional six weeks and roughly $340,000 in contractor labor not contemplated in the original budget, though it was absorbed within contingency at the time and not treated as a red flag.

Store hardware installation — handheld scanners for associates, a dedicated pickup counter built into the front of each pilot store, and network upgrades to support real-time inventory transactions — ran through the fall, concentrated on the twelve pilot stores selected to go first. The pilot stores were chosen to represent a cross-section of the chain: three in the Knoxville metro, three in the Tri-Cities cluster, three in the Chattanooga-adjacent exurbs, and three in the southwest Virginia stores around Abingdon and Bristol. Store Operations Director Bo Nakashima, who had joined the program formally in September 2023 after a reorganization moved BOPIS operational ownership from IT to store operations, later noted that store manager training for the new system ran an average of six hours per location — training focused overwhelmingly on how to use the new point-of-sale and picking workflows, with comparatively little time spent on the cycle-count responsibilities the system now assumed each store would carry out weekly.

Marketing launched the nine-minute promise publicly in late October 2023, running radio spots across the pilot markets and in-store signage promising "order online, and we'll have it ready in nine minutes." The timing — six weeks before the traditional holiday shopping surge that runs from Thanksgiving through the week before Christmas — was chosen deliberately, to capture what the marketing team projected would be the highest-value proof point for the new service: harried holiday shoppers who would value speed and reliability enough to become repeat BOPIS users afterward.

In retrospect, several people involved in the program would identify the holiday timing as a compounding error layered on top of the cycle-count gap, rather than the primary cause of what followed. But it meant that whatever the new system's underlying weaknesses were, they would be stress-tested immediately, at volume, under conditions store staff had no slack to absorb.

## Part Four: The Pilot, November 2023 to January 2024

The twelve pilot stores went live in a staggered launch between November 6 and November 20, 2023. Within the first two weeks, it was apparent to store managers, though not yet fully visible at the corporate level through the dashboards Cardinal had built, that something was badly wrong.

The core problem showed up first as pick accuracy — the rate at which an associate pulling an online order from the shelf actually retrieved the correct item in the correct quantity. Across the twelve pilot stores over the six-week measurement window from launch through the end of December, pick accuracy averaged 62 percent. That number requires some unpacking to appreciate how severe it was in practice: it meant that nearly four in ten BOPIS orders contained at least one item that was wrong, missing, or substituted without authorization. Some of that was substitution — an associate, unable to find the ordered item because the system said it was in stock when it was not, would grab something similar and hope the customer wouldn't mind, or would flag the order as partially fulfilled and hope the customer would accept a refund for the missing item on pickup. Some of it was simple wrong-item picking, exacerbated by the fact that the system's location data — which aisle and bay a SKU should be in — was itself unreliable, since store staff had spent years quietly relocating fast-moving items without updating any system of record.

The second visible failure was speed. Against the nine-minute promise, actual time from order-ready notification to customer pickup averaged 41 minutes across the pilot period — more than four and a half times the marketed commitment. The bottleneck was almost entirely upstream of the pickup counter itself: associates were spending the bulk of that time searching for items the system said were in stock but that could not be found on the shelf, then escalating to a manager, then either finding the item elsewhere in the store or contacting the customer to negotiate a substitution or refund. The pickup counter handoff itself, once an order was actually assembled, typically took under two minutes — which meant the nine-minute promise, as a piece of operational engineering, was not fundamentally unreasonable for the handoff step, but had never accounted for the reality that a meaningful share of orders could not be picked accurately or quickly against a 71-percent-accurate inventory record.

The third and most visible-to-customers failure was cancellations. Over the same six-week window, 2,300 orders across the twelve pilot stores were cancelled — either by the customer, frustrated after waiting well beyond the promised time or informed that an ordered item was not actually available, or by store staff themselves, unable to fulfill an order and choosing to cancel rather than continue searching. Against a pilot base of twelve stores over six weeks during what should have been peak holiday volume, 2,300 cancellations represented a meaningful fraction of total BOPIS orders attempted — internal tracking put the cancellation rate at approximately 34 percent of all orders placed through the channel during the period, though Blue Ridge's public reporting at the time did not disclose that figure.

Perhaps the most telling and, in hindsight, most diagnostic detail from the pilot period was behavioral rather than statistical: store managers across multiple pilot locations began independently keeping their own handwritten inventory counts, in notebooks and on clipboards, rather than trusting the new system's on-hand figures. This was not defiance so much as pragmatic self-preservation — managers were being held accountable for fulfillment performance on a system whose underlying data they had already learned, through repeated failed picks, could not be trusted. Nakashima, visiting pilot stores in December, described finding a three-ring binder at the customer service counter of the Bristol, Virginia location with a full physical count of the store's fastener aisle, maintained daily by the assistant manager because "she said she didn't believe the screen, and she was right not to."

By the first week of January 2024, with the holiday surge past and the pilot's numbers fully visible in the data, Ashby-Ng and Trumbo directed Karagiannis and Nakashima to lead a formal diagnostic review before any decision was made about expanding, pausing, or cancelling the rollout to the remaining 26 stores.

## Part Five: The Diagnostic

Karagiannis and Nakashima's review ran roughly two months, from mid-January through mid-March 2024. It combined store visits to all twelve pilot locations, structured interviews with store managers and pick associates, a statistical audit comparing system on-hand records against physical counts for a stratified sample of SKUs across the pilot stores, and a review of Cardinal's implementation documentation against what had actually been operationalized at the store level.

The finding, stated in the diagnostic's executive summary and repeated in various forms by nearly everyone involved in the program afterward, was this: Cardinal's system, like most modern inventory and fulfillment platforms, was built on an assumption that stores would perform disciplined, recurring cycle counts — typically a rolling program where a defined subset of the store's SKUs is physically counted and reconciled against the system record every week, such that the full store assortment gets counted several times a year. This is standard practice in retail operations that support real-time inventory visibility and fulfillment promises; it is, in a meaningful sense, the operational precondition for the entire category of BOPIS service. Blue Ridge had never operated this way. The prior system had nominally included cycle-count functionality, but it had atrophied into an occasional, inconsistently performed activity, deprioritized for years in favor of whatever a given store manager judged more urgent on a given day — which was almost always customer-facing work rather than back-office reconciliation.

The audit's stratified sample found that at the twelve pilot stores, only 34 percent of scheduled cycle counts in the six months prior to go-live had actually been completed, and of those completed, a further review found that a meaningful share — Karagiannis's team estimated around a quarter — had been recorded without an actual physical count having taken place, essentially rubber-stamped to clear the task from a manager's queue. The diagnostic was careful to note this was not a story of individual store manager negligence; it was a story of an operating model, inherited over many years, in which cycle counting had never been resourced, trained, or held accountable as a real job function, because the old system's inaccuracy had simply been priced in as background noise the company had learned to route around informally.

The report's central conclusion was blunt: Blue Ridge had implemented a fulfillment promise on top of an inventory foundation that could not support it, and no amount of software configuration, associate retraining, or marketing adjustment would fix the pilot's numbers until the underlying inventory accuracy problem was solved directly and durably. Nakashima's operational assessment, appended to the report, made a related point about labor: pick associates at the pilot stores were, in practice, being asked to absorb BOPIS picking on top of existing responsibilities with no dedicated labor hours allocated to the function, which meant that on any day where in-store customer traffic was heavy — which was most days during the pilot period, given the holiday timing — BOPIS picking was the task that got deprioritized, compounding the accuracy problem with a speed problem.

Ashby-Ng, presenting the diagnostic to the board in late March 2024, framed the choice facing the company in three options: cancel the BOPIS initiative entirely and absorb the sunk cost, push forward with expansion on the existing timeline and hope operational performance improved with volume and experience, or pause the rollout, fund a direct remediation of the inventory accuracy problem, and restart on a revised timeline and revised customer promise. The board approved the third option in April 2024.

## Part Six: The Reset, Spring Through Fall 2024

The reset had four components, implemented largely in parallel starting in April 2024.

**A dedicated picker role at every store.** Rather than continuing to ask existing sales-floor associates to absorb BOPIS picking within their regular duties, Blue Ridge created and funded a dedicated picker position — typically one full-time-equivalent per store, scaled to 1.5 FTE at the highest-volume locations — whose primary job was fulfilling online orders and, critically, participating in the cycle-count program described below. This was the single largest addition to the program's cost structure that had not been contemplated in the original 2023 budget; it added approximately $1.4 million in incremental annualized store labor across the chain, a figure that became a permanent addition to store operating budgets rather than a one-time program cost, since the picker role was intended to be a permanent operational function rather than a launch-period bridge.

**A six-month cycle count program.** Karagiannis's merchandising team, working with Nakashima's store operations team, designed and rolled out a structured cycle-count program across all 38 stores beginning in May 2024, regardless of whether a given store was part of the BOPIS pilot or rollout schedule. The program assigned each store's dedicated picker, along with rotating support from other store staff during slower periods, a defined weekly count quota calibrated so that the full store assortment would be physically counted at least twice within the six-month window, with the fastest-moving and highest-dollar-value SKUs — including the top 200 items tracked for out-of-stock performance — counted on a more frequent rolling basis. District managers were given weekly visibility into count completion rates by store, and count completion was added, for the first time, as a formal metric in store manager performance reviews. The program ran from May through October 2024.

**A revised customer promise.** In May 2024, alongside the launch of the cycle-count program, Blue Ridge publicly revised its BOPIS pickup promise from nine minutes to two hours. This was, by every account from those involved, an uncomfortable decision — it required walking back a marketing commitment made only seven months earlier, and it invited direct comparison to national competitors who were by then routinely offering one-hour or same-day pickup. But the leadership team judged, correctly as it turned out, that a promise the company could reliably keep was more valuable to rebuilding customer trust than an aspirational number the company had already publicly failed to deliver. Trumbo later described the decision as "the least glamorous, most important call we made in the whole program," noting that two hours gave stores enough buffer to pick orders correctly against inventory data that, even as it improved, was not going to reach the reliability needed for a nine-minute promise for a long time, if ever, given the format and assortment complexity of a hardware store.

**A store-by-store restart rather than a full-chain relaunch.** Rather than resuming the rollout to the remaining 26 stores on anything resembling the original aggressive timeline, Blue Ridge restarted BOPIS store by store, gating each store's go-live on that store having completed at least one full cycle-count pass and reaching a minimum inventory accuracy threshold — initially set at 85 percent, later raised to 90 percent — verified through the diagnostic team's audit methodology rather than self-reported by the store. The twelve original pilot stores were held in a "paused" BOPIS status — order fulfillment through the channel was suspended for roughly ten weeks in spring 2024 while their cycle counts caught up — and restarted individually as each cleared the accuracy threshold, starting in July 2024. The remaining 26 stores were brought onto the platform in waves through the balance of 2024 and into early 2025, with roughly four to six stores added per wave and each wave gated by the same accuracy threshold rather than by calendar date.

This restart approach meant the full-chain rollout, originally envisioned as complete by mid-2024, was not finished until February 2025 — roughly eight months later than the original plan, on top of the four-month pause. Ashby-Ng's assessment, offered candidly in later internal reviews, was that the delay was the correct trade against the alternative of rolling out a broken customer experience to 26 more stores.

## Part Seven: The Results, Measured Through March 2025

By March 2025, the operational and financial picture had changed substantially from where it stood at the pilot's low point in January 2024.

**Inventory record accuracy** across the chain reached 94 percent, up from the 71 percent baseline measured in late 2022 and dramatically improved from the 62 percent pick-accuracy experience of the pilot period (a related but distinct metric, since pick accuracy also reflects picking process quality on top of underlying record accuracy). The 94 percent figure was verified through the same stratified physical-count audit methodology the diagnostic team had used originally, applied on a rolling basis across all 38 stores rather than self-reported.

**Out-of-stock rates** on the top 200 tracked SKUs fell to 6 percent, down from 18 percent at the program's outset — a reduction of two-thirds in the rate at which the chain's highest-traffic items were unavailable to customers. This metric mattered beyond the BOPIS channel itself, since stockouts on high-velocity items affect walk-in customers and general store traffic regardless of whether they ever use online ordering; several store managers, in later interviews, credited the improved in-stock position as much as the pickup service itself for improved customer sentiment.

**The pickup channel** had scaled to carry 11 percent of total company sales, running at an annualized rate of $34 million. Given total company revenue of roughly $310 million at the program's outset, this represented a meaningful new sales channel built essentially from zero, though it is worth noting that not all of that volume was necessarily incremental — some share represented existing customers shifting purchase behavior from in-store shopping to online ordering with pickup, a substitution effect the company's analytics team estimated at perhaps a third to forty percent of channel volume, meaning genuinely incremental sales attributable to the channel were more likely in the range of $20 million to $23 million annualized.

**Comparable store sales**, negative for six consecutive quarters entering the program at an average of minus 4.2 percent, turned positive, running at plus 3.8 percent as of the most recent reported quarter before March 2025. Leadership was careful, in board and investor communications, not to attribute the full swing to the BOPIS and inventory program alone — broader category demand, pricing actions, and store-level merchandising changes made over the same period all plausibly contributed — but the improved in-stock position and the added foot traffic associated with BOPIS pickup visits (many of which, store managers reported anecdotally, converted into additional in-store purchases beyond the online order itself) were cited internally as material contributors to the swing.

Actual pickup time performance against the revised two-hour promise, tracked chainwide as the rollout completed in early 2025, averaged 47 minutes — comfortably inside the promised window, with the company's internal target being to keep actual performance under half the promised time as a buffer against variability. Pick accuracy, the metric that had registered 62 percent during the failed pilot, reached 96 percent chainwide by March 2025. Order cancellation rates, which had run at approximately 34 percent during the pilot period, fell to under 4 percent.

## Part Eight: The Full Cost

The original program authorization in January 2023 was $6.8 million. The final accounting, as of March 2025, showed total program cost of $9.1 million in direct program spending, plus $1.4 million in added, ongoing annualized store labor from the dedicated picker roles — a total incremental investment of $10.5 million when the first full year of picker labor is included, against an original authorization of $6.8 million, an overrun of approximately 54 percent.

The components of the $9.1 million, reconstructed from program financial reviews, broke down roughly as follows. Original implementation costs — software, integration, hardware, initial training, and initial marketing — came in close to the original $6.8 million budget, at approximately $7.0 million, reflecting mostly the SKU-master reconciliation overrun noted earlier plus modest hardware cost increases. The additional roughly $2.1 million reflected costs specific to the reset: the diagnostic review itself (Karagiannis and Nakashima's team, plus outside audit support for the physical-count verification methodology, at approximately $380,000), the six-month cycle-count program's direct costs beyond the ongoing picker labor — training materials, district manager oversight time, and temporary labor brought in at several stores to accelerate initial count completion (approximately $640,000), a second marketing campaign in mid-2024 to relaunch the service under the revised two-hour promise, including cost to unwind and replace signage and materials built around the nine-minute promise (approximately $310,000), and extended program management, consulting, and Cardinal engineering support required to extend the phased rollout timeline by roughly eight months beyond the original plan (approximately $770,000).

The $1.4 million in added store labor represented the annualized cost of the dedicated picker roles across all 38 stores once the rollout was complete — a cost that, unlike most of the other categories, was not a one-time program expense but a permanent addition to Blue Ridge's store operating cost base going forward, a point the CFO's office flagged explicitly in board materials as relevant to how the channel's ongoing economics should be evaluated.

Set against this cost, the $34 million annualized pickup channel volume, together with the comparable-sales improvement, gave the program a plausible, if not overwhelming, return by the twelve-month mark following full rollout completion — internal finance estimates, using conservative assumptions about channel margin and the incremental-versus-substitution sales split described above, put the program on a path to fully repay its total cost within approximately three years of the March 2025 measurement point, assuming performance held. Ashby-Ng's framing to the board was that the number that mattered most was not the cost overrun in isolation but the fact that the company had gone from a business with structurally negative comparable sales and an inventory system nobody trusted to a business with positive comps and a verified 94 percent inventory accuracy rate — a foundational capability the company would need regardless of how the BOPIS channel itself performed going forward, since accurate inventory records support in-store operations, purchasing, and merchandising decisions well beyond the online pickup channel specifically.

## Part Nine: What Leadership Would Do Differently

In a retrospective session with the board in April 2025, Ashby-Ng, Trumbo, Karagiannis, and Nakashima each offered assessments of what they would change if approaching the same problem again. Their answers, while distinct, converged on a small number of themes.

**Sequence the operational foundation before the customer promise.** Nearly everyone involved identified the core error as having launched a customer-facing speed commitment before validating that the underlying inventory operation could support it. Karagiannis was the most direct on this point: "If I could redo one thing, it would be refusing to let marketing build a campaign around a number from someone else's stores before we had a single week of our own data. Cardinal's nine minutes was real, at their reference accounts. It was never going to be real at ours until we did the work those accounts had already done before they went live." The broader lesson, as the team articulated it for the case record, is that when a technology vendor cites reference-account performance, the receiving organization needs to explicitly diagnose whether it has already built the operational preconditions those reference accounts had in place — not assume that installing the same software will produce the same result.

**Budget for the labor the new capability actually requires, not the labor the old system used.** Trumbo was candid that the absence of a dedicated picker labor line in the original budget reflected an implicit and incorrect assumption that new software would create efficiency sufficient to absorb a new function within existing headcount. "We budgeted for the system," he said, "and we didn't budget for the work. Cycle counting and order picking are jobs. They take hours. We should have modeled the labor hours those jobs required first, and then decided if the economics worked, rather than assuming the software would somehow make the hours disappear."

**Pilot for diagnosis, not for confirmation.** Nakashima's reflection focused on the choice to launch the pilot at twelve stores simultaneously, during the highest-volume period of the year. "We ran the pilot like we were trying to prove the thing worked, not like we were trying to find out whether it worked. If we'd started with two or three stores, in a slower season, with real intention to look hard for what was broken before we scaled it, we would have found the cycle-count problem in three weeks instead of finding it in the middle of the holiday rush with twelve stores and a marketing campaign already live." The team's related point was that pilot store selection and pilot timing should be chosen to make problems easy to find and cheap to fix, which typically means smaller scale and off-peak timing — the opposite of the instinct to pilot under conditions that maximize the chance of an impressive result if things go well.

**Make store-level operational discipline a tracked, accountable metric well before any technology change, not as a response to one.** Ashby-Ng's summary observation was the broadest: the company had known, at some level, for years that inventory accuracy was a problem, but had never made cycle-count discipline a management priority with real accountability until the BOPIS pilot forced the issue in the most painful possible way. "We could have fixed the cycle-count problem in 2021, for a fraction of what it cost us to discover it live in front of holiday customers in 2023. We didn't, because nothing was forcing our hand. The lesson I keep coming back to is that we shouldn't need a public failure to go fix a foundational problem we already knew existed."

**Be willing to change the public promise once the data says the original promise was wrong.** The final point of consensus, offered jointly, was that the willingness to publicly walk back the nine-minute commitment to two hours — an uncomfortable, faintly embarrassing decision in the moment — was ultimately the single highest-leverage choice made in the entire program. Setting a promise the company could actually keep, even a much less impressive one, did more to rebuild customer trust over the following year than any further engineering effort aimed at somehow salvaging the original number would have. As Trumbo put it, "A kept two-hour promise beats a broken nine-minute promise every single time, and it took us longer than it should have to accept that the number we'd already told customers wasn't the number that mattered. What mattered was whether we did what we said."

---

## Conclusion

Blue Ridge Hardware and Supply's BOPIS and inventory system program is, in its final accounting, a qualified success: a $6.8 million bet that became a $10.5 million investment, delivered roughly eighteen months later than originally planned, but which ultimately produced a 94 percent accurate inventory system, a stockout rate cut by two-thirds, a pickup channel generating $34 million in annualized sales, and a return to positive comparable store sales after six consecutive negative quarters. The path to that outcome ran directly through a public, painful pilot failure — 62 percent pick accuracy, 41-minute waits against a nine-minute promise, and 2,300 cancelled orders in six weeks — that exposed a gap between what the new technology assumed about the business and what the business actually was.

The case's central lesson is less about inventory systems or BOPIS specifically than about the relationship between technology promises and operational foundations. Blue Ridge did not fail because it chose the wrong vendor or the wrong software architecture; Cardinal's platform, once paired with a real cycle-count discipline and appropriately staffed picking function, performed close to its advertised capability. Blue Ridge failed, in the pilot, because it built a customer promise on top of an operational discipline it had never actually established, and it succeeded, eventually, only after pausing to build that discipline directly rather than hoping the software would produce it as a byproduct. For any organization evaluating a similar technology-enabled customer promise, the transferable question is not "can the vendor's software do this," but "does our organization already do the underlying operational work the software assumes we're doing" — and if the honest answer is no, the sequencing, budget, and pilot design need to treat building that operational foundation as the actual first phase of the program, not as a footnote to it.
