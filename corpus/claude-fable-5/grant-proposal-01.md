## Statement of Need

Kings Basin Unified School District serves 6,420 students across eleven schools in Hanford, California, in the heart of the southern San Joaquin Valley. Our community is rural and agricultural. Families here work the dairies, orchards, and row crops of Kings County, and the district's demographics reflect that economy. Seventy-four percent of our students are Hispanic or Latino. Nine percent speak Punjabi at home, part of a longstanding Sikh farming community whose children are frequently the first in their families to attend American schools. Eighty-two percent of our students qualify for free or reduced-price meals. Thirty-six percent are English learners, and twelve percent are eligible for migrant education services — a proportion far above the state average, and one that means a meaningful share of our children change schools mid-year, sometimes more than once, as their families follow the harvest.

Against this backdrop sits the number that drives this application: **24 percent of Kings Basin third graders read proficiently in English language arts**, as measured by the California Assessment of Student Performance and Progress. Three of every four children in our district reach the end of third grade — the point at which instruction pivots from learning to read to reading to learn — without the foundational literacy skills they need for everything that follows. The research consequences are well documented: students who are not reading proficiently by the end of third grade are four times more likely to leave high school without a diploma, and the risk compounds for students living in poverty, for English learners, and for students with interrupted schooling. Our district concentrates all three risk factors at once.

The causes are structural, not personal. Our early-grade teachers are committed, but they are stretched. Kings Basin, like most rural districts in the Central Valley, competes for teachers against larger districts in Fresno and Visalia that offer higher salaries and shorter commutes. We staff a meaningful share of our primary classrooms with teachers in their first three years of practice, many on intern or provisional credentials, and turnover at our highest-need sites erodes whatever instructional coherence a school builds. New teachers arrive with uneven preparation in structured literacy. Veteran teachers trained a generation ago received little instruction in the science of reading. Our English learners need teachers fluent in integrated and designated English language development; our Punjabi-speaking families need schools that can communicate with them at all; our migrant students need schools that can assess them quickly on arrival and place them in intervention without losing weeks.

Chronic absenteeism compounds the instructional challenge. When students in the primary grades miss ten percent or more of the school year — and in our district, absenteeism rates climbed sharply after the pandemic and have not fully recovered — no reading curriculum, however well designed, can reach them consistently.

We also know what works here, because we have done it. From 2019 to 2023, with support from the Delaney Trust and district funds, Kings Basin operated an instructional coaching model at three of our elementary schools. Site-based literacy coaches worked shoulder to shoulder with K–3 teachers: modeling lessons, observing and giving feedback, leading grade-level data teams around DIBELS benchmark and progress-monitoring data, and organizing targeted small-group intervention. At those three schools, third-grade ELA proficiency rose from 21 percent to 34 percent over four years — a thirteen-point gain — **while the district as a whole stayed flat**. The comparison is as close to a natural experiment as a small district produces: same labor market, same demographics, same curriculum adoption, same pandemic disruption. The variable was coaching, and the variable moved the outcome.

The need, then, is not for a new theory. It is for the resources to extend a proven model to the schools and students who have not yet had it, and to do so with the durability that our first attempt lacked. We are transparent in this application about that history: our prior coaching grant lapsed in 2024 with funds undrawn after the departure of our coaching lead, and our FY2024 single audit carried a finding on time-and-effort documentation that we have since corrected. Those experiences shaped the design of this proposal — its partnership with the county office of education for coach supervision, its succession planning, and its strengthened fiscal controls — and we address them directly in the Organizational Capacity section below. The children of Kings Basin cannot afford for us to learn those lessons twice. With the Rural Readers Initiative's support, we will not.

## Project Description

**Every Reader, Every School** will extend Kings Basin's proven K–3 literacy coaching model from three pilot elementary schools to all seven of the district's elementary schools, reaching approximately 2,100 students in kindergarten through third grade each year, with the explicit aim of raising districtwide third-grade ELA proficiency from 24 percent to at least 36 percent by the end of the grant period. The project has four integrated components.

### Component 1: Site-Based Literacy Coaching at All Seven Elementary Schools

The core of the project is people. The district will employ **five full-time literacy coaches** — three serving the four expansion schools (with the two smallest schools sharing a coach), and two sustaining and deepening the model at the three original pilot schools — under the direction of a **1.0 FTE Coaching Lead** housed in the Curriculum and Instruction department and reporting to Director of Curriculum Elena Vasquez-Ortiz.

Coaches will follow the practice model validated in our 2019–2023 pilot:

- **Instructional coaching cycles.** Each K–3 teacher participates in at least three formal coaching cycles per year — a planning conference, a modeled or co-taught lesson, an observation, and a debrief — focused on explicit, systematic instruction in phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary, and comprehension aligned to the science of reading.
- **Data team facilitation.** Coaches lead grade-level data meetings every six weeks, using DIBELS 8th Edition benchmark and progress-monitoring data to regroup students for tiered intervention. This routine is particularly important for our migrant students: coaches ensure every newly enrolled student is assessed within five instructional days and placed in intervention immediately, rather than waiting for the next benchmark window.
- **English learner integration.** Every coaching cycle addresses integrated English language development. Coaches receive training in supporting teachers of English learners, and the district will produce family-facing literacy materials in Spanish and Punjabi so that home practice is accessible to the 83 percent of our families whose home language is not English.
- **New teacher intensive support.** Teachers in their first three years, and any teacher on an intern or provisional credential, receive doubled coaching contact — a design feature that our pilot data suggest contributed to improved retention at coached sites, and that we will now measure formally.

### Component 2: County Office of Education Partnership for Coach Supervision and Continuity

The **Kings County Office of Education (KCOE)** will serve, under a memorandum of understanding, as the professional supervisor and developer of the coaching corps. KCOE literacy specialists will provide monthly coach professional learning, quarterly fidelity observations of each coach using a structured practice rubric, and an annual calibration institute. Critically, the KCOE partnership is also our continuity insurance: because the coaching model, its training materials, and its fidelity tools will live at the county office as well as in the district, the departure of any single individual — including the Coaching Lead — cannot again strand the program. KCOE has committed in writing to provide interim coaching supervision within ten business days of any vacancy in the Coaching Lead position. This design responds directly to the failure mode that ended our prior grant.

### Component 3: West Hills Community College Paraeducator-to-Teacher Pathway

Kings Basin cannot coach its way out of a teacher pipeline problem; it must also grow its own teachers. **West Hills Community College** will partner with the district to enroll a cohort of **twelve district paraeducators per year** in a structured pathway toward a teaching credential with an early literacy emphasis. The district will provide tuition assistance, release time for coursework, and coach mentorship; West Hills will provide advising, articulated coursework, and guaranteed transfer pathways to CSU partner programs. Pathway participants will complete their fieldwork in coached K–3 classrooms, so that they enter the profession already fluent in the district's literacy model. Our paraeducators overwhelmingly live in Hanford, reflect our students' languages and cultures — including Spanish- and Punjabi-speaking staff — and, when they earn credentials, stay. This component converts the grant's four-year investment into a permanent local supply of literacy-trained teachers.

### Component 4: County Library Summer Reading Bridge

Summer learning loss falls hardest on the students this project serves. The **Kings County Library**, through its Hanford branch, will operate a **six-week summer reading program** each year for rising first through fourth graders at the seven project schools, co-designed with district coaches so that summer activities extend the school-year scope and sequence rather than diverging from it. The program will include family literacy nights delivered in English, Spanish, and Punjabi; a book-distribution component placing at least eight books per participating child into homes each summer; and a mobile outreach schedule aligned to the neighborhoods and labor camps where our migrant families live, acknowledging that transportation is a genuine barrier in a rural district. Target enrollment is 400 students per summer, prioritizing students below benchmark on the spring DIBELS composite.

### Implementation Timeline

- **Year 1 (2027–28):** Hire and train coaching corps by August; launch full model at all seven schools; establish KCOE supervision routines; enroll first West Hills cohort; first summer program in summer 2028.
- **Years 2–3:** Full operation; annual fidelity reviews; second and third West Hills cohorts; midpoint external evaluation report after Year 2.
- **Year 4:** Full operation; transition planning executed per the Sustainability section; final evaluation and cost-effectiveness report; district assumes ongoing costs beginning July 1, 2031, using supplemental state funds already committed through 2030 and renewed in the district's multi-year budget.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

The project's goals map directly to the five outcome measures the Foundation requires for annual reporting. Baselines are drawn from 2025–26 district data; targets are cumulative and districtwide unless noted.

### Goal 1: Increase early literacy achievement.

- **Objective 1.1:** Districtwide third-grade ELA proficiency on CAASPP will rise from 24 percent (baseline) to 27 percent by end of Year 1, 30 percent by Year 2, 33 percent by Year 3, and **at least 36 percent by end of Year 4**, matching the trajectory achieved by the three pilot schools between 2019 and 2023.
- **Objective 1.2:** The proficiency gap between English learners and all students on third-grade ELA will narrow by at least five percentage points over the grant period.
- **Objective 1.3:** The percentage of K–3 students meeting the DIBELS 8th Edition composite benchmark will increase by at least four percentage points per year at each participating school, and **at least 65 percent of students below benchmark in the fall will demonstrate above-typical growth** on the DIBELS composite by spring, each year.

### Goal 2: Reduce chronic absenteeism among K–3 students.

- **Objective 2.1:** K–3 chronic absenteeism at participating schools will decline by at least two percentage points per year from the 2025–26 baseline, for a cumulative reduction of at least eight percentage points by Year 4, supported by coach-facilitated family engagement, attendance data reviews embedded in six-week data team cycles, and summer program continuity.

### Goal 3: Strengthen teacher retention and the local teacher pipeline.

- **Objective 3.1:** Year-over-year retention of K–3 teachers at participating schools will improve from the district's three-year rolling baseline to **at least 85 percent by Year 2 and at least 88 percent by Year 4**.
- **Objective 3.2:** Forty-eight paraeducators (twelve per year) will enroll in the West Hills pathway; at least 75 percent will persist year to year, and at least fifteen will complete an associate degree or transfer to a credential program by end of Year 4.

### Goal 4: Deliver the model with fidelity and cost discipline.

- **Objective 4.1:** One hundred percent of K–3 teachers at participating schools will complete at least three coaching cycles per year, verified by coaching logs; each coach will score proficient or above on the KCOE fidelity rubric by the end of Year 1 and maintain that rating thereafter.
- **Objective 4.2:** Every migrant student enrolling mid-year will be benchmark-assessed within five instructional days of enrollment, 100 percent compliance verified quarterly.
- **Objective 4.3:** Cost per student served (total project expenditures, grant plus match, divided by unduplicated K–3 students at participating schools) will not exceed **$360 per student per year**, reported annually with the methodology in the Evaluation Plan.
- **Objective 4.4:** Grant drawdowns will track within ten percent of the approved budget each year, verified through quarterly budget-to-actual reviews — a direct control responding to the underspending that characterized the prior grant.

## Evaluation Plan

Evaluation for this project serves two masters honestly: the Foundation's need for credible annual evidence on its five required measures, and the district's need for actionable data every six weeks. The design addresses both.

**External evaluator.** The district will contract with an independent evaluator experienced in rural education research (procured competitively in the first quarter of Year 1; the Central Valley Educational Research Consortium and two university-based evaluation centers have confirmed capacity and interest). The evaluator will produce an annual report to the Foundation each fall covering the prior school year, a midpoint report after Year 2, and a summative report in Year 4.

**Design.** Because the model expands to all seven elementary schools, a contemporaneous control group within the district is not available — nor, given the pilot results, would withholding coaching be defensible. The evaluation will therefore use three complementary approaches: (1) an **interrupted time-series design** comparing each expansion school's post-implementation trajectory on CAASPP and DIBELS against its own five-year pre-implementation trend; (2) **benchmark comparisons** against demographically matched districts in Kings, Tulare, and Fresno counties using public CAASPP and chronic absenteeism data; and (3) a **replication test** of whether expansion schools reproduce the pilot schools' 2019–2023 gain trajectory, which functions as the project's strongest internal evidence standard.

**Measures, sources, and cadence** — aligned to the Foundation's five required reporting metrics:

1. **Third-grade ELA proficiency:** CAASPP, annually, districtwide and by school, disaggregated for English learners, migrant students, and socioeconomically disadvantaged students.
2. **DIBELS composite growth:** DIBELS 8th Edition administered to all K–3 students at fall, winter, and spring benchmarks, with progress monitoring every two to three weeks for students in intervention. Growth reported as percentage meeting composite benchmark and percentage of below-benchmark students achieving above-typical growth.
3. **Chronic absenteeism:** District student information system, reported monthly to data teams and annually to the Foundation using the state's chronic absenteeism definition (missing ten percent or more of enrolled days).
4. **Teacher retention at participating schools:** Human resources records, reported annually as the percentage of K–3 teachers returning to the same school, with exit-survey data distinguishing retirements and involuntary separations from attrition the project can influence.
5. **Cost per student served:** Total annual project expenditures (grant plus district match) divided by unduplicated K–3 enrollment at the seven schools, certified by the Assistant Superintendent of Business and reconciled to the general ledger.

**Implementation and fidelity data.** Coaching logs (cycles per teacher, minutes, focus areas), KCOE fidelity rubric scores, data-team meeting records, migrant-student assessment timeliness, West Hills cohort enrollment and persistence, and summer program attendance and book distribution counts will be collected continuously and reviewed quarterly by the project leadership team.

**Use of data for improvement.** Every six weeks, school data teams act on DIBELS progress-monitoring results. Quarterly, the Coaching Lead, Director Vasquez-Ortiz, and KCOE review fidelity and implementation data and adjust coach deployment. Annually, the leadership team and the external evaluator conduct a formal improvement review, and findings are reported to the Board of Trustees in public session. The district commits to sharing all annual reports with the Foundation on its reporting schedule and to participating in any cross-site learning the Rural Readers Initiative convenes.

## Organizational Capacity

Kings Basin Unified brings to this project a leadership team that has run the model before, a partner structure designed around the lessons of our own recent failures, and a fiscal office that has confronted and corrected an audit finding rather than minimized it. We describe all three plainly.

**Leadership.** Superintendent **Dr. Priyanka Raghunathan**, in her sixth year leading the district, has made early literacy the district's first Local Control and Accountability Plan goal and has committed the cash match from unrestricted funds in the Board-adopted multi-year budget. Director of Curriculum **Elena Vasquez-Ortiz** designed and supervised the 2019–2023 pilot; she will serve as project director at no cost to the grant. Assistant Superintendent of Business **Kaori Tanabe** oversees a district budget of roughly $95 million and will supervise all fiscal administration of the award. Grants Manager **DeAndre Booker** will manage compliance, drawdowns, and Foundation reporting, with grant management as his sole professional responsibility.

**Demonstrated results.** The pilot's outcomes — a thirteen-point third-grade proficiency gain at coached schools against a flat district — are described above. Equally relevant is what the district built to achieve them: a coaching practice model, training sequence, and data-team protocol that exist in documented form and will be transferred, not reinvented, in this expansion.

**The Delaney Trust grant, addressed directly.** In 2024, a $600,000 Delaney Trust award supporting the pilot's continuation lapsed with only $410,000 drawn. The proximate cause was the resignation of the coaching lead in 2023; without that position filled, the district could not deploy coach salaries and training funds at the planned pace, and the award expired before a successor was seated. We take three lessons from this, and each is engineered into the present proposal. First, **key-person risk is now shared institutionally**: the KCOE memorandum of understanding places coach supervision, training materials, and fidelity tools at the county office as well as the district, and commits KCOE to interim supervision within ten business days of any Coaching Lead vacancy. Second, **succession is planned in advance**: the two senior coaches from the pilot schools will be designated and trained as acting Coaching Leads, and the position's compensation has been benchmarked against regional districts to improve retention. Third, **spending pace is now monitored as a compliance obligation**: Objective 4.4 commits the district to quarterly budget-to-actual reviews, with any variance beyond ten percent triggering a written corrective plan to the Superintendent and, where material, proactive communication with the Foundation. We would rather tell a funder early that a plan needs adjusting than return money late.

**The FY2024 audit finding, addressed directly.** The district's FY2024 single audit included a finding on time-and-effort certification for federally funded staff: personnel activity documentation for a subset of split-funded positions was incomplete or untimely. Assistant Superintendent Tanabe has implemented a corrective action plan that is now fully in effect: semiannual certifications for single-cost-objective positions and monthly personnel activity reports for split-funded staff, generated through a new module of the district's financial system rather than by manual collection; mandatory annual training for all supervisors who certify time; and a quarterly internal review by the business office of a sample of certifications, with results reported to the Board audit committee. The district's FY2025 audit, issued in spring 2026, carried **no repeat finding**. Although Foundation funds are not federal, the district will apply these same documentation standards to all staff charged to this grant, so that every salary dollar the Foundation invests is supported by contemporaneous, certified effort records.

**Partner capacity.** The Kings County Office of Education operates literacy support programs across the county's districts and employs credentialed literacy specialists with coaching supervision experience. West Hills Community College operates established teacher-preparation pathway programs with CSU articulation agreements. The Kings County Library has run summer reading programming in Hanford for over two decades and operates the mobile outreach capacity this project will use. Letters of commitment and draft memoranda of understanding from all three partners accompany this application.

**Serving our families.** District capacity includes bilingual Spanish staff at every elementary school, Punjabi-speaking community liaisons, and a migrant education program with dedicated recruiters who maintain contact with families as they move — infrastructure this project will use for enrollment, assessment timeliness, and summer program outreach.

## Sustainability

The Foundation's central sustainability question — how does coaching continue after year four? — has a specific answer in Kings Basin, not a hopeful one.

**Committed funding already in place.** The district receives supplemental and concentration funding under California's Local Control Funding Formula, driven by our 82 percent unduplicated pupil count, and the Board of Trustees has **already committed supplemental state funds to early literacy staffing through 2030** in the adopted Local Control and Accountability Plan and multi-year budget. The grant period (2027–2031) therefore overlaps substantially with funding the district has already programmed. The transition the Foundation is being asked to bridge is not from grant funding to nothing; it is from a grant-supported expansion to a state-funded steady state.

**Stepped absorption of costs.** The district's plan phases coaching positions onto district funding sources during the grant, not after it. The district cash match ($150,000 per year) covers a portion of coaching costs from Year 1. Beginning in Year 3, the district will shift one coach position to LCFF supplemental funds; in Year 4, a second. By July 1, 2031, all five coach positions and the Coaching Lead will be carried on district and state funds, with the LCAP renewal cycle in 2029–30 the vehicle for extending the existing through-2030 commitment — a renewal the Superintendent will bring to the Board with four years of outcome data behind it. Our experience is that school boards sustain what demonstrably works; the pilot schools' coaching positions were sustained on district funds after 2023 for precisely this reason.

**Cost reduction through capacity built.** Two design features lower the model's long-run cost. The West Hills pathway produces locally rooted, literacy-trained teachers, reducing turnover costs and the volume of intensive new-teacher coaching required. And the coaching model itself builds distributed expertise: by Year 4, each school will have trained grade-level leads capable of facilitating data teams, allowing the coach-to-teacher ratio to widen modestly without loss of fidelity — a transition KCOE will supervise.

**Institutional durability.** The KCOE partnership ensures the model's intellectual property — training sequences, fidelity rubrics, data protocols — survives any personnel change. The county library's summer program, once co-designed, continues under the library's own operating budget with district coordination. Sustainability, in short, does not depend on a future grant; it depends on commitments the district has already made and a cost structure the project deliberately shrinks.

## Budget Narrative

**Total project cost:** $3,000,000 over four years — $2,400,000 requested from the Rural Readers Initiative ($600,000 per year) and $600,000 in district cash match ($150,000 per year, 25 percent of the annual request), committed from unrestricted general fund and LCFF supplemental resources in the Board-adopted multi-year budget. All figures below are annual and consistent across the four years except where noted; a detailed line-item budget accompanies this narrative.

**Personnel — Literacy Coaches: $390,000 grant / $110,000 match ($500,000 total).** Five full-time literacy coaches at an average of $100,000 per year in salary and statutory and health benefits, consistent with the district's certificated salary schedule plus a coaching stipend benchmarked to retain experienced practitioners. The grant carries 3.9 FTE; the district match carries 1.1 FTE from Year 1, rising as positions shift to district funds in Years 3 and 4 (in those years, match and district LCFF funds together carry two full positions, and grant funds redirect proportionally to sustainability transition costs, coach-development capacity, and the summer program, per the line-item budget).

**Personnel — Coaching Lead: $95,000 grant / $30,000 match ($125,000 total).** One 1.0 FTE Coaching Lead responsible for coach deployment, model fidelity, and the KCOE partnership. Compensation reflects the regional benchmarking described in Organizational Capacity, a deliberate retention investment given the program's history. Effort for all grant-funded staff will be documented under the strengthened time-and-effort procedures described above.

**Contracted Services — Kings County Office of Education: $45,000 grant.** Annual MOU covering monthly coach professional learning, quarterly fidelity observations for each coach, the summer calibration institute, and the interim-supervision guarantee. Priced at KCOE's standard interagency rates.

**Contracted Services — External Evaluation: $30,000 grant.** Competitively procured evaluation services covering annual Foundation reports, the Year 2 midpoint report, and the Year 4 summative report, including all disaggregated analyses and the cost-per-student certification methodology.

**West Hills Pathway Support: $30,000 grant / $6,000 match ($36,000 total).** Tuition and fee assistance ($2,000 per participant for twelve participants annually), textbooks and testing fees, and substitute or release-time costs enabling paraeducators to attend daytime fieldwork and advising. West Hills contributes advising and articulation services in kind.

**Summer Reading Bridge: $25,000 grant / $4,000 match ($29,000 total).** District share of the co-operated library program: books for home distribution (eight per child for 400 children, purchased at library consortium pricing), trilingual family literacy night materials, mobile outreach fuel and staffing supplements, and coach summer stipends for curriculum alignment. The county library contributes facilities, librarian staffing, and its mobile unit.

**Instructional Materials and Assessment: $20,000 grant.** Decodable text sets and intervention materials for expansion-school classrooms, DIBELS 8 licensing and materials for approximately 2,100 K–3 students, and family take-home literacy kits produced in English, Spanish, and Punjabi.

**Professional Development and Travel: $10,000 grant.** Registration and travel for coaches and the Coaching Lead to attend state literacy conferences and structured-literacy training, and mileage for coaches serving multiple rural sites.

**Grant Administration and Indirect Costs: $55,000 grant.** Includes 0.25 FTE of the Grants Manager's time dedicated to compliance, quarterly budget-to-actual monitoring, drawdown management, and Foundation reporting ($28,000), plus indirect costs at the district's state-approved rate applied to modified direct costs ($27,000) — covering payroll, accounting, audit, and facilities support. We note that administration is intentionally funded rather than absorbed invisibly: disciplined drawdown management is itself a corrective commitment arising from the Delaney experience.

**Cost per student.** At full implementation, total annual project cost of $750,000 (grant plus match) serving approximately 2,100 unduplicated K–3 students yields a cost of roughly **$357 per student per year**, within the $360 ceiling set in Objective 4.3 and reported annually as the Foundation requires. Set against the documented thirteen-point proficiency gain the model produced at pilot schools — and against the lifetime cost of a child who reaches fourth grade unable to read — the district believes this represents the highest-leverage investment available in Kings Basin, and we respectfully request the Foundation's partnership in making it at every one of our schools.

## Closing Statement

Kings Basin Unified comes to the Rural Readers Initiative with evidence, candor, and a plan. The evidence is four years of results at three schools where coaching moved third-grade reading proficiency thirteen points while everything around it stayed still. The candor concerns the two blemishes in our file — a lapsed grant and an audit finding — both of which we have named in this application, diagnosed honestly, and engineered against in the design of this project. The plan is to give the other four elementary schools, and the 2,100 kindergartners through third graders who fill all seven, the same instructional support that changed outcomes at the first three, and to hand the finished program to a district budget that has already made room for it.

Three of every four third graders in Hanford cannot yet read proficiently. We know the intervention that changes that number, we have the partners to deliver it durably, and we have the state funding commitment to carry it past the grant's horizon. What we ask of the Foundation is the four-year bridge between the model we proved and the district that sustains it.
