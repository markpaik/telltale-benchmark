**TO:** All Employees
**FROM:** Dolores Kaminski-Lim, Chief Product Officer
**DATE:** November 9, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Launch of the Gateway Migration Program and the March 31, 2027 Deadline

I am writing to open a program that will be the company's central operating priority for the next five months, and to be direct about why it cannot slip.

Our card-present processor has confirmed in writing that it will decommission the legacy gateway on March 31, 2027. This is not a soft date, a negotiating position, or a deadline we can extend by escalating to their account team. We have asked. The gateway hardware they run it on is itself end-of-life, their own certifications lapse in the second quarter, and they have told us plainly that there will be no exceptions for any independent software vendor on the platform, regardless of size. On April 1, 2027, any merchant of ours still transacting through the legacy gateway will be unable to accept card payments. For a restaurant, that is not a degraded experience; that is a closed restaurant.

The scope of our exposure is as follows. Of our 4,100 merchants, roughly 3,200 run terminals that can accept the firmware update to the new gateway remotely or with a brief supported session. The remaining 900 run terminal models that cannot take the update at all. Those merchants need replacement hardware installed, provisioned, and verified before the deadline. There is no software path around this. Nathan Rosenblum will circulate a separate memo on how we intend to fund the hardware replacement, and I will let his memo speak to the economics. What I want everyone to understand from this memo is the operational math: we need to complete migrations at a rate of roughly 75 merchants per week, every week, from now through late March, to land every merchant safely. As of last week we were completing about 40.

Effective today, I am establishing the Gateway Migration Program with the following structure. Kwabena Asante owns overall program delivery and will report weekly to the executive team and monthly to the board. Amara Bakr owns merchant communication, outreach sequencing, and support during the migration window; her team will publish the escalation and outreach policy within the month. Rohan Talwar owns platform readiness on the new gateway, including the remediation work coming out of the October payment card industry assessment, which interacts with this program in ways his memo will explain. Every product and engineering roadmap item that does not directly serve the migration, the assessment remediation, or contractual commitments already signed is deferred until April. I know that sentence lands hard on teams that have worked on features scheduled for the first quarter. I have made this call myself and I will answer for it. A feature that ships in February to merchants who cannot take payment in April is not a feature.

I also want to acknowledge the context everyone is working in. The board approved a reduction in force last week, and Nathan's memo will address it. Asking a smaller company to run its largest-ever operational program is a hard thing, and I will not pretend otherwise. But I would rather be honest about the sequencing than let anyone believe these are unrelated events. The migration protects the revenue that pays for everything else we want to do. Our merchants are single-location restaurants, most of them family-run, and the trust they place in us is the entire business. We are going to get every one of them across.

Weekly program reviews begin Thursday. Attendance for named workstream owners is mandatory. Questions about the program should go to Kwabena; questions about this memo can come to me directly.

**TO:** All Employees
**FROM:** Nathan Rosenblum, Chief Financial Officer
**DATE:** November 20, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Revised FY2027 Operating Plan: Terminal Hardware Subsidy and Reduction in Force

This memo revises the operating plan the board approved in September and explains two decisions made at the November board meeting: the funding of terminal replacements for the 900 merchants who cannot migrate on existing hardware, and a reduction in force of 29 positions. These decisions were made in the same meeting because they are, financially, the same decision, and I owe everyone a clear accounting of how we got here rather than two separate announcements that pretend otherwise.

Start with the hardware. Replacing 900 terminals at list pricing costs approximately $2.6 million. Our hardware vendor has offered volume pricing that brings the cost to approximately $1.5 million if we commit to 3,000 units by December 15. The 2,100 units beyond immediate need are not waste: our installed base includes another roughly 1,400 terminals that reach end-of-support within 30 months, and the remainder covers new merchant signings at our historical pace through 2028. At the committed price, we are effectively pre-buying two years of hardware at a 42 percent discount. The board has authorized the 3,000-unit commitment, and I intend to sign before December 15. The alternative — passing hardware cost to merchants — was considered seriously and rejected. Our merchants are independent restaurants running on margins thinner than ours. A surprise $1,700 invoice in the middle of winter, attached to a deadline we set in their eyes even if the processor set it in fact, would drive churn we cannot afford and would be, in my judgment, the wrong thing to do to people who have paid us reliably for years. We will subsidize the hardware fully. Merchants pay nothing for the terminal itself.

Now the harder part. The company is not currently generating the cash to absorb a $1.5 million unplanned capital outlay, roughly $400,000 in migration program costs beyond hardware, and the remediation spend the assessment requires, while holding our existing cost structure. We evaluated debt; the terms available to us in the current environment would cost more over 24 months than the restructuring saves, and the covenants would constrain exactly the flexibility this year demands. The board approved a reduction of 29 positions, about 14 percent of the company, effective in phases between December 1 and January 15. The reduction falls across every department, including six of the 22 positions in merchant support.

I want to address the support reduction specifically, because I know it looks indefensible on its face: we are cutting support headcount at the moment support volume is projected to triple. Amara argued forcefully against the depth of the cut in her organization, and the plan changed because of her arguments — the original proposal was nine positions, and the final plan funds a pool of temporary contract agents for the January-through-March window, trained on migration scenarios only, at roughly one-third the fully loaded cost of the permanent positions. This is not a costless trade and I will not describe it as one. It is the trade that keeps the hardware subsidy funded, and the hardware subsidy is what keeps 900 merchants in business with us.

Affected employees are being told individually this week and next by their managers and by me; no one will learn their status from a list. Severance is eight weeks base plus one week per year of service, health coverage continuation for four months, and we have engaged an outplacement firm. If you have questions about the plan's numbers, bring them to me. I would rather answer a hard question in the open than have the hallway version of this memo circulate instead of the real one.

**TO:** Engineering and Platform Teams
**FROM:** Rohan Talwar, Head of Platform Reliability
**DATE:** November 24, 2026
**SUBJECT:** October PCI Assessment: Seven Findings, Remediation Plan, and Encryption Key Rotation Schedule

The October payment card industry assessment identified seven gaps that must be remediated before our next attestation in May 2027. This memo assigns owners and dates for all seven, and spends most of its length on the finding that worries me most, because I want no ambiguity in the organization about its seriousness.

The seven findings, in the assessor's numbering, are: (1) encryption key rotation on the card-present tokenization service has not executed since November 2023; (2) two internal services communicate over channels within the cardholder data environment without current transport certificates; (3) access reviews for the payments database have been performed annually rather than quarterly; (4) the segmentation test between the corporate network and the cardholder data environment is eleven months out of date; (5) logging retention on three gateway-adjacent hosts is set below the twelve-month requirement; (6) two former employees retained credentials to a deployment tool with reach into the payment environment for between four and nine weeks after departure; and (7) our incident response plan has not been exercised through a tabletop in over two years.

Findings two, four, five, and seven are straightforward and are assigned to the platform team with completion dates in December and January; the detailed tracker is in the program workspace. Finding three moves access reviews to a quarterly calendar starting December; finding six is remediated immediately — the credentials are already revoked — with a permanent fix tying deprovisioning to the HR termination workflow, due January 31, and I have asked for that one to be verified by someone outside the team that built it.

Finding one is the reason this memo exists. Rotating encryption keys on a live tokenization service serving 4,100 merchants is delicate work: done wrong, it renders stored tokens undecryptable and every card-on-file transaction fails at once. That risk is precisely why rotation was deferred in 2024, deferred again in 2025, and why we are now three years overdue on a control we attest to annually. I am not interested in litigating how we got here; I was in some of those rooms. I am interested in the fact that we will not defer it a fourth time.

The interaction with the migration matters and cuts in our favor if we sequence correctly. The new gateway uses a rebuilt tokenization path with rotation designed in. Every merchant we migrate is a merchant whose tokens move to infrastructure where this finding cannot recur. The remediation plan therefore has two tracks. Track one: rotation on the new gateway runs on an automated 90-day schedule, verified in staging by December 19 and confirmed in production with the first migrated cohorts in January. Track two: the legacy service still holds tokens for every unmigrated merchant, and the assessor will not accept "we are decommissioning it" as remediation for a control that will have been dead for 42 months by attestation. We will execute one supervised rotation on the legacy service, in four merchant cohorts, during low-volume windows in February — Sunday and Monday overnight — with a rehearsed rollback at each stage. Priya's team owns the runbook, due January 9; we rehearse against a production data snapshot twice before the first live cohort. If rehearsal surfaces problems we cannot resolve by mid-February, I will escalate to Kwabena and Dolores immediately rather than compress the schedule, because the one outcome worse than a finding at attestation is a self-inflicted outage during migration season.

Two requests of everyone in engineering. First, no changes land in the cardholder data environment outside the change process between now and May — no exceptions, including mine. Second, if you see a gap the assessor missed, tell me. A finding we surface ourselves is a plan; a finding someone else surfaces is an incident.

**TO:** Merchant Support, Account Management, and Field Operations
**FROM:** Amara Bakr, Director of Merchant Support
**DATE:** December 8, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Support Escalation and Merchant Outreach Policy for the Migration Window

This memo sets the outreach and escalation policy governing merchant contact from now through March 31. It supersedes the standard support tiering for any contact related to the migration. Read all of it; the policy has teeth, and I will hold the team to it, because the alternative to a strict policy is 900 restaurants discovering on April 1 that their card readers are decorative.

First, the outreach sequence. Every merchant receives a minimum of four touches before their scheduled migration date: a letter and email at six weeks out, a phone call at four weeks, a scheduling confirmation at two weeks, and a day-before confirmation by the merchant's stated preferred channel. For the 900 hardware-replacement merchants, add a fifth touch: a call from a named agent — the same agent each time, who owns that merchant through completion — walking through what installation day looks like. These are restaurant owners; many do their office work between the lunch rush and dinner prep. Outbound calls to merchants happen between 2:00 and 4:30 p.m. unless the merchant tells us otherwise. Calling a restaurant at 12:15 is how you train a merchant to stop answering our number.

Second, the non-responder ladder. A merchant who has not scheduled by four weeks out moves to daily contact attempts, alternating channels. At three weeks, their account manager is looped in and makes a personal call. At two weeks, the merchant's name comes to me, and I decide between a field visit and an executive call — Dolores has agreed to make calls personally for merchants inside the two-week line, and I intend to use her. At one week unscheduled, the merchant goes on the daily risk report that Kwabena reads to the executive team. No merchant reaches March 15 unscheduled without every rung of this ladder documented in the CRM. If the record isn't in the CRM, the contact didn't happen. That is not bureaucracy; that is how the next agent, the temp covering a sick day, or I at eleven at night can see exactly where a merchant stands.

Third, escalation during the window. Any inbound contact mentioning the migration bypasses the general queue and routes to the migration desk. Any migrated merchant reporting a payment failure is a Priority 1 regardless of apparent cause, with a 15-minute response target and a live handoff — no ticket-and-wait — to the on-call platform engineer if not resolved within 45 minutes. A restaurant that cannot take cards on a Friday night is losing its week's profit in real time, and it will not wait politely in a queue behind a password reset. Rohan's on-call rotation has agreed to this routing through March.

Fourth, staffing honestly. As you all know, this department is losing six positions in the reduction while volume triples. I argued against the depth of that cut and I won part of the argument: we have budget for twelve temporary contract agents from January through March. Temps handle migration scheduling, confirmations, and scripted status inquiries only. They do not handle payment failures, hardware troubleshooting, or any merchant on the risk report — those stay with permanent staff. I am asking the permanent team to carry the hardest calls at the highest volume this company has ever seen, three months after watching colleagues leave. I know exactly what I am asking. Overtime is pre-approved through March for anyone on the migration desk, comp days accrue at time and a half for weekend installation coverage, and I have Nathan's written commitment to a retention payment for permanent support staff who see the program through to April 15.

Last: tone. Some merchants will be angry, and some of that anger will be earned — a deadline they didn't choose, disruption they didn't ask for. The line I want every agent to internalize is that we are not doing this to them, we are doing this with them, and the hardware is free, and we will be there on installation day. Say it plainly, mean it, and log the call.

**TO:** Executive Team; copy to Board of Directors
**FROM:** Kwabena Asante, Vice President of Engineering
**DATE:** January 16, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Gateway Migration Program: January Status, Revised Pace, and Response to the Account Team's Objection on the Hardware Subsidy

This is the January program status. I will give the numbers first, the risks second, and then respond at some length to the objection raised by the account management team in the January 8 review — that the $1.5 million hardware commitment should have been directed to merchant retention incentives instead — because it deserves a real answer on the record rather than a hallway rebuttal.

**Status and pace.** As of Friday, January 15, we have completed 1,240 migrations: 1,090 firmware-path merchants and 150 hardware replacements. Weekly throughput has climbed from the 40 we were running in November to 82 last week, above the 75 target for the first time. Three things drove the improvement. The batch provisioning tool the platform team shipped December 22 cut firmware-path migration time from 50 minutes of agent involvement to under 15, which is where most of the gain came from. Amara's outreach ladder has scheduling running at 91 percent of merchants confirmed by the two-week mark, against 60 percent in November. And the first hardware shipment under the December vendor commitment arrived on schedule January 5, so replacement installations are no longer supply-constrained. Remaining to migrate: 2,860 merchants, 750 of them hardware replacements, against roughly ten and a half usable weeks. That requires a sustained pace of about 272 per week — no, it requires about 272 total including a February ramp; concretely, the plan holds 85 per week through January, 120 per week through February as the second temp cohort finishes training and we open Saturday installation slots, and 130 per week in March, which lands the final merchant on March 24 and preserves one week of buffer. That buffer is thinner than I would like and I will not pretend otherwise.

**Risks.** Three. First, the risk report currently carries 41 merchants inside the two-week line without a confirmed date; Dolores has made eleven executive calls and converted nine, and Amara is dispatching field visits for six merchants who do not answer any channel. My honest expectation is that between five and fifteen merchants will simply not engage no matter what we do, and we need a February decision on whether we ship pre-provisioned hardware to those locations unsolicited with a walk-through card, which I recommend. Second, the legacy key rotation Rohan's team runs in February shares an overnight window with migration cutover batches; we have deconflicted the calendars, but a rollback on either side that night consumes the other's window, so the first rotation cohort runs on a weekend with no migrations scheduled. Third, winter. A single February storm week at New England restaurant density can cost us 60 installations. The buffer week exists for exactly this, which is why I will resist every proposal to spend it on anything else.

**The subsidy objection.** At the January 8 review, the account team argued that with churn ticking up after the fall price adjustment, the $1.5 million would return more as retention incentives — discounts and credits for at-risk merchants — than as free terminals, and that merchants should have been asked to share hardware cost. I take the concern behind the objection seriously: the account team lives closest to churn and is right that it is rising. But the objection fails on its own terms, and I want to show the arithmetic rather than assert it.

The 900 hardware-replacement merchants represent roughly $10.2 million of annual recurring revenue. A merchant whose terminal stops accepting cards on April 1 does not churn in the ordinary sense — a gradual decision we can intervene in — they churn instantly, involuntarily, and angrily, and they tell every restaurant owner they know why. If even a quarter of the 900 were lost to a charge-the-merchant approach, through refusal, delay, or defection to a competitor offering free hardware — and our two largest competitors are both running exactly that offer in our territory right now — we would lose $2.5 million of recurring revenue annually to save a one-time $1.5 million, and we would lose it forever, every year. Retention incentives, by contrast, address merchants who can stay and might not; the subsidy addresses merchants who cannot stay at all without it. Spending retention money on a merchant whose terminal goes dark in April is buying a discount on a subscription that ends itself. The comparison the objection wants to make — subsidy dollars versus retention dollars — is not available to us, because without the subsidy there is no retained merchant to incent.

There is also evidence, early but real, that the subsidy is retention. Of the 150 hardware-replacement merchants migrated so far, three have received competitive offers during the window and all three stayed; two cited the free terminal and the installation experience specifically. Amara's team is logging these instances and we will have a fuller read by the March review. Meanwhile, the 2,100 surplus units from the volume commitment give the account team something it has asked for for two years: free hardware refresh as a renewal incentive for the 1,400 merchants approaching end-of-support. I have asked Nathan to release 200 units to account management for exactly that use starting in April. The subsidy and retention are not competing programs. They are the same program, sequenced.

Next full review is Thursday, February 5. The risk report goes out daily; if a merchant you own appears on it, that is your top priority for the day, and mine.

**TO:** Executive Team; copy to Board of Directors
**FROM:** Rohan Talwar, Head of Platform Reliability
**DATE:** February 9, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Legacy Key Rotation: Cohort One Results and Adjustment to the Remaining Schedule

Cohort one of the legacy encryption key rotation ran Sunday night, February 7, in the window from 11:40 p.m. to 4:15 a.m. This memo reports what happened, because part of what happened was not in the runbook, and the executive team should hear it from me before it circulates in any other form.

The rotation itself succeeded. Approximately 1,050 merchants' stored tokens — the first quarter of the legacy population by merchant identifier range — were re-encrypted under new keys, verified by automated decrypt-and-compare against a control sample, and confirmed transacting normally by 6:00 a.m. Monday. The 90-day automated rotation on the new gateway, for comparison, has now completed two scheduled cycles in production without incident, and I consider that finding closed on the new platform.

What was not in the runbook: at 1:55 a.m., the verification pass flagged 34 tokens that failed decrypt-and-compare. Under the runbook, a failure rate above ten tokens triggers rollback of the full cohort. Priya's on-call team held at the decision point, investigated for 40 minutes, and determined that all 34 tokens belonged to merchants who had churned between 2021 and 2023 and whose records had been partially purged by the data retention job — the tokens were already orphaned and undecryptable before we touched them. The team made the call to proceed rather than roll back, completed the cohort, and documented the reasoning in real time. They were right on the substance, and I have told them so. I have also told them that the next time reality departs from the runbook at two in the morning, the runbook gets amended before the next cohort rather than interpreted in the moment, and the escalation call to me gets made even if the answer seems obvious. They made that call correctly; I want the habit, not just the outcome. The runbook now carries an explicit orphaned-token exception with a verification query that distinguishes orphaned records from genuine corruption, and cohort two will run against a pre-cleaned identifier range.

One schedule adjustment. Cohort two was set for the night of February 15. The National Weather Service is carrying a significant storm signal for that Sunday into Monday, and Kwabena's migration calendar shows the following weekend clear of installations. Rather than run a delicate overnight procedure while half the on-call bench may be without power at home, I am moving cohort two to February 21–22 and compressing cohorts three and four into the first week of March, which the rehearsal timings support comfortably now that the verification step is faster. This keeps all four cohorts complete by March 8, three weeks ahead of gateway decommission and well ahead of the evidence-collection window for the May attestation. The assessor has been notified of the revised dates and has confirmed that a completed rotation with documented cohort evidence satisfies finding one in full.

Status on the remaining findings, briefly: two, three, five, and six are closed with evidence filed; four, the segmentation test, completed January 28 with one low-severity note already remediated; seven, the incident response tabletop, ran January 21 with the executive team participating, and the after-action items — two, both procedural — are assigned with February dates. As of this memo, we are on track to enter the May attestation with all seven findings closed and evidenced. I will send a final pre-attestation summary in April.

**TO:** All Employees
**FROM:** Dolores Kaminski-Lim, Chief Product Officer

**SUBJECT:** Gateway Migration Program: Closure Report

The legacy gateway was decommissioned by the processor at 12:01 a.m. on March 31. This memo closes the program I opened on November 9 and gives the company the final accounting it is owed.

Of 4,100 merchants, 4,089 completed migration before decommission. The last scheduled migration finished March 22, one day inside the buffer week, which we then needed: a March 25 verification sweep caught six merchants whose terminals had migrated but whose card-on-file tokens had not fully cut over, and the buffer absorbed the fix. Of the eleven non-responders, two were closed businesses, six activated their couriered terminals after the daily calls — three of them in the final week, one on March 30 at 9:40 p.m., talked through activation by the agent who had called him twenty-two consecutive days — and three did not. Two of the three have since called us; both were reactivated within four hours of calling, on April 1 and April 2 respectively. One merchant has told us he is retiring and closing the restaurant in May, and asked us to stop calling, which we have. Total involuntary revenue loss from the program: one merchant, closing for reasons unrelated to us. Churn among the 900 hardware-replacement merchants over the program window was 0.4 percent, against 2.1 percent in the base as a whole. Kwabena's January arithmetic has its answer.

The first four days of April, the window Amara staffed at full strength, produced 61 migration-related Priority 1 contacts, all resolved same-day, none involving a merchant unable to trade through a dinner service. Rohan enters the May attestation with all seven findings closed and evidenced; the legacy rotation completed March 6, and the legacy tokenization service itself was decommissioned entirely on April 3, which is the most permanent remediation there is. The 200 refresh units are released to account management as of this week, and the deferred product roadmap resumes with a re-planning session on April 14 — deferred, as promised, not cancelled.

I want to end with three honest sentences. This program succeeded in the same five months in which 29 of our colleagues lost their jobs, and I will not let the success story swallow that fact; both things happened, both were real, and the people who left contributed to the foundation this program stood on. The people who stayed did the finest sustained operational work I have seen in thirty years in this industry, and the retention payments going out April 30 are the smallest part of what is owed. And 4,088 restaurants opened their doors this week and took payment without a moment's thought about any of it — which was, from the first memo to this one, the entire point.
