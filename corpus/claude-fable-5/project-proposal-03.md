## Proposal to the Executive Committee of the Board of Directors

### Southern Network Rebuild: Oklahoma City Consolidation Cross-Dock and Linehaul Redesign

**Submitted by:** Fatima Bouzid, Chief Operating Officer; Deshawn Whitlock, Vice President, Network Planning
**Sponsored by:** Sanjay Doshi, Chief Executive Officer
**Financial review:** Desmond Okoye, Chief Financial Officer
**For decision at the Executive Committee meeting of February 12**

## 1. Executive Summary

Management requests Executive Committee approval of a $38.5 million capital program to rebuild Prairie Gate's southern network. The program consists of three linked elements: construction of a 112-door consolidation cross-dock in Oklahoma City; closure of the Ponca City and Enid terminals, whose freight and workforce would fold into the new facility; and a re-cut of 31 linehaul lanes executed in phases over 22 months. The program would be funded with a $30 million equipment-backed term loan arranged by the CFO and $8.5 million of cash on hand.

The case for acting now is a service and cost problem that is no longer containable within the current footprint. Company-wide on-time service has fallen from 96.1 percent to 87.4 percent in two years, with the steepest deterioration on freight moving through the southern tier. Driver turnover is running 94 percent annually, driven in large part by unproductive linehaul schedules built around two undersized, poorly located terminals. Kesterson Ag Supply — 16 percent of company revenue at roughly $49.6 million annually — has placed Prairie Gate on a 90-day service watch under a contract that expires June 30. Losing Kesterson would remove the revenue that covers a substantial share of the southern network's fixed cost and would likely trigger a broader repricing of the book as other shippers reassess.

The Committee declined a smaller version of this plan in 2023, in the shadow of a Springfield dock expansion that overran budget by 41 percent. That history is addressed directly in this proposal. The current plan carries a fixed-price construction contract structure, a dedicated program office with independent cost reporting to the CFO, a 12 percent contingency held at the Committee level rather than within the project budget, and defined go/no-go gates at three points in the schedule.

Management also examined the two principal alternatives — leasing an existing Oklahoma City building, and repairing service inside the current footprint without new construction — and presents the analysis in Section 8. Neither alternative resolves the door-count and lane-structure problems that are the root cause of the service decline, and both carry costs that approach the proposed program without its benefits.

**The decision requested on February 12 is set out in full in Section 10:** approval of the $38.5 million program, authorization of the $30 million term loan, and authorization to execute the land purchase and construction contract for the Oklahoma City facility.

## 2. The Service and Cost Problem

### 2.1 Service deterioration

Two years ago Prairie Gate delivered 96.1 percent of shipments on time, a figure competitive with the strongest regional LTL carriers in our lanes. Today that figure is 87.4 percent. The decline has not been uniform across the network. The northern terminals — Salina, Hastings, Grand Island, and the Missouri facilities — have held between 93 and 95 percent. The collapse is concentrated in the southern tier, where freight routed through Ponca City and Enid is running at 79 to 82 percent on-time, and where missed service now cascades into downstream lanes across the rest of the network.

The root causes are structural, not operational. Ponca City operates 22 doors and Enid operates 18, both built for freight profiles that no longer exist. Oklahoma-bound and Oklahoma-origin volume has grown 34 percent over five years, driven by agricultural inputs, oilfield supply, and distribution activity along the I-35 corridor. Neither terminal can turn the door volume it now receives. Freight dwells on the dock an average of 9.4 hours at Ponca City against a network standard of 4.5 hours. Trailers hold at the gate waiting for doors. Linehaul schedules built to protect connections at these terminals have been padded repeatedly, which degrades transit standards network-wide, and the padding is still not enough: 41 percent of southern-tier service failures in the last two quarters trace to missed connections at Ponca City or Enid.

Neither facility can be meaningfully expanded. Ponca City is landlocked on three sides; Enid's site could add perhaps six doors at a cost approaching $4 million, which does not close the gap and commits capital to a location that is wrong for the freight flows. Neither city sits on the density center of the southern freight. Oklahoma City does. Roughly 58 percent of southern-tier freight either originates within 60 miles of Oklahoma City, terminates there, or passes through the metro on its natural linehaul path. We currently pull that freight north to Ponca City or west to Enid, work it, and send much of it back through the Oklahoma City metro a second time. The network design taxes every shipment in the region with excess miles, excess handling, and excess time.

### 2.2 Cost consequences

The service problem is also a cost problem. The southern tier's cost per hundredweight runs 19 percent above the network average. The excess miles alone — freight backhauled to Ponca City and Enid and re-run south — consume an estimated $4.1 million annually in linehaul cost. Extra handling at overloaded docks adds an estimated $2.3 million in labor and claims. Cargo claims frequency at Ponca City is 2.4 times the network average, a direct function of dock congestion and double-handling.

Driver turnover compounds all of it. Company turnover of 94 percent is above the LTL industry norm and far above where Prairie Gate ran five years ago. Exit interviews are consistent: linehaul drivers on southern schedules cite unpredictable dispatch, long dock waits counted against their hours, and schedules rebuilt so often that home time cannot be planned. Each driver replacement costs an estimated $9,200 in recruiting, orientation, and productivity ramp. At current turnover we are spending roughly $5.5 million a year replacing drivers, and the southern schedules are the largest single contributor to quits.

### 2.3 The Kesterson exposure

Kesterson Ag Supply is Prairie Gate's largest customer at 16 percent of revenue, approximately $49.6 million annually. Kesterson's distribution pattern — Wichita and Enid origins into Oklahoma, north Texas panhandle interline points, and southern Missouri — runs squarely through the broken part of the network. Kesterson's measured on-time performance on Prairie Gate has fallen to 81 percent. In December, Kesterson placed us on a formal 90-day service watch. Their contract expires June 30, and their logistics leadership has told our sales organization plainly that renewal requires both a credible recovery on current performance and a structural answer for the future.

Management's judgment is that the near-term service watch can be survived with the tactical measures already underway (Section 8.2 describes them), but that renewal on June 30 — and retention beyond it — requires Prairie Gate to show Kesterson a committed, funded plan for the southern network. A board-approved program with a construction start date is that plan. A deferred decision is, in Kesterson's eyes, a decision.

The financial consequence of losing Kesterson would extend beyond the revenue itself. Kesterson volume provides the density that supports schedule frequency on eleven southern lanes. Without it, those lanes thin, service to remaining customers degrades further, and the fixed costs of the southern terminals spread across a smaller base. Management estimates the full-year operating income impact of a Kesterson loss at $7 to $9 million, before accounting for secondary attrition.

## 3. The Proposed Facility and Network Change

### 3.1 The Oklahoma City cross-dock

The centerpiece of the program is a new 112-door consolidation cross-dock on a 38-acre parcel in southeast Oklahoma City, near the I-35/I-240 interchange, under option through a site-control agreement executed in November. The facility specification:

- 112 dock doors in a rectangular cross-dock configuration, approximately 138,000 square feet under roof, sized for current southern volume plus 40 percent growth headroom.
- Yard capacity for 290 trailers, with paved circulation designed for hostler operations and a two-lane automated gate.
- A 14-bay maintenance shop, replacing the four-bay shops at Ponca City and Enid and reducing outside-repair spend on the southern fleet.
- Dock-management and load-planning systems consistent with the standard being deployed at Wichita, so the facility opens on the company's current technology rather than inheriting legacy systems.
- Driver amenities — parking, break facilities, and a bid room — designed with direct input from a driver advisory group, a deliberate element of the turnover response.

The facility would operate as the consolidation point for the southern network: freight originating in or destined to Oklahoma, north Texas interline points, and the southern Kansas markets would be worked once, at the density center, instead of being hauled to peripheral terminals and worked under congestion.

### 3.2 Terminal closures

Ponca City (22 doors, 61 employees) and Enid (18 doors, 46 employees) would close as freight-handling terminals in Phases 2 and 3 respectively. Local pickup-and-delivery coverage in both markets would continue, dispatched from Oklahoma City with a small satellite domicile arrangement for city drivers in each market, so customer-facing service in Ponca City and Enid does not withdraw — it is the behind-the-scenes dock work that relocates.

The Enid lease runs through 2029 with no early-termination right. Section 6.4 addresses the lease disposition strategy; the program budget carries the full remaining obligation as a conservative assumption.

### 3.3 The linehaul re-cut

Thirty-one linehaul lanes — roughly 40 percent of the southern schedule — would be redesigned in three tranches synchronized with the facility phases. The re-cut removes the Ponca City and Enid relay legs, routes southern freight through Oklahoma City on direct schedules, and rebuilds driver bid schedules around predictable dispatch windows. Network modeling, validated against twelve months of actual shipment data, projects:

- 1.9 million fewer linehaul miles annually, worth approximately $4.1 million at current cost per mile.
- Average southern-tier transit improvement of 0.7 days, restoring published standards on 24 of the 31 lanes.
- Dock handlings per shipment falling from 2.6 to 1.9 in the southern tier.
- Projected southern on-time performance of 95 percent or better at full run rate, lifting the company figure back above 94 percent.

### 3.4 Financial returns

At full run rate (month 26, four months after Phase 3 completion), the program is projected to deliver $9.6 million in annual operating benefit: $4.1 million linehaul savings, $2.3 million handling and claims reduction, $1.4 million facility-cost consolidation net of the new building's operating cost, and $1.8 million from reduced driver turnover on southern schedules. Against the $38.5 million investment, the unlevered payback is approximately 4.0 years and the ten-year internal rate of return is 18.7 percent at conservative volume assumptions — flat Kesterson volume and 2 percent underlying growth. These figures assign no value to Kesterson retention itself; if the program is credited with securing the renewal, the effective return is substantially higher.

## 5. Phasing and Freight Continuity During Cutover

The single most common failure in network rebuilds of this kind is a "big-bang" cutover that breaks service during the transition and destroys the customer confidence the project exists to restore. This program is phased specifically to avoid that failure mode. At no point does freight depend on capacity that has not been proven in live operation.

**Phase 0 — Months 1–3: Site, permits, and stabilization.** Land closing, permits, groundbreaking. In parallel, the tactical service-recovery actions already underway (added Wichita relay capacity, dedicated Kesterson trailer pool, surge staffing at Ponca City) continue through the entire construction period. These actions cost approximately $1.6 million annually in operating expense and are the bridge that holds Kesterson service through the watch period and the renewal; they are in the operating plan, not the capital budget.

**Phase 1 — Months 4–12: Construction and pre-cut.** Facility construction. In month 9, the first tranche of the linehaul re-cut — eight lanes that can be improved without the new building, by removing redundant relay legs — goes live. This tranche delivers early service improvement, gives the network planning team a live rehearsal of the re-cut process, and produces measurable results before the first terminal closes. **Gate 1 (month 10):** the Committee reviews construction cost performance and Tranche 1 service results before Phase 2 proceeds.

**Phase 2 — Months 13–17: Oklahoma City opens; Ponca City closes.** The facility opens in month 13 at partial capacity — roughly 60 doors active — running the second linehaul tranche (14 lanes) for four weeks in parallel with Ponca City. During the parallel period, freight is progressively shifted lane by lane; Ponca City remains fully staffed and operational as the fallback. Only when Oklahoma City has run four consecutive weeks at or above 94 percent on-time on the shifted lanes does Ponca City's dock close (target: month 16). This parallel-running period costs approximately $0.9 million in duplicated operating expense and is the program's insurance policy; it is carried in the operating plan. **Gate 2 (month 16):** Committee confirmation of the Ponca City closure based on demonstrated Oklahoma City performance.

**Phase 3 — Months 18–22: Full activation; Enid closes.** The remaining doors activate, the third linehaul tranche (nine lanes) cuts over with the same four-week parallel discipline, and Enid's dock closes at month 21. The maintenance shop consolidates in month 22. **Gate 3 (month 21):** closure confirmation on the same evidentiary standard as Gate 2.

**Customer communication.** A named transition manager is assigned to each of the top 40 southern accounts, with weekly service reporting during cutover windows. Kesterson receives a dedicated program liaison and a standing monthly operating review, beginning immediately upon approval — before construction starts — so the customer sees the plan, the milestones, and the performance data throughout.

Throughout all phases, published transit standards are guaranteed under the existing money-back service commitment. The program plan budgets for elevated service-recovery expense during cutover months; the modeling assumes a temporary 1.5-point on-time dip during each parallel-running window, recovering within six weeks. If actual performance breaches 92 percent company-wide for two consecutive weeks during a cutover, the shift pauses and freight reverts to the legacy routing until the cause is corrected — reversion is possible precisely because the legacy terminal remains open through each parallel period.

## 6. Staffing, Labor Exposure, and the Enid Lease

### 6.1 Workforce plan

The program affects 107 employees at the two closing terminals: 61 at Ponca City and 46 at Enid, where Terminal Manager Kathleen O'Rourke leads the operation. The Oklahoma City facility will require approximately 135 employees at full activation. The staffing plan:

- **Relocation offers to all 107 affected employees.** Based on structured interviews conducted by HR in December and January, we project approximately 38 acceptances — twelve from Enid, including Ms. O'Rourke, who has accepted the role of Terminal Manager, Oklahoma City, and roughly 26 from Ponca City. Relocation packages include moving expense reimbursement, a transition allowance, and a 12-month retention component.
- **Severance for non-relocating employees** at policy terms plus an enhancement of two weeks' pay per year of service above policy, in recognition that these closures are a company decision, not a performance issue. Outplacement support is contracted with a regional firm; both local markets have active freight and distribution employers, and we assess re-employment prospects as reasonable.
- **City driver domiciles remain in both markets.** Approximately 14 pickup-and-delivery drivers across Ponca City and Enid keep local domiciles, dispatched from Oklahoma City. These employees do not relocate and are not severed.
- **Net new hiring of approximately 97 positions in Oklahoma City**, phased with facility activation. The Oklahoma City labor market is materially deeper than either Ponca City or Enid for dock and driver hiring, and starting-wage benchmarking indicates we can hire at parity with our current southern wage structure.

Retention through closure is the critical workforce risk: we need the Ponca City and Enid teams performing at full capability up to their final day, since they are the fallback during parallel running. The plan includes stay bonuses of 15 percent of annual pay for employees who remain through their terminal's closure date, budgeted within the $5 million workforce line.

### 6.2 Labor relations exposure

Teamsters organizers have been active at the Salina and Enid docks since March. The Committee should weigh this program with that exposure squarely in view, and management has done so with outside labor counsel. The assessment:

- The Enid closure occurs at a location with active organizing. Counsel advises that a closure announced and executed for demonstrated business reasons, with documentation predating the organizing activity — which exists in the form of the 2023 proposal, network studies from that period, and the service data — is defensible. The decision record for this program has been built with that standard in mind.
- Timing and communication matter more than the decision itself. The workforce plan's terms — universal relocation offers, enhanced severance, and stay bonuses — are set at a level intended to be demonstrably fair, because the strongest organizing argument available to the Teamsters is a closure handled poorly. Announcement communications will be delivered in person by the COO and terminal management, with counsel review, and will make no reference to organizing activity.
- Salina is not affected by this program. Management is separately addressing the underlying grievances at Salina (dock scheduling and shift-bid administration) through the normal employee-relations channel; that work is outside this proposal's scope but is noted for completeness.
- Residual risk remains. An unfair labor practice charge in connection with the Enid closure is possible regardless of the quality of the record. Counsel's judgment is that the business case is strong, the documentation predates the organizing, and the exposure is manageable. A charge would create cost and distraction but would not, in counsel's assessment, enjoin the closure.

### 6.3 Driver turnover response

The program's driver benefits deserve emphasis because turnover is both a cost line and an operational-risk multiplier. The re-cut linehaul schedules are built around fixed dispatch windows and protected home time on 26 of the 31 lanes, replacing the padded, frequently rebuilt schedules that drivers cite in exit interviews. The Oklahoma City facility's driver amenities and the reduction in dock-wait time (drivers currently lose an average of 74 minutes per Ponca City stop waiting for doors) are direct responses to the top three quit reasons in our exit data. Management's target is southern-schedule turnover below 70 percent within a year of full activation; each ten points of improvement on the affected schedules is worth approximately $0.6 million annually.

### 6.4 The Enid lease

The Enid lease runs through 2029 with no early-termination right, an obligation of approximately $1.7 million over the remaining term after closure. The program budget conservatively assumes the full obligation is paid with no offset. The disposition strategy, in order of preference: sublease to an industrial user (the Enid industrial market is thin but not dead — two logistics users have taken comparable space in the past 18 months); a negotiated buyout with the landlord, for which our broker estimates a range of 55 to 70 cents on the dollar; or retention of the yard portion as a trailer-drop and city-driver domicile, which has modest ongoing operational value and would offset a portion of the obligation. Any recovery on the lease is upside to the program economics as presented.

## 7. Governance: Why This Program Will Not Repeat Springfield

The Committee declined the 2023 version of this plan four months after the Springfield dock expansion closed out 41 percent over budget. That decision was reasonable, and this proposal does not ask the Committee to set the Springfield experience aside. It asks the Committee to weigh the specific controls that answer each of Springfield's failure modes.

The post-completion review of Springfield identified four causes: an incomplete design released to construction, which generated 63 change orders; a cost-plus contract structure that transferred change-order risk to Prairie Gate; project reporting that ran through the same operations team executing the work, so cost deterioration surfaced late; and contingency absorbed into scope growth without escalation. The corresponding controls in this program:

1. **Design completion before contract execution.** The facility design is at 85 percent completion today, produced by a firm that has delivered nine LTL cross-docks of comparable scale, and will reach 100 percent before the construction contract is signed. Springfield broke ground at roughly 40 percent design.
2. **Guaranteed-maximum-price contract.** The construction contract is structured as a GMP with the general contractor holding design-development risk within the maximum. Three qualified contractors have bid; the GMP figures underlie the $19.3 million construction line.
3. **Independent program office.** A dedicated program director — an external hire with cross-dock construction delivery experience, recruitment underway with a target start in Phase 0 — reports on cost and schedule directly to the CFO, not to operations. The Committee receives a standing monthly cost-and-schedule report against the approved baseline.
4. **Committee-held contingency and stage gates.** The $2.6 million contingency sits outside the program budget and is drawn only with CFO certification and Committee notification. Gates 1 through 3 give the Committee three formal opportunities to pause, redirect, or stop the program on evidence, with the maximum committed exposure at each gate quantified in the risk section below.

## 8. Alternatives Considered

### 8.1 Lease an existing Oklahoma City building

Management engaged two industrial brokers to canvass the Oklahoma City market for existing cross-dock capacity. The findings: three buildings of adequate size exist; none exceeds 68 doors; two are configured as distribution warehouses whose conversion to cross-dock operation (door additions, floor loading, yard reconfiguration) would cost an estimated $9 to $12 million on a building we would not own; and none offers yard capacity above 160 trailers against our requirement of 290. The best available candidate, on a ten-year lease with conversion costs, produces a total occupancy cost within 15 percent of owning the purpose-built facility — while capping the network at 68 doors, which is below the 90-door minimum the network model requires to consolidate both closing terminals. The lease alternative would therefore force us to keep one of Ponca City or Enid open, forfeiting roughly 40 percent of the linehaul and handling savings. It solves neither the door-count problem nor the lane-structure problem, at most of the cost. Management recommends against it.

### 8.2 Repair service inside the current footprint

The second alternative is to fix service without construction: added relay capacity, surge staffing, schedule padding removal through tighter dock discipline, and the Enid six-door expansion. Management has, in fact, implemented the operating-expense elements of this alternative since November as the tactical bridge (Section 5, Phase 0), so we have live data on its ceiling. The results: southern on-time performance has recovered from 79 to approximately 84 percent, at an annualized cost of $1.6 million. Modeling indicates the full version of this alternative — including the $4 million Enid expansion — plateaus at 89 to 91 percent southern on-time, permanently short of the 95 percent standard Kesterson and the broader market require, while adding $2.5 to $3 million of permanent annual operating cost and $4 million of capital sunk into a mislocated facility. It does not address excess miles, excess handling, or the driver-schedule problems driving turnover. It is a bridge, not a destination, and management recommends it only as what it already is: the interim measure that holds service while the rebuild is executed.

### 8.3 Do nothing

Presented for completeness. The status quo projects continued service erosion, a probable Kesterson loss with $7 to $9 million of annual operating income impact, and continued excess cost of approximately $6.4 million annually in the southern tier. Doing nothing is the most expensive option on the table.

## 9. Risks and Mitigations

**Construction cost overrun.** *Exposure:* the Springfield precedent. *Mitigation:* GMP contract, 100 percent design before contract execution, independent program office, Committee-held contingency, Gate 1 review at month 10. Maximum construction exposure above the GMP is limited to owner-directed scope changes, which require Committee approval above $250,000 cumulative.

**Service failure during cutover.** *Exposure:* freight breaks during Phase 2 or 3, damaging the customer relationships the program exists to protect. *Mitigation:* four-week parallel running with the legacy terminal fully operational as fallback; lane-by-lane migration; automatic reversion trigger at 92 percent company on-time for two consecutive weeks; Ponca City and Enid closures gated on demonstrated Oklahoma City performance, not on the calendar.

**Kesterson loss before program completion.** *Exposure:* the program's density assumptions weaken if Kesterson departs at June 30 despite the plan. *Mitigation:* the tactical bridge holds current service through the watch period; the board-approved program with construction underway is presented to Kesterson as the structural answer during renewal negotiations; the program economics were stress-tested at Kesterson volumes reduced 50 percent and remain positive (IRR of 11.2 percent), because the cost-reduction elements do not depend on Kesterson freight. If Kesterson formally terminates before Gate 1, the gate review would reassess facility sizing before further commitment.

**Labor action at Enid.** *Exposure:* unfair labor practice charge or organizing acceleration tied to the closure. *Mitigation:* decision record predating organizing activity, counsel-reviewed communications, workforce terms set above policy, closure executed on documented business rationale. *Residual:* a charge is possible and is accepted as a manageable cost of a necessary decision.

**Workforce attrition before closure.** *Exposure:* Ponca City or Enid staff leave early, degrading the fallback capacity that the parallel-running plan depends on. *Mitigation:* 15 percent stay bonuses through closure dates; cross-training of Oklahoma City new hires beginning two months before each cutover; Wichita surge team on standby.

**Enid lease stranding.** *Exposure:* $1.7 million obligation through 2029. *Mitigation:* fully budgeted with no assumed recovery; sublease, buyout, and partial-retention strategies pursued in parallel; any recovery is upside.

**Financing and rate risk.** *Exposure:* leverage during construction. *Mitigation:* fixed-rate committed terms; liquidity maintained above internal minimums under a 10 percent revenue-decline stress case; covenant headroom detailed in the CFO's appendix.

**Committed exposure at each gate.** If the Committee stops the program at Gate 1 (month 10), sunk commitment is approximately $14 million (land, design, construction to date), against which the land and partially built facility retain material recovery value. At Gate 2 (month 16), commitment is approximately $33 million, but the facility is operational and generating benefit; the decision at that point concerns the closures, not the building. This gate structure means the Committee's approval on February 12 is, in substance, a commitment through Gate 1 with preserved off-ramps thereafter.

1. **Approve** the Southern Network Rebuild program as described in this proposal, with a total capital budget of $38.5 million, comprising $26.0 million for the Oklahoma City facility, $7.5 million for dock and yard equipment, and $5.0 million for severance and relocation.

The service decline, the Kesterson deadline, and the construction calendar converge on this meeting. Approval on February 12 permits land closing in March, groundbreaking in the second quarter, a funded and visible structural plan in hand for the Kesterson renewal negotiation before June 30, and facility opening within thirteen months. Management is confident the program as governed here will not repeat the Springfield experience, and respectfully requests the Committee's approval.
