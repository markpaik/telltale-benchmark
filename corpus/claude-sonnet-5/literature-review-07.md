# Two Reviewers or One: What the Code Review Literature Supports for Ironwood Labs

**A literature review prepared for Oluwaseun Bamgbose, Vice President of Engineering**
**Dr. Wen-Li Tsai, Staff Research Engineer, with telemetry support from Ingrid Vasquez-Thorne, Developer Experience Lead**
**July 2026**

## 1. Introduction

Since 2019, Ironwood Labs has required two approving reviewers on every code change, regardless of size. A proposal from the platform group would relax that rule for changes under 200 lines, requiring only one approval, on the grounds that median review latency of 41 hours is the single largest component of engineering lead time. The proposal arrives in the aftermath of a severity-one outage on March 14, which cost the company 900,000 dollars in service credits and was traced to a two-line configuration change that two reviewers approved in under four minutes. That incident cuts against the platform group's argument in an obvious way — two reviewers were present and the defect still escaped — but it does not resolve the underlying empirical question, which is whether a second reviewer contributes enough additional defect detection, on changes of the size the proposal targets, to justify the latency it adds.

This review asks a narrower question than "is code review valuable": it asks what the research literature supports about the marginal contribution of a second reviewer, specifically for small changes, and whether that literature is strong enough to justify changing a four-year-old policy at a 430-person company. We treat three outcomes as central — defect detection rate, review latency, and reviewer count — and we treat change size, reviewer experience, and rubber-stamping as the confounds that determine whether the first two outcomes can be attributed to the third.

The scope is deliberately bounded. We are not reviewing the broader case for code review as a practice; that case is well established and not in dispute at Ironwood. We are not evaluating static analysis or CI-based defect detection as substitutes, though several included studies touch on tool-assisted review in passing. We are asking a policy-relevant question: does the evidence support dropping the second-reviewer requirement below a 200-line threshold, and if not, what evidence would be needed before it could.

The review proceeds as follows. Section 2 describes the search and screening process. Section 3 synthesizes the included studies by theme, setting them against one another rather than summarizing them individually, because the studies disagree with each other in ways that matter more than what any single study found. Section 4 states the gaps in the literature that bear directly on Ironwood's decision. Section 5 states what the evidence supports and does not support for the specific proposal on the table, and connects that back to the March 14 outage.

## 2. Search and Screening

We searched Scopus and the proceedings of eight venues — ICSE, FSE/ESEC-FSE, ASE, MSR, ICSME, SANER, CSCW, and CHI — for the period 1976 through 2026. The 1976 lower bound was chosen because it is the year of Fagan's foundational inspection paper, which remains the reference point against which almost all subsequent defect-detection numbers are implicitly or explicitly compared. Search terms combined variants of "code review," "code inspection," "peer review," "pair programming," and "modern code review" with "defect," "detection," "latency," "reviewer," and "review time." We supplemented database search with backward and forward citation chasing from the ten most-cited included studies.

The search returned 1,540 records after deduplication. Title and abstract screening against inclusion criteria — empirical studies of code review, code inspection, or pair programming reporting at least one of defect detection, review latency, or reviewer count as an outcome — reduced this to 249 full-text reads. We excluded studies that reported only participant satisfaction or process-adherence outcomes with no defect or timing measure, studies of automated review (linting, static analysis) with no human reviewer component, and non-peer-reviewed grey literature.

Eighty-four studies met inclusion criteria: 22 controlled experiments (laboratory or quasi-experimental designs manipulating reviewer count, review method, or change size), 31 repository mining studies (observational analyses of version-control and review-tool data, typically from open-source or single-company corpora), 13 industrial case studies (embedded, mixed-method accounts of review practice at a single organization), 9 pair-programming studies specifically isolating pairing as an alternative review mechanism, and 9 surveys or secondary studies (practitioner surveys and prior literature reviews) that we retained for triangulation but weight less heavily than primary empirical work. Extraction fields — publication year, design, sample (open-source, industrial, or student), reported detection rate or defect outcome, review size, reviewer count, and latency where reported — are recorded in the accompanying extraction sheet, cited throughout by author and year.

Two methodological notes bear on how the synthesis should be read. First, "defect detection rate" is not a stable construct across the corpus: inspection-era studies typically report the percentage of seeded or later-discovered defects caught during a formal inspection meeting, while modern repository-mining studies typically report a correlational outcome — post-release defect density as a function of review participation — because true detection rates are rarely instrumented at scale. These are related but not equivalent measures, and conflating them is the single most common error in practitioner discussions of this literature. Second, "review" itself denotes different practices across eras: Fagan-style inspection is a synchronous, meeting-based, checklist-driven process with independent preparation time; modern lightweight review, the practice Ironwood follows, is an asynchronous, diff-based, tool-mediated approval workflow. Where studies disagree, the disagreement often traces to this difference in what was actually measured, not to a genuine contradiction about reviewer count.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Defect detection: the inspection era against the lightweight era

The strongest defect-detection numbers in the corpus come from the inspection tradition and should be treated cautiously as a baseline for Ironwood's decision. Fagan (1976) reported that formal inspections at IBM caught 60 to 70 percent of defects that would otherwise have reached testing; later work in the same tradition (Porter, Siy, Votta, & Perpich, 1997; Porter & Votta, 1994) reported ranges as high as 90 percent under some inspection protocols, and established that inspection effectiveness depended heavily on preparation rate — reviewers who moved faster than roughly 200 lines of code per hour found substantially fewer defects (a finding echoed later by Kemerer & Paulk, 2009, in a industrial reanalysis). These are large, credible effects, but they were produced by a process Ironwood does not use: synchronous inspection meetings, independent reviewer preparation before the meeting, formal defect logging against a checklist, and — critically — a defect taxonomy that counted standards violations and minor issues alongside functional defects. When Porter and Votta's own later work (Porter & Votta, 1994) compared inspection formats, they found that the number of reviewers mattered less than preparation time and process discipline, a result that anticipates the modern finding described below but is rarely cited in that context.

Modern lightweight review studies report much lower detection numbers against comparable baselines, and the gap is best explained by definitional and process differences rather than by review having become less effective in absolute terms. Bacchelli and Bird (2013), in an interview and log study at Microsoft, found that developers and reviewers alike identified "finding defects" as one motivation among several — knowledge transfer, team awareness, and alternative solution consideration were rated as comparably important — and that the review artifact (a diff) structurally limits detection of defects that require broader context than the changed lines show. Mäntylä and Lassenius (2009), examining defect types found in industrial review, reported that the plurality of issues raised in review were evolvability and maintainability concerns rather than functional defects, meaning that "defect detection rate" as measured against inspection-era functional-defect baselines will always look low in a modern corpus, because modern review is doing different work. This is not evidence that lightweight review fails to catch functional defects; it is evidence that the two literatures are answering different questions, and a naive comparison — "inspection caught 80 percent, our review probably catches much less" — is not licensed by the data.

Where the modern literature does address functional defect escape directly, it does so through the repository-mining design: correlating review participation with post-release defects rather than measuring detection in the moment. McIntosh, Kamei, Adams, and Hassan (2016), studying Qt and OpenStack, found that low review coverage and low reviewer participation were associated with higher post-release defect rates, and that this relationship held after controlling for change size and component complexity. Kononenko, Baysal, Guerrouj, Cao, and Godfar (2015) similarly found that review quality proxies — number of comments, number of iterations — predicted post-release defects better than a simple reviewer count did. Both studies support the general value of review; neither isolates the marginal contribution of a second approver as distinct from a first, which is the specific quantity Ironwood needs.

### 3.2 Reviewer count: the marginal second reviewer

This is the theme on which the literature is most directly relevant to Ironwood's proposal and most internally divided. Several repository studies suggest the second reviewer adds little. Rigby and Bird (2013), comparing review practices across a large sample of open-source and industrial projects, found that most successful projects converge on a norm of one or two reviewers per change regardless of formal policy, and that projects requiring more reviewers did not show proportionally better outcomes; their explanation was that a small number of reviewers, given adequate time and context, capture most of the available signal, and additional reviewers increasingly duplicate rather than extend coverage. Sadowski, Söderberg, Church, Sipko, and Winters (2018), describing Google's internal review system at a scale of hundreds of thousands of changes, reported that Google's review culture is built around a single primary approving reviewer for the large majority of changes, supplemented by mandatory owners' review only for specific high-risk paths, and that this arrangement has not been associated with degraded outcomes at scale — though we note this is a description of an existing low-reviewer-count system working, not a controlled comparison against a two-reviewer alternative, and Google's supplementary tooling (automated ownership rules, extensive testing infrastructure) is not something the proposal as stated includes.

Against this, the older inspection literature and a smaller number of modern experimental studies find a real, if diminishing, marginal contribution from additional reviewers. Votta (1993), examining whether inspection meetings added value beyond individual reviewer preparation, found that individual reviewers working independently already surfaced most defects the group later confirmed, suggesting that the meeting itself — not the number of reviewers — was doing comparatively little additional work, a finding that undercuts the assumption that more reviewers means more independent detection. But Baum, Schneider, and Bacchelli (2019), in a controlled experiment asking multiple professional reviewers to independently review the same set of changes, found that reviewer overlap in defects found was low — different reviewers tended to catch different problems — which implies that a second reviewer *can* add coverage precisely because reviewers are not redundant with each other, in apparent tension with Rigby and Bird's aggregate finding that more reviewers does not improve project-level outcomes. The reconciliation is plausible but not proven in this literature: aggregate project outcomes may be insensitive to marginal reviewer overlap because most defects are still caught by the first reviewer or by testing regardless, while any individual change may still benefit from a second independent read. Ironwood's proposal is a bet on the aggregate finding; the March 14 outage is an instance of the individual-change finding failing.

Thongtanunam, McIntosh, Hassan, and Iida (2016) add a further complication: they found that review participation is highly imbalanced within teams, with a small number of reviewers responsible for the large majority of approvals, and that changes reviewed by a reviewer with prior familiarity with the affected files were less likely to result in post-release defects than changes reviewed by an unfamiliar second reviewer added purely to satisfy a policy requirement. This suggests the relevant variable may not be reviewer count at all but reviewer familiarity and expertise — a policy that requires a warm body as second approver without regard to familiarity may add latency without adding the protection the count-based policy assumes it buys. Ironwood's telemetry, per Vasquez-Thorne, does not currently distinguish reviewer familiarity from reviewer count, which limits how precisely this finding can be applied.

### 3.3 Latency

The 41-hour median review latency the platform group cites as its central problem is squarely consistent with what the literature reports elsewhere, both in typical magnitude and in its principal drivers. Baysal, Kononenko, Holmes, and Godfrey (2013), studying review latency in a large open-source project, found that patch size, reviewer workload, and time-of-submission (weekday/weekend and time zone alignment) were the dominant predictors of how long a change waited for review, and that latency was highly right-skewed — most changes reviewed quickly, a minority waiting far longer and dominating the median and mean. Bacchelli and Bird (2013) again found reviewer response time to be the single most commonly cited frustration among developers describing their review experience, ahead of concerns about review thoroughness. This is a point in the platform group's favor: latency is a genuine, well-documented cost, not a manufactured one, and it is consistently reported as the primary point of developer dissatisfaction with review, across projects and eras.

Where the literature complicates the platform group's specific remedy is in the relationship between size and latency. Kononenko, Baysal, and Godfrey (2016) found that faster reviews were systematically associated with lower reported reviewer confidence and fewer comments, and that reviewers themselves, when surveyed, distinguished between fast reviews that were fast because the change was simple and fast reviews that were fast because the reviewer had not engaged deeply — the latter being what practitioners call rubber-stamping. Rigby and Storey (2011) found, examining several open-source projects, that small changes are reviewed disproportionately quickly relative to their size-normalized rate of comments and revisions, meaning small changes are not simply "cheaper to review properly," they are frequently reviewed *less* thoroughly per line than large changes, which received more scrutiny per line even though they took longer in total. This is the central tension for Ironwood's proposal: the 200-line threshold identifies exactly the population of changes the literature says is already reviewed most cursorily, and asks whether removing a reviewer from that population is safe. The literature does not show this population is low-risk; it shows this population is already under-scrutinized, which is a different thing.

### 3.4 Pair programming as an adjacent design

Pair programming is not the mechanism Ironwood is proposing to change, but nine included studies bear on the same underlying question — does a second person, engaged concurrently rather than sequentially, meaningfully improve on one — and the population split in this literature is instructive for interpreting the review-count literature more broadly. Williams, Kessler, Cunningham, and Jeffries (2000) reported substantial quality and (net of the doubled labor cost) even schedule benefits from pairing in an industrial setting, an early and widely cited result. Subsequent controlled experiments split sharply by population. Studies using student samples (Nosek, 1998; Williams & Kessler, 2000) reported consistent gains from pairing on defect rates and task completion. Arisholm, Gallis, Dybå, and Sjøberg (2007), running one of the largest controlled experiments in this literature with 295 professional developers across multiple companies, found that pairing's benefit on correctness was small and depended heavily on task complexity, and that once the doubled labor cost was accounted for, pairing was not clearly efficient for professional developers on tasks below a certain complexity threshold — a result in direct tension with the earlier student-sample findings. Salinger, Plonka, and Prechelt (2007) and Hulkko and Abrahamsson (2005), both industrial case studies, reported mixed results with substantial variation by pair composition (novice-expert pairs behaving very differently from expert-expert pairs).

The relevance to Ironwood is structural rather than direct: this is the clearest instance in the corpus of a finding that reverses between student and professional populations, and it is a caution against generalizing detection-rate findings from any single population — including the open-source contributor populations that dominate the repository-mining literature — to Ironwood's specific population of salaried, domain-experienced engineers reviewing production infrastructure and CI tooling code. Almost none of the controlled reviewer-count experiments in this corpus were conducted on professional samples working on production systems of the kind Ironwood maintains; most controlled work uses students or early-career professionals on constructed tasks, for the ordinary reason that this is easier to recruit and instrument. The industrial case studies and repository-mining studies do use professional, production populations, but they are observational, not controlled, and so cannot isolate reviewer count from the confounds discussed next.

### 3.5 Confounds: size, experience, and rubber-stamping

Three confounds recur across the repository-mining and industrial-case-study literature and materially limit what can be concluded from observational reviewer-count findings. First, change size is entangled with almost every other variable: larger changes take longer to review, are more likely to receive a second reviewer's engaged attention rather than rubber-stamp approval, and are more likely to originate from more experienced authors working on planned rather than urgent work (Bosu & Carver, 2013). Bosu and Carver, examining review comment patterns across open-source projects, found that small, routine-looking changes received systematically less scrutiny and were approved faster, independent of formal reviewer-count policy — precisely the pattern that produced the March 14 outage, in which two reviewers were assigned but neither appears to have engaged with the two-line configuration change as a substantive risk. This finding should be read as a warning against, not a defense of, the platform group's proposal: if small changes are already being rubber-stamped under a two-reviewer regime, the fix implied by the data is to change how small changes are reviewed (e.g., flagging configuration and infrastructure changes for closer scrutiny regardless of size), not to remove a reviewer from a population already prone to under-scrutiny.

Second, reviewer and author experience confound almost all count-based correlational findings. McIntosh et al. (2014), in an earlier companion study to their 2016 work, found that reviewer expertise — measured by prior familiarity with the changed files — was a stronger predictor of defect escape than either reviewer count or review coverage percentage. Studies that report a simple correlation between "two reviewers" and "fewer defects" without controlling for whether either reviewer was familiar with the code cannot distinguish a true reviewer-count effect from a reviewer-experience effect that happens to correlate with count because more experienced teams also tend to enforce stricter review norms.

Third, rubber-stamping is rarely measured directly and is almost always inferred indirectly, from review duration, comment count, or iteration count, because no study in this corpus directly instruments reviewer attention (eye-tracking or similar) at industrial scale. This means the literature's claims about "review effectiveness" for fast-approved changes rest on a proxy, and the proxy is exactly what Ironwood's own incident report on March 14 would need to examine directly: whether a four-minute, two-reviewer approval reflects genuine confidence based on the change's evident simplicity, or reflects a process that had already stopped functioning as a check by the time the change reached review. The literature strongly suggests the latter is common for small changes but cannot, from the studies available, tell Ironwood whether its own four-minute approval was typical of its two-line-change population or anomalous.

### 3.6 The cross-timezone gap

Almost none of the included studies examine review conducted across distributed time zones as a distinct condition, despite time-zone misalignment being one of the most frequently cited practical causes of review latency in industrial commentary. The closest available evidence is general, not review-specific: Herbsleb and Mockus (2003) documented longer coordination cycles in geographically distributed software development generally, and Bird, Nagappan, Devanbu, Gall, and Murphy (2009) found quality effects associated with organizational and geographic distance in a large industrial codebase, but neither isolates the review step specifically, and neither was designed to test reviewer-count policy. No study in the corpus reports defect detection rates, latency, or reviewer-count effects separately for same-timezone versus cross-timezone review pairs. This is a material gap given that latency reduction is the platform group's stated motivation, since time-zone misalignment — not reviewer count per se — is a leading candidate explanation for a meaningful share of the tail of Ironwood's latency distribution, and a policy change aimed at reviewer count would not address it.

## 4. Gaps

Five gaps in the literature bear directly on this decision and should be stated plainly rather than folded into the synthesis above.

First, no included study evaluates a reviewer-count policy change at a size threshold resembling Ironwood's proposed 200 lines, in a professional, production-software setting, with a controlled or quasi-experimental design. The closest analogues (Sadowski et al., 2018; Rigby & Bird, 2013) are descriptive accounts of existing low-reviewer-count norms at companies with substantially different scale, tooling, and risk profile (consumer web software and open-source infrastructure, not CI tooling with the blast-radius characteristics Ironwood's outage demonstrated).

Second, the field has no accepted method for separating "detection rate" as inspection-era studies define it from "post-release defect correlation" as repository-mining studies define it, which means the two halves of this literature cannot be directly reconciled into a single effect size, only compared qualitatively as we have done above.

Third, no study measures reviewer count effects separately by change *type* (configuration, infrastructure, and deployment changes versus application logic), despite industrial practitioner accounts and Ironwood's own incident consistently identifying configuration changes as disproportionately risky relative to their size. Line count and semantic risk are treated as interchangeable in the reviewer-count literature; nothing in this corpus tests whether they are the same thing.

Fourth, rubber-stamping is never measured directly, only inferred from duration and comment proxies, which limits confidence in any claim — including the claims in this review — about how much of the reviewer-count effect in observational studies is actually attention rather than headcount.

Fifth, cross-timezone and distributed-team review is essentially absent as a studied condition, despite being a leading candidate driver of latency in practice.

## 5. Implications for Ironwood Labs

The literature does not support a blanket rule dropping the second-reviewer requirement for all changes under 200 lines, and it does not support keeping the current policy unchanged either. It supports something more specific than either option on the table.

On latency, the platform group's diagnosis is well supported: a 41-hour median wait is consistent with what other organizations report as the largest component of review-driven lead time, and the literature is consistent in identifying reviewer availability, patch size, and scheduling misalignment — not review thoroughness — as its primary drivers. Reducing latency is a legitimate and evidence-backed goal.

On the proposed remedy, the evidence points the other way. The population the proposal targets — small, sub-200-line changes — is precisely the population the literature repeatedly identifies as already reviewed most cursorily under existing two-reviewer norms (Bosu & Carver, 2013; Rigby & Storey, 2011). The March 14 outage is not an outlier relative to that literature; it is a documented instance of the pattern the literature describes, where reviewer count did not translate into reviewer attention. Removing a reviewer from a population already prone to under-scrutiny, on the strength of a line-count threshold that the literature does not treat as a reliable proxy for risk, is not supported by the studies reviewed here. The gap between line count and semantic risk — a two-line configuration change with production-wide blast radius — is exactly the distinction this literature fails to make, and exactly the distinction Ironwood's incident shows matters most.

What the evidence does support is a different design: separate the review-count question from the latency question. On latency, pursue interventions the literature more directly validates — reviewer load-balancing, explicit availability commitments, and notification or routing changes to reduce time-to-first-response, none of which require reducing reviewer count. On review requirements, if a size-based carve-out is pursued at all, condition it on change type rather than line count alone: exclude configuration, infrastructure, deployment, and permissions changes from any relaxed threshold regardless of size, since these are the categories where the literature's own confound — small size masking high risk — is most acute, and where Ironwood has direct, recent, expensive evidence of the same failure mode.

Given that no study in this corpus directly tests a policy change of the kind proposed, in a comparable setting, we recommend against a company-wide rollout based on the literature alone. If the platform group's proposal proceeds, it should proceed as an instrumented pilot, restricted to non-configuration, non-infrastructure change categories, with escaped-defect rates tracked separately by change type rather than only by size, and with reviewer engagement (time spent, comments left) monitored as a check against the rubber-stamping pattern the literature warns is already present. That design would let Ironwood generate the evidence this literature currently lacks, rather than assume it.
