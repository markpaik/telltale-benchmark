## 1. Summary of the Quarter

The second quarter produced a divided result. Two of four store regions grew, two declined, and the consolidated comparable-store number landed slightly negative. The larger story is not the comp line but the operational one: a distribution center relocation that was supposed to consume two weekends consumed six weeks, and a seasonal outdoor furniture buy that was sized for a normal spring sold into a broken supply chain and an unhelpful weather pattern. Those two events, taken together, account for the majority of the gross margin erosion in the quarter and nearly all of the inventory position we now carry into the back half.

The e-commerce channel grew 18 percent and remains our fastest-growing revenue source. It also lost money at the contribution line for the first time since we began reporting channel contribution separately. That is a solvable problem, but it is not a problem that solves itself, and the fixes require decisions in the third quarter rather than the fourth.

Four items require executive decision before the end of September: the Charleston lease renewal, the Ohio Valley management staffing plan, the e-commerce shipping and returns policy, and the fall inventory receipt schedule in light of current on-hand. Each is addressed in the sections that follow, with a recommendation.

Both outcomes hurt margin, and the second hurts it twice because we lose the attachment sale as well.

## 2. Regional Store Performance

The four regions diverged more sharply this quarter than in any period since the 2023 acquisition. The spread between the best region (+5.6 percent) and the worst (−6.2 percent) is 1,180 basis points. That spread is not primarily a demand story. Three of the four regions face similar consumer conditions; what separates them is real estate quality, management continuity, and recent capital investment.

### 2.2 Bluegrass Region — 12 Stores, +2.1 Percent

The Bluegrass region is the company's anchor and behaved like it. Twelve stores comped up 2.1 percent at a 34.2 percent gross margin, the highest of any region and 240 basis points above the consolidated figure. Ten of the twelve stores comped positive. The region carries our most tenured management bench — average store manager tenure is 6.4 years — and it absorbed the distribution center disruption better than any other region because Georgetown sits inside its trade area and the stores could and did send trucks directly to the dock when replenishment failed. That is not a scalable workaround, but it kept the region's in-stock rate at 91 percent against a company average of 87.

The two stores adjacent to the closed department store anchor comped down 9.0 percent. These are the Ashland Terrace and Nicholasville Road locations, both in enclosed centers where the anchor closure removed an estimated 340,000 annual visits from the property. Traffic at those two stores is down 14 percent; conversion is actually up 60 basis points, which tells us the customers still arriving are intentional shoppers coming for us specifically rather than incidental mall traffic. The revenue loss is a traffic loss, not a merchandising or execution failure.

Excluding those two locations, the Bluegrass region comped up 4.3 percent. That is the number worth carrying forward when we model the region's underlying health.

**What the region requires.** Both anchor-affected stores have leases expiring within 30 months. Neither should be renewed at current terms in current locations. We should begin a relocation study in the third quarter for both, targeting open-air centers within a three-mile radius where we can capture the same trade area without paying enclosed-mall occupancy for traffic the mall no longer delivers. Preliminary rent comparisons suggest a relocation could reduce occupancy cost per square foot by 18 to 22 percent while improving visibility. Capital required for two relocations is estimated at $4.2 million, and I recommend we scope it formally rather than defer, because the alternative is to renew into a declining property and repeat this conversation in 2029.

### 2.3 Ohio Valley Region — 10 Stores, −3.4 Percent

The Ohio Valley region comped down 3.4 percent at a 30.9 percent gross margin, 90 basis points below consolidated. The dominant fact of the quarter in this region is that we replaced four of ten store managers — 40 percent of the leadership bench — inside thirteen weeks.

Two of those departures were planned: one retirement and one promotion into a district role. Two were performance-based terminations. All four created a period of interim leadership that averaged 31 days per store before a permanent manager was seated, and the four affected stores comped down 7.1 percent against a regional average of −3.4 percent. The six stores with continuous management comped down 1.2 percent, which is roughly the regional market condition absent the disruption.

I want to be precise about causation here, because it is easy to misread. The management turnover did not cause the region's underperformance in the sense of being its origin; two of the four managers were removed because their stores were already underperforming. But the turnover unquestionably deepened and extended the shortfall. A furniture store without a seated manager loses control of the selling floor schedule, the special-order follow-up queue, and the delivery exception process, all three of which convert directly into lost revenue and customer complaints. Our net promoter score in the four affected stores fell 11 points during the interim period and has recovered 7 of those points in the four weeks since permanent managers were seated.

The region also carries the company's oldest store fleet. Average time since last remodel in Ohio Valley is 11.3 years, against 7.8 years company-wide and 4.1 years in Northern following this year's investment. There is a visible relationship in our own data between remodel recency and comp performance, and Ohio Valley sits on the wrong end of it.

**What the region requires.** Three things, in sequence. First, stabilize management — all ten stores now have permanent managers seated, and I recommend a 90-day no-transfer policy in the region to protect that. Second, complete the district manager restructuring that was paused during the distribution center transition; the region currently runs two district managers against a span of five stores each, which was appropriate when the region was performing and is inadequate during a turnaround. Third, put two Ohio Valley stores into the FY2027 remodel plan. Given the Northern region's remodel results, a $2.2 million investment in the two highest-volume Ohio Valley locations is the most defensible incremental capital request in the company.

### 2.4 Northern Region — 9 Stores in Indiana, +5.6 Percent

The Northern region comped up 5.6 percent, the best result in the company, following $6.8 million of remodels completed at three locations in the fourth quarter of FY2025 and the first quarter of FY2026. This is the clearest read we have on remodel return, and it is worth documenting carefully because it should govern how we allocate capital for the next two years.

| Northern region detail | Remodeled (3 stores) | Non-remodeled (6 stores) | Region total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comparable sales | +11.4% | +2.4% | +5.6% |
| Gross margin | 33.8% | 31.9% | 32.6% |
| Average ticket | $1,486 | $1,203 | $1,298 |
| Conversion rate | 24.1% | 19.7% | 21.3% |
| Traffic | +3.2% | +0.9% | +1.7% |

The remodeled stores comped up 11.4 percent against 2.4 percent for the non-remodeled six. The margin difference is nearly as important as the comp difference: remodeled stores ran 190 basis points higher, driven almost entirely by mix. The new floor layouts present upholstery and bedroom collections as configured rooms rather than as category runs, and customers buying a configured room buy more pieces at higher price points. Average ticket in remodeled stores is $1,486 against $1,203 elsewhere, a 24 percent difference.

At $2.27 million average investment per store and an incremental gross profit contribution of roughly $640,000 annually per remodeled store, simple payback lands between 3.4 and 3.7 years. That is a good return in this business, and it is a better return than any other use of capital currently on the table.

Two cautions. First, three stores is a small sample, and two of the three were in markets where a competitor closed a location in the same period, which flatters the comp. Adjusting roughly for that, I would model the remodel lift at 7 to 9 percent rather than 11.4 percent for planning purposes. Second, remodel comp lift decays; industry experience and our own 2019 remodel cohort suggest the lift halves by year three. The investment still clears our hurdle at a 7 percent lift with decay, but the plan should not be built on the headline number.

**What the region requires.** Protect the result. The Northern region's inventory position is the tightest in the company, and the remodeled stores were disproportionately hurt by the distribution center outage because their higher-velocity floor sets need faster replenishment. Northern should receive priority allocation on fall receipts. I also recommend we hold the region's promotional cadence rather than increasing it; the region is winning on presentation and assortment, and discounting into strength would trade margin for volume we are already getting.

### 2.5 Appalachian Region — 7 Stores, −6.2 Percent

The Appalachian region comped down 6.2 percent at a 29.4 percent gross margin, the weakest result on both measures. Seven stores in eastern Kentucky and West Virginia serve trade areas where the underlying economics have been deteriorating for several years. Regional population in our seven trade areas declined an estimated 1.1 percent over the trailing year, and the household formation rate — the single best predictor of furniture demand — is negative in five of the seven.

This is not a management or execution problem. Store manager tenure in Appalachian is 5.8 years, second-highest in the company. Customer satisfaction scores are in line with the chain. Conversion is 20.4 percent, near the company average. The stores are being operated competently in markets that are shrinking.

The region's gross margin of 29.4 percent reflects a more promotional customer and a mix skewed toward opening price points. It also reflects a higher share of the outdoor furniture markdown, discussed in Section 4, because the Appalachian assortment plan allocated outdoor a larger share of floor space relative to its sales productivity.

**The Charleston lease.** The Charleston, West Virginia store lease expires in March 2027, and the landlord has proposed a renewal at a 31 percent increase. The store's current performance:

| Charleston store | FY2026 YTD | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue (annualized) | $4.1M | Fifth of seven in region |
| Comparable sales | (7.8%) | Below regional average |
| Four-wall contribution | $186,000 | 4.5% of revenue |
| Current occupancy cost | $412,000 | 10.0% of revenue |
| Proposed occupancy cost | $540,000 | 13.2% of revenue |
| Pro forma contribution at proposed rent | $58,000 | 1.4% of revenue |

At the proposed rent, the store generates $58,000 of four-wall contribution against a declining sales trend. One more year at −7.8 percent puts it negative. I do not recommend renewing at 31 percent.

Three alternatives are available. We can counter at flat-to-5-percent with a shorter term — three years rather than ten — which preserves optionality and reflects the fact that the landlord's alternative tenant pool in that submarket is thin. We can relocate within the Charleston market to a smaller footprint; the store currently occupies 34,000 square feet and sells $121 per square foot, and a 24,000-square-foot box would carry the productive assortment at meaningfully lower cost. Or we can close, serve the trade area through e-commerce and the Huntington store 50 miles away, and accept the revenue loss.

My recommendation is to counter at flat with a three-year term and simultaneously begin a relocation search, using the search as leverage. We should be prepared to close. The decision needs to be made by the end of October to allow orderly execution either way.

**What the region requires.** The Appalachian region should be managed for cash rather than growth. That means holding capital investment to maintenance levels, resisting the temptation to remodel our way out of a demographic problem, tightening the assortment toward the price points and categories that actually sell in these markets, and reviewing every lease at expiration with genuine willingness to close. Two additional Appalachian leases expire in FY2028 and should be evaluated on the same basis. The region generated $12.3 million of revenue and, at 29.4 percent margin, still contributes positively at the four-wall line in six of seven stores. It is worth operating. It is not worth investing in.

## 3. E-Commerce Channel

E-commerce grew 18 percent to $44 million on a trailing-twelve-month basis, contributing $11.3 million in the quarter. It is now 10.7 percent of total revenue, up from 9.3 percent a year ago, and it is the only part of the business growing at a double-digit rate. It also ran a negative 2.1 percent contribution margin in the quarter, meaning the channel consumed cash rather than generating it.

### 3.1 Channel Economics

| Line item | % of channel revenue | Q2 dollars |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 100.0% | $11.3M |
| Product cost | (72.9%) | ($8.2M) |
| Gross margin | 27.1% | $3.1M |
| Outbound shipping and surcharge | (14.8%) | ($1.7M) |
| Returns cost (net of recovery) | (9.1%) | ($1.0M) |
| Digital marketing | (3.9%) | ($0.4M) |
| Fulfillment labor and packaging | (1.4%) | ($0.2M) |
| **Contribution margin** | **(2.1%)** | **($0.2M)** |

Two lines account for the loss: shipping and returns.

**The carrier surcharge.** Our primary parcel and freight carrier implemented a dimensional-weight surcharge in April that applies to oversized parcel shipments — precisely the profile of a substantial share of our e-commerce assortment. The surcharge added an estimated $680,000 of cost in the quarter, equal to 6.0 points of channel contribution margin. Absent the surcharge, the channel would have run positive 3.9 percent.

We did not reprice in response. Our free-shipping threshold has been $299 since 2022 and was set against a cost structure that no longer exists. The threshold now sits below the point at which many orders become unprofitable to ship. A $340 accent chair shipping free to a rural West Virginia address costs us $94 in freight against $92 of gross margin.

**The return rate.** Returns ran 21 percent of gross e-commerce revenue in the quarter, against 8 percent in stores. This is not unusual for online furniture, but the cost structure makes it punishing: a returned upholstery item incurs return freight, inspection, and in most cases a markdown to sell as open-box, recovering roughly 62 percent of original value. Net cost per return averaged $214.

Return reasons, from customer service coding:

| Return reason | Share of returns |
|---|---|
| Color or finish not as expected | 34% |
| Size or scale not as expected | 27% |
| Damaged in transit | 18% |
| Changed mind / no reason given | 13% |
| Wrong item shipped | 5% |
| Quality below expectation | 3% |

Sixty-one percent of returns come from expectation mismatch on color and size. That is a product-content problem, not a product-quality problem, and it is addressable without touching the assortment. Our current product pages average 4.1 images per item with no dimensional overlay, no room-scale rendering, and inconsistent color calibration across photography vendors. Competitors in this category run 9 to 14 images with scale references and swatch-accurate color rendering, and they report return rates 5 to 7 points below ours.

The 18 percent damaged-in-transit figure is a separate problem and points at packaging specification rather than carrier handling; three SKU families account for 41 percent of transit damage.

### 3.2 What the Channel Requires

Four actions, all executable in the third quarter:

1. **Raise the free-shipping threshold to $599 and introduce a flat $79 delivery charge below it.** Modeling on Q2 order distribution suggests this recovers $1.9 million of annualized shipping cost against an estimated $700,000 revenue loss from order abandonment, a net contribution improvement of roughly $1.2 million and 2.7 points of channel margin.
2. **Rebuild product content for the top 400 SKUs by e-commerce volume.** These represent 71 percent of channel revenue. Photography and dimensional overlay at approximately $340 per SKU is a $136,000 investment. A 4-point reduction in return rate on those SKUs is worth $1.4 million annually.
3. **Re-specify packaging on the three damage-prone SKU families,** with vendor cost sharing negotiated by merchandising. Target a 40 percent reduction in transit damage on those families.
4. **Institute channel contribution reporting monthly rather than quarterly,** with the shipping and returns lines visible to merchandising and marketing. We did not see this deterioration until it was a quarter old.

Executed together, these move the channel from −2.1 percent to a projected +4.5 to +5.5 percent contribution by the fourth quarter. The channel's growth is genuine and worth having. It simply has to be sold at a price that covers the cost of delivering it.

## 4. Distribution Center Transition

The relocation of the distribution center to Georgetown, Kentucky was approved in FY2025 to consolidate two aging facilities, add 180,000 square feet of capacity, and reduce average outbound stem mileage by 14 percent. The strategic rationale remains sound. The execution failed.

### 4.1 What Happened

The transition plan called for a phased cutover across two weekends in early May, with the legacy facility maintaining shipping capability as backup for fourteen days. In practice:

- The warehouse management system cutover encountered a data-mapping failure between legacy location codes and the new slotting scheme. Approximately 22 percent of inventory records showed incorrect bin locations for the first eleven days.
- The legacy facility's backup capability was released on schedule at day fourteen, before the new facility reached stable throughput, removing our fallback.
- New-facility labor was staffed at 78 percent of plan through May as the Georgetown hiring pipeline underdelivered. Pick rates ran 54 percent of standard for the first three weeks and did not reach 90 percent of standard until late June.
- Inbound receiving backed up. At peak, 41 trailers sat undocked, and vendors began diverting shipments or assessing detention charges.

The practical result was a six-week replenishment failure spanning most of May and June. In-stock averaged 87 percent against our 96 percent standard, with a trough of 79 percent in the week of May 24.

### 4.2 Quantified Impact

| Impact category | Estimated cost |
|---|---|
| Lost sales from out-of-stock (est. gross margin forgone) | $2.4M |
| Expedited freight and emergency inbound | $1.1M |
| Temporary labor and overtime | $0.8M |
| Vendor detention and diversion charges | $0.3M |
| Extended dual-facility occupancy | $0.4M |
| **Total estimated transition cost above plan** | **$5.0M** |

The lost-sales estimate is the least precise figure in this report and deserves a caveat. It is built from category-level sell-through in weeks when in-stock was depressed, compared against the same categories in the prior year and adjusted for regional comp trend. It could reasonably be $1.8 million or $3.1 million. The direct costs — freight, labor, detention, occupancy — total $2.6 million and are firm.

The impact was not evenly distributed. The Northern region, with the highest inventory turns and the tightest floor sets, lost the most relative sales. The Bluegrass region, geographically closest to Georgetown, mitigated through direct store pickup. Appalachian, farthest out and last on the outbound routing sequence, ran the lowest in-stock at 83 percent.

### 4.3 Current Status and Forward View

As of the last week of July, the facility is running at 96 percent of standard pick rate, staffed at 94 percent of plan, with in-stock recovered to 93 percent and improving roughly a point per week. I expect a return to the 96 percent standard by the third week of September, ahead of the fall selling season but after the Labor Day promotional event.

The Labor Day timing matters. That event represents 6 to 7 percent of annual revenue, and we will enter it at approximately 94 percent in-stock. Merchandising should build the event around depth in confirmed-available SKUs rather than breadth across the assortment, and marketing creative should be finalized against the actual availability file rather than the planned assortment. That coordination needs to happen in the next two weeks.

### 4.4 What This Cost Us in Learning

Three failures are worth recording, because we will do another systems cutover eventually.

The backup facility was released on a calendar date rather than on a performance trigger. The release criterion should have been "new facility sustains 90 percent of standard throughput for five consecutive days," not "day fourteen."

The data-mapping validation was performed on a sample rather than a full reconciliation. A full reconciliation of 340,000 location records was estimated at 200 hours of work. It would have cost roughly $12,000 and prevented several million.

Labor readiness was tracked as a hiring number rather than a productivity number. We hired to 78 percent of headcount and reported that as a staffing gap. The real gap was that new pickers reach standard productivity in six to eight weeks, so a facility staffed at 78 percent of headcount with entirely new labor was operating at closer to 45 percent of effective capacity. That distinction was not in any status report before cutover.

I recommend a written post-implementation review, owned by operations with FP&A support, delivered to the executive team by September 30.

## 5. Outdoor Furniture Markdown

Merchandising committed $9.4 million to outdoor furniture for the spring and summer season, an increase of 26 percent over the prior year's $7.5 million buy. The category sold through at 54 percent against a 78 percent plan, requiring $2.8 million of markdown to clear.

### 5.1 The Buy Decision

The increase was defensible on the information available in the fall of 2025. Outdoor had comped up 14 percent in FY2025 on a constrained inventory position, with sell-through of 91 percent and multiple weeks of stockout in the peak weeks. Store managers reported lost sales. The category carried a 38.4 percent initial margin, above the chain average. Expanding it looked like capturing demand we had demonstrably failed to serve.

Three things went wrong.

**The comparison base was distorted.** FY2025 outdoor sell-through of 91 percent reflected an underbuy, not underlying demand strength. When a category stocks out in peak weeks, sell-through approaches 100 percent regardless of demand. We read a supply-constrained number as a demand signal. The correct read would have used sales per store per week during in-stock periods, which showed growth closer to 6 percent.

**The distribution center outage hit outdoor at exactly the wrong moment.** Outdoor furniture sells in a compressed window — roughly 62 percent of the season's volume moves between the last week of April and the third week of June. That window is precisely when replenishment was broken. Floor sets went incomplete, umbrella and cushion attachments were unavailable, and stores could not recover from regional imbalances because inter-store transfers were suspended during the WMS instability. Outdoor in-stock bottomed at 74 percent in the week of May 24.

**Weather did not cooperate.** Rainfall across our four-state footprint ran 31 percent above the ten-year average in May, with fourteen weekend rain days against a typical seven. Outdoor furniture is the most weather-elastic category we carry.

### 5.2 Markdown Detail

| Outdoor category | Buy | Sell-through | Markdown taken | Remaining at cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seating sets | $4.1M | 51% | $1.4M | $1.2M |
| Dining sets | $2.6M | 58% | $0.7M | $0.6M |
| Umbrellas and shade | $1.2M | 44% | $0.4M | $0.4M |
| Cushions and accessories | $0.9M | 71% | $0.1M | $0.1M |
| Fire and heat | $0.6M | 62% | $0.2M | $0.1M |
| **Total** | **$9.4M** | **54%** | **$2.8M** | **$2.4M** |

We carry $2.4 million of outdoor inventory at cost into the back half. Roughly $1.6 million is in-line current-season product that will carry over and sell next spring at full margin, assuming we hold it. The remaining $800,000 is broken assortment — orphaned pieces, discontinued finishes, single umbrellas without matching bases — that will not improve with age.

**Recommendation.** Hold the $1.6 million of carryover and reduce next spring's buy by that amount, which recovers the margin rather than destroying it. Clear the $800,000 of broken assortment in a September event at whatever it brings, likely 35 to 40 percent of cost. Storage cost on carryover is approximately $90,000 for eight months, well below the margin recovered.

### 5.3 Process Change

The outdoor buy is the second seasonal category in three years to overrun its plan. I recommend three changes to seasonal buying:

1. **Seasonal sell-through targets must be evaluated against in-stock-adjusted demand,** not raw sell-through. FP&A will supply the adjusted series for all seasonal categories by October 1.
2. **Seasonal buys above $5 million require a written downside case** covering weather, supply disruption, and demand-shortfall scenarios, with a pre-agreed markdown reserve.
3. **Seasonal buys should be split into an initial commitment and a reorder tranche** where vendor terms allow. Vendors will resist and will price the flexibility. For a $9.4 million category, paying 2 to 3 percent for the option to size the back half of the buy against actual early-season performance is worth it. This is a merchandising negotiation priority for the FY2027 outdoor buy, which begins in October.

## 6. Margin and Inventory Position

### 6.1 Gross Margin Bridge

Consolidated gross margin declined 230 basis points to 31.8 percent. The bridge from prior year:

| Driver | Basis point impact |
|---|---|
| Q2 FY2025 gross margin | 34.1% |
| Outdoor furniture markdown | (135) bps |
| Distribution center expedite and freight | (58) bps |
| E-commerce channel mix shift | (44) bps |
| Carrier surcharge (store delivery portion) | (19) bps |
| Regional mix (Appalachian, Ohio Valley weight) | (16) bps |
| Northern remodel mix benefit | +32 bps |
| Vendor cost negotiations | +10 bps |
| **Q2 FY2026 gross margin** | **31.8%** |

Two-thirds of the decline traces to the outdoor markdown and the distribution center. Both are non-recurring in the sense that neither repeats automatically. Neither is non-recurring in the sense that we should exclude them from how we judge the quarter — they happened, they cost money, and the decisions that produced them were ours.

The e-commerce mix shift of 44 basis points is structural and will grow. E-commerce carries a 27.1 percent gross margin against 32.9 percent in stores. As the channel grows from 10.7 percent toward the 15 percent we project by FY2028, consolidated gross margin faces roughly 30 basis points of annual dilution from mix alone. That is manageable but must be planned for, and it strengthens the case for the pricing actions in Section 3.

### 6.2 Inventory Position

Inventory at cost closed the quarter at $71.2 million, up 14.1 percent against revenue growth of 2.3 percent. Turns fell from 3.4 to 2.9.

| Inventory composition | At cost | vs. prior year | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core in-line goods | $52.8M | +4.2% | Appropriate |
| Outdoor carryover | $2.4M | +$1.9M | $1.6M productive, $0.8M clear |
| DC transition buffer | $6.1M | +$6.1M | Excess; unwind by Q4 |
| Aged (>270 days) | $5.7M | +$1.4M | Reserve review required |
| In-transit | $4.2M | +$0.7M | Normal |
| **Total** | **$71.2M** | **+14.1%** | |

The distribution center transition buffer requires explanation. When the facility destabilized in May, we placed emergency inbound orders across core replenishment SKUs to rebuild in-stock. Much of that inventory arrived after the crisis passed. It is good inventory — current in-line goods that will sell — but it is roughly six weeks of excess supply on affected SKUs. It should unwind through the fall selling season by reducing fourth-quarter receipts rather than by discounting. That requires merchandising and planning to cut Q4 open-to-buy by approximately $6 million, which is the single most important working-capital action available to us this year.

The aged inventory of $5.7 million, up $1.4 million, is the line I am least comfortable with. Our current reserve is $1.9 million against $5.7 million of aged goods, a 33 percent coverage rate that was set when aged inventory was largely current-season overstock. It is now weighted toward discontinued SKUs with thinner recovery prospects. I recommend increasing the reserve to 45 percent, a $700,000 charge in the third quarter. Better to take it now than to discover it in the year-end audit.

### 6.3 Working Capital and Cash

The inventory build consumed $8.8 million of cash against prior year. Combined with the $2.6 million of direct distribution center costs, cash conversion in the quarter was materially below plan. We remain in compliance with all credit facility covenants, with the fixed-charge coverage ratio at 1.42x against a 1.25x minimum and the leverage ratio at 3.61x against a 4.25x maximum. Headroom exists but has narrowed from the prior quarter's 1.58x and 3.34x.

The fourth-quarter receipt reduction described above, combined with normal seasonal sell-down, should return inventory to $61 to $63 million by fiscal year end and turns to 3.2. That is the plan. It requires discipline in October and November, when the instinct will be to buy into holiday.

## 7. What the Back Half Requires

The first half is done and cost us roughly $7.8 million of gross margin against plan between the outdoor markdown and the distribution center. The back half has to make some of that back, and the levers are known.

### 7.1 Revised Full-Year Outlook

| Measure | Original FY2026 plan | Revised outlook | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|

### 7.2 Priorities for Q3 and Q4

**Restore in-stock to 96 percent by September 20.** Everything else depends on this. Nothing in the assortment, the marketing calendar, or the store experience works at 87 percent in-stock. Operations owns it; weekly reporting to the executive team until the standard is met.

**Reduce Q4 open-to-buy by $6 million.** Merchandising and planning own it. This is the difference between ending the year at $62 million of inventory and ending at $68 million, and the latter would put covenant headroom under real pressure.

### 7.3 Note to the Board Observer

The quarter was worse than plan, and the two largest causes — the distribution center transition and the outdoor buy — were both self-inflicted. Neither reflects a deterioration in the underlying business. The Bluegrass region's 4.3 percent underlying comp and the Northern region's remodel results are the two most informative numbers in this report, and both are good. The Appalachian region is in secular decline and is being managed accordingly. Ohio Valley is a fixable operating problem with a known remedy.

What the quarter did reveal is that our operational risk controls were thinner than our financial controls. We modeled the distribution center relocation's benefits carefully and its failure modes hardly at all. We sized the outdoor buy on a metric that was structurally misleading. Both are process gaps rather than judgment failures by any individual, and both have specific remedies proposed in this report.

The back half is achievable. It requires the receipt discipline and the in-stock recovery above all else. I will report progress against each of the eight priorities in the monthly package beginning with August results.
