# Executive Summary I — Skilled Trades Advancement Initiative

**To:** Yvonne Delacroix-Baptiste, President and Chief Executive; Lamar Etheridge, Board Treasurer; Members of the Donor Advisory Council
**From:** Hana Kobayashi, Director of Evaluation
**Date:** October 6, 2025
**Subject:** Renewal review — Northside Trade Collective, Skilled Trades Advancement Initiative, $1,800,000 (January 2023 – December 2025)

## Finding and recommendation

The Skilled Trades Advancement Initiative is the strongest performer in the Opportunity Portfolio and the only one of the four grants whose results can be stated in the terms the original proposal promised. Northside Trade Collective enrolled 480 adults against a target of 450, placed 341 of them into employment, moved median starting wages from $13.10 to $21.40 an hour, and retained 64 percent of those placed at the twelve-month mark. I recommend that the council renew the grant for three years at $2,250,000, an increase of $450,000 over the closing term, with the entire increment restricted to retention and follow-up infrastructure rather than to additional enrollment. I further recommend that renewal be conditioned on an independent wage verification through state unemployment insurance records, described below.

This is a recommendation to fund a program that works, not a program that has finished learning. The retention figure is the honest weak point, and the increase I propose is aimed squarely at it.

## What the grant bought

The $1.8 million over three years purchased 4.5 full-time positions — two shop instructors, a career navigator, an employer partnerships manager, and a half-time data coordinator — at a fully loaded cost of $1,010,000. It purchased credential and examination fees for all 480 enrollees across commercial driver's licensing, HVAC EPA 608 certification, and NCCER electrical and welding levels, at $186,000. It purchased tool sets and personal protective equipment issued to participants and retained by them at completion, averaging $610 per enrollee, or $293,000 in total. It purchased $214,000 in participant support: transportation vouchers, emergency childcare, and a hardship fund that made 138 disbursements averaging $412, most of them for car repair, rent shortfall, or a licensing fine that stood between a graduate and a job offer. It purchased leased bay and classroom space on Moncrief Road at $79,000 a year. Indirect cost recovery was held to 12 percent, or $193,000, below the foundation's 15 percent allowance.

The all-in cost per enrollee was $3,750. The cost per placement was $5,279. The cost per placement still employed at twelve months was $8,257.

## What the evidence shows

The wage movement is the finding that matters. A median gain of $8.30 an hour, annualized at full-time hours, is roughly $17,300 in additional gross earnings per placed worker in the first year, or approximately $5.9 million across the 341 placements against a $1.8 million investment. Even discounted for part-time schedules, seasonal layoff in the trades, and the 36 percent who were no longer employed at twelve months, the first-year earnings gain among the group we can verify exceeds the full three-year cost of the grant by a wide margin. Northside's placements are concentrated in commercial driving, mechanical and sheet metal work, and residential and light commercial electrical — sectors with genuine local demand and, in the case of the electrical trade, a registered apprenticeship ladder that continues to raise wages after our involvement ends.

The 71 percent placement rate should be read alongside who did not place. The 139 enrollees who left without a job were disproportionately those who entered with an open criminal matter, no high school credential, or no reliable vehicle. Northside did not screen these participants out at intake, which I regard as a credit rather than a deficiency; a program that admitted only the job-ready would show a higher placement rate and produce less value. But it means the placement rate understates what the program does for the participants it can serve and overstates how well it currently serves the hardest cases. Northside's own analysis identifies driver's license reinstatement and record-sealing assistance as the two interventions most likely to convert this group, and the organization has not had staff capacity to provide either.

Retention is where the program is weakest and where the evidence is thinnest. Sixty-four percent employed at twelve months is respectable for the sector and unremarkable for the population, but it is measured through participant self-report at a 78 percent response rate. The 22 percent we cannot reach are unlikely to resemble the 78 percent we can. If non-respondents are employed at half the rate of respondents, true twelve-month retention falls to roughly 57 percent. That is still a defensible result. It is not the result now in circulation, and the council should not adopt the higher number as settled.

Northside also cannot yet say why people leave. Exit interviews were conducted with 41 of the 123 known separations. Of those 41, the most common reasons were schedule conflict with childcare, transportation failure, and a supervisor conflict in the first ninety days — none of them skill deficits, all of them addressable with a retention coach and a modest continuation of the hardship fund past placement. The current grant funds participant support only through placement, which is a design error we approved.

## What I recommend the council attach to renewal

Renewal at $2,250,000 should carry three conditions. First, Northside should execute a data-sharing agreement with the state workforce agency permitting a quarterly wage-record match for all enrollees from 2023 forward, replacing self-report as the system of record by the end of the first renewal year. Second, the increment should fund a retention coach, a twelve-month post-placement support fund, and follow-up at eighteen and twenty-four months rather than twelve. Third, enrollment targets should be set at 500 over three years, not the 700 Northside proposed in its concept note; the marginal cost of the next 200 enrollees is higher than the average cost of the first 480, and the portfolio's value here lies in depth.

## What we owe if funding changes

I am recommending renewal, so the immediate obligations are ones of continuity rather than repair. They are still worth stating, because the council's vote is not the only decision that could change this program's funding, and because a grantee that has performed this well is owed clarity about the floor beneath it.

If the council renews at a reduced amount, reductions should fall on indirect recovery and administrative positions before they fall on participant-facing supports, and the foundation should say so explicitly in the award letter rather than leaving Northside to guess. The tool sets, the credential fees, and the hardship fund are the components with the shortest line to participant outcomes and the least ability to absorb a cut.

If the council declines renewal, Northside is entitled to a teach-out rather than a stop. Two cohorts totaling 61 participants will be mid-training on December 31, 2025, and a third cohort will have been recruited on the representation that training will be available in January. Every one of those participants was told, at intake, that tools, exam fees, and placement support were part of the program. That representation was made on our money and carries our name in the enrollment materials. A non-renewal vote should therefore be accompanied by a wind-down grant sufficient to carry enrolled and recruited participants through placement — I estimate $340,000 for nine months — and by ninety days' written notice to the organization so that staff separations are managed rather than improvised.

Finally, participants own their records. Any change in funding should be accompanied by our written instruction that credential documentation, hours logs, and apprenticeship sponsor letters be delivered to each participant directly, not archived. A welder with no proof of her certification hours has lost the thing we bought for her.

# Executive Summary II — Eviction Defense and Housing Stability Project

**To:** Yvonne Delacroix-Baptiste, President and Chief Executive; Lamar Etheridge, Board Treasurer; Members of the Donor Advisory Council
**From:** Hana Kobayashi, Director of Evaluation
**Date:** October 9, 2025
**Subject:** Renewal review — Northeast Florida Legal Aid Society, Eviction Defense and Housing Stability Project, $2,200,000 (January 2023 – December 2025)

## Finding and recommendation

The Eviction Defense and Housing Stability Project reached 610 households against a three-year target of 1,500 and cost $3,607 per household against a planned $1,467. It lost two of its four staff attorneys within nine months of launch and has never recovered its intended capacity. Those are the facts that will draw the council's attention. They are not, in my judgment, the facts that should determine the vote.

The project's per-case results are good. Of the 270 matters closed to date, 199 — 74 percent — ended without a judgment for possession entered against the tenant. Its problem is throughput, and its throughput problem has a specific, diagnosable, and largely fixable cause: the grant funded four attorneys at $62,000 in a market that pays $78,000, and then assumed each would carry 375 cases a year, a caseload roughly four times what eviction defense practice sustains.

I therefore recommend against renewal at $2,200,000 over three years, and recommend instead that the council approve an eighteen-month restructuring and disposition grant of $1,050,000, non-renewable, released in four restricted tranches with a decision gate in March 2027. The largest single element of that recommendation is not programmatic. It is the closeout of 340 open client matters, which the foundation is obligated to fund whether or not it ever gives this grantee another dollar.

## What the grant bought

The $2.2 million purchased four staff attorney positions, two paralegals, and an intake coordinator, at $1,412,000 fully loaded across the term — a figure inflated by five months of vacancy costs and $61,000 in recruitment expense following the two departures. It purchased a court-based help desk operating two days a week at the Duval County Courthouse, staffed on a rotation, which conducted 1,930 brief advice contacts distinct from the 610 represented households. It purchased Spanish, Haitian Creole, and Tagalog interpretation at $34,000. It purchased $178,000 in an emergency rental arrears fund that cleared arrears for 122 households at an average of $1,459 each, in most cases as the consideration that made a settlement possible. It purchased filing fees, service costs, and expert appraisals in habitability defenses at $47,000. Indirect recovery was $286,000.

## What the evidence shows

Two attorneys resigned in the first nine months. One left for the Office of the Public Defender at a $19,000 increase; one left for private practice. Neither cited the work. The posted salary was $16,000 below the local market for a licensed attorney with two years of experience, and the grant budget contained no escalator. Time to fill each vacancy was five months. During those months, the two remaining attorneys absorbed the departing caseloads and reached open-matter counts of approximately 160 each, against a professionally sustainable range of 60 to 80 for contested housing work.

The consequence is the 340-matter backlog, and the backlog is not a queue of paperwork. It includes 94 matters with trial or case management settings in the first quarter of 2026, 51 in which a writ of possession has been stayed pending a pending motion, and 22 involving habitability counterclaims that the tenant cannot pursue without counsel. These are live legal obligations owed by named lawyers to named clients.

The quality evidence runs the other way from the volume evidence. Among closed matters, 74 percent avoided a judgment for possession. Median tenancy preserved or negotiated relocation time was 5.5 months. Twenty-nine matters produced habitability abatements or repair orders. In a jurisdiction where the represented tenant rate in county court remains in the low single digits, the project's clients did substantially better than the unrepresented baseline, and that comparison — while not a controlled one — is the closest thing to a counterfactual available here.

What the project could not do was scale. The theory of change assumed volume would produce a landlord-behavior effect; at 610 households across three years in a county with roughly 12,000 annual filings, no such effect is detectable and none should have been expected.

## What the restructured grant should require

The eighteen-month grant should be released as follows, with the first tranche unconditional:

1. **$340,000, disposition tranche, released on execution and not contingent on any performance condition.** Restricted to bringing the 340 open matters to closure or to transfer to successor counsel, including trial coverage in the first quarter of 2026.
2. **$430,000, restructured practice tranche.** Two staff attorneys at $78,000, one paralegal, and an intake triage function; annual represented-household target of 220, not 500.
3. **$180,000, limited-scope tranche.** Unbundled representation and same-day drafting at the courthouse help desk, targeting 600 households a year at a fraction of full-representation cost.
4. **$100,000, evaluation and gate tranche.** An independent review, delivered to the council in January 2027, reporting outcomes by representation type and cost per outcome.

## What we owe if funding changes

This is the summary in which the obligation section is not a courtesy paragraph. It is the operative recommendation.

Three hundred forty households are currently represented by lawyers whose salaries we pay. Under Rule 4-1.16 of the Rules Regulating the Florida Bar, counsel may not withdraw where withdrawal would materially prejudice the client's interests, and a funder's decision to stop paying does not relieve the lawyer of that duty. If this council votes non-renewal on November 6 with an effective date of December 31, it will have created a situation in which either those clients are abandoned in violation of counsel's professional obligations or the Legal Aid Society absorbs the cost of representing them from reserves it does not have. Neither outcome is acceptable, and neither is necessary. The disposition tranche exists precisely so that the council's programmatic judgment and its ethical exposure can be separated. I recommend the council fund it even if it rejects every other element of this recommendation.

Beyond the tranche, three obligations follow. No client should learn of a change in this program from a court docket; each of the 340 households is entitled to written notice, in the language of the case file, stating who their lawyer is, what happens next, and what date matters. Successor counsel must be identified before, not after, any transfer — I have confirmed that the regional pro bono panel can absorb no more than 40 matters, which is why an orderly eighteen-month disposition, rather than a three-month one, is the only workable design. And the 122 households whose arrears we cleared as settlement consideration are subject to payment plans that assumed continuing counsel; the closeout should include compliance monitoring through the term of those plans.

To the organization itself, we owe an accurate account. The Legal Aid Society did not underperform a reasonable plan; it overperformed an unreasonable one that we approved. Our docket materials should say so, and the reference we give this grantee elsewhere should say so.

# Executive Summary III — School-Based Behavioral Health Access Initiative

**To:** Yvonne Delacroix-Baptiste, President and Chief Executive; Lamar Etheridge, Board Treasurer; Members of the Donor Advisory Council
**From:** Hana Kobayashi, Director of Evaluation
**Date:** October 13, 2025
**Subject:** Renewal review — Bridgeway Youth Services, School-Based Behavioral Health Access Initiative, in partnership with Duval County Public Schools, Clay County District Schools, and Nassau County School District, $1,500,000 (January 2023 – December 2025)

## Finding and recommendation

The School-Based Behavioral Health Access Initiative completed 1,140 screenings across eleven campuses in three districts, identified 412 students meeting clinical referral thresholds, and secured a first appointment for 157 of them. Thirty-eight percent conversion means that 255 young people were screened, found to warrant care, and did not receive it. The initiative cannot report outcomes for any of them, because the data-sharing agreement among Bridgeway and the three districts was not executed until March 2025 — the twenty-seventh month of a thirty-six-month grant.

I recommend that the council decline renewal as a three-year initiative and instead approve a twelve-month completion and transition grant of $450,000, non-renewable, restricted to three purposes: re-contacting and resolving the 255 unconverted referrals and any student screened in the fall 2025 term; producing, under the now-executed agreement, the outcome report the original grant promised; and transferring the screening function to district multi-tiered support staff. No further consideration of this initiative should be scheduled before that report is in hand.

I want to be direct with the council about responsibility. The central failure of this grant is a legal instrument that no party owned, in a grant agreement we wrote without an execution milestone, monitored by a foundation that did not escalate the delay for twenty months. Bridgeway is accountable for its share. So are we.

## What the grant bought

The $1.5 million purchased four master's-level clinicians deployed across eleven campuses — six in Duval, three in Clay, two in Nassau — at $712,000 across the term. It purchased a screening coordinator and a family engagement specialist at $268,000. It purchased licensing and scoring for a validated pediatric symptom screening instrument, along with tablets and secure network access on campuses where district IT restrictions initially prohibited both, at $94,000. It purchased translation of consent materials into Spanish and Haitian Creole and a telephonic interpretation line at $31,000. It purchased $86,000 in legal and compliance work, most of it incurred in the negotiation of the FERPA and HIPAA cross-walk that became the agreement signed in March. It purchased an evaluation subcontract at $118,000 that has produced process reporting and no outcome reporting, for the reason stated. Indirect recovery was $191,000.

Against 1,140 completed screenings, the cost was $1,316 per screening and $9,554 per student who reached a first appointment.

## What the evidence shows

The screening operation worked. Consent yield exceeded 70 percent on the campuses where the family engagement specialist was present at open house and registration events, against 41 percent where she was not — a replicable and useful finding. Instrument administration was faithful, and the clinicians identified a referral-threshold rate of 36 percent, consistent with published prevalence for comparable populations. Bridgeway found the students it set out to find.

What failed was the handoff. Of the 255 students who did not reach a first appointment, Bridgeway's own tracking attributes 87 to unsuccessful family contact after three attempts, 61 to guardian non-response to the second consent required for treatment as distinct from screening, 48 to scheduling conflict where no evening or weekend appointment existed, 34 to transportation, and 25 to insurance or Medicaid enrollment gaps that the initiative had no staff capacity to resolve. Median wait from referral to offered appointment was 34 days. For an adolescent identified through a school screening, 34 days is a very long time, and the design contained no bridge — no check-in, no interim contact, nothing between the screening conversation and a phone call weeks later.

The absence of the data agreement compounded this. Without it, clinicians could not confirm to a school counselor that a student had been seen, and school staff could not tell Bridgeway that a student had transferred, been chronically absent, or entered a crisis. Twenty-six months of the grant were spent operating a referral system in which the two ends could not speak to each other about individual students. The March 2025 execution has repaired this prospectively. It cannot repair the record retroactively, and any outcome claim about the 2023 and 2024 cohorts would be reconstruction rather than measurement.

## What we owe if funding changes

The obligation here attaches to individuals, and it is unusually clear.

Two hundred fifty-five young people were asked, by an adult in a school building, a series of questions about how they were sleeping, whether they had felt hopeless, and whether they had thought about hurting themselves. They answered. They were told someone would follow up. For each of them, the follow-up either failed or never occurred. A screening is not a neutral act; it elicits disclosure, and disclosure creates an expectation of response. The foundation funded the elicitation. It cannot walk away from the response.

The completion grant should therefore require, and fund, documented re-contact with every one of the 255 — a minimum of three attempts across two modalities, in the family's preferred language, with a warm handoff to a named provider and a written disposition for each student. Where the student is now in high school elsewhere or has aged out of the campus, Bridgeway should still close the record with a referral to a community provider. I would ask the council to treat completion of this re-contact, not any programmatic metric, as the primary condition of the grant.

Second, we owe the districts a school-year boundary. Four clinicians are currently assigned to caseloads on eleven campuses, and students who did reach care are now in a therapeutic relationship. Terminating that mid-semester is clinically contraindicated and would fall hardest on the students who did everything right. The twelve-month structure I recommend allows clinical caseloads to be closed or transferred at the end of the 2025–26 academic year, with transition planning beginning in January rather than in May.

Third, we owe the four clinicians honest notice. They were recruited into positions described as three-year roles with likely renewal. A December vote that leaves them reading about the initiative's future in a district memo is a failure of ordinary decency. The completion grant should carry a sixty-day notice provision and severance at four weeks.

Finally, we owe the families a correction. Some were told at the 2024 registration events that this program would be at their child's school for the foreseeable future. Whatever the council decides, that statement needs to be superseded in writing, from us, and not left to the grantee to explain.

# Executive Summary IV — First Coast Predevelopment Loan Fund

**To:** Yvonne Delacroix-Baptiste, President and Chief Executive; Lamar Etheridge, Board Treasurer; Members of the Donor Advisory Council
**From:** Hana Kobayashi, Director of Evaluation
**Date:** October 16, 2025
**Subject:** Renewal review — Gateway Community Development Fund, manager, First Coast Predevelopment Loan Fund, $4,000,000 (January 2023 – December 2025)

**Independence note.** This grant was developed and championed by Chief Program Officer Omar Zaidi, who serves as a non-voting observer on the fund's investment committee. Under the foundation's related-review protocol, Mr. Zaidi provided documents and sat for an interview but has not reviewed, commented on, or received this summary in advance of its transmission to the council. I ask that the council receive it directly and that questions be routed to me.

## Finding and recommendation

The First Coast Predevelopment Loan Fund has deployed $6.5 million against total funded capital of $6.25 million, has obtained development permits for one of three sites, and has drawn the foundation's entire $4 million commitment fourteen months ahead of the schedule in the grant agreement. The anchor donor whose $3 million pledge was to complete the fund's capitalization has placed that pledge on hold pending this review. The fund is, as of this writing, approximately $250,000 over its funded capital, carried on a bank line, with no committed source for its next drawdown.

I recommend that the council decline renewal and place the existing commitment in standstill effective on the date of its vote: no new site approvals, no further disbursement of undrawn foundation funds — of which there are none — and no consent to additional leverage. I recommend that the council direct staff to obtain from Gateway, within sixty days, a site-by-site capital and recovery plan; that the Treasurer commission an independent fund audit at foundation expense, which I estimate at $45,000; and that the foundation and Gateway jointly brief the anchor donor in writing, with the actual figures, before the donor's own board meets in January.

A standstill is not a declaration of default and should not be communicated as one. It is a decision to stop adding capital to a position we do not currently understand.

## What the grant bought

The foundation's $4 million was structured as $3,250,000 in recoverable capital at 1 percent, repayable at construction closing, and $750,000 in outright grant support for fund operations, underwriting, and pipeline development. It sat alongside $1,750,000 from a regional bank's community development corporation, $500,000 from a family foundation, and the $3 million anchor pledge, uncalled, for total commitments of $9,250,000 and funded capital of $6,250,000.

Deployments of $6.5 million bought predevelopment work on three sites totaling 214 planned affordable units. Site A, in Grand Park, 96 units: site control, environmental, civil engineering, architectural through construction documents, and permitting — permits issued July 2025, with a nine percent low-income housing tax credit application submitted and awards to be announced in December. Deployed: $2.8 million. Site B, in northwest Jacksonville, 78 units: site control, survey, environmental, and two rezoning applications, the second of which was deferred in June following a stormwater finding and a Phase II environmental result requiring additional remediation scoping. No entitlement; earliest realistic permit is late 2026. Deployed: $2.1 million. Site C, a Springfield adaptive reuse of 40 units: option payments extended twice, schematic design, structural assessment, and a revised cost estimate 31 percent above underwriting. The purchase option expires in February 2026. Deployed: $1.6 million.

## What the evidence shows

Predevelopment capital is the most at-risk money in affordable housing finance. It funds the work required to determine whether a project is feasible, and it repays only when the project closes on construction financing. If entitlements fail or the deal does not close, the money is gone. That is the instrument's nature and was disclosed at approval. What was not contemplated was concentration: a single fund received $4 million, one-third of the entire Opportunity Portfolio, in the highest-risk instrument the foundation deployed, with credit decisions made by a committee on which foundation staff sat and with no independent underwriting review.

On the base case, recovery looks like this. Site A repays at construction closing, which requires a tax credit allocation in December; the historical award rate in this cycle is roughly one in four, and Gateway has no committed alternative if the application fails. Site B has no path to closing before 2027 and a meaningful probability of abandonment. Site C's option expires in February with a cost basis 31 percent above underwriting and no identified gap financing. If Site A wins its allocation and closes on schedule, the fund recovers approximately $2.8 million in 2026 or early 2027. If it does not, recovery in the near term is close to zero. Against the foundation's $3.25 million recoverable position, I would advise the Treasurer to model realistic recovery in the range of $1.2 to $1.6 million and to discuss an impairment reserve with the audit committee before the fiscal year closes.

The overdrawn position — $6.5 million deployed against $6.25 million funded — was reported to us as a timing item. It is not a timing item if the pledge that was to fund it is on hold. Gateway's management has been forthcoming in interviews and produced every document requested. The problem is not candor. It is that the fund's governance placed a $9.25 million capitalization behind three deals when prudent practice would have placed it behind six or seven, and placed a foundation officer close enough to the credit decisions that no one in our building was positioned to say so.

## What we owe if funding changes

To Gateway, we owe an orderly standstill rather than an exit. The foundation should not accelerate its recoverable position, should not demand repayment on a schedule the fund cannot meet, and should not force a distressed disposition of the Site C option in the eleven weeks before it expires. We should pay for the audit ourselves rather than charging it to a fund with no liquidity, and Gateway's management should be given the opportunity to present its own capital plan to the council in January before any further decision is taken. Gateway did not misrepresent this portfolio to us; it built a portfolio our own approval process encouraged it to build.

To the residents who do not yet live in these buildings, we owe honesty about what 214 units means. It means 96 units with permits and a pending application, and 118 units that exist as drawings. Our obligation is to protect the one live transaction: the standstill should carry an explicit carve-out permitting Gateway to complete any work required to preserve Site A's tax credit application and closing timeline, and the council should be prepared to consider a targeted bridge in the first quarter of 2026 if an allocation is awarded and a closing gap appears. If Site B is abandoned, we owe the northwest Jacksonville residents who attended two design charrettes in 2023 a direct communication from the fund and the foundation, not silence and a for-sale sign. I recommend $25,000 within the standstill for that notification and for a community process on the property's disposition.

To the co-investors, we owe pari passu treatment. The bank CDC and the family foundation invested alongside us on the representation that no investor held preferential recovery rights. Nothing in a standstill should alter that, and no side arrangement should be entertained.

To the anchor donor, we owe accuracy and speed. A $3 million pledge is currently suspended on the strength of an incomplete account. Whether the donor resumes or withdraws, that decision should rest on the numbers in this summary, delivered by us and by Gateway together, in writing, before January.

And to ourselves, we owe a policy change. I recommend that the council direct staff to bring forward, by the first quarter of 2026, a concentration limit for high-risk instruments within any single portfolio, a prohibition on foundation staff participating in credit decisions for entities they have championed, and a requirement of independent underwriting review for any recoverable commitment above $1 million. The Opportunity Portfolio's most instructive finding is not about a fund in Jacksonville. It is about how a $4 million position came to be approved without a second set of eyes.
