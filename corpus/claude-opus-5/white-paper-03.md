## A Program Model, Financing Schedule, and Legal Framework for Small and Mid-Sized Publicly Owned Water Systems in the Upper Midwest

Lansing, Michigan

**Authors:**

## Abstract

The Lead and Copper Rule Improvements require nearly every water system in the United States to replace its lead and galvanized-requiring-replacement service lines at an average rate of ten percent per year over a decade beginning in 2027. For the 412 publicly owned systems represented by this League, that mandate translates into 214,000 confirmed replacements, an additional 118,000 service lines of unknown material that must be resolved or presumed lead, and a capital requirement on the order of $1.95 billion before contingency. State revolving fund allocations, at current levels, cover roughly one-third of that need. The remainder must come from rates, local debt, and whatever supplemental federal appropriation survives the next several budget cycles.

This paper does three things. First, it sets out what member inventories actually show, including the cost distribution that drives most of the variance in program budgets. Second, it compares the five delivery approaches members have adopted, with an assessment of unit cost, schedule reliability, and legal exposure for each. Third, it builds a complete program model for a representative 22,000-connection system — crew capacity, a $2.1 million annual bonding schedule, a right-of-entry ordinance structure, and a customer affordability package — and then answers the three objections that dominate every board discussion the League has observed: private property access, plumber supply, and rate shock in low-income neighborhoods.

The central finding is that no system in our membership can meet the 2037 deadline through opportunistic replacement alone, and no system can control unit cost through neighborhood-by-neighborhood full replacement alone. The programs that will finish on time and on budget are hybrids, sequenced deliberately, with the legal instruments for private-property access adopted before the first crew mobilizes rather than after the first refusal. The single most consequential decision a utility board will make in 2026 is whether to treat customer-side replacement as a utility obligation funded by rates or as a homeowner obligation subsidized by reimbursement. The evidence from member programs is unambiguous on this point, and it favors the utility obligation.

## 1. The Problem Stated Plainly

### 1.1 What the Rule Requires

The Lead and Copper Rule Improvements, finalized in October 2024, require water systems to replace all lead service lines and all galvanized service lines requiring replacement within ten years of the compliance date. For systems in the League's four-state territory, the replacement period runs from calendar year 2027 through calendar year 2037. Systems must replace an annual average of ten percent of their baseline inventory. The rule does not permit partial replacement — removing the utility-side portion while leaving the customer-side lead in place — except in narrow circumstances involving emergency repair, and even then the remaining portion must be replaced within forty-five days.

Two features of the rule create most of the operational difficulty. The first is that the ten percent annual obligation is measured against the inventory, not against what the utility can access. A homeowner who refuses entry does not reduce the denominator. The rule provides a deferral pathway for documented refusals, but that pathway requires a record of repeated, documented outreach attempts using multiple contact methods, and it does not relieve the system of the obligation to continue attempting access in subsequent years. Refusals accumulate. They do not disappear.

The second feature is that unknown-material service lines are treated as lead until proven otherwise. A system with 118,000 unknowns across the membership does not have 118,000 optional investigations; it has 118,000 lines that will count against the replacement obligation unless the system produces evidence of non-lead material. Records-based verification is therefore not an administrative housekeeping exercise. It is the single highest-return activity available to a water system in the eighteen months before the compliance clock starts.

### 1.2 Why This Is Harder for Publicly Owned Systems in This Region

The League's membership skews small. The median member system serves approximately 9,400 connections. Roughly 60 percent of member systems serve fewer than 15,000 connections. These systems typically operate with engineering staffs of one to four people, have no dedicated construction management capacity, and rely on a small number of local contractors who also serve the private development market.

The housing stock compounds the problem. Lead service lines in the Upper Midwest concentrate in neighborhoods built between roughly 1900 and 1955, which in most member cities are also the neighborhoods with the oldest water mains, the narrowest rights-of-way, the highest proportion of rental housing, and the lowest median household incomes. The engineering difficulty, the access difficulty, and the affordability difficulty all land in the same census tracts. That is not a coincidence, and program design has to account for it.

Finally, one member city is currently defending a putative class action arising from partial service line replacements performed in 2018 under a main replacement contract. The plaintiffs allege that the partial replacements disturbed lead scale and produced elevated lead exposure in the months following the work. The League takes no position on the merits. The relevance here is that the litigation has made member boards acutely aware that doing this work badly carries liability that doing it slowly does not. That awareness is healthy, but it has in several cases produced paralysis. This paper is written in part to break that paralysis.

## 2. What Member Inventories Show

### 2.1 Inventory Composition

Member systems filed initial service line inventories covering 1.06 million service connections. Table 1 summarizes the composition.

**Table 1. Aggregate Service Line Inventory, 412 Member Systems**

| Material Classification | Lines | Share of Total |
|---|---:|---:|
| Non-lead (confirmed) | 728,000 | 68.7% |
| Lead (confirmed) | 141,000 | 13.3% |
| Galvanized requiring replacement | 73,000 | 6.9% |
| Unknown material | 118,000 | 11.1% |
| **Total** | **1,060,000** | **100.0%** |
| **Replacement obligation (lead + GRR)** | **214,000** | **20.2%** |

*Note: Galvanized requiring replacement includes galvanized lines currently or previously downstream of a lead service line or lead connector. Unknown-material lines are presumed lead for planning purposes pending verification.*

The 118,000 unknowns deserve particular attention. Member systems report that unknowns cluster in two situations: pre-1945 properties where no tap card survives, and properties where the tap card survives but records only the utility-side material with no notation of the customer-side. The second category is the larger of the two and the more tractable. Member systems that have run structured records-verification programs report resolving 45 to 70 percent of unknowns to a non-lead classification without excavation, using a combination of tap cards, plumbing permits, building permits, assessor construction-date records, and prior meter-set or meter-replacement work orders that documented interior piping material.

If the membership resolves unknowns at the midpoint of that range — call it 57 percent to non-lead — the aggregate replacement obligation rises from 214,000 to approximately 265,000 lines rather than to 332,000. That difference, roughly 67,000 lines at $9,100 each, is $610 million. Records verification is the cheapest $610 million any system in this membership will ever save.

### 2.2 Cost Distribution

Replacement cost across member programs averages $9,100 per line with a reported range of $4,200 to $21,000. The range is wide enough that an average is close to useless for budgeting. Table 2 decomposes it.

*Note: Low column approximates a bore-through installation on a short setback with lawn restoration, performed within a bundled neighborhood contract. High column approximates an open-cut replacement under a recently resurfaced street with a long setback, finished basement interior work, and a standalone work order.*

Three observations follow from Table 2.

First, restoration is the largest single swing factor. The difference between replacing a line under a gravel alley and replacing the same line under a street resurfaced two years ago is $4,800 in restoration alone, before any consideration of the pavement moratorium penalties many member cities impose on their own utilities. This is the entire argument for coordinating replacement with the paving program, and it is a strong argument.

Second, customer-side work — excavation from curb stop to meter, plus the interior connection — accounts for $2,750 of the $9,100 typical cost, or 30 percent. Programs that shift this cost to homeowners are shifting roughly 30 percent of program cost, and as Section 4 shows, they are also purchasing a refusal rate that makes the schedule unattainable.

Third, program administration at $650 per line typical is not overhead to be trimmed. It is the documentation that establishes compliance, the inspection that prevents the partial-replacement liability the League's member city is now litigating, and the customer contact that converts non-response into consent. Systems that have cut administration below roughly $500 per line report materially higher refusal rates and materially worse recordkeeping.

### 2.3 Aggregate Capital Requirement and the Funding Gap

Applying $9,100 to the confirmed 214,000-line obligation yields $1.95 billion. Applying it to the projected post-verification obligation of approximately 265,000 lines yields $2.41 billion. Neither figure includes inflation over an eleven-year program. Member programs are currently budgeting construction cost escalation at 4.0 to 4.5 percent annually for this work, which is above general construction inflation because the demand shock is specific to this trade.

State revolving fund allocations across the four states, including the lead service line replacement set-asides and the portion of the base capitalization grants realistically available to member systems, cover approximately one-third of estimated member need. Table 3 sketches the resulting gap.

Program design has to start from that assumption, because a program designed around grant availability will stall the first year the grant does not arrive.

Modeled rate impact on a median member residential bill of $52 per month ranges from $6.40 to $18.90, a 12.3 to 36.3 percent increase. The variance is driven by four factors, in descending order of importance: the proportion of the system's inventory requiring replacement; the share of program cost captured by grants and principal forgiveness; the customer base over which the cost is spread; and the financing term.

## 3. The Five Approaches Compared

Member systems have converged on five delivery approaches. Most successful programs use two or three in combination. Table 5 summarizes.

**Table 5. Comparison of Five Replacement Approaches**

| Approach | Typical Unit Cost | Annual Throughput (22k-connection system) | Schedule Reliability | Key Constraint |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| 1. Neighborhood full replacement | $7,200–8,600 | 400–700 | High | Requires access to every parcel in the block |
| 2. Opportunistic with main/paving work | $5,400–7,100 | 90–260 | Low | Governed by the CIP, not the LSLR schedule |
| 3. Homeowner reimbursement | $8,800–11,500 (utility share lower) | 40–150 | Very low | Participation collapses below ~80% reimbursement |
| 4. Regional shared procurement | $6,900–8,200 | 350–650 | Medium-high | Requires intergovernmental agreement and aligned schedules |
| 5. Records-based verification | $70–240 per line resolved | 1,500–4,000 unknowns resolved | High | Quality of surviving records |

### 3.1 Neighborhood-by-Neighborhood Full Replacement

The utility selects a contiguous area, notifies every property, and replaces all lead and galvanized lines in that area within a defined construction window, utility side and customer side, in a single operation.

This is the approach with the best unit economics and the best schedule reliability, for a straightforward reason: it amortizes mobilization, traffic control, and administrative cost across a dense cluster of work, and it lets a crew develop a rhythm. Member systems running this model report crews reaching a steady state of 4 to 6 lines per day after roughly three weeks on a given block pattern, compared with 1.5 to 2.5 lines per day for scattered work orders.

It also produces the best public communication environment. Door-hanger and door-knock outreach is dramatically more effective when the neighbor's line was replaced yesterday and the street is full of visible activity. Member programs report refusal rates 8 to 14 percentage points lower in neighborhood campaigns than in scattered replacement, holding demographics constant.

The constraints are real. The approach requires the utility to have resolved unknowns in the target area before mobilization, or to be prepared to pothole and verify as part of the work. It requires access to essentially every parcel, which means the right-of-entry and refusal-handling process must be mature before the first neighborhood. And it is inflexible: once a neighborhood is scheduled, deferring it to chase a paving opportunity elsewhere costs more than it saves.

### 3.2 Opportunistic Replacement During Main and Paving Work

The utility replaces service lines when the street is already open for main replacement, or immediately before a scheduled resurfacing.

The unit cost advantage is genuine and large — $5,400 to $7,100, driven almost entirely by avoided restoration and shared mobilization. Every member system should be doing this wherever the capital improvement program and the lead inventory intersect.

The problem is that they do not intersect nearly enough. In the League's review of nine member CIPs, the overlap between scheduled main replacement and lead service line concentration over a ten-year horizon ranged from 11 to 34 percent of the lead inventory. For a 22,000-connection system with 4,400 lines to replace, opportunistic work might deliver 500 to 1,500 lines across the entire decade — 90 to 260 per year against an obligation of 440.

Opportunistic replacement is a cost-reduction strategy layered onto a compliance program. It is not a compliance program. Member systems that built their compliance schedule around the CIP are, without exception, behind.

There is a second-order effect worth naming: once the lead program is running at scale, it becomes rational to re-sequence the CIP around the lead inventory rather than the other way around. Several member systems have moved main replacement projects forward or back by two to four years to align with lead neighborhoods. Where main condition permits it, this is the single best cost-avoidance move available.

### 3.3 Homeowner Reimbursement Programs

The utility replaces the public-side portion and offers homeowners partial or full reimbursement for hiring a licensed plumber to replace the private side.

Member experience with this model is poor, and the League recommends against it as a primary approach. Three findings.

First, participation is highly sensitive to the reimbursement percentage and to the requirement that the homeowner front the cost. Programs reimbursing 50 to 75 percent with the homeowner paying upfront report participation of 18 to 34 percent. Programs reimbursing 100 percent with direct contractor payment report participation of 61 to 78 percent — better, but still not compliance-grade, and at that point the utility is paying full cost while retaining none of the procurement leverage.

Second, reimbursement programs select against exactly the households the rule exists to protect. Participation correlates strongly with owner-occupancy, household income, and English-language proficiency. In three member programs with adequate demographic data, participation in census tracts above area median income exceeded participation in tracts below 60 percent of AMI by 29 to 41 percentage points. A reimbursement program will systematically leave lead in the ground in low-income and renter-occupied housing.

Third, reimbursement fragments quality control. The utility does not select the contractor, does not inspect the work to a consistent standard, and often does not receive documentation adequate for the inventory. Several member systems have had to re-verify reimbursed replacements at their own cost.

Reimbursement has a legitimate narrow role: as a pathway for the homeowner who insists on using their own plumber, and as a mechanism for properties the utility's crews cannot practically reach on the neighborhood schedule. It should be an exception process, not a program.

### 3.4 Regional Shared Procurement

Two or more systems jointly procure replacement crews, materials, or both, under a shared contract with allocated schedule blocks.

This is the most underused approach in the membership and, for small systems, the most promising. A 6,000-connection system with 900 lines to replace cannot support a dedicated crew, cannot achieve meaningful pipe and fitting pricing, and cannot attract competitive bids — it is too small to be worth a serious contractor's mobilization. Four such systems acting together represent 3,600 lines and a real contract.

Member consortia report unit costs 12 to 19 percent below what the participating systems achieved individually, plus materially better bid response — a consortium of five Wisconsin members that received one and two bids individually received five bids as a group.

The mechanics require care. The intergovernmental agreement must address allocation of crew time when schedules slip, allocation of unit-price risk when one member's soil conditions prove worse than another's, joint versus several liability, and the process for a member to exit. The League maintains a model intergovernmental agreement and recommends a lead-agency structure in which one system administers the contract for a defined fee rather than a fully joint board, which in member experience makes decisions too slowly for construction.

### 3.5 Records-Based Verification

The utility resolves unknown-material service lines through documentary evidence rather than excavation.

At $70 to $240 per line resolved against $9,100 per line replaced, this is by an order of magnitude the highest-return activity available. Every member system with material unknowns should complete a structured verification campaign before the 2027 compliance date.

The productive sources, in the order member systems report finding them useful: original tap cards and water service application records; plumbing permits, which in most member cities record service material for work after roughly 1940; building permits and certificates of occupancy establishing construction date, since post-1988 construction can generally be classified non-lead by regulation; meter installation and meter replacement work orders, which frequently note the material at the meter connection; sewer lateral records, which sometimes document a simultaneous water service installation; and assessor records for construction date and major renovation history.

Two cautions. First, a documentary classification must be defensible. Construction date alone is sufficient for post-1988 properties; for earlier properties it is corroborating evidence, not proof. Systems should adopt a written evidentiary standard, apply it consistently, and document the basis for each reclassification, because these determinations will be reviewed. Second, verification is labor that small systems do not have. This is well suited to a shared regional service, to a term contract with a records firm, or to seasonal staffing — several members have used college interns effectively at roughly $95 per line resolved fully loaded.

## 4. The Program Model: A 22,000-Connection System

The League has built out a complete program for a representative member system. The profile: 22,000 connections; 4,400 confirmed lead and galvanized lines after verification; median residential bill of $52; existing water utility debt service coverage of 1.42; roughly 38 percent of the lead inventory in census tracts below 60 percent of area median income.

The obligation is 440 lines per year for ten years. The model targets 480 per year to build float, front-loaded slightly to absorb the inevitable late-program access difficulties.

### 4.1 Delivery Mix

|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Neighborhood full replacement (core) | 310 | 7,900 | 2,449,000 | 64.6% |
| Opportunistic with main/paving | 105 | 6,200 | 651,000 | 21.9% |
| Regional consortium blocks | 45 | 7,400 | 333,000 | 9.4% |

*Note: Weighted average unit cost of $7,578 is below the $9,100 membership average because the mix is deliberately weighted toward the low-cost approaches. Systems with less CIP overlap or no consortium access should model $8,300–8,700.*

Total ten-year program cost in constant dollars is approximately $36.4 million; escalated at 4.2 percent, approximately $44.1 million.

### 4.2 Crew Capacity

At a steady-state productivity of 4.5 lines per crew-day in neighborhood mode and a 165-day effective construction season in this climate, one crew delivers roughly 740 lines per year in neighborhood work. The core neighborhood program at 310 lines per year therefore requires slightly under half a crew — which in practice means one crew working roughly 70 days per season, or a shared crew under the regional consortium.

This is the finding that changes small-system thinking. A 22,000-connection system does not need a standing replacement crew. It needs reliable access to roughly half a crew, which is exactly the procurement problem regional consortia solve. Two 22,000-connection systems plus two 8,000-connection systems together support one full-time crew with schedule float, and that is a contract a serious contractor will bid.

**Table 7. Crew and Schedule Model**

The utility staffing requirement is separate and often underestimated: one full-time program coordinator, 0.5 FTE inspection, and 0.4 FTE administrative and records support, at roughly $312,000 annually fully loaded — $650 per line, matching the administration line in Table 2.

### 4.3 Financing: The $2.1 Million Annual Bonding Schedule

The model assumes 30 percent of program cost is covered by SRF principal forgiveness and other grants, leaving approximately $30.9 million escalated to be financed. The League recommends annual or biennial revenue bond issuance sized to roughly $2.1 million per year rather than a single large issuance, for four reasons: it matches debt to expenditure and avoids negative arbitrage; it preserves flexibility if grant awards exceed projections; it allows the rate increase to phase in rather than land at once; and it keeps each issuance small enough for bank placement or state bond bank participation, which for issuances under $3 million typically beats public offering on all-in cost.

Annual rate votes during an eleven-year program produce at least three years in which the increase is deferred, and deferred increases compound.

### 4.5 The Right-of-Entry Ordinance

Every member system should adopt, before mobilizing, an ordinance that does the following:

**Declares the full service line a utility facility.** The most effective structure the League has reviewed defines the service line from the main to the first fitting inside the structure as part of the water system, regardless of the property line, for purposes of maintenance, replacement, and inspection. This resolves the customer-side cost question by making it a utility cost, and it establishes the legal predicate for access.

**Establishes a right of entry for replacement and inspection.** The ordinance should provide for entry upon reasonable notice — the model uses two written notices at least fourteen days apart, plus one attempted in-person contact — for the purpose of inspecting and replacing service lines. It should specify that continued water service is conditioned on reasonable access for compliance with state and federal drinking water requirements.

**Defines the refusal process.** The ordinance should require a signed refusal form, provide that refusal is documented in the inventory, require the utility to re-attempt contact annually, and — this is the provision members most often omit — provide that the refusal runs with the occupant, not the property, so that a change in ownership or tenancy triggers a fresh outreach cycle. Member programs report converting 30 to 45 percent of standing refusals at property transfer.

**Addresses rental property.** The ordinance should place the consent obligation on the property owner, not the tenant, and should require owner consent as a condition of rental registration or certificate of occupancy renewal where the municipality operates such a program. This single provision has done more for access in member cities than any other.

**Provides for restoration and warranty.** The ordinance should commit the utility to restoration to pre-existing condition and to a defined warranty on the installed line, and should specify the claims process. This is both fair and effective; unaddressed restoration anxiety is a leading stated reason for refusal.

The League's model ordinance is available to members and has been adopted, with local modification, in twenty-three member municipalities. It has not been successfully challenged in any of them.

## 5. Sequencing: The First Eighteen Months

Compliance begins in 2027, which means the work that determines whether a system succeeds is happening now.

**2026, Q3–Q4.** Complete records-based verification of unknowns. Adopt the right-of-entry ordinance. Model the ten-year rate path and hold the first public sessions. Initiate consortium discussions with neighboring systems — these take nine to fifteen months to reach a signed agreement, so a system that starts in 2027 will not have a consortium contract until 2029.

**2027, Q1–Q2.** Adopt the ten-year rate ordinance with scheduled steps. Issue the first procurement. Select and survey the first two neighborhood campaign areas. Stand up the program coordinator position. Build the customer contact database and the outreach materials, in the languages actually spoken in the target neighborhoods.

**2027, Q2–Q4.** First construction season. Target 60 to 70 percent of the annual goal in year one, not 100 percent. Every member program that attempted full production in year one experienced quality or documentation failures that cost more to remediate than the shortfall would have.

**2028.** First full production year. Complete the first grant application cycle informed by actual unit costs rather than estimates — member systems report that second-round SRF applications supported by first-year actuals score materially better.

## 6. Answering the Objections

### 6.1 "We Cannot Enter Private Property"

This objection is usually stated as a legal constraint and is usually, on examination, a political one.

Municipalities in all four League states possess adequate authority to condition water service on reasonable access for compliance with drinking water regulations, and to define the scope of the utility's own facilities by ordinance. The League is aware of no decision in the four states invalidating a properly drafted right-of-entry provision of the type described in Section 4.5. The authority exists. What is usually missing is the ordinance.

The practical answer is that access is a communications problem with a legal backstop, not a legal problem. Member programs report the following distribution of reasons for non-consent, from door-to-door follow-up on initial non-response: 34 percent never received or never opened the notice; 21 percent believed the program was a scam or a sales pitch; 18 percent feared cost liability despite the program being free; 12 percent had restoration or property damage concerns; 9 percent were absentee owners or had a landlord-tenant coordination failure; 6 percent were genuine, informed refusals.

Six percent. The 22 to 31 percent figure that appears in member reporting is overwhelmingly non-response, not refusal, and non-response is addressable. Member programs that added in-person door-knocking with trained canvassers, evening and Saturday scheduling, notices in the languages of the neighborhood, and a signed consent collected at the door rather than mailed back reduced effective non-participation from the high twenties to 7 to 12 percent. Canvassing costs $180 to $340 per household contacted. Against a $9,100 replacement and a compliance obligation that does not go away, that is not a close call.

For the residual genuine refusals, document them, re-approach them annually, capture them at property transfer, and — for the small number that persist — rely on the rule's deferral provisions and on the service-condition provision of the ordinance as a last resort. The League does not recommend water shutoff as an enforcement tool. It recommends having the authority, using it rarely, and being visibly reluctant.

### 6.2 "There Are Not Enough Licensed Plumbers"

This objection is real, but it is frequently misdiagnosed. The binding constraint in service line replacement is not licensed plumbers in general; it is the specific scope of work that requires a licensed plumber under state law — typically the interior connection at or near the meter — which represents perhaps 90 minutes of a multi-hour job.

Four responses.

**Restructure the crew.** A replacement crew is primarily an excavation and boring operation. One licensed plumber can support two or three excavation crews if the work is sequenced so that the plumber moves between sites performing interior connections while excavation proceeds elsewhere. Member programs that adopted this structure increased throughput per licensed plumber by a factor of 2.3 to 2.8.

**Use the consortium to hold the scarce resource.** A regional consortium can contract directly for licensed plumbing capacity and allocate it across members, rather than each member competing for the same three local firms.

**Invest in the pipeline.** Several member systems have partnered with regional apprenticeship programs and community colleges, in some cases funding tuition or tools in exchange for a commitment to work in the region. An eleven-year program justifies workforce investment that a two-year program does not. The League is coordinating a four-state effort on this and encourages member participation.

**Verify the actual statutory scope.** Two member systems discovered, after eighteen months of scheduling their programs around plumber availability, that their state's licensing law did not require a licensed plumber for the specific work they were performing outside the building envelope. Have counsel confirm the actual scope before designing around an assumed constraint.

### 6.3 "This Will Cause Rate Shock in Low-Income Neighborhoods"

It will, absent deliberate design. Table 4's high case of $18.90 per month is $227 per year, which for a household at 50 percent of AMI in a member city is a genuine hardship and, in practice, an arrearage.

The League recommends a four-part affordability package, all four parts adopted simultaneously with the rate ordinance:

**A percentage-of-income discount tier.** Eligible households — the League recommends enrollment tied to existing LIHEAP, SNAP, or Medicaid eligibility to avoid a separate application burden — receive a fixed monthly credit sized to hold the lead program surcharge at or near zero for that household. At an assumed 14 percent participation among eligible households and an average credit of $9.50, the cost for a 22,000-connection system is approximately $137,000 annually, or roughly $0.55 per month spread across non-enrolled customers.

**Arrearage forgiveness at enrollment.** One-time forgiveness of pre-existing arrears for households enrolling in the discount tier. This costs money once and produces a substantial improvement in ongoing collection.

**A fixed-charge review.** Many member systems recover 45 to 60 percent of revenue through fixed charges, which is regressive. Shifting the lead surcharge to the volumetric component, or to a fixed charge scaled by meter size and structure type, moderates the impact on small, low-consumption households. This costs the utility nothing.

**Explicit prioritization of low-income neighborhoods in the replacement schedule.** If low-income households are paying for the program through rates, they should receive the benefit first, not last. The League recommends that the first three years of neighborhood campaigns prioritize census tracts below 60 percent of AMI, subject to inventory concentration. This is also defensible on public health grounds — blood lead surveillance data in member cities consistently shows the highest elevated-blood-lead prevalence in exactly these tracts — and it is materially advantageous in SRF scoring in all four states.

One further point on equity. The counterfactual to a rate-funded replacement program is not "no cost." It is continued lead exposure concentrated in low-income neighborhoods, plus eventual replacement under enforcement pressure at higher unit cost, plus the litigation risk illustrated by the League member currently defending a partial-replacement class action. The affordability question is how to distribute the cost of the program, not whether to bear it.

### 6.4 The Partial Replacement Question

Given the pending litigation involving a League member, the League offers one operational recommendation without commenting on the merits of that case: do not perform partial replacements. Where an emergency repair makes a partial replacement temporarily unavoidable, complete the customer-side portion within the rule's forty-five-day window without exception, provide certified filters and flushing instructions immediately, and document every step. The cost of returning to a property to finish a line is a fraction of the cost of defending the decision not to.

## 7. Conclusion

The membership faces 214,000 confirmed replacements, 118,000 unknowns that will substantially resolve favorably if systems do the records work, a $2.9 billion escalated capital requirement, and one-third funding. The deadline does not move.

Five conclusions follow from the data presented here.

**First, resolve the unknowns before 2027.** At $70 to $240 per line against $9,100, records-based verification is the highest-return activity available to any member system, and the window for doing it without construction pressure closes in eighteen months.

**Second, treat the full service line as a utility facility.** The customer-side portion is 30 percent of cost and close to 100 percent of the access problem. Systems that make it a utility obligation by ordinance solve the cost allocation, the access authority, the quality control, and the equity problem in one instrument. Systems that leave it with the homeowner will not meet the schedule, and the lines they fail to replace will be in the neighborhoods that most need them replaced.

**Third, build the program around neighborhood campaigns and treat opportunistic work as savings, not as schedule.** The CIP overlap will deliver 11 to 34 percent of the inventory. It will not deliver a compliance program.

**Fourth, for systems under roughly 15,000 connections, join or form a consortium now.** A nine-to-fifteen-month formation timeline means the work has to start this year to have a contract in place for the 2028 construction season. The unit cost advantage is 12 to 19 percent and the bid response advantage is larger.

**Fifth, adopt the ten-year rate ordinance, the right-of-entry ordinance, and the affordability package as a single package, at the outset.** Each of the three is difficult alone. Together they are a coherent proposition: the utility takes responsibility for the whole line, the utility gets the access it needs to do the work, and the households least able to pay are protected while being served first. Boards that have presented the package this way have adopted it. Boards that have presented the rate increase alone, and promised the rest later, have generally not.

The eleven years between now and 2037 are enough time to do this well. They are not enough time to do it twice.
