# Delta Provisions Foundation — Food Access Enterprise Program

## Application Narrative

**Applicant:** Pine Bluff Community Grocery Cooperative
**Location:** Pine Bluff, Jefferson County, Arkansas
**Request:** $2,200,000 over four years ($1,400,000 grant; $800,000 program-related investment at 2%)
**Contacts:** Bernadette Okafor, General Manager; Curtis Yamashita, Board President
**Submission Deadline:** February 28

## Statement of Need

Pine Bluff sits at the center of one of the most persistent food access gaps in the Arkansas Delta. The Pine Bluff Community Grocery Cooperative's service area is 76 percent Black, 31 percent of residents live below the federal poverty line, and 22 percent of households have no vehicle. Jefferson County's food insecurity rate stands at 21.4 percent — more than one in five of our neighbors is uncertain where their next meal will come from, a rate roughly double the national average. These are not abstract statistics for our cooperative. They describe our member-owners, our employees, and the customers who walk through our doors every day.

The geography of the problem is precise. The census tract where we propose to open a second store is home to 12,400 residents who have had no full-line grocery store since 2018, when the last conventional supermarket in the neighborhood closed. For eight years, residents of that tract have faced a choice between paying convenience-store and dollar-store prices for a narrow selection of shelf-stable goods, or traveling across town — a trip that, for the more than one in five households without a vehicle, means a bus transfer, a ride from a neighbor, or a taxi fare that can consume a meaningful share of a grocery budget. Research on food deserts consistently shows what our members tell us directly: when the trip to fresh food is expensive and difficult, diets shift toward processed foods, produce consumption falls, and diet-related chronic disease follows. Our regional hospital partner reports elevated rates of hypertension and type 2 diabetes in exactly the neighborhoods this project would serve, which is why the hospital has committed to routing produce prescription referrals through the cooperative.

The need extends beyond the city limits. The rural reaches of Jefferson County and adjacent communities contain small towns and unincorporated settlements where the nearest full-line grocery is fifteen to thirty miles away. Residents there are disproportionately elderly, low-income, and transportation-constrained. No fixed-site store can serve them economically, which is why our project includes a mobile market serving six rural sites on a regular weekly schedule.

There is also a supply-side dimension to the need. The Arkansas Delta is farm country, yet very little of what area farms grow moves through local retail channels. Small and mid-sized growers in our region lack aggregation infrastructure — above all, refrigerated storage — that would let them sell into grocery, institutional, and prescription-produce markets at volume. Our cooperative currently buys from 24 area farms, and demand at our register outstrips what those farms can reliably deliver, in large part because there is no shared cold chain between the field and our loading dock. The proposed shared refrigerated warehouse addresses a bottleneck that constrains both food access and farm income simultaneously.

Finally, the need is for an enterprise that can last. Pine Bluff has watched grocery stores come and go — including, candidly, our cooperative's own first store, which opened in 2019 and closed after 22 months. The neighborhood does not need another short-lived experiment. It needs a grocery enterprise with sufficient scale, diversified revenue, community ownership, and disciplined financial management to remain open through thin years. Our current store — 12,000 square feet, opened in 2022, now generating $4.9 million in annual sales with 18 percent growth last year — demonstrates that community-owned grocery can work here. But a single store cannot close a county-wide access gap, and a single store's margins cannot alone fund expansion into the harder-to-serve tracts and rural routes where the need is greatest. That is the gap the Food Access Enterprise Program investment would close.

The evidence of demand is already visible in our registers. SNAP redemption at our store reached $610,000 last year — roughly one dollar in eight of our total sales — and our produce incentive program matched $88,000 in fresh fruit and vegetable purchases for low-income shoppers. When affordable fresh food is placed within reach in this community, people buy it. The question this application answers is how to place it within reach of the 12,400 residents of the unserved tract, the six rural communities on our proposed mobile route, and the growers who could supply them all.

## Project Description

The Pine Bluff Community Grocery Cooperative requests $2.2 million over four years — a $1.4 million grant and an $800,000 program-related investment at 2 percent — to execute a three-part expansion of our food access enterprise, developed jointly by General Manager Bernadette Okafor, Board President Curtis Yamashita, and our board of directors, with formal commitments from four institutional partners.

**Component 1: Second store in the unserved tract.** We will open a second cooperative grocery store of approximately 8,500 square feet in the census tract of 12,400 residents that has lacked a full-line grocery since 2018. The store will be a smaller-format, efficiency-designed operation carrying a full grocery range — fresh produce, meat, dairy, frozen, and center-store staples — with a merchandising mix calibrated to the neighborhood's price sensitivity: strong private-label and value-tier assortment, SNAP-optimized signage and checkout, and a produce department stocked substantially from area farms via the new warehouse. We have identified a leased retail site on a transit line at the commercial heart of the tract; leasehold improvements, refrigeration, fixtures, point-of-sale systems, and opening inventory are the primary uses of the program-related investment. The store will open in month 11 of the grant period and will employ approximately 18 people at opening, hired with preference for tract residents, growing to 24 as sales mature.

**Component 2: Shared refrigerated warehouse.** We will develop an approximately 6,000-square-foot refrigerated warehouse and aggregation facility, operated by the cooperative and shared with the regional farmers alliance. The warehouse serves three functions: (1) it consolidates receiving and cold storage for both stores and the mobile market, lowering per-store distribution cost and shrink; (2) it gives our 24 current farm suppliers — and the additional growers the farmers alliance will recruit — a licensed, refrigerated aggregation point that lets them wash, pack, cool, and hold product to grocery specification, unlocking volume they cannot currently deliver; and (3) it generates fee revenue from third-party cold storage and aggregation services to alliance members selling into schools, the hospital, and other institutional buyers. The county extension office will deliver food safety and post-harvest handling training to participating farms at the facility.

**Component 3: Mobile market serving six rural sites.** We will purchase and operate a refrigerated mobile market vehicle running a fixed weekly schedule to six rural sites selected with the county extension office based on distance from full-line grocery, poverty rate, and senior population. Each site will offer a curated selection of produce, dairy, proteins, and staples at store-shelf prices, with full SNAP/EBT acceptance and produce incentive matching on board. The mobile market will also serve as a redemption point for the produce prescription program, extending the hospital partnership into communities that cannot reach either store.

**Integrated programming across all three components.** The regional hospital will refer patients with diet-related chronic conditions into a produce prescription program redeemable at both stores and the mobile market; the hospital's care coordination staff will handle enrollment and clinical tracking, while the cooperative handles redemption, reporting, and the fresh supply chain behind it. Our existing produce incentive program — which matched $88,000 last year — will be expanded and extended to all three retail channels. The community development bank, through loan officer Nikhil Sundaram, has structured a companion loan supporting the warehouse and will provide ongoing financial advisory services, including quarterly covenant-style reviews of our financial performance — a discipline we describe further under Organizational Capacity.

**Timeline.**

- *Year 1:* Warehouse site acquisition and fit-out (months 1–8); second-store lease execution, design, permitting, and build-out (months 1–10); mobile market vehicle procurement and route establishment (operational by month 6); produce prescription enrollment begins at the existing store (month 3); second store opens month 11.
- *Year 2:* Second store ramps toward sales targets; warehouse reaches full receiving operations for both stores; farmers alliance recruits growers toward a 40-farm supplier base; first-cohort 12-month food security follow-ups collected.
- *Year 3:* Second store reaches monthly operating breakeven (projected month 26 of operation); warehouse third-party fee revenue matures; 24-month food security follow-ups collected; amortization of the program-related investment begins.
- *Year 4:* Consolidated enterprise reaches 2.0 percent operating margin; independent evaluation completed; sustainability plan and post-grant financial projections delivered to the Foundation.

The three components are designed as one enterprise, not three projects. The warehouse lowers cost of goods for both stores and the mobile market while raising farm sales; the second store gives the warehouse the volume that makes shared infrastructure economical; the mobile market extends the fixed assets' reach into communities that will never support a store; and the prescription and incentive programs convert clinical demand into retail revenue that strengthens all three channels. This mutual reinforcement is what distinguishes the proposal from our 2019 store — a single, undercapitalized outlet with no distribution infrastructure behind it — and it is the core of our answer to the question of durability.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

Our goals and objectives are stated in the Food Access Enterprise Program's own tracked metrics: households served, SNAP redemption, produce prescription redemption, farm sales to the cooperative, store-level operating margin, and food security scores at twelve and 24 months. All baselines below are from our most recently completed fiscal year and are drawn from point-of-sale, loyalty, EBT processor, and accounts payable records maintained by Finance Manager Lorena Escamilla.

**Goal 1: Expand fresh food access to unserved urban and rural households.**

- *Objective 1.1 — Households served.* Increase unique households served annually across all cooperative retail channels from approximately 4,200 (baseline, existing store) to 6,500 by end of Year 2 and **9,500 by end of Year 4**, including at least 3,200 households from the second store's tract and at least 900 households at mobile market sites. Measured by loyalty program, EBT transaction, and prescription enrollment data, de-duplicated across channels.
- *Objective 1.2 — Second store opening and utilization.* Open the second store by month 11 of Year 1 and reach 2,000 transactions per week by end of Year 2 and 3,000 per week by end of Year 4.
- *Objective 1.3 — Mobile market.* Complete at least 280 mobile market site-visits per year (six sites, weekly, allowing for weather closures) beginning in Year 1, with average sales of $2,200 per site-visit by end of Year 3.

**Goal 2: Increase food purchasing power for low-income households.**

- *Objective 2.1 — SNAP redemption.* Grow annual SNAP redemption across all channels from $610,000 (baseline) to $900,000 by end of Year 2 and **$1,350,000 by end of Year 4**, with SNAP maintained at no less than 12 percent of total sales enterprise-wide.
- *Objective 2.2 — Produce incentive matching.* Grow annual produce incentive matches from $88,000 (baseline) to $150,000 by end of Year 2 and **$240,000 by end of Year 4**.
- *Objective 2.3 — Produce prescription redemption.* Enroll at least 400 hospital-referred patients by end of Year 2 and 500 annually thereafter, achieving prescription redemption of $90,000 in Year 2 and **$150,000 annually by Year 3**, with a redemption rate (dollars redeemed as a share of dollars prescribed) of at least 70 percent.

**Goal 3: Grow local farm income through cooperative purchasing and shared infrastructure.**

- *Objective 3.1 — Farm sales to the co-op.* Increase annual cooperative purchases from area farms from $612,000 across 24 farms (baseline) to $850,000 by end of Year 2 and **$1,280,000 across at least 40 farms by end of Year 4**.
- *Objective 3.2 — Warehouse utilization.* Bring the refrigerated warehouse to full operation by month 8 of Year 1; achieve third-party storage and aggregation fee revenue of $48,000 annually by end of Year 3.
- *Objective 3.3 — Farm readiness.* In partnership with the county extension office, deliver food safety and post-harvest handling training to at least 30 farms by end of Year 2, with at least 25 achieving grocery-specification certification.

**Goal 4: Achieve durable enterprise-level financial health.**

- *Objective 4.1 — Store-level operating margin.* Raise the existing store's operating margin from 1.2 percent (baseline, with losses in two of the last four quarters) to 2.0 percent by end of Year 2 and 2.4 percent by end of Year 4; bring the second store to monthly operating breakeven by month 26 of operation (Year 3 of the grant) and to a 1.2 percent full-year operating margin in Year 4.
- *Objective 4.2 — Consolidated margin and debt service.* Achieve a consolidated enterprise operating margin of **2.0 percent by end of Year 4**, with debt service coverage of at least 1.4× on the combined program-related investment and community development bank loan (detailed under Sustainability).

**Goal 5: Improve household food security among enrolled participants.**

- *Objective 5.1 — Food security score.* Among households enrolled in the produce prescription and incentive programs, reduce the share scoring "low" or "very low" food security on the USDA six-item short-form module by at least 15 percentage points at **twelve months** and at least 25 percentage points at **24 months**, relative to enrollment baseline.

## Evaluation Plan

Our evaluation is built directly around the six metrics the Food Access Enterprise Program tracks, uses data systems largely already in place at the cooperative, and pairs internal continuous monitoring with an independent external evaluation.

**Data infrastructure.** The cooperative's point-of-sale system captures transaction-level data at both stores and, via a mobile POS unit, on the market vehicle. EBT processor reports provide SNAP redemption by channel and month. The produce incentive and prescription programs run on card-linked loyalty accounts, allowing us to track redemption dollars, redemption rates, basket composition, and repeat participation at the household level while protecting individual privacy. Farm purchasing is tracked through accounts payable, coded by supplier, giving an auditable record of farm sales to the co-op by farm and by month. Finance Manager Lorena Escamilla produces monthly store-level profit-and-loss statements — a practice instituted after our 2019–2021 store closure specifically so that store-level margin problems surface within 30 days rather than accumulating unseen. A dedicated evaluation and data coordinator, funded in the project budget, will maintain a consolidated dashboard covering all funder metrics, refreshed monthly.

**Measurement of funder-tracked metrics.**

- *Households served:* unique households identified through loyalty accounts, EBT card numbers (hashed), and prescription enrollment, de-duplicated across the two stores and mobile market; reported quarterly.
- *SNAP redemption:* EBT processor settlement reports by channel; reported quarterly against annual targets.
- *Produce prescription redemption:* dollars prescribed, dollars redeemed, redemption rate, and enrollee counts, reconciled monthly between hospital enrollment records and cooperative redemption records under a data-sharing agreement already drafted with the hospital's compliance office.
- *Farm sales to the co-op:* accounts payable totals by farm supplier, plus warehouse throughput volumes; reported quarterly, with an annual roster of participating farms.
- *Store-level operating margin:* monthly P&L for each store and for the mobile market and warehouse as separate cost/revenue centers, prepared by the finance manager, reviewed by the board finance committee, and reviewed quarterly with Nikhil Sundaram at the community development bank; reported to the Foundation quarterly.
- *Food security score:* the USDA six-item short-form food security module administered at enrollment, twelve months, and 24 months to all produce prescription enrollees and a consented sample of at least 300 incentive-program households. Surveys will be administered by hospital care coordinators (for prescription patients) and trained cooperative staff (for incentive households), in person or by phone, with a $15 store credit for survey completion to support retention. We target 70 percent follow-up completion at twelve months and 60 percent at 24 months.

**Independent evaluation.** We will contract an external evaluator — we are in discussion with the public health faculty at a state university with an established Delta health research program — to (1) validate our internal metric reporting annually, (2) analyze food security score changes at twelve and 24 months, including subgroup analysis by channel (store, mobile market) and by SNAP participation, and (3) conduct a comparison analysis using hospital clinical population data to contextualize observed changes, acknowledging that without randomization the evaluation is a rigorous pre/post design with a comparison group rather than a causal trial. The evaluator will produce annual reports to the Foundation and a final Year 4 report suitable for publication and field sharing.

**Learning and course correction.** Evaluation is not only for reporting. The board has adopted a quarterly review cycle in which the general manager presents the metric dashboard alongside store-level financials; any store or channel missing its margin or sales targets for two consecutive quarters triggers a written corrective action plan. This mechanism — the direct institutional lesson of our first store's closure — ensures that evaluation findings change operations in real time. We will also convene our four partners (hospital, farmers alliance, community development bank, county extension) twice yearly to review results and adjust referral flows, farm recruitment, mobile route composition, and credit terms as the data warrant.

## Organizational Capacity

The Pine Bluff Community Grocery Cooperative is a member-owned grocery cooperative with 1,840 member-owners, 31 employees, and $4.9 million in annual sales from a single 12,000-square-foot store opened in 2022. Sales grew 18 percent last year. We are governed by a nine-member elected board, chaired by Board President Curtis Yamashita, with standing finance, membership, and expansion committees. Day-to-day operations are led by General Manager Bernadette Okafor, who came to the cooperative with fifteen years of conventional and cooperative grocery management experience, and Finance Manager Lorena Escamilla, who oversees monthly close, store-level P&L reporting, and cash management.

**We will address our history directly, because the Foundation should hear it from us.** The cooperative's first store opened in 2019 in the same neighborhood as our current store and closed after 22 months, in early 2021, leaving $340,000 in vendor debt. Every dollar of that debt has since been repaid in full — a repayment the board treated as a non-negotiable obligation to the local suppliers and farms who had trusted us — and several of those vendors, including area farms, sell to us again today. But repayment does not erase the lesson, and we do not ask the Foundation to overlook the closure. We ask the Foundation to examine what changed because of it.

The 2019 store failed for identifiable reasons: it was undercapitalized at opening, with no working capital reserve to absorb a slow ramp; it lacked experienced grocery management, relying on a committed but inexperienced founding team; it had no store-level financial reporting, so mounting losses were not visible to the board until they were acute; and it had no distribution infrastructure, leaving it with high cost of goods and unreliable fresh supply. The pandemic's disruption in 2020 accelerated a failure whose causes predated it.

The rebuilt cooperative that opened the current store in 2022 was designed against each of those failure modes. We recruited professional grocery management in Ms. Okafor. We capitalized the 2022 store with a working capital reserve sized to twelve months of projected operating losses. We instituted monthly store-level P&L reporting under Ms. Escamilla, with quarterly board finance committee review. And we built supplier relationships — now 24 area farms — with payment terms we have honored without exception since reopening. The result is a store that reached $4.9 million in sales in its third full year, grew 18 percent last year, redeemed $610,000 in SNAP, and operates at a positive though thin 1.2 percent margin.

We are equally candid about current performance: we posted losses in two of the last four quarters. Ms. Escamilla's analysis attributes those quarters to wholesale cost inflation outpacing our deliberately restrained retail pricing, and to seasonal sales softness in a single-store operation with no volume diversification. Both problems are directly addressed by this project — the warehouse lowers cost of goods and shrink, and the second store, mobile market, and fee revenue diversify volume — and our margin objectives (2.0 percent consolidated by Year 4) are set accordingly. The relevant capacity question is not whether we have had hard quarters; it is whether we see them within 30 days, understand their causes, and act. We do, and the record since 2022 shows it.

**Partner capacity.** Each partner brings institutional depth the cooperative does not need to duplicate. The regional hospital operates established chronic disease management programs and will handle prescription enrollment, clinical screening, and referral through its care coordination staff. The farmers alliance represents the region's small and mid-sized growers and will manage grower recruitment and scheduling for the shared warehouse. The community development bank has underwritten the cooperative twice — including the 2022 store — and loan officer Nikhil Sundaram serves as our designated relationship officer; the bank's companion loan for this project reflects an underwriting review of the same financials presented in this application, and its quarterly advisory reviews add an external financial discipline that we welcome. The county extension office brings food safety training capacity and deep knowledge of the six rural communities on the mobile route, which its agents helped select.

**Staffing for the project.** The grant funds a project director reporting to Ms. Okafor, a second-store manager (recruited with grocery management experience, per our post-2021 policy), a warehouse coordinator, a two-person mobile market team, a produce prescription coordinator, and the evaluation and data coordinator. Board oversight of the project rests with the expansion committee, chaired by Mr. Yamashita, reporting to the full board monthly.

## Sustainability

The Foundation's central sustainability questions are fair ones for an applicant with our history: how will the program-related investment be serviced, and when does the second store carry itself? We answer both with specific numbers, and we distinguish carefully between operating breakeven and full debt-service coverage.

**Debt service on the program-related investment.** We propose to structure the $800,000 program-related investment at 2 percent with a ten-year term: interest-only payments of approximately $16,000 in Year 1, followed by level amortization over nine years at approximately $8,100 per month, or roughly $97,200 annually, beginning in Year 2 of the grant period. Total interest over the life of the investment is approximately $80,000. Alongside the PRI, the community development bank's companion loan of $475,000 (twelve-year term at market-rate terms underwritten by Mr. Sundaram) carries annual debt service of approximately $56,300. Peak combined annual debt service is therefore approximately **$153,500** from Year 3 forward.

**Sources of coverage.** Debt service is covered at the consolidated enterprise level from four streams. By end of Year 4, we project: the existing store generating approximately $151,000 in annual operating income ($6.3 million in sales at a 2.4 percent margin, reflecting the cost-of-goods improvement from the warehouse); the second store generating approximately $41,000 ($3.4 million at 1.2 percent); the warehouse generating approximately $48,000 in net third-party fee income; and modest net contribution from the mobile market once grant-funded startup costs conclude. Consolidated operating income of approximately $240,000 against $153,500 in debt service yields a projected debt service coverage ratio of roughly **1.56×** in Year 4, comfortably above our 1.4× objective and the bank's covenant threshold. During Years 1–2, before the second store matures, the grant's working capital reserve and the existing store's improving margin bridge the gap; our projections show coverage never falling below 1.2× in any year, and the interest-only first year on the PRI is specifically designed to match the second store's ramp.

**When the second store carries itself.** The second store's monthly operating breakeven — the point at which it covers its own occupancy, labor, cost of goods, and direct overhead — occurs at approximately $255,000 in monthly sales, roughly a $3.05 million annual run rate. Our ramp model, benchmarked against our 2022 store's actual trajectory in a comparable trade area, projects the store reaching that run rate in **month 26 of operation, during Year 3 of the grant period**. From that point the store no longer draws on reserves for operations. Full coverage of the store's allocated share of PRI debt service is a higher bar: we project the store covering its full allocated debt service from its own margin in its fifth year of operation (the year following the grant period), as sales approach $4.2 million and margin reaches approximately 2.3 percent. We state this plainly rather than compressing the timeline, because the enterprise-level coverage described above does not depend on the second store hitting that milestone early — the consolidated cooperative services the debt from Year 1, and the second store's maturation steadily deepens the cushion.

**Sustainability beyond financials.** Program sustainability rests on revenue-generating design. The produce prescription program transitions from grant-funded to hospital-funded: the hospital has indicated intent, subject to demonstrated twelve- and 24-month food security outcomes, to fund prescription dollars from its community benefit obligation from Year 3 forward. The produce incentive program is a candidate for USDA GusNIP continuation funding, for which our evaluation data will make us a strong applicant. Warehouse fees, membership equity from an expanding member-owner base (we project 2,600 member-owners by Year 4), and the mobile market's retail revenue all continue without philanthropic support. What the Foundation's investment buys is not an ongoing subsidy but a one-time capitalization of infrastructure — store, warehouse, vehicle — whose operating economics sustain themselves, backed by a management team and board that have already demonstrated, through the full repayment of $340,000 in vendor debt and the successful 2022 relaunch, that this cooperative honors its obligations and learns from its mistakes.

**Second store ($1,240,000).** The program-related investment of $800,000, supplemented by $315,000 of grant funds and $125,000 of cooperative cash reserves, funds leasehold improvements to the 8,500-square-foot leased site ($540,000), refrigeration cases, walk-ins, and compressor systems ($370,000), shelving, fixtures, and point-of-sale ($150,000), and opening inventory ($180,000). Deploying the PRI against revenue-producing store assets, rather than program costs, is deliberate: the asset generates the sales that service the debt, and the interest-only first year matches the store's ramp as described under Sustainability.

**Refrigerated warehouse ($560,000).** The community development bank's $475,000 companion loan, structured by Nikhil Sundaram, funds the acquisition and cold-chain fit-out of the 6,000-square-foot facility, with $60,000 of grant funds covering aggregation equipment (pack line, pallet jacks, scales) used for farmer services and $25,000 of co-op equity covering closing and commissioning costs. Third-party storage fees repay the operating share of this asset over time.

**Mobile market ($210,000).** Grant funds purchase a refrigerated mobile market vehicle with onboard POS and EBT capability ($185,000) and site equipment — canopies, signage, cold totes, generators — for six rural sites ($25,000).

**Program staffing ($470,000 over four years).** Grant funds support the project director (0.75 FTE, four years), the produce prescription coordinator (1.0 FTE, four years), the evaluation and data coordinator (0.5 FTE, four years), and first-year salary support for the two-person mobile market team, whose costs shift to mobile market retail revenue and store margins from Year 2. Second-store retail staff (18 positions at opening) and the warehouse coordinator are funded from store and warehouse operating revenue, not grant funds, consistent with our principle that the grant capitalizes the enterprise rather than subsidizing its ongoing payroll.

**Produce prescription and incentive pool ($180,000).** Grant funds seed prescription redemption dollars and incentive matches in Years 1–3 ($90,000, $60,000, and $30,000 respectively), on a deliberately declining schedule as hospital community-benefit funding and USDA incentive funding assume these costs, per the transition plan under Sustainability.

**Evaluation and data systems ($95,000).** Covers the external evaluator contract ($60,000 across four years), survey administration and participant completion credits ($20,000), and dashboard and data integration costs ($15,000).

**Working capital reserve ($70,000).** A dedicated reserve against second-store ramp-up variance — the single budget line most directly informed by our 2019 experience. Any unspent reserve at the end of Year 3 will be reallocated, with Foundation approval, to the produce incentive pool.

**Leverage and match.** Beyond the $475,000 bank loan and $150,000 in cooperative equity, the project carries substantial in-kind commitments not shown in the table: hospital care coordination staff time for enrollment and screening, county extension training delivery, and farmers alliance grower recruitment, conservatively valued at $110,000 over four years. Every Foundation dollar is thus matched by approximately $0.52 in cash and in-kind leverage — evidence that the region's institutions are invested alongside the Foundation in the cooperative's success, and in the 9,500 households this enterprise will serve by the close of the grant period.
