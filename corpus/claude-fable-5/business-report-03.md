## 1. Executive Summary

Foxglove Home and Hearth posted second-quarter net revenue of $109.8 million, up 6.6 percent against the prior-year quarter, with essentially all of the growth carried by the e-commerce channel and the Northern region. Beneath the topline, the quarter was the most operationally disrupted period the company has experienced since the 2023 acquisition, and the profit line shows it. Adjusted EBITDA of $7.3 million, a 6.6 percent margin, compares with $10.1 million and 9.8 percent a year ago. Three discrete events explain the gap, and all three are identified, quantified, and carry remediation plans in this report.

First, the relocation of the distribution center from Winchester to Georgetown broke replenishment for six weeks spanning May and early June. In-stock averaged 87 percent for the quarter against our 96 percent standard, troughing at 81 percent the week of June 6. We estimate $2.9 million of lost sales and approximately $1.0 million of lost gross profit, on top of $1.9 million of one-time transition costs. In-stock recovered to 93.4 percent by quarter end and is forecast to reach standard by September 26.

Second, the $9.4 million outdoor furniture program sold through at 54 percent against an 85 percent plan, forcing $2.8 million of markdown that cost approximately 255 basis points of gross margin in the quarter. Roughly $2.1 million of clearance inventory remains, with a liquidation plan targeting a clean exit by September 30 and an identified residual markdown risk of up to $0.4 million.

Third, e-commerce grew 18.0 percent to $44.0 million but ran a negative 2.1 percent contribution margin, a $2.2 million year-over-year contribution swing, driven by a carrier surcharge, a 21.3 percent return rate, and elevated split shipments during the distribution center transition. A carrier re-bid, a returns-reduction program, and a revised shipping-fee architecture are in flight, with the carrier decision due in September.

Store performance diverged sharply by region. The Northern region comped up 5.6 percent on the strength of three remodels that are tracking ahead of their underwriting. Bluegrass comped up 2.1 percent at the company's best gross margin, dragged by two stores adjacent to a closed department store anchor. Ohio Valley comped down 3.4 percent amid the replacement of four of ten store managers. Appalachian comped down 6.2 percent, and a Charleston lease decision with a 31 percent proposed renewal increase must be made by November 15.

Ending inventory of $126.7 million is $7.7 million above plan. Receipt cuts of $6.0 million in the third quarter will return inventory to plan by the November 1 holiday set. We now guide full-year revenue of $421 to $426 million against a $428 million plan, and adjusted EBITDA of $31.5 to $33.5 million against a $36.5 million plan. The back half requires disciplined execution on six priorities, detailed in Section 9, and we believe the second-quarter damage is contained and largely non-recurring.

Store payroll was managed tightly at 11.2 percent of store revenue against an 11.4 percent standard, which is to Curtis Yamamoto's team's credit given the disruption the stores absorbed.

## 3. Bluegrass Region

**12 stores | Revenue $23.4M | Comp +2.1% | Gross margin 34.2%**

The Bluegrass region remains the company's profit anchor. Twelve stores produced $23.4 million in revenue, a positive 2.1 percent comp, and a 34.2 percent gross margin — the best margin performance of any region and 300 basis points above the company blend. The Lexington, Georgetown, and Frankfort stores benefited from proximity to the new distribution center once it stabilized; these three locations were the first lanes restored to full replenishment in June and finished the quarter at a 95 percent in-stock rate, effectively at standard.

The regional headline understates the health of the core fleet. Ten of the twelve stores comped up 3.9 percent in aggregate. The drag came entirely from the two stores situated beside the former Hargrove's department store anchors at their respective centers — locations that lost their anchor when Hargrove's closed both units in the first quarter. Those two stores comped down 9.0 percent on combined quarterly revenue of $2.9 million, as center traffic counts provided by the landlords show declines of 14 and 17 percent since the anchor closures.

Both stores remain four-wall profitable, but the trajectory demands a decision framework rather than passive monitoring. We see three variables: the landlords' anchor backfill pipelines, our lease expirations (October 2027 and June 2028 respectively), and the pace of traffic decay. One landlord has a letter of intent with a discount grocery operator for a late-2027 opening; the other has no identified replacement. FP&A will maintain a quarterly four-wall model for both locations, and we recommend that any capital request for either store be deferred until the backfill picture clarifies. Neither store requires action this fiscal year, but the June 2028 location should enter the fiscal 2027 portfolio review with a relocation scenario fully costed.

Beyond the anchor issue, the region's execution metrics were strong: conversion up 40 basis points, average ticket up 2.8 percent on attachment of accent furniture and lighting to upholstery sales, and clienteling-driven repeat purchases now representing 31 percent of regional revenue, the highest in the fleet. The region also absorbed the outdoor markdown better than others because its stores received their outdoor floor sets on time in March, ahead of the distribution center cutover, and sold through at 61 percent against the 54 percent company average.

## 4. Ohio Valley Region

**10 stores | Revenue $16.8M | Comp −3.4% | Gross margin 31.6%**

The Ohio Valley region comped down 3.4 percent on revenue of $16.8 million, and the quarter is best understood as a leadership disruption story. Four of the region's ten store managers were replaced during the quarter: two resigned to a competing regional furniture chain that has been recruiting aggressively in the Cincinnati and Columbus markets, one was terminated for performance, and one was promoted into the vacant regional operations manager role, which was itself a necessary backfill.

The comp data isolates the impact cleanly. The four stores that changed managers comped down 6.8 percent in aggregate; the six stores with stable leadership comped down 1.2 percent, roughly in line with what the in-stock disruption alone would predict. The manager-transition stores showed the expected pattern: conversion down 90 basis points, scheduled-hours compliance slipping to 91 percent against a 97 percent standard, and delivery-experience survey scores falling six points, which matters because delivery follow-up drives the region's repeat business.

Curtis Yamamoto's team has moved on three fronts. First, all four replacement managers were in seat by July 11, three hired internally from assistant manager benches and one externally from a mattress retailer. Second, the region has instituted a retention review covering the remaining six managers and all assistant managers, resulting in market-based compensation adjustments for five individuals at an annualized cost of $0.11 million — inexpensive insurance against a competitor that has now hired four people out of this region in twelve months. Third, the regional vice president is running a ninety-day integration plan for each new manager with weekly scorecards on conversion, staffing compliance, and delivery follow-up.

The financial expectation is that the manager-transition stores return to the regional trend line by the end of the third quarter and to positive comp territory in the fourth, consistent with our historical experience that a store manager transition costs roughly two quarters of comp performance. The region's gross margin of 31.6 percent ran below company average primarily on outdoor markdown exposure — the region took the deepest clearance pricing because its stores received outdoor sets latest and carried the heaviest residuals.

One structural note for the board observer: Ohio Valley operates in the most competitive markets in our footprint, and its manager compensation had drifted to the 45th percentile of market. The retention adjustments move the region to the 60th percentile. We view this as a permanent cost of doing business in these markets, not a one-time fix.

## 5. Northern Region

**9 stores | Revenue $15.9M | Comp +5.6% | Gross margin 33.4%**

The Northern region's nine Indiana stores delivered the best comp in the fleet at positive 5.6 percent on revenue of $15.9 million, and the story is the remodel program. The three stores remodeled with the $6.8 million capital investment completed in the first quarter — Carmel, Fort Wayne, and Greenwood — comped up 12.9 percent in aggregate. The six non-remodeled stores comped up 1.5 percent, which is itself the best base-fleet performance of any region and suggests the Indiana markets are simply healthier than our Kentucky and West Virginia markets right now.

### 5.1 Remodel Performance Against Underwriting

The remodel program was underwritten to a 10 percent first-year comp lift, a 150 basis point four-wall margin improvement from the expanded upholstery and design-services footprint, and a five-year payback. Two quarters in, the program is ahead on every measure.

| Metric | Underwriting | Actual (2 quarters) |
|---|---|---|
| Comp lift, remodeled stores | +10.0% | +12.9% |
| Four-wall margin improvement | +150 bps | +180 bps |
| Design-services attachment rate | 12% of transactions | 15% of transactions |
| Implied payback | 5.0 years | ~4.2 years |

The design-services attachment is the most important finding. Transactions that include a design consultation carry an average ticket 2.4 times the store average and return at less than half the e-commerce return rate, because the product is specified correctly the first time. This has direct implications for both the fiscal 2027 remodel pipeline and the e-commerce returns problem discussed in Section 6.

### 5.2 Fiscal 2027 Remodel Pipeline

The results support advancing the next remodel tranche. FP&A has scoped a four-store fiscal 2027 program at an estimated $8.6 million: two additional Northern stores, one Bluegrass store, and one Ohio Valley store, sequenced to avoid remodeling in any market where a manager has been in seat less than six months. A full capital request will be presented at the October planning session. We flag for Devon Marsh that this request will compete with the Georgetown facility's remaining automation investment and the e-commerce platform work; the October session should rank all three.

The region's gross margin of 33.4 percent ran above company average despite full participation in the outdoor markdown, because the remodeled stores' richer upholstery mix carries structurally higher margin. In-stock in the region averaged 88 percent for the quarter, roughly at the company average, with the longer lanes from Georgetown recovering by early July.

## 6. Appalachian Region

**7 stores | Revenue $9.7M | Comp −6.2% | Gross margin 30.7%**

The Appalachian region's seven stores comped down 6.2 percent on revenue of $9.7 million, the weakest performance in the fleet, and the causes are three-layered: the softest consumer backdrop in our footprint, the worst in-stock position of any region during the distribution center transition, and a fleet of smaller stores with less merchandising flexibility to work around gaps.

On the consumer backdrop, the region's markets continue to show employment softness and the lowest housing turnover in our footprint; housing turnover is the single best external predictor of our comp performance, and the region's markets ran 11 percent below prior year in transactions. On the operational layer, the region sits at the end of the longest replenishment lanes from Georgetown and averaged 84 percent in-stock for the quarter, three points below the company average and twelve below standard. The lanes were the last restored, in mid-July. We attribute roughly half the region's comp decline to the in-stock gap and half to the market backdrop, which implies an underlying trend of approximately negative 3 percent — weak, but not deteriorating versus the first quarter's underlying trend.

### 6.1 Charleston Lease Decision

The material near-term decision in this region is the Charleston store. The lease on the 24,000 square foot location expires in March 2027, and the landlord's renewal proposal carries a 31 percent rent increase, from $16.40 to $21.48 per square foot, or approximately $122,000 of incremental annual occupancy cost.

| Charleston store | Trailing 12 months |
|---|---|
| Revenue | $4.3M |
| Four-wall EBITDA | $0.39M (9.0% of revenue) |
| Four-wall EBITDA at proposed rent | $0.26M (6.1% of revenue) |
| Revenue trend | −5.8% |
| Lease notice deadline | November 15, 2026 |

Three options are on the table. Renew at the proposed rate, which preserves market presence but locks a declining-trend store into a materially worse cost structure for a five-year term. Relocate to a smaller-format box — a broker survey has identified two candidate sites of 14,000 to 16,000 square feet at $14 to $16 per square foot, which would reduce occupancy cost roughly 40 percent but require an estimated $1.4 million in buildout and transition capital. Or close, which would strand approximately $1.9 million of transferable revenue (our transfer models suggest only 20 to 25 percent recapture by Huntington and the e-commerce channel given the distances involved).

FP&A's preliminary view is that relocation to the smaller format is the strongest option if the negative comp trend persists through the third quarter, and that a counterproposal to the landlord — a two-year renewal at a 12 percent increase — is worth tabling immediately to preserve optionality. Curtis Yamamoto and the real estate committee should bring a recommendation to the executive team by October 31, two weeks ahead of the notice deadline. This decision should also set the template for the region: three more Appalachian leases expire in fiscal 2028, and the smaller-format economics being proven or disproven in Charleston will inform all of them.

## 7. E-Commerce Channel

**Revenue $44.0M | Growth +18.0% | Contribution margin −2.1%**

E-commerce is simultaneously the company's growth engine and its most urgent profitability problem. Revenue of $44.0 million grew 18.0 percent over the prior year's $37.3 million and now represents 40.1 percent of company revenue. Order volume grew 15.4 percent to approximately 71,900 orders and average order value rose 2.3 percent to $612. But the channel produced a contribution margin of negative 2.1 percent — a loss of approximately $0.9 million — against a positive 3.4 percent contribution a year ago. The $2.2 million year-over-year contribution swing is the single largest profit variance in the company, larger than the outdoor markdown.

### 7.1 Diagnosis

| Metric | Q2 FY2026 | Q2 FY2025 |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $44.0M | $37.3M |
| Orders | ~71,900 | ~62,300 |
| Average order value | $612 | $598 |
| Return rate | 21.3% | 16.8% |
| Delivery cost per order | $71 | $58 |
| Split-shipment rate | 31% | 18% |
| Contribution margin | −2.1% | +3.4% |

Three drivers, in order of magnitude:

**Carrier surcharge.** Our primary big-and-bulky delivery carrier imposed a 14 percent fuel and capacity surcharge effective April 1, and small-parcel rates rose at renewal. Combined, delivery cost per order rose from $58 to $71, worth approximately $0.9 million in the quarter. This is a rate problem, and the answer is competition: a full carrier RFP is in market with three qualified bidders responding, including a regional final-mile network that would use Georgetown as its origin hub. Bids are due September 5 and a decision is due September 19, with modeled savings of $8 to $12 per big-and-bulky order at current volumes.

**Return rate.** Returns rose from 16.8 percent to 21.3 percent, concentrated in upholstery, where the stated return reasons cluster on color and scale mismatch — the customer's expectation formed online did not survive the product's arrival. Each upholstery return costs an average of $118 in reverse logistics and remarketing loss. The lesson from the Northern remodels is instructive: design-assisted purchases return at less than half this rate. The remediation program has four elements: rebuilt product imagery and dimensional content for the top 200 upholstery SKUs by September 30; a free fabric-swatch program launching in October; virtual design consultations offered at checkout for orders above $1,500; and a fourth-quarter test of a restocking fee on select oversized categories, which competitors have adopted and which our modeling suggests deters marginal orders more than it deters purchases.

**Split shipments.** The distribution center disruption pushed the split-shipment rate from 18 to 31 percent as orders were filled from whatever node had stock. This is self-healing as Georgetown stabilizes; July exited at 22 percent, and the integration of e-commerce fulfillment into Georgetown's expanded footprint should bring the structural rate below the historical 18 percent by year end.

### 7.2 Pricing Architecture

Separately from cost remediation, we are correcting a shipping-revenue problem. The $49 free-shipping threshold, set in 2021, no longer reflects delivery economics. Effective October 1, the small-parcel threshold moves to $99, and big-and-bulky orders below $1,499 will carry a $79 delivery fee (waived above that level and for design-service customers). Modeled annualized contribution benefit is $1.6 to $2.0 million against a modeled revenue risk of 1 to 2 points of channel growth, a trade we recommend taking. With the carrier re-bid, returns program, split-shipment recovery, and fee architecture combined, the channel is modeled to return to breakeven contribution in the fourth quarter and a positive 4 to 5 percent contribution margin in fiscal 2027. An 18 percent growth channel at 40 percent of revenue cannot remain contribution-negative; this is the company's first-priority fix.

## 8. Distribution Center Transition

The relocation from the 240,000 square foot Winchester facility to the 410,000 square foot Georgetown facility was the right strategic decision executed with insufficient contingency, and this section is deliberately candid because the sponsor will ask, and should.

### 8.1 What Happened

Georgetown went live May 4 with a new warehouse management system on a hard-cutover plan. Three failures compounded. First, item master data conversion carried dimensional errors on roughly 8 percent of SKUs, which broke slotting logic and cartonization and forced manual intervention on a large share of outbound orders. Second, the facility opened at 68 percent of planned labor, as the Georgetown labor market proved tighter than the staffing study projected and the Winchester workforce's transfer rate came in at 44 percent against a 60 percent assumption. Third, outbound carrier appointment scheduling in the new system created yard congestion that at its worst held loaded trailers three days.

The consequence was a six-week replenishment break through May and early June. Company in-stock averaged 87 percent for the quarter against the 96 percent standard, with a trough of 81 percent the week of June 6. Regions were affected unevenly by lane length: Bluegrass recovered first, Appalachian last.

### 8.2 Financial Impact

| Impact | Amount |
|---|---|
| Estimated lost sales (in-stock gap modeled against demand) | $2.9M |
| Estimated lost gross profit | ~$1.0M |
| One-time transition costs, of which: | $1.9M |
| — Expedited and redirected freight | $0.6M |
| — Temporary labor and overtime | $0.5M |
| — Dual-facility occupancy overlap | $0.4M |
| — WMS remediation and consulting | $0.4M |

The $1.9 million of one-time costs is excluded from adjusted EBITDA; the lost gross profit is not, and flows through reported results.

### 8.3 Recovery and Go-Forward

Recovery is on track. In-stock exited the quarter at 93.4 percent, stood at 94.1 percent in the week ending August 8, and is forecast to reach the 96 percent standard by September 26 — comfortably ahead of holiday receipt flow. Georgetown labor is now at 94 percent of plan following a wage adjustment and a referral program. The dimensional data cleanup completed July 18. Dual occupancy at Winchester ends October 31 when the lease terminates.

The strategic case for Georgetown is unchanged: capacity to serve 55 stores against today's 38, integration of e-commerce fulfillment that eliminates the third-party fulfillment node and its costs, and modeled annual operating savings of $1.1 million beginning in fiscal 2027 once automation is fully commissioned. The governance lesson is recorded for future projects of this scale: hard cutovers of facility and systems simultaneously will not be approved again without a parallel-run period and a staffing contingency, and FP&A will include this in the post-mortem being delivered to the executive team on September 12.

## 9. Outdoor Furniture Markdown

The spring outdoor furniture program was a $9.4 million receipt commitment — the largest seasonal buy in company history and 38 percent above prior year on a unit basis — underwritten to an 85 percent sell-through by the end of July. Actual sell-through reached 54 percent, forcing $2.8 million of markdown in the quarter, approximately 255 basis points of gross margin.

Four causes, in Lena Ostrowski's and FP&A's joint assessment. Weather was genuinely adverse — April and May were the wettest in our core markets in a decade, and outdoor is the most weather-elastic category we sell. But weather does not carry the full explanation. The buy itself was oversized relative to demonstrated demand, scaling last year's strong season without discounting the possibility that fiscal 2025 was the outlier. The price architecture skewed premium, with 40 percent of receipts above $1,999 sets in markets where the demand center is $999 to $1,499. And the distribution center transition delayed outdoor floor sets by three weeks at 21 of 38 stores, compressing the selling season precisely when the category needed every available week.

### 9.1 Residual Position and Clearance Plan

After the markdown, approximately $2.1 million of outdoor inventory at current carrying value remains. The clearance plan consolidates residual units to four designated clearance-role stores plus a dedicated online clearance shop, with progressive price steps through Labor Day and a target of a clean exit by September 30. If sell-through at clearance pricing underperforms, incremental markdown exposure is estimated at up to $0.4 million, which is reflected in the low end of full-year guidance. We will not carry outdoor furniture into holiday floor sets or into fiscal 2027 as pack-away; the carrying cost and floor-space displacement exceed any recovery.

### 9.2 Fiscal 2027 Buy Discipline

The fiscal 2027 outdoor buy will be governed differently: a receipt cap of $6.5 million; 20 percent of the commitment held in an in-season reserve with vendor cancel dates in April, purchasing the option to chase or cut based on actual selling; price architecture re-centered on the $999 to $1,499 band; and floor-set dates protected as a supply chain commitment with executive escalation rights. FP&A will build the weather-adjusted demand model into the buy review, and the buy plan comes to the executive team in November before commitments are placed.

## 10. Margin and Inventory Position

### 10.1 Margin Position

Second-quarter gross margin of 31.2 percent is 280 basis points below prior year, but the composition matters more than the headline. Per the bridge in Section 2.2, roughly 310 basis points of the decline is attributable to the outdoor markdown and DC transition freight — both non-recurring — while underlying regular-price selling contributed 70 basis points of improvement. Normalizing for the two events, the quarter's run-rate gross margin is approximately 34.3 percent, modestly above prior year.

The back-half margin plan is 33.6 percent against 33.1 percent in the prior-year back half, built on three planks: no repetition of the markdown event (outdoor residual risk is capped and quantified), continued promotional discipline holding storewide events at two per quarter, and channel-mix pressure partly offset by the e-commerce fee architecture. The principal margin risks to the back half are the residual outdoor clearance ($0.4 million), any slippage in the in-stock recovery that forces promotional traffic-driving, and holiday competitive promotional intensity, which we will read in real time through the November events.

### 10.2 Inventory Position

| $ millions | Q2 FY2026 | Q2 FY2025 | Plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ending inventory | 126.7 | 114.9 | 119.0 |
| Aged >12 months, % of total | 6.1% | 4.3% | ≤4.5% |
| Trailing 12-month turns | 2.2x | 2.4x | 2.4x |

Ending inventory of $126.7 million is 10.3 percent above prior year and $7.7 million above plan. The overage decomposes into three parts: $2.1 million of outdoor clearance covered above; approximately $4.8 million of catch-up receipts and in-transit inventory deliberately pulled forward to rebuild in-stock after the DC disruption; and roughly $0.8 million of general drift. The catch-up receipts are a feature, not a bug — they are what funds the in-stock recovery — but they must be worked down.

The correction plan cuts third-quarter receipts by $6.0 million against original plan, concentrated in categories where in-stock has already recovered, and returns total inventory to plan by the November 1 holiday floor set. Open-to-buy is now reviewed weekly by Lena Ostrowski and FP&A rather than monthly, a cadence that continues through year end. Aged inventory at 6.1 percent is above standard almost entirely on the outdoor residual and clears with it. We flag for the sponsor that the inventory build carries a working-capital cost of roughly $0.2 million in incremental interest at the revolver rate for the period it persists; the receipt cuts recover the position before the seasonal borrowing peak.

### 11.3 What the Back Half Requires

The back half of fiscal 2026 does not require heroics; it requires the disruptions of the second quarter to stay fixed and the decisions now on the table to be made on schedule. The holiday season is the company's largest volume period, and every priority above is sequenced to be resolved before holiday receipt flow begins in earnest in October. A distribution center at standard, an inventory position at plan, an e-commerce channel that no longer loses money on growth, and a store organization with settled leadership constitute the entire operating agenda.

Two decisions deserve executive-session time before the October planning cycle. First, capital allocation: the fiscal 2027 remodel tranche ($8.6 million), Georgetown automation completion, and the e-commerce platform work will compete for a constrained capital budget, and the remodel program's demonstrated returns argue for a rigorous, ranked comparison rather than incremental approvals. Second, the Appalachian region's structure: Charleston is the first of four lease decisions in that region over the next two fiscal years, and the executive team should decide whether the smaller-format model is the region's future before the fiscal 2027 plan is built.

For Devon Marsh and the sponsor board, our summary judgment is this: the second quarter's $2.8 million EBITDA shortfall versus prior year is fully explained by three identified events, of which the distribution center disruption and the outdoor markdown are behind us and the e-commerce contribution problem has a funded, dated remediation plan. The underlying franchise indicators — regular-price selling, remodel returns, Bluegrass margin, Northern comps, and store payroll discipline — are intact or improving. FP&A will issue a monthly one-page tracker against the six priorities beginning September 5, and the third-quarter report will reconcile actual recovery against every commitment made in this document.
