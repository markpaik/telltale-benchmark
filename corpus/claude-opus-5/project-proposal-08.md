# Palm Grove Inn Acquisition and Conversion

## A Proposal to the Board of Directors of Vista Alta Community Development Corporation

**Submitted by:** Ines Barrantes, Executive Director
**Prepared with:** Trevor Lindgren, Director of Real Estate; Marcus Delahoussaye, Director of Supportive Services; Priya Raghunathan, Controller
**Date:** September 24, 2026
**For action at:** Regular meeting of the Board of Directors, October 9, 2026

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Vista Alta Community Development Corporation asks the Board of Directors to authorize the acquisition of the Palm Grove Inn at 2140 East Highland Avenue in San Bernardino and its conversion into 88 units of permanent supportive housing for people exiting homelessness. Total development cost is $19,400,000. The financing plan combines an $8,600,000 award from the San Bernardino County Homeless Housing Trust, $4,200,000 in equity from the sale of 4 percent low-income housing tax credits, a $3,500,000 construction loan from Inland Pacific Bank that converts to a permanent loan at stabilization, $1,900,000 in federal Community Development Block Grant funds committed by the City of San Bernardino, and $1,200,000 from Vista Alta's unrestricted reserve.

The decision cannot wait. The county award carries an expenditure deadline of March 31, 2027, after which the funds return to the county pool and are redirected to competing applicants. The purchase agreement Trevor Lindgren negotiated with the seller requires a nonrefundable deposit of $600,000 at signing and a closing on or before January 15, 2027. If the board does not authorize the transaction on October 9, the agreement lapses by its own terms and the county award is redirected. There is no version of this project that survives a deferral to the December meeting.

We do not present this as a low-risk proposal. It is not. The $1,200,000 sponsor contribution represents a substantial share of an unrestricted reserve that currently stands at 41 days of cash. The $600,000 deposit is at risk from the moment the agreement is signed. The supportive services model carries an annual cost of $1,400,000 against $780,000 in reasonably assured Medi-Cal reimbursement, leaving $620,000 per year with no committed source beyond the second year of operations. Our most recent major rehabilitation, Loma Verde in 2023, finished eleven months late and $1,800,000 over budget, and the board is entitled to weigh that history heavily. The council district representative has convened two town halls at which neighbors spoke in opposition.

What follows sets out the site, the population, the budget, the schedule, the services model and staffing, the alternatives we examined and rejected, the risks and what we propose to do about them, and the specific authorizations we request. We have tried to write it so that a director who votes no can point to the paragraph that persuaded her.

## 2. Why This, Why Now

San Bernardino County's 2026 point-in-time count identified 4,195 people experiencing homelessness, of whom 2,880 were unsheltered. The county's Continuum of Care has 1,340 permanent supportive housing units in its inventory. The arithmetic is not subtle. Every serious analysis of the gap — the county's own 2025 Housing Needs Assessment, the Inland Empire Regional Homelessness Collaborative's three-year plan, our own waiting list — converges on the same conclusion: the region needs roughly 2,000 additional permanent supportive housing units, and it needs them faster than ground-up construction can deliver them.

Adaptive reuse of hotels and motels is the only production method that has demonstrated the ability to deliver units at that pace. Statewide, Homekey and its successor programs have converted more than 15,000 rooms since 2020, at a median cost per unit roughly 40 percent below new construction and a median timeline roughly 18 months shorter. The Palm Grove Inn is a credible candidate: it is structurally sound, it sits on a commercial corridor with transit and services, and it is available at a price that pencils.

The urgency is externally imposed. The county's award letter, dated May 14, 2026, conditions the $8,600,000 on the recipient having site control and a closed acquisition by March 31, 2027, with construction commencement within 120 days of closing. County staff have told us plainly that no extension will be granted; the county is itself operating under a state expenditure deadline and has three backup applicants ranked below us. The seller, a family partnership that has operated the Palm Grove for 31 years, has an offer from a competing hospitality buyer and has agreed to hold the property for us only through October 20, 2026.

That is why this proposal comes to the October meeting rather than to December, and why it comes with a request for authorization rather than a request for further study.

## 3. The Site

The Palm Grove Inn occupies 2.4 acres at 2140 East Highland Avenue, in the eastern portion of the City of San Bernardino, roughly 1.2 miles from the Highland Avenue commercial node and 0.4 miles from the nearest Omnitrans Route 3 stop. The property comprises two three-story buildings of Type V wood-frame construction over slab, built in 1987 and substantially reroofed in 2014, with 96 guest rooms, a former restaurant and lobby structure of 4,200 square feet, an outdoor pool, and 118 surface parking spaces.

Room configuration is favorable. Eighty-two rooms are standard king or double-queen units of approximately 320 square feet with a full bathroom, a wet bar counter, and a window wall; fourteen are "suite" configurations of approximately 480 square feet with a separate sitting area. The wet bar counters, which include a sink and a dedicated 20-amp circuit, materially reduce the cost of converting each room into a studio apartment with a kitchenette. Our architect, Belmonte Hurtado Architects, estimates that 76 percent of the required kitchenette plumbing can be tied into existing stacks.

The conversion program yields 88 residential units rather than 96 because eight ground-floor rooms in the north building will be combined and reconfigured into a community room, a computer and workforce area, and two accessible units meeting Uniform Federal Accessibility Standards requirements. Of the 88 units, 79 will be studios of 320 to 350 square feet and 9 will be one-bedroom units of 480 square feet created from the existing suites. All units will have a private full bathroom, a kitchenette with a two-burner cooktop, an under-counter refrigerator, a microwave, and a sink, and individual through-wall heating and cooling units replaced as part of the scope.

The former restaurant building converts to program space: a 1,400 square foot dining and community room, offices for six case managers and the property manager, a health suite for the part-time nurse and visiting providers, a laundry room, and the 24-hour front desk. The pool will be filled and the area converted to a shaded courtyard with seating and raised planting beds, a change the seller has already priced into the transaction.

**Physical condition.** Our Phase I environmental site assessment, completed August 8, 2026, identified no recognized environmental conditions requiring further investigation. Asbestos and lead surveys found asbestos-containing floor tile mastic in 41 rooms and lead-based paint on exterior door frames in the south building; abatement is scoped and budgeted at $310,000. The property condition assessment by Kessler Engineering rates the roof at eight to ten years of remaining life, the electrical service as adequate at 1,200 amps but requiring panel replacement in both buildings, and the domestic water piping as galvanized and at end of life. Full repiping is in the scope at $840,000. The fire sprinkler system is present throughout and requires head replacement and a new riser, budgeted at $265,000.

**Zoning and entitlements.** The parcel is zoned CG-1, Commercial General, which permits hotels by right and permits residential uses above the ground floor by conditional use permit. However, Assembly Bill 2011 and the 2023 amendments to Government Code section 65913.4 provide a ministerial approval pathway for 100 percent affordable residential projects on commercially zoned parcels meeting objective standards. Our land use counsel, Ondrejka & Whitfield, has opined in a memorandum dated August 27, 2026, that the project qualifies for ministerial streamlined approval, which means no conditional use permit, no discretionary hearing, and no California Environmental Quality Act review. City planning staff have confirmed this reading in writing. We discuss below what this does and does not do about neighborhood opposition.

## 4. Who Would Live Here

All 88 units serve households earning at or below 30 percent of area median income, with rents set at 30 percent of tenant income and the difference covered by project-based vouchers. The San Bernardino County Housing Authority has issued a commitment letter for 88 project-based vouchers contingent on closing, dated June 30, 2026.

Referrals come exclusively through the county's Coordinated Entry System. Based on the current CES queue, we project the resident population as follows:

- **Chronically homeless single adults: approximately 70 units.** Median age 52. Roughly 60 percent report a disabling condition involving serious mental illness, 45 percent report a substance use disorder, and a substantial share report both. Median length of homelessness at entry exceeds three years.
- **Older adults aged 62 and over: approximately 14 units.** This group is the fastest-growing segment of the county's homeless population and the one for which the Palm Grove's elevator-served, single-level-unit configuration is best suited.
- **Households with a disabled adult and no minor children: approximately 4 units,** including the two UFAS units.

The building will not serve families with minor children. This is a deliberate design decision, not an exclusion policy: studio units of 320 square feet cannot accommodate families under our own habitability standards, and the site lacks the outdoor play area and proximity to schools that family housing requires. Applicants with children referred through CES will be redirected to family-appropriate inventory, including our own Alameda Court and Rialto Family Housing properties.

Tenancy is governed by a standard California residential lease with no program participation requirement. Residents are tenants with full tenant rights. Participation in case management, health services, and community programming is voluntary and repeatedly offered but never a condition of tenancy. This is the Housing First standard required by both the county award and the state Medi-Cal pathway, and it is also, in our experience across 340 existing units, the model that produces the highest retention.

### 5.3 The Reserve Exposure

This is the part of the proposal the board should read twice.

Vista Alta's unrestricted operating reserve stood at $1,847,000 as of the August 31, 2026 financial statements, against an annualized unrestricted operating expense base of $16,430,000. That is 41 days of cash. Our board-adopted reserve policy sets a target of 90 days and a floor of 30 days, with any drawdown below 45 days requiring affirmative board action.

The proposed $1,200,000 sponsor contribution would be deployed in two tranches:

- **$600,000 at execution of the purchase agreement,** as a nonrefundable deposit. This money is at risk from the day it is wired. If the transaction fails for any reason other than seller default — including failure to secure bond allocation, failure of bank credit approval, or an adverse Part 58 determination — the deposit is lost.
- **$600,000 at closing,** as the balance of the sponsor equity contribution.

Post-deposit, the reserve drops to $1,247,000, or 28 days of cash. Post-closing, it drops to $647,000, or 14 days of cash.

Fourteen days of cash is not a sustainable position for an organization with 62 employees and an $11.4 million operating budget, and we do not propose to occupy it for long. Three mechanisms restore the reserve:

1. **Developer fee recovery.** Of the $980,000 developer fee, $480,000 is payable in cash at draws during construction, with the first $250,000 payable at closing from the tax credit equity's initial installment. This returns roughly $250,000 within days of closing, restoring the reserve to approximately 19 days, and a further $230,000 across the construction period, reaching approximately 29 days by construction completion. The deferred $500,000 is repaid from cash flow over the first eight to twelve years of operations.
2. **Asset management and property management fees.** Vista Alta's affiliate will earn approximately $88,000 annually in property management fees and $24,000 in partnership asset management fees beginning at lease-up, plus a one-time construction management fee of $180,000 already reflected in the budget.
3. **A line of credit.** We have negotiated with Inland Pacific Bank a $750,000 unsecured revolving line of credit at prime plus 2.0 percent, available for general operating purposes, with a term sheet issued September 10, 2026. Drawing on this line is not free and not intended as a plan, but it converts a 14-day cash position into a 30-day equivalent liquidity position, and its availability is a condition we propose to attach to the authorization.

We want to state the residual exposure without softening it. Between closing in January 2027 and the first substantial developer fee draw, Vista Alta will operate with less liquidity than at any point in its 22-year history. A significant unbudgeted event during that window — a major uninsured casualty at an existing property, a payroll tax assessment, the loss of a large contract — would force us to draw the line of credit immediately and could, in a severe case, require a board-authorized bridge from restricted funds with associated compliance consequences. The probability is low. The consequence is serious. The board should vote knowing both.

## 7. Services Model, Operations, and Staffing

### 7.1 Model

The services model follows the Housing First and harm reduction framework we operate at our Del Rosa and Arrowhead Commons properties. Its components:

**Twenty-four-hour front desk.** Staffed continuously by desk coordinators who manage access control, guest sign-in, package handling, wellness checks, and first response to incidents. In supportive housing this position is not security in the conventional sense; it is the relational spine of the building. Our data at Arrowhead Commons shows that residents disclose crises to desk staff before they disclose them to case managers roughly two-thirds of the time.

**Case management at a 1:20 ratio.** Four full-time case managers and one senior case manager carrying a reduced load of 8. The 1:20 ratio is the standard required by the county award and matches the ratio at which our existing properties achieve 91 percent twelve-month housing retention. Case management includes housing stabilization planning, benefits advocacy (SSI/SSDI, CalFresh, Medi-Cal enrollment), connection to primary and behavioral health care, crisis intervention, and eviction prevention.

**Part-time nursing.** A registered nurse at 20 hours per week providing health assessments, medication management support, chronic disease monitoring, wound care, and coordination with the county's Federally Qualified Health Center partner, Inland Community Health, which will hold twice-weekly clinic hours on site under a memorandum of understanding executed July 2026.

**Peer support and community programming.** One full-time peer support specialist with lived experience of homelessness, coordinating resident council, community meals, and a weekly programming calendar.

**Property management,** provided by Vista Alta's in-house management affiliate: one property manager, one assistant manager, and two maintenance technicians.

## 8. Alternatives Considered

### 8.1 New construction on the city-owned parcel at Baseline and Waterman

The City of San Bernardino has offered a 1.8-acre surplus parcel at Baseline Street and Waterman Avenue for $1 under the Surplus Land Act, with a preference for permanent supportive housing. The site supports approximately 74 units at achievable density.

We modeled it. Total development cost is $41,600,000, or $562,000 per unit — 2.6 times the per-unit cost of Palm Grove. The gap between our identified sources and that cost is roughly $23,000,000, which would require a competitive 9 percent tax credit award. The California Tax Credit Allocation Committee's Inland Empire region awarded 9 percent credits to 4 of 19 applicants in the 2026 rounds. Assuming a first-round win, which would be unusual, the timeline runs: site control and predevelopment through 2027, 9 percent application March 2028, award July 2028, closing March 2029, construction 22 months, occupancy early 2031.

Palm Grove delivers 88 units in May 2028. Baseline and Waterman delivers 74 units in early 2031 at best, with a realistic probability of award below 40 percent in any given round. The county's $8,600,000 cannot be held for it. We recommend the city parcel remain in our pipeline as a 2028 application — it is a good site and we intend to pursue it — but it is not an alternative to this decision.

### 8.2 Acquisition without conversion: interim housing

We examined acquiring the Palm Grove and operating it as non-congregate interim shelter, deferring conversion. This eliminates the construction risk and shortens time-to-occupancy to roughly 90 days. It also eliminates the tax credit equity ($4,200,000), because there are no qualified residential units; it makes the CDBG award ineligible; and it converts the county's capital award into an operating expenditure that county staff have confirmed is not permitted under the award terms. The financing simply does not assemble. Additionally, interim shelter operating costs run $28,000 to $34,000 per bed annually with no rental revenue, an ongoing obligation of roughly $2,700,000 per year against no committed source.

### 8.3 A smaller conversion scope

We examined a reduced scope: defer the repipe, the elevator modernization, and the community building conversion, saving approximately $1,690,000 and eliminating the sponsor equity requirement entirely. The repipe cannot be deferred; galvanized piping at end of life in a building with 88 kitchenettes added to it is a predictable catastrophic failure and our investor's construction consultant flagged it independently. Elevator modernization is required for accessibility compliance given the population's mobility profile. The community building conversion could theoretically be deferred, saving $585,000, but it houses the front desk, the case management offices, and the health suite — the entire services model. Deferring it means the services model does not exist at occupancy. We rejected this alternative.

### 8.4 Passing on the site

The board can decline. The consequences are specific and worth stating plainly.

The $8,600,000 county award is redirected to the next-ranked applicant. It does not return to us. The $1,900,000 CDBG appropriation lapses at the end of the city's program year. Our tax credit investor relationship, our bank relationship, and our standing with county housing staff are all diminished but not destroyed; these are professional relationships that survive a declined transaction, though they do not survive many. The Palm Grove is sold to a hospitality operator and continues as a motel. Eighty-eight units of permanent supportive housing that would have existed do not exist, and the roughly 88 households who would have occupied them remain in the CES queue, where median wait time currently exceeds 19 months.

Vista Alta preserves 41 days of cash and takes no development risk in 2027. That is a real benefit and the board should weigh it. Our judgment is that it does not outweigh the rest, but it is a judgment, not a calculation.

## 9. Risks and Mitigations

### 9.1 Cost overrun and schedule delay — the Loma Verde problem

Treasurer Liu is right to raise Loma Verde, and we would raise it ourselves if she did not. The 2023 rehabilitation of Loma Verde Apartments finished eleven months late and $1,800,000 over budget. The causes, documented in the post-project review the board received in March 2024, were: (1) a fixed-price contract awarded to a contractor with no prior experience in occupied rehabilitation, who submitted 41 change orders; (2) unforeseen structural conditions discovered after demolition that a more thorough pre-construction investigation would have identified; (3) a 5 percent contingency that was exhausted in month four; and (4) resident relocation complexity in an occupied building.

What is different here:

- **The building is vacant at closing.** The seller will deliver the property with no occupants other than approximately 14 extended-stay guests, for whom we have budgeted $75,000 in relocation assistance and whom the seller is contractually obligated to relocate before close. There is no occupied rehabilitation. This alone removes the single largest source of Loma Verde's delay.
- **Contingency is 10 percent of hard costs, not 5 percent.** $946,000 in hard cost contingency plus $261,000 in soft cost contingency, totaling $1,207,000, or 6.2 percent of total development cost.
- **The contractor has done this before.** Verrado Construction Group has completed four Inland Empire motel conversions, including the 62-unit Casa Ramona conversion in Colton delivered on schedule in 2025. We have checked all four references.
- **Pre-construction investigation is more thorough.** We have completed destructive testing at eight locations, opened walls in six rooms, scoped the sanitary and storm lines by camera, and load-tested the electrical service. Kessler Engineering's report runs 214 pages.
- **The contract structure is different.** Guaranteed maximum price with a shared savings provision, an owner-controlled contingency requiring our written approval for any draw, liquidated damages of $2,400 per day beyond substantial completion, and a payment and performance bond at 100 percent of contract value.
- **Governance is different.** We propose a standing Development Oversight Committee of three directors meeting monthly during construction, receiving a written report from Lindgren covering budget-to-date, contingency drawn, schedule variance, and change order log. Any single change order exceeding $75,000, or cumulative change orders exceeding $400,000, requires committee approval. Any draw on contingency beyond $650,000 requires full board notification within five business days.

If cost overruns exceed contingency, the sources of last resort are, in order: (1) additional deferred developer fee, up to the remaining $480,000 of cash fee; (2) an increase in the bank construction loan, for which Inland Pacific has indicated capacity of up to $350,000 subject to appraisal; (3) scope reduction in the community building and site work, worth up to $400,000; and (4) a further draw on Vista Alta reserves, which we would bring to the board as a separate action. Tiers one through three total $1,230,000 beyond contingency, giving a combined overrun tolerance of approximately $2,437,000, or 12.6 percent of total development cost. Loma Verde's overrun was 14 percent of a $12,900,000 budget.

### 9.2 Entitlement and neighborhood opposition

The council district representative has held two town halls at which neighbors spoke in opposition. The stated concerns are property values, public safety, and the concentration of social services in the district. We take them seriously as a political matter and as a community relations matter. As a legal matter, the project's ministerial approval pathway under AB 2011 means there is no discretionary approval to oppose: no conditional use permit, no planning commission hearing, no council vote on the entitlement, no CEQA document to challenge. Our land use counsel considers the risk of a successful legal challenge to be low.

Two vulnerabilities remain. First, the CDBG Part 58 environmental review has a 15-day public comment period and a subsequent 15-day HUD objection period during which a party may file an objection on specified grounds. An objection would not likely succeed but could delay the Authority to Use Grant Funds by 30 to 60 days, which would compress our closing timeline. Mitigation: we have asked the city to publish the notice the week of October 12, creating a 60-day buffer before the January 15 closing, and we have prepared the response documentation in advance. If the $1,900,000 CDBG cannot close by January 15, our fallback is a short-term bridge from Inland Pacific, which has indicated willingness at prime plus 2.5 percent for up to 120 days at a cost of approximately $52,000.

Second, the City Council could attempt to rescind the CDBG appropriation. The June vote was 5–2. Rescission would require a new agenda item and a majority; city staff advise that funds already committed by council action and moving through Part 58 are practically difficult to rescind, and that any rescission would trigger HUD program review. We rate this as unlikely but not negligible.

Our community engagement plan, which begins the week following board authorization regardless of the entitlement posture: a Good Neighbor Agreement offered to the two adjacent property owners' associations covering exterior lighting, landscape maintenance standards, a 24-hour community contact line answered by the front desk, and a quarterly community meeting; a Community Advisory Committee including two neighborhood representatives, a council district staff member, and the San Bernardino Police Department's area lieutenant; individual briefings with the eleven businesses on the Highland Avenue frontage; and a standing offer to any neighbor to tour our Arrowhead Commons property, which sits in a comparable neighborhood and has generated three police calls for service in the past twelve months.

### 9.3 Services funding beyond year two

This is the risk we can least mitigate before October 9, and we propose to manage it through board-imposed discipline rather than assurances.

We recommend the board condition its authorization on the following, and we recommend it because we think it is right, not because we think it is easy:

- **A written services sustainability plan** presented to the board no later than the June 2027 meeting, identifying committed and probable sources for years three through five with named funders, amounts, and decision dates.
- **A trigger and review point at month 30 of operations** (approximately November 2030), at which, if committed services funding for the following twelve months is below $1,100,000, management will present the board with a reduced services model and its expected effect on retention and on our county contract compliance.
- **Quarterly reporting** to the Finance Committee on Medi-Cal Community Supports enrollment, authorization, and realization rates beginning at lease-up.
- **A prohibition on cross-subsidy from Vista Alta's unrestricted operating funds** to the Palm Grove services budget in excess of $100,000 in any fiscal year without prior board approval.

The last condition matters most. The failure mode for supportive housing sponsors is not usually the building; it is the slow absorption of an unfunded services obligation into the parent organization's general fund until the parent organization is impaired. We would rather the board bind us now.

### 9.4 Financing risks

**Bond allocation.** CDLAC demand for 4 percent bond allocation in the Inland Empire has exceeded supply in recent rounds, and allocation is scored. Our project scores well on cost containment, readiness, and homeless population served. Failure to receive January allocation would push closing past the county's March 31 deadline. Mitigation: we have confirmed with county staff that a fully executed acquisition prior to March 31 satisfies the deadline even if the tax credit closing follows; we would structure a bridge acquisition using the county award, the CDBG, the bank loan, and the sponsor equity, closing the tax credit transaction in a second closing by June 2027. Our counsel has structured this before. It adds approximately $85,000 in cost.

**Equity pricing.** A decline from $0.87 to $0.79 per credit dollar costs $386,000. Mitigation: additional deferred developer fee (up to $480,000 available), which is the standard adjustment and does not require additional board action beyond the authorization requested.

**Interest rates.** The permanent loan rate is estimated at 6.40 percent and is not locked until conversion. Each 50 basis points of increase reduces supportable debt by approximately $270,000. Mitigation: the pro forma is stress-tested to 7.40 percent, at which the permanent loan sizes to approximately $2,960,000, creating a $540,000 gap fillable through deferred fee and a request to the county for a portion of its award to convert to a residual receipts permanent note — a conversion county staff have indicated they would consider.

### 9.5 Lease-up and operations

Lease-up of supportive housing depends on the Coordinated Entry System producing referrals and on Housing Authority inspections keeping pace. Our experience at Del Rosa was a 7.5-month lease-up against a 5-month projection. We have modeled a 7-month lease-up here and capitalized a six-month operating reserve. Mitigation includes a lease-up coordination agreement with the county CES administrator executed at closing, a pre-referral pipeline built during the final construction quarter, and Housing Authority agreement to batch-inspect in groups of 20 units.

## 11. Closing Note

Two years from this October, if the board votes yes and everything works, 88 people who are sleeping outdoors in San Bernardino tonight will have their own front door, their own bathroom, a nurse down the hall twice a week, and a lease that says they can stay. It costs $19,400,000 and it puts $1,200,000 of this organization's money at risk in a way that will make the spring of 2027 uncomfortable for everyone in the finance office.

We think the risk is well understood, adequately bounded, and worth taking. We recognize that the board may weigh Loma Verde, the reserve position, or the services gap differently than we do, and that a reasoned no is available. What is not available is a delay. We ask for the board's authorization on October 9.
