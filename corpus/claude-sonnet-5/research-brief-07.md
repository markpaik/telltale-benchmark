# Does Attendance Mentoring Move the Needle? A Two-Year Look at the Ninth-Grade Program

**Prepared by the Office of Research and Evaluation, Monocacy Valley Public Schools**
**Imani Fitzgerald, Research Analyst, with Andre Beauchamp (Student Services) and Rosa Iglesias (Mentor Program)**
**July 2026 Board Session — Prepared at the direction of Superintendent Terrence Blackwell, in response to a question from Board Member Lorraine Hovde**

---

## The Question and What We Found

At the April board meeting, Lorraine Hovde asked a direct question: does the attendance mentoring program actually work? The program in question placed 22 part-time attendance mentors in four Monocacy Valley high schools beginning in fall 2024, funded by a $1.9 million state grant that expires in June 2027. Mentors were assigned to incoming ninth graders who had been chronically absent — missing 10 percent or more of school days — during eighth grade. The board must decide in July whether to absorb the mentor positions into the local budget once the grant ends, and Superintendent Blackwell asked this office to provide evidence before that vote.

The short answer is: probably yes, for some students, and the honest answer is more complicated than either a clean success story or a clean failure. Mentored ninth graders improved their attendance by 4.3 percentage points on average between 2024-25 and 2025-26, compared with 2.6 points for a comparison group of students who met the same eligibility criteria but were not assigned a mentor — a 1.7 point gap favoring the mentored group. But that headline gap conceals sharp variation. Nearly the entire advantage is concentrated among students whose prior-year absence rate was in the 10-to-19 percent range; among the most severely absent students, those above 30 percent, mentored and non-mentored students improved by almost the same amount. One of the four schools did not begin assigning mentors until November, and its results look markedly weaker. English learners, who make up 18 percent of the mentored group, showed no measurable difference from their comparison-group peers. And a change in how the district codes period-level attendance, adopted in August 2025, adds roughly 1.5 points to measured attendance districtwide, which means a meaningful share of the "improvement" in both groups is a measurement artifact rather than a behavioral change.

None of this means the program has failed. It means the evidence supports a narrower, more conditional claim than "the program works": mentoring appears to help moderately-at-risk ninth graders, delivered at full dosage, more than it helps the most severely disengaged students or students served for only part of the year. The sections below walk through the data, the methods, the results at each level of detail the board asked for, and what a defensible July decision might look like given what we know and don't know.

---

## Background

Monocacy Valley Public Schools enrolls approximately 11,400 students across 17 schools in and around Frederick. In 2024, the district used a $1.9 million state grant — set to expire in June 2027 — to hire 22 part-time attendance mentors. Mentors were placed in four comprehensive high schools and assigned specifically to incoming ninth graders identified as chronically absent during their eighth-grade year, on the theory that the transition to high school is a high-leverage moment to intervene before absence patterns harden.

The mentors' role, as described by program coordinator Rosa Iglesias, combines regular check-ins, coordination with families, and referrals to school-based support services. Caseloads vary by school and by mentor availability; not every eligible student could be served, which is itself important context for how the comparison group came to exist (see below).

The program has now completed two school years, 2024-25 and 2025-26, which is the first point at which a before-and-after comparison is possible for any single cohort of mentored ninth graders. With the grant expiring in June 2027, the board must decide this July whether to fund the mentor positions locally going forward, phase them out, or seek an extension or alternative grant. That decision is what this brief is meant to inform.

## Data and Methods

The analysis draws on attendance records maintained in the district's student information system for the 2024-25 and 2025-26 school years, verified by the Office of Student Services under Andre Beauchamp. Program rosters identifying which students were assigned a mentor, and in which school and starting month, were supplied by Rosa Iglesias's office.

The sample includes two groups of ninth graders at the four high schools with mentor programs:

- **Mentored group (n = 612):** Students who met the district's chronic absenteeism threshold (10 percent or more of enrolled days absent) in eighth grade and were subsequently assigned an attendance mentor in ninth grade.
- **Comparison group (n = 1,180):** Students who met the identical eighth-grade chronic absenteeism threshold at the same four high schools but were not assigned a mentor, generally because mentor caseloads were full.

For each student, we calculated an attendance rate for the eighth-grade year (2024-25, prior to any mentoring) and an attendance rate for ninth grade (2025-26, the year mentoring occurred). The core outcome measure is each student's change in attendance rate — the percentage-point difference between the two years. This is a "gain score" or difference-in-differences approach: rather than simply comparing how mentored students ended up relative to non-mentored students, we compare how much each group *changed*, since both groups started from a similarly elevated absence baseline by design.

We also disaggregated results by three factors the board specifically asked about: the severity of a student's prior-year absence, the high school attended (and, relatedly, when mentoring began at that school), and student group membership, with particular attention to English learners given their share of the mentored population. Ninety-four students changed schools mid-year during 2025-26; their attendance records were retained where a full-year rate could be reconstructed across schools, but this introduces some noise discussed further under Limitations.

## The August 2025 Coding Change

In August 2025 — partway through the observation window for this study — the district changed how period-level attendance is coded, primarily affecting how partial-day absences and excused period skips are tallied into a daily attendance figure. Based on early comparisons run by Student Services, this change raised measured attendance rates districtwide by approximately 1.5 percentage points, independent of any actual change in student behavior. In effect, a student attending exactly as often as they did the year before would now show up as having "improved" by about 1.5 points simply because the district is counting differently.

This matters directly for this study because the 2024-25 baseline year was measured under the old coding rule and the 2025-26 outcome year was measured under the new one. Both the mentored group and the comparison group are subject to this same before-and-after coding shift, since both are drawn from the same four schools and the same two years. If the coding change affected all students in the same way regardless of mentoring status, it would inflate the *absolute* gain figures for both groups by a similar amount but leave the *difference between groups* — the 1.7-point gap — largely intact, because the artifact would cancel out in the comparison.

We flag two reasons this reassurance is only partial. First, we have no way to confirm from the available data that the coding change affected mentored and non-mentored students identically. If, for example, the four high schools' course-scheduling structures interact with the new period-coding rule differently than schedules at the comparison group's home schools — recall the comparison group is drawn from the same four schools, so this risk is smaller than it would otherwise be, but attendance-coding effects can still vary by grade-level schedule, block structure, or the specific courses a student is enrolled in — some of the apparent gap could still reflect measurement rather than mentoring. Second, and more simply, the board should not read the headline figures of "4.3 points" and "2.6 points" of improvement as entirely reflecting real behavioral change. A meaningful share of both numbers — on the order of a third to a half of the mentored group's gain — is very likely attributable to the coding change rather than to anything happening in students' actual attendance patterns. The relative comparison between groups is more trustworthy than the absolute size of either group's gain.

---

## How the Comparison Group Was Formed, and What That Does to Interpretation

This is the single most important methodological issue in the study, and it deserves to be stated plainly: **students were not randomly assigned to mentoring or non-mentoring.** Both groups were selected using the same criterion — chronic absenteeism in eighth grade — which means both groups were, by construction, drawn from the tail of the attendance distribution. Students assigned a mentor were the ones for whom mentor capacity happened to be available; students who did not receive a mentor were, as far as we can determine from the rosters, primarily those for whom the 22 available mentors' caseloads were already full, not students screened out for any programmatic reason.

Why does this matter? Any group of students selected because they had unusually bad attendance in one year will tend to look somewhat better the following year even with no intervention at all, a statistical phenomenon known as regression to the mean. A single bad year of attendance reflects some combination of a genuinely worsening pattern and simple year-to-year noise — a family crisis, an illness, a scheduling problem — and noise tends not to repeat. This is precisely why the study uses a comparison group rather than simply reporting the mentored group's improvement on its own: the comparison group, selected under the identical criterion, should experience roughly the same amount of natural regression to the mean, and comparing the two groups' gains should net that effect out, leaving something closer to the mentoring program's actual contribution.

That logic holds only if assignment to mentoring versus non-mentoring is unrelated to anything else that predicts how a student's attendance would trend regardless of mentoring — family stability, engagement with school staff, disciplinary history, housing situation, and so on. We do not have evidence that mentor assignment was based on such factors; Rosa Iglesias's office describes assignment as driven mainly by caseload capacity and school-level rostering logistics rather than a deliberate selection of "easier" or "harder" cases. But we also cannot rule out more subtle version of this problem — for instance, mentors and school staff may have identified students to prioritize for the limited caseload slots based on informal judgments about which students seemed likely to respond, and those judgments could correlate with real attendance trajectories in ways the data cannot detect after the fact.

In short: the comparison-group design is a real improvement over simply tracking mentored students' attendance year over year, and it substantially reduces (though does not eliminate) the regression-to-the-mean problem. The 1.7-point gap between groups should be read as our best available estimate of the program's differential effect, not as a rigorously isolated causal effect in the way a randomized trial would provide. The board should weigh the results accordingly — as suggestive and directionally useful, not as definitive proof of impact.

## Results: Overall

Averaged across all four schools and all severity levels, mentored ninth graders improved their attendance rate by 4.3 percentage points from eighth to ninth grade; the comparison group improved by 2.6 points. The 1.7-point difference favors the mentored group and is the headline figure the board has been waiting for. Recall, however, two qualifications already discussed: a portion of both figures (very roughly 1.5 points) likely reflects the August 2025 coding change rather than real behavioral change, and the comparison itself rests on a non-randomized design. Even accepting those caveats, a 1.7-point relative advantage, sustained across a full school year and nearly 1,800 students combined, is not trivial — but as the next two sections show, this district-wide average obscures very different stories depending on how absent a student was to begin with and which school, and when, they were served.

---

## Results by Prior-Year Absence Level

The board asked specifically whether the program's effect differs by how absent a student was before entering the program. It does, substantially. The table below shows the average gain in attendance rate for both groups, broken into three bands based on eighth-grade absence rate.

| Prior-year absence band | Mentored (n) | Mentored gain | Comparison (n) | Comparison gain | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10–19% | 290 | +6.2 pts | 540 | +3.7 pts | +2.5 pts |
| 20–29% | 224 | +3.2 pts | 430 | +2.0 pts | +1.2 pts |
| 30% or higher | 98 | +1.1 pts | 210 | +1.0 pts | +0.1 pts |
| **Overall** | **612** | **+4.3 pts** | **1,180** | **+2.6 pts** | **+1.7 pts** |

*Totals may not sum exactly due to rounding.*

The pattern is striking and worth sitting with. Among students who were moderately chronically absent (10 to 19 percent of days missed), mentored students improved more than a full attendance-rate category more than their comparison-group peers — a 2.5-point gap. Among students who were severely absent (30 percent or more of days missed, meaning they were missing roughly six weeks or more of school), the mentoring program produced essentially no detectable advantage: both groups improved by about a point, a difference well within what ordinary year-to-year noise would produce.

One might expect the opposite pattern if regression to the mean were driving these results, since regression to the mean typically predicts that the most extreme prior-year scores — here, the 30-percent-plus group — should show the largest natural rebound the following year, mentored or not. That is not what we see. Instead, the smallest gains appear in the most severe band, for both groups alike. This suggests the flat result for severely absent students is not simply an artifact of the comparison design; it more likely reflects that severe chronic absence is often tied to conditions — housing instability, transportation barriers, mental health needs, employment obligations — that a part-time mentor's check-ins and referrals are not well positioned to resolve within a single school year. The program appears to be doing real work for the moderately-at-risk group and comparatively little for the most disengaged one.

## Results by School

The four high schools with mentors did not all start the program at the same time. Three began assigning mentors at the start of the 2025-26 school year; the fourth, referred to here by school for clarity as Carroll Creek High School, did not begin assigning mentors until November 2025, roughly two months into the school year, because of a hiring delay. Results by school track this timing closely.

| School | Mentored (n) | Mentored gain | Comparison (n) | Comparison gain | Difference | Mentoring start |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ballenger Creek HS | 180 | +4.8 pts | 310 | +2.8 pts | +2.0 pts | August |
| Monocacy River HS | 165 | +4.6 pts | 295 | +2.7 pts | +1.9 pts | August |
| Catoctin Ridge HS | 150 | +4.2 pts | 290 | +2.5 pts | +1.6 pts | August |
| Carroll Creek HS | 117 | +2.9 pts | 285 | +2.3 pts | +0.6 pts | November |

*Totals may not sum exactly due to rounding.*

The three schools that started mentoring in August show a consistent, meaningful advantage for mentored students, ranging from 1.6 to 2.0 points. Carroll Creek, which lost roughly two months of the school year before mentors were in place, shows a much smaller advantage — 0.6 points — that is close to the margin one would expect from noise alone given the sample size. This is consistent with a genuine dose-response relationship: students who received a fuller year of mentoring showed a larger benefit than those who received a partial year. It also means the district-wide average understates what the program can do when implemented on schedule and somewhat overstates what should be expected if similar late-start delays recur.

## Results by Student Group

The board's question about "whether the program works" reasonably includes whether it works for all the students it serves, not only on average. Two findings stand out.

**English learners.** English learners make up 18 percent of the mentored group (110 students) and a comparable share of the comparison pool. Mentored English learners improved by 2.5 points, compared with 2.4 points for English learners in the comparison group — a gap of roughly 0.1 points, which is not a meaningfully measurable difference given the sample size involved. By contrast, non-English-learner mentored students (502 students) improved by 4.7 points against a comparison gain of roughly 2.65 points among non-English-learner comparison students, a substantially larger gap of about 2.1 points. In short, the overall 1.7-point program advantage is being generated almost entirely by non-English-learner students; the program does not show a detectable benefit for English learners as currently designed and delivered. This may reflect language or communication barriers in how mentoring is delivered, staffing that does not include bilingual mentors in all cases, or eligibility and referral processes that connect English learner families to different or additional supports outside the mentor structure. We do not have data in this study to distinguish between these explanations, but the pattern itself is clear enough to flag for program design purposes regardless of the July funding decision.

**Student mobility.** Ninety-four students in the combined sample changed schools during the 2025-26 school year. For mentored students who moved, continuity of mentoring was often disrupted, since mentors are assigned by school rather than following a student to a new placement; for comparison-group students who moved, attendance coding practices are not perfectly uniform across receiving schools even under the new districtwide rule, which introduces some additional noise into their year-over-year comparison. We retained these students in the analysis where a full-year attendance rate could be reconstructed, but we did not have sufficient sample size to analyze movers as a separate subgroup with any precision. Their inclusion is unlikely to have driven the overall results, given that 94 students represent about 5 percent of the combined sample, but it is a source of measurement noise worth naming rather than ignoring.

## Limitations

Several limitations bear directly on how much weight the board should place on these results, beyond those already discussed above.

First, this is an observational comparison, not a randomized evaluation. Assignment to mentoring was governed by caseload capacity rather than by lottery, and while we have no evidence of deliberate selection on likely outcomes, we cannot fully rule it out. A future cohort in which mentor capacity is expanded and assignment is made by lottery among eligible students, even a partial lottery limited to whichever students exceed available slots, would substantially strengthen the evidence available for the next funding decision.

Second, the August 2025 coding change overlaps with the outcome year for this entire study. We believe its effect on the group-level *difference* is limited, but we cannot fully verify that the change affected mentored and non-mentored students identically, and the absolute size of both groups' improvement should be discounted accordingly.

Third, the sample is limited to four high schools and two school years, all within one district's implementation of this specific mentoring model. The findings should not be assumed to generalize to a different grade level, a different absence threshold, or a different mentoring caseload ratio than the one in place here.

Fourth, uneven implementation timing at Carroll Creek High School means the district-wide results combine a near-full dose of the program with a substantially reduced one. This limits our ability to state a single, clean estimate of "the" program effect; the more accurate statement is that effects vary by how fully the program was implemented.

Fifth, the subgroup analyses — particularly by absence band and by school — involve smaller sample sizes than the overall comparison, and estimates at that level of disaggregation carry wider uncertainty than the headline figures. The general pattern (larger effects for moderately-at-risk students, smaller effects for severely-at-risk students; larger effects at schools with a full year of mentoring) is consistent enough across measures that we have reasonable confidence in the direction of these findings, but the precise magnitudes should be treated as approximate.

Sixth, this study measures attendance rate change only. It does not evaluate whether attendance improvements translated into better course grades, credit accumulation, or persistence toward graduation, which are presumably the ultimate goals of the intervention. A student attending school more often without improved academic engagement would represent only a partial success. This is a natural next question for the Office of Research and Evaluation, resources permitting, but it is outside the scope of what could be answered before the July vote.

## Implications for the July Funding Decision

The board is not choosing between a program that clearly works and one that clearly doesn't; the evidence does not support either of those clean stories. What it supports is a more targeted claim: attendance mentoring, delivered on schedule for a full school year, appears to produce a meaningful attendance benefit for ninth graders whose prior-year absenteeism was moderate — roughly 10 to 19 percent of days missed — and little to no detectable benefit for students whose prior-year absenteeism was severe, above 30 percent, or for English learners as currently served.

Several implications follow for the July vote and for how the program might be structured going forward, regardless of which way the vote goes.

If the board's primary interest is sustaining the program at its current scope, the evidence provides reasonable, though not ironclad, support for doing so, particularly if implementation problems like the Carroll Creek delay can be avoided — the difference between a 2.0-point effect and a 0.6-point effect at otherwise similar schools suggests that timely hiring and placement of mentors each fall matters as much as whether the program exists at all.

If the board is weighing whether to fund the program at its current scale or to reallocate resources, the disaggregated results suggest a possible middle path worth discussing: continuing or expanding mentoring capacity for moderately-at-risk ninth graders, where the evidence of benefit is clearest, while reconsidering whether the current mentor model is the right tool for the most severely chronically absent students, who may need a different kind of intervention — such as case management addressing housing, health, or family circumstances — that a caseload-based check-in mentor is not resourced to provide.

If the board is concerned about equity of impact, the absence of a measurable effect for English learners deserves attention independent of the overall funding decision. Continuing the program without addressing this gap would mean continuing to serve one part of the eligible population well and another part not at all, an outcome the board may want to name explicitly rather than let pass unremarked in a "the program works" framing.

Finally, whatever the board decides about funding for the 2026-27 school year, we recommend building in the elements a stronger evaluation would need before the grant's final expiration in June 2027: a plan for randomizing or otherwise more rigorously allocating any mentor capacity that falls short of covering all eligible students, a stable attendance-coding rule that does not change mid-study, and an explicit tracking mechanism for students who change schools. Absent those changes, any evaluation conducted before the next major funding decision will face the same interpretive constraints described in this brief.

## Appendix A: Data Verification and Definitions

Before this brief was finalized, Andre Beauchamp's office ran three independent checks against the student information system extract used for the analysis. The first compared total enrollment counts at each of the four high schools against the official October and March enrollment snapshots submitted to the state, to confirm that the extract used for this study was not missing or duplicating student records. All four schools matched within two students, a discrepancy attributable to ordinary mid-month enrollment changes rather than a data error. The second check recalculated each student's annual attendance rate directly from daily attendance codes rather than relying on the system's pre-calculated summary field, since the August 2025 coding change altered how certain codes roll up into that summary field. Recalculating from the underlying daily codes, rather than trusting the summary rate, is what allowed us to apply a consistent definition across both years for this study, though it does not fully resolve the measurement questions discussed in the body of the brief regarding the August 2025 change itself. The third check confirmed that every student in the mentored group actually appeared on a mentor caseload roster supplied by Rosa Iglesias's office for at least one full marking period, which excluded 11 students originally flagged as mentored who turned out to have been assigned a mentor for fewer than four weeks before withdrawing from the caseload; those 11 students are not included in the mentored group of 612 reported throughout this brief, and are also excluded from the comparison group to avoid double-counting.

For clarity, chronic absenteeism is defined throughout this brief using the state's standard threshold of missing 10 percent or more of enrolled school days in a given year, regardless of whether the absences were excused or unexcused. This is the same definition used for the district's public reporting on chronic absenteeism, which means the eligibility criteria for this program align with the metric the state uses to evaluate the district as a whole. The "prior-year absence band" categories used in the by-severity results (10–19 percent, 20–29 percent, 30 percent and above) were not designed in advance of this study; they were constructed afterward, based on where natural breaks in the distribution of eighth-grade absence rates appeared among students in the sample. Different band cutoffs would likely shift the precise numbers reported above by a point or two but would not change the general pattern of stronger effects among less-severe cases and weaker effects among more-severe ones, which held up under two alternative banding schemes we tested before settling on the three-band structure presented here.

English learner status was drawn from each student's active designation in the student information system as of the first day of ninth grade, using the state's standard identification and reclassification criteria. Students who exited English learner status during the ninth-grade year itself, of whom there were nine in the mentored group, were coded according to their status at the start of the year rather than treated as a separate transitional category, since a finer breakdown was not supportable given the small number of students involved.

## Appendix B: Notes on the Statistical Approach

The core comparisons in this brief are simple differences in mean gain scores between the mentored and comparison groups, reported without formal significance testing in the body of the brief in order to keep the presentation accessible to a board audience. For internal purposes, we note that the overall 1.7-point difference between groups is unlikely to be due to chance alone, given the combined sample size of nearly 1,800 students; a two-sample comparison of means on this gap produces a result that would conventionally be considered statistically distinguishable from zero. The by-band and by-school differences, calculated on considerably smaller subsamples, are less precisely estimated. The 30-percent-and-above band comparison in particular, with 98 mentored students and 210 comparison students, has enough sampling variability that the observed 0.1-point gap should be read as "no detectable difference" rather than as evidence that the true effect for this group is precisely zero; a true effect of a point or so in either direction would not be distinguishable from this result given the sample size available.

We considered, and set aside for this brief, a regression-adjusted approach that would control simultaneously for prior-year absence rate, school, English learner status, and other observable characteristics in a single model rather than presenting each breakdown separately. Such a model would allow, for instance, an estimate of the mentoring effect for a moderately-absent English learner at Carroll Creek specifically, net of all other factors at once. We did not pursue this for the current brief for two reasons: first, cell sizes for some combinations of these characteristics are small enough that a fully interacted model would produce estimates with very wide uncertainty bands, more likely to mislead than inform a board audience under a tight timeline; and second, the simpler, one-variable-at-a-time breakdowns presented above map directly onto the specific questions the board asked in April, and we judged that transparency and interpretability were more valuable than statistical efficiency for this particular audience and decision. A regression-adjusted follow-up analysis is a reasonable next step if the board wants a more precise accounting before the program's next review point, and could be completed well before the grant's June 2027 expiration if the board directs this office to prioritize it.

We also examined, but do not report in detail above, whether the gap between mentored and comparison students widened or narrowed over the course of the ninth-grade year using quarterly attendance snapshots rather than only a single annual figure. The pattern there is roughly consistent with the annual figures reported throughout this brief: the gap between groups was smallest in the first quarter, when mentoring relationships were still being established, and grew somewhat through the second and third quarters before leveling off in the fourth. This is consistent with a program that takes some months to produce its full effect, which is a relevant consideration in interpreting the Carroll Creek results, where mentoring began in November and therefore had less time within the year to reach whatever its mature effect size might be even beyond the simple loss of two months of contact.

## Appendix C: Questions the Board May Wish to Raise Before the July Vote

In anticipation of board discussion, this office offers the following questions as ones the data can and cannot answer, to help focus deliberation.

The data can speak reasonably well to: whether mentored students' attendance improved more than similar non-mentored students' attendance did, on average and within subgroups; whether that advantage varies by how severely absent a student was beforehand; and whether full-year implementation produced different results than a partial-year start.

The data cannot speak well to: what would happen if the program were scaled up substantially beyond its current 22-mentor capacity, since we have no evidence on students who differ meaningfully from those currently being served; whether the specific mechanism of mentoring, as opposed to the additional adult attention or the referrals it generates, is what produces the observed effect; or whether the attendance gains documented here will persist into tenth grade and beyond, since no student in this sample has yet been tracked past their ninth-grade year.

Given these boundaries, the board may find it useful to frame the July decision not as a single up-or-down question about whether "the program works," but as a set of narrower questions: whether to continue mentoring for moderately-at-risk ninth graders, given the clearest evidence of benefit in that group; whether to redesign or supplement the approach for severely chronically absent students, given the absence of a detectable benefit there; whether to invest in mentor capacity, training, or staffing changes aimed specifically at improving outcomes for English learners; and whether to condition continued local funding on stronger evaluation infrastructure going forward, including more consistent implementation timing across schools and, ideally, a lottery-based allocation mechanism for any future year in which mentor capacity again falls short of the eligible population.
