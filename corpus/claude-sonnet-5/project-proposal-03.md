# Southern Network Rebuild: Oklahoma City Cross-Dock and Terminal Consolidation

**A Capital Proposal to the Executive Committee of the Board of Directors**

**Prairie Gate Freight Systems, Inc.**

**Prepared by:** Fatima Bouzid, Chief Operating Officer, and Deshawn Whitlock, Vice President of Network Planning
**Sponsored by:** Sanjay Doshi, Chief Executive Officer
**Financing prepared by:** Desmond Okoye, Chief Financial Officer
**Date:** February 12, 2026 Executive Committee Meeting
**Decision requested:** Authorization to commit $38.5 million in capital and enter into associated financing to construct a 112-door cross-dock in Oklahoma City, close the Ponca City and Enid terminals, and re-cut 31 linehaul lanes over a 22-month implementation period.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Prairie Gate's southern Kansas–Oklahoma network is no longer capable of delivering the service level our largest customers require, and the cost of operating it in its current configuration is compounding rather than resolving the problem. On-time service system-wide has fallen from 96.1 percent to 87.4 percent over two years. Driver turnover stands at 94 percent annually. Kesterson Ag Supply, which represents 16 percent of company revenue, has placed us on a 90-day formal service watch under a contract that expires June 30, 2026, and has told Ms. Bouzid directly that failure to show sustained improvement will result in the business going out to bid.

This is not a driver problem, a dispatch problem, or a customer-service problem in isolation. It is a network design problem. Our southern terminals — Ponca City, Enid, and the freight that flows through them toward Oklahoma City, Dallas, and points south — were built for a lane structure and a freight density that no longer exist. Rehandling, misconnects, and excess dwell time are structural to the current footprint, not incidental to it.

We are proposing a $38.5 million rebuild: construction of a 112-door cross-dock facility in Oklahoma City, the closure of the Ponca City and Enid terminals, and a re-cut of 31 linehaul lanes, phased over 22 months so that no customer experiences a service gap during the transition. The capital would be financed with a $30 million equipment-backed term loan and $8.5 million of company cash, keeping leverage within the bank group's existing covenant package.

We recognize the committee declined a smaller version of this plan in 2023, and we recognize why: the Springfield dock expansion overran its budget by 41 percent, and this committee was right not to fund a second capital commitment on the heels of that experience without better cost discipline and a harder look at alternatives. This proposal is built differently. It includes a guaranteed-maximum-price construction contract, an independent owner's-representative firm reporting directly to this committee on cost and schedule, and a full accounting of the two alternatives we evaluated and rejected — leasing an existing Oklahoma City building, and repairing service inside the current footprint without new capital. We address each of those substitutes on their merits, not to dismiss them, but because we believe the record should show they were seriously considered.

The decision we are asking this committee to make on February 12 is narrow: authorize the $38.5 million capital commitment and the associated financing, and authorize management to execute the Enid and Ponca City closure notices on the schedule set out in Section 7. Everything else in this document is context for that decision.

## 2. The Problem: Service Failure and Its Cost

### 2.1 The service trend

Two years ago, Prairie Gate delivered 96.1 percent of shipments on time system-wide, a figure competitive with any regional LTL carrier in our lane set. That number has fallen steadily, not in a single bad quarter but across eight consecutive quarters, to 87.4 percent today. The decline is not evenly distributed. Our northern network — the Nebraska and northern Kansas terminals — has held service in the low 90s throughout. The erosion is concentrated in the southern corridor: freight moving through Ponca City and Enid toward Oklahoma City and onward to our interline partners serving Texas.

The mechanism is straightforward and Mr. Whitlock's team has documented it lane by lane. Ponca City and Enid were built decades ago as breakbulk points for a network in which Oklahoma City freight was a secondary flow. Today it is the dominant flow in the southern network, and it is being forced through two undersized, geographically redundant facilities thirty-five miles apart, each running partial trailer loads to preserve service that neither facility has the door capacity or the yard depth to actually preserve. Freight is rehandled twice in some lanes where it should be handled once. Linehaul trailers depart Ponca City partially loaded because Enid's inbound freight has not yet cleared, and vice versa. The result is missed cutoffs, missed connections at Oklahoma City interchange, and late deliveries that compound through the week.

### 2.2 The cost trend

The same structural inefficiency that is degrading service is also degrading cost. Rehandling freight through two facilities instead of one adds direct labor and dock touches that do not appear as a single identifiable overrun but accumulate across thousands of shipments a month. Deadhead miles between Ponca City and Enid to balance trailer loads run substantially above the system average for comparable lane pairs. Overtime in both terminals has risen as dock supervisors work extra shifts to unwind the congestion created by the split facility structure. None of this shows up as a dramatic line item; all of it shows up in a P&L that has revenue holding near $310 million while margin compresses.

### 2.3 Driver turnover

Ninety-four percent annual driver turnover is, on its own, a crisis independent of the network question, but it is not independent in practice. Drivers assigned to the Ponca City–Enid corridor are disproportionately represented in our exit interviews, and the reasons cited are consistent: unpredictable dispatch times driven by the rehandling problem described above, extended hours waiting for freight that should have already cleared, and home-time commitments broken by delays outside the driver's control. We are not proposing that this rebuild solves driver turnover by itself. We are stating, based on exit-interview data Ms. Bouzid's team has compiled over the past six months, that the current network structure is a material and identifiable driver of the turnover that is separately degrading service and inflating training and recruiting cost.

### 2.4 The Kesterson watch

Kesterson Ag Supply is 16 percent of company revenue, concentrated in agricultural inputs and equipment moving through the exact corridor described above — Wichita and Salina origin freight destined for Oklahoma and Texas delivery points through the Ponca City–Enid–Oklahoma City chain. Kesterson placed Prairie Gate on a formal 90-day service watch in November, has communicated specific on-time thresholds we must sustain, and has a contract expiring June 30, 2026, with no automatic renewal. Mr. Doshi and Ms. Bouzid have both spoken directly with Kesterson's logistics leadership. Their message has been consistent: they are not interested in explanations of network architecture. They are interested in delivery performance, and they have alternatives.

Losing Kesterson would remove approximately $49.6 million of annual revenue and, given the density that customer provides in the exact lanes this proposal addresses, would materially worsen the economics of every other shipment moving through that corridor. This is the customer and revenue base the current network is failing, and it is the customer and revenue base this proposal is built to protect.

### 2.5 Why the current footprint cannot be fixed without capital

We address this more fully in Section 8 as a formal alternative, but the essential point belongs here: the service and cost problems described above are not the product of poor execution inside two adequate facilities. They are the product of two facilities that are individually and jointly undersized and misplaced relative to current freight flow. Ponca City has 24 doors. Enid has 28. Combined, at 52 doors split across two yards with duplicated management overhead, duplicated linehaul dispatch, and freight that must frequently move between them before it can move onward, they perform worse than a single well-located facility with fewer total doors would perform. This is the core finding that drives the proposal in Section 3.

## 3. The Proposal: Facility and Network Redesign

### 3.1 The facility

We propose construction of a 112-door cross-dock terminal in Oklahoma City, sited to serve as the consolidated breakbulk and interchange point for the entire southern network. Oklahoma City is the natural center of freight gravity for this network today: it is the interchange point with our two largest interline partners serving Texas and the southern plains, it sits at the intersection of the interstate corridors our linehaul lanes actually use, and — critically — it is where the freight is. Mr. Whitlock's lane analysis shows that a majority of southern-network tonnage either originates, terminates, or interchanges within 40 miles of the proposed site today, moving through Ponca City and Enid only because that is where the legacy facilities happen to sit.

A single 112-door facility eliminates the rehandling described in Section 2 by design: freight moving south from Wichita and Salina, and freight interchanging with our partners, touches one dock instead of two. Door count is sized not for current volume alone but for the volume we expect to retain and grow once service is restored to the Kesterson threshold and to competitive levels generally; Mr. Whitlock's team modeled door requirements at 78 doors for current volume and built in headroom for five years of moderate growth plus consolidated cross-dock flow that does not exist in the current split-terminal structure.

### 3.2 The network re-cut

Consolidating the physical facility only realizes its value if the linehaul network is redesigned around it. The proposal includes a re-cut of 31 linehaul lanes — the full set of lanes that currently either originate, terminate, or transit through Ponca City or Enid. Lane re-cuts of this scope are the largest single execution risk in this proposal and are addressed in detail in Section 7's phasing plan; they cannot be done in a single cutover weekend without unacceptable service disruption, which is why the plan is built around a staged, lane-group-by-lane-group transition over 22 months.

### 3.3 Terminal closures

The proposal closes Ponca City and Enid on the schedule set out in Section 7, once their freight volume has been migrated to the new facility. We are not proposing to close either terminal immediately upon the new facility's opening; both remain operational, at reduced volume, through the transition period described below, both to preserve service continuity and because the Enid lease, discussed in Section 6, carries obligations that affect the closure schedule directly.

## 4. Why Now

Three factors converge to make this the moment for this decision, not a later one.

**The Kesterson deadline is fixed.** The contract expires June 30, 2026, and the 90-day service watch means Kesterson will be evaluating our performance well before that date. A facility that breaks ground in the second quarter of 2026 will not open until late 2027 under any construction schedule; it will not save the Kesterson contract directly. What this proposal buys, if approved February 12, is the ability to tell Kesterson — and to tell the board, honestly, twelve months from now — that the structural cause of the service failure is being addressed with a funded, executing plan, not a promise. We believe that distinction matters to a customer relationship that has already been damaged by prior promises that were not backed by capital commitment. Separately, Ms. Bouzid's service-recovery plan, funded from existing operating budget, targets interim improvement inside the current footprint through the Kesterson watch period; that plan is described in Section 8.2 and is not a substitute for this proposal, but it is the bridge to it.

**The cost of delay compounds.** Every month the current footprint remains in place, the rehandling cost, the deadhead cost, and the turnover cost described in Section 2 continue to accrue. Mr. Okoye's finance team estimates the fully loaded annual cost of the current network inefficiency, including turnover-driven training and recruiting cost, at approximately $6.8 million. Deferring this decision by a year does not preserve $38.5 million of capital; it spends roughly $6.8 million of it anyway, on inefficiency rather than on a fix.

**Financing conditions are favorable now.** Mr. Okoye's financing plan, detailed in Section 5, relies on equipment-backed term debt priced against current market rates and against the company's current leverage position. Both are more favorable today than they are likely to be if this decision is deferred a year and the company's covenant headroom is further compressed by continued margin erosion in the southern network.

### 5.3 Cost control commitments

Given the Springfield overrun in 2023, we are proposing three specific controls that were not in place on that project:

1. **Guaranteed-maximum-price construction contract.** The $26 million facility figure is not a preliminary estimate; it is the ceiling in a GMP contract to be executed with the selected general contractor prior to any capital drawdown, with the contractor bearing cost risk above that figure absent scope changes authorized by the company.

2. **Independent owner's representative.** We propose engaging an owner's-representative firm, independent of both the contractor and Prairie Gate operations management, to monitor cost and schedule and to report directly to this committee on a quarterly basis through construction. This function did not exist on the Springfield project.

3. **Contingency held at the corporate level, not the project level.** A 6 percent contingency ($2.3 million) is included within the $38.5 million total but will be released only against documented change orders approved by Mr. Okoye and Ms. Bouzid jointly, not managed as discretionary budget by the project team.

## 6. Phasing and Cutover: Keeping Freight Moving

Network transitions of this scope carry real risk of service disruption during the cutover itself — the last thing this company can afford, given the Kesterson watch and the broader service recovery this proposal is meant to protect. The phasing plan is built around a single principle: no lane converts to the new facility until the new facility has demonstrated it can handle that lane's volume reliably, and Ponca City and Enid remain operational at reduced scale until their volume has fully migrated.

**Months 1–3: Site work and mobilization.** Site acquisition finalized, GMP contract executed, owner's representative engaged, permitting completed. No operational change; Ponca City and Enid continue to operate at full current volume.

**Months 4–16: Construction.** Facility and dock equipment installation. In parallel, Mr. Whitlock's team executes the detailed lane-by-lane re-cut design and begins driver and dock-staff hiring and training for the new facility, so that trained staff are in place before the facility opens rather than hired reactively after opening.

**Months 17–18: Facility commissioning and pilot lane group.** The facility opens with a limited pilot group of 6 of the 31 lanes — selected as the lanes with the lowest current on-time performance and the clearest structural benefit from consolidation, so the pilot demonstrates value quickly while limiting exposure. Ponca City and Enid continue operating all other lanes at full volume during this period. Pilot performance is reviewed against defined on-time and cost thresholds before proceeding.

**Months 18–21: Staged lane migration.** The remaining 25 lanes migrate in four additional groups, roughly monthly, in order of volume and complexity, from simplest to most complex. Each group's migration is preceded by a two-week parallel-run period in which both the legacy routing and the new routing are staffed and ready, so that any group that underperforms can be reverted without a service gap while the issue is corrected. This is the mechanism, specifically, by which freight keeps moving during cutover: at no point does a lane's service depend entirely on a system that has not already been proven on a smaller scale.

**Month 22: Terminal closure.** Once all 31 lanes have fully migrated and sustained performance for at least 60 days, Ponca City and Enid cease operations. Closure notices to affected employees, discussed in Section 6 below, are issued on a schedule that satisfies WARN Act requirements and gives Ms. O'Rourke's team adequate transition time, timed against this operational closure date rather than against the capital approval date.

This phasing extends the project to 22 months specifically to avoid the alternative — a faster, single-event cutover — which Mr. Whitlock's team modeled and rejected as carrying unacceptable service risk during exactly the period when the company can least afford it.

## 7. Staffing and Labor Exposure

### 7.1 Enid and Ponca City workforce

Enid employs 46 people under Terminal Manager Kathleen O'Rourke. Of those, 12 have indicated willingness to relocate to Oklahoma City and will be offered positions at the new facility, with relocation assistance funded from the $5 million severance and relocation line in Section 5. The remaining 34 Enid employees, along with the Ponca City workforce, will be offered severance under a package Ms. O'Rourke and the company's HR leadership are finalizing, sized to be competitive with regional LTL industry practice and structured to encourage retention through the closure date rather than early attrition that would create staffing gaps during the transition period described in Section 7.

We are conscious that asking employees to stay through a facility's closure while offering only 12 of 46 positions elsewhere is a difficult message, and one that carries real risk of early departures that could undermine service during the exact period when Ponca City and Enid must continue operating reliably. Ms. O'Rourke's retention-bonus structure, funded within the severance line, is designed specifically to address this: bonuses vest at defined service milestones through the closure date, not solely at the closure date itself, so that partial retention risk is mitigated even if some employees leave early.

### 7.2 The Enid lease

The Enid facility lease runs through 2029 with no early termination provision. This is a real and unavoidable cost of this proposal: closing Enid before 2029 means the company continues paying rent on a facility it is not using, for the balance of the lease term, unless it can be subleased. We have not included sublease proceeds in the capital plan in Section 5 because we do not consider them reliable enough to underwrite; Mr. Whitlock's real estate advisor is exploring sublease and disposal options but the financial plan in this proposal assumes the company carries the remaining lease obligation to term. This obligation is not part of the $38.5 million capital request — it is an ongoing operating cost the committee should weigh as part of the full economic picture, and Mr. Okoye can provide the net present value of the remaining lease obligation at the February 12 meeting.

### 7.3 Teamsters organizing activity

Teamsters organizers have been present at the Salina and Enid docks since March. We want to be direct with the committee about this rather than treat it as a side issue. A terminal closure announcement, even one phased over 22 months and paired with relocation offers, is the kind of event that can accelerate organizing activity, both at the affected terminals and, potentially, more broadly across the network, if employees perceive the closure as being handled in a way that confirms rather than addresses their underlying concerns.

Our approach is to manage this directly rather than to avoid the subject: Ms. O'Rourke and Ms. Bouzid will communicate the closure plan to Enid employees in person, before any public announcement, with the retention and relocation terms fully specified rather than promised in general terms. We believe the alternative — continuing to operate a network structure that is itself a significant driver of the working conditions organizers are pointing to, described in Section 2.3 — carries at least as much labor relations risk as the closure does, and we would rather address the underlying cause than manage around it. Legal counsel has reviewed the communication and timing plan for compliance with NLRA obligations regarding organizing activity, and Ms. Bouzid will brief the committee separately on that plan in more detail than is appropriate for this document.

### 7.4 Staffing the new facility

The Oklahoma City facility will require dock, yard, clerical, and management staffing beyond the 12 relocating Enid employees. Mr. Whitlock's hiring plan, built into the Months 4–16 construction window in Section 7, targets full staffing in place and trained approximately 60 days before the facility opens. Given the 94 percent turnover figure cited in Section 2.3, we want to be candid that hiring for a new facility in the same labor market and industry that is producing that turnover rate carries real risk, and our hiring and retention plan for the new facility — competitive starting wages benchmarked against the Oklahoma City market, predictable scheduling enabled directly by the network redesign described in Section 3, and a driver-facing communication plan explaining how the new network reduces the unpredictability drivers have cited as their primary complaint — is designed to address the root cause rather than simply restaff around it.

## 8. Alternatives Considered

We evaluated two alternatives to new construction and want to present both fairly, because we believe this committee, informed by the 2023 decision and the Springfield experience, will and should ask why a lower-capital option was not sufficient.

### 8.1 Leasing an existing Oklahoma City building

Mr. Whitlock's team identified and toured four existing industrial properties in the Oklahoma City market that could plausibly be converted to LTL cross-dock use. The best of these, a 68-door former distribution facility in southwest Oklahoma City, was evaluated in detail.

The lease alternative offers a real advantage: substantially faster occupancy, potentially 8 to 10 months versus the 17 months to facility commissioning under the construction plan, and a lower upfront capital requirement, since leasehold improvements would run an estimated $9 million against a facility we do not own rather than $26 million for a purpose-built structure.

We rejected it for three reasons. First, door count: 68 doors falls short of even the 78-door current-volume requirement identified in Section 3.1, before accounting for growth headroom, meaning the company would likely face a second capital project within three to five years to address a door shortfall the new-build option avoids for the foreseeable future. Second, yard depth and trailer storage: the existing building's site does not accommodate the trailer yard capacity our linehaul model requires without acquiring adjacent parcels, which are not currently available for purchase or lease from the property's owner. Third, and most fundamentally, a leased building constrains the cross-dock configuration itself; LTL cross-dock efficiency depends heavily on dock layout and building depth, and retrofitting an existing structure not designed for cross-dock flow would leave meaningful efficiency on the table relative to a purpose-built facility, undermining the core service and cost benefit this proposal is meant to deliver. We would be trading a faster, cheaper facility for one that does not fully solve the problem described in Section 2 and that likely requires a second capital request within the medium term.

We do not consider this a closed question if the committee's judgment differs from ours on the trade-off between speed, cost, and long-term adequacy, and Mr. Whitlock has the full site evaluation available for review.

### 8.2 Repairing service inside the current footprint

The second alternative is to make no facility change and instead address the service problem through operational measures inside Ponca City and Enid as they exist today: added dock labor, revised dispatch scheduling, and tighter linehaul coordination between the two terminals.

Ms. Bouzid has, in fact, already begun a version of this as an interim measure, independent of this proposal, funded from existing operating budget, specifically to give the company the best possible service position with Kesterson through the 90-day watch period regardless of what the committee decides on February 12. Early results are modestly positive: on-time performance in the southern corridor has improved roughly two points since this interim plan began in December.

We do not believe this is a sufficient answer to the underlying problem, for the reason stated in Section 2.5: the two-facility structure creates rehandling and coordination costs that are structural, not operational, and no amount of added labor or improved scheduling changes the fact that freight is being touched twice where it should be touched once, and that combined 52-door capacity across two disconnected yards is inherently less efficient than 112 doors in one connected facility. The interim measures can move on-time performance from 87.4 percent toward, we estimate, the low 90s at best, and at a run-rate cost — primarily added labor — of approximately $2.4 million annually indefinitely, without ever closing the gap to the 96.1 percent baseline the company delivered two years ago, and without addressing the turnover and cost pressures described in Section 2. We view the interim plan as a necessary bridge through the Kesterson decision point, not as a substitute for the structural fix this proposal represents.

## 9. Risks and Mitigations

We have tried throughout this document to identify risk in context rather than isolate it in a single section for form's sake, but we summarize the principal risks here together with their mitigations, both because the committee will reasonably want this in one place and because we owe this committee direct acknowledgment of what could go wrong, particularly given the 2023 and Springfield history.

**Construction cost overrun.** The single risk most directly tied to the 2023 decision. Mitigated by the guaranteed-maximum-price contract, independent owner's-representative oversight, and corporate-level contingency control described in Section 5.3.

**Construction schedule delay.** A delayed facility opening extends the period during which Ponca City and Enid must continue absorbing full volume and extends the Enid dual-cost period described in Section 6.2. Mitigated by GMP contract schedule guarantees with liquidated-damages provisions, and by the owner's representative's quarterly schedule reporting to this committee.

**Service disruption during cutover.** Addressed at length in Section 7; mitigated by the staged, lane-group phasing with parallel-run periods and reversion capability, rather than a single-event cutover.

**Labor disruption and organizing acceleration.** Addressed in Section 6.3; mitigated by direct, early, specific communication to affected employees and by legal counsel's review of communication timing and content.

**Employee attrition at Ponca City and Enid before closure, creating operational gaps.** Mitigated by the milestone-vesting retention bonus structure described in Section 6.1.

**Kesterson departure regardless of this proposal.** This is the risk this proposal cannot fully mitigate on the timeline that matters most: the facility will not be open before the June 30, 2026 contract expiration. We are managing this risk through the interim service-recovery plan described in Section 8.2, run in parallel with, not instead of, this proposal, and through direct communication to Kesterson that a funded structural plan is underway. We cannot represent to the committee that this proposal guarantees Kesterson's retention; we can represent that it is the most credible path to the sustained service level Kesterson has said it requires.

**New-facility hiring difficulty given 94 percent regional turnover.** Addressed in Section 7.4; mitigated by wage benchmarking, predictable scheduling enabled by the network redesign itself, and advance hiring built into the construction timeline.

**Financing and covenant risk.** Mitigated by the financing structure in Section 5.2, kept within existing covenant headroom under Mr. Okoye's base case, with sensitivity analysis available for the committee's review.

We are asking the Executive Committee, at its February 12 meeting, to:

1. **Authorize capital expenditure of $38.5 million** for the Oklahoma City cross-dock facility, dock and yard equipment, and severance and relocation costs, as detailed in Section 5.1.

We believe this proposal answers the questions this committee properly asked in 2023: it is priced with a cost ceiling, not an estimate; it is overseen by a party independent of the project team; and it addresses, on its own record, the two lower-capital alternatives available to us. We believe the cost of not acting — continued service decline, continued turnover, and the material risk of losing 16 percent of company revenue on June 30 — is now greater than the cost and risk of this proposal. We are prepared to answer the committee's questions on any section of this document at the February 12 meeting.
