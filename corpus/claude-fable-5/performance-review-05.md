# Annual Performance Review

**Employee:** Alonzo Ferrer
**Title:** Housing Navigation Supervisor
**Review Period:** July 1, 2025 – June 30, 2026
**Reviewer:** Renata Osei, Chief Program Officer
**Rating Scale:** 5 – Exceptional | 4 – Exceeds Expectations | 3 – Meets Expectations | 2 – Needs Improvement | 1 – Unsatisfactory

## Performance Against Annual Goals

**Goal 1: Place 180 households through the housing navigation program.**

Alonzo's team placed 214 households against a target of 180, a 119 percent achievement rate, in a year when the El Paso rental market tightened and two of our largest landlord partners reduced the units they set aside for our referrals. This did not happen by luck. Alonzo restructured how his eight navigators divided the caseload, moving from a geographic assignment model to one based on household complexity, so that his most experienced navigators carried the families with eviction histories and criminal-background barriers while newer staff built confidence on more straightforward placements. He also personally recruited eleven new landlords into our network, several of whom had previously declined to work with us. When I visited the housing team in February, three navigators independently described his weekly case conferencing as the most useful supervision they had received in their careers here.

**Rating: 5 – Exceptional**

**Goal 2: Reduce average time from program enrollment to housing placement from 71 days to 60 days.**

The team finished the year at 49 days, beating the target by eleven days and cutting the prior-year figure by 22 days. Alonzo achieved this primarily by building a document-readiness checklist that navigators now complete at intake rather than at application, which eliminated the most common source of delay — families arriving at a unit showing without identification, income verification, or the landlord packet in order. This checklist has since been requested by two peer agencies in the region, and I have asked Alonzo to present it at the Texas Homeless Network conference in the fall if funding permits his travel.

**Rating: 5 – Exceptional**

**Goal 3: Maintain full staffing on the navigation team and complete quarterly performance conversations with all direct reports.**

All eight navigator positions were filled at year end; one navigator departed in October and Alonzo filled the vacancy within six weeks, faster than our organizational average of eleven weeks for direct-service roles. Documentation shows quarterly check-ins completed for all staff. Staff feedback in the anonymous annual survey was strong: seven of eight team members rated his supervision "very effective," and written comments used the words "fair," "available," and "protects the team" repeatedly. One comment noted he can be slow to escalate interpersonal conflicts between team members, preferring to let staff work things out themselves; this is a minor development area, not a deficiency.

**Rating: 4 – Exceeds Expectations**

## Assumption of Program Director Duties

This section falls outside the goals set last July, but it would be a false record that omitted it. From late September through February — five months — Alonzo covered the vacant Program Director role for housing services in addition to his own. He ran the monthly funder calls, prepared the HUD annual performance report narrative, represented Puente at the Continuum of Care board, and supervised the coordinated-entry specialist who normally reports to the director. He did this without the title, without additional compensation, and without a reduction in his own team's output, as the numbers above demonstrate. He raised the compensation question with me twice, in December and in April, appropriately and professionally both times. I want the record to state plainly that the organization asked more of Alonzo this year than his role or salary contemplated, and he delivered.

## Audit and Funder Findings

The single audit's material weakness on subrecipient monitoring does not touch Alonzo's area. His program's HUD file review in March returned no findings; the reviewer noted the intake documentation as a model practice. The closure of the $340,000 federal grant affected a workforce-linked component of housing navigation, and Alonzo managed the wind-down of two grant-funded activities without service interruption to enrolled households.

## Self-Assessment

Alonzo submitted a self-assessment on July 14, 2026. The following are his words, quoted directly:

> "I'm proud of what the team did this year, and I want to be clear that the placement numbers belong to the navigators, not to me. My job was to remove obstacles and I think I mostly did. On the director coverage: I took it on because the families we serve couldn't wait for a hiring process, and I'd do it again. But I told Renata in December and again in April that I can't keep absorbing director-level work at supervisor pay indefinitely, and I need the organization to tell me honestly whether there is a path here. I have a family and I have to make decisions by September. That's not a threat; it's just my situation."

**Reviewer response:** I find nothing in this self-assessment to dispute. Alonzo's account of his own performance is, if anything, more modest than the record supports. His statement about needing a decision by September is fair, and I address it below as directly as I am able.

## Overall Rating: 5 – Exceptional

## Compensation and the Salary Freeze

This is the hardest paragraph in this review to write. The board, responding to the audit finding and the loss of federal funding, has frozen all salaries above $55,000 and authorized a 4 percent adjustment only below that line. Alonzo's current salary is above the freeze threshold, which means that despite a rating of Exceptional and five months of uncompensated acting-director duties, I cannot award him a merit increase this cycle. I have told him this in person before he reads it here, because he deserved not to learn it from a form.

What I can do, and have committed to in writing, is the following. First, I have formally recommended to the Executive Director and the board's personnel committee that Alonzo receive a one-time acting-duty stipend for the September–February coverage period, funded from the housing program's administrative line, which is not subject to the freeze in the same way as base salary; that recommendation is pending and I will have an answer before September 1. Second, the Program Director position will be posted internally by August 22, and I have told Alonzo that I regard him as the strongest internal candidate; I cannot promise an outcome of an open process, but I can promise the process will be real and fast. Third, if the director role is filled — by him or otherwise — his out-of-title work ends, permanently. I recognize that a peer agency has interviewed him and that he may receive an offer we cannot match this year. If he stays, it will be because Puente earned it, and I intend for us to try to earn it.

## Goals for FY 2026–2027

1. **Placements:** 200 households placed, a target set deliberately above last year's goal but below last year's actual, in recognition of the tightening rental market and the loss of grant-funded capacity.
2. **Timeliness:** Sustain average time-to-placement at or below 55 days.
3. **Landlord retention:** Retain 85 percent of active landlord partners year over year and add eight new units-committed landlords.
4. **Succession depth:** Identify and develop one senior navigator capable of covering supervisory duties, so that no future vacancy requires Alonzo to absorb two jobs.
5. **Conflict escalation:** Bring team interpersonal issues to HR or the CPO within two weeks of identification rather than managing them solely within the team.

# Annual Performance Review

**Employee:** Sunita Kapoor
**Title:** Grants and Compliance Manager
**Review Period:** July 1, 2025 – June 30, 2026
**Reviewer:** Renata Osei, Chief Program Officer
**Rating Scale:** 5 – Exceptional | 4 – Exceeds Expectations | 3 – Meets Expectations | 2 – Needs Improvement | 1 – Unsatisfactory

## Performance Against Annual Goals

**Goal 1: Submit all federal and state quarterly reports on time and without material corrections.**

Four of nine quarterly reports due during the review period were submitted late — the Q1 and Q3 reports for our HUD grant, the Q2 report for the Department of Labor workforce grant, and the Q2 report for the state family-services contract. The delays ranged from four days to nineteen days. Two of the four required funder contact to request extensions after the deadline had already passed, which is the reverse of the order in which those conversations should happen. One late report contributed to a temporary hold on a drawdown that delayed reimbursement of roughly $87,000 for approximately five weeks and created a cash-flow strain the finance director had to bridge.

I want to be precise about causes, because precision matters in a review like this one. Sunita holds the only signature authority on federal reports, and the reporting calendar this year included two new awards without any added staffing in her unit. Some of the workload concentration is an organizational design failure — mine and the Executive Director's — not hers alone. But the record also shows that Sunita did not raise the capacity problem until after the second late report, did not delegate the assembly work that does not require her signature, and did not use the reporting calendar tool that finance built for her in the fall. Timeliness is the core function of this role, and the core function was not met.

**Rating: 1 – Unsatisfactory**

**Goal 2: Maintain a compliant subrecipient monitoring system, including annual risk assessments and site visits for all subrecipients.**

The single audit returned a material weakness on subrecipient monitoring — the most serious finding in Puente's history. The auditors found that risk assessments were completed for only two of our five subrecipients, that no site visits were documented for the fiscal year, and that one subrecipient's single-audit certification was never collected. This is Sunita's area of direct responsibility, and the finding names it.

In our conversations since the audit, Sunita has not disputed the facts. She has pointed out, accurately, that the monitoring calendar collapsed during the same months the unexpected state application was being written, and that she flagged in a February email that "something is going to slip." That email exists and I have read it. It mitigates the picture somewhat: she gave warning. But the warning was one line in a long email, it proposed no solution, and when something did slip, it was the federally required function rather than a discretionary one. Part of the judgment this role requires is knowing which balls are made of glass. A corrective action plan is now due to the cognizant agency by October 15, and its execution sits at the center of Sunita's goals for next year.

**Rating: 1 – Unsatisfactory**

**Goal 3: Provide timely budget and compliance support to program teams (standard: substantive response within three business days).**

Program staff report, consistently and across teams, that budget questions submitted to the grants office sit for around two weeks before receiving an answer. The annual internal-services survey scored the grants function 2.1 out of 5 on responsiveness, the lowest of any administrative unit. Written comments were pointed: staff described "a black hole," and two supervisors said they had stopped asking and simply guessed at allowability, which is itself a compliance risk that lands back on Sunita's own area. When answers do come, staff describe them as thorough and correct — the problem is speed and communication, not competence. A simple acknowledgment with a realistic timeline would resolve much of the frustration, and it costs nothing.

**Rating: 2 – Needs Improvement**

**Goal 4: Maintain and grow the funding portfolio.**

Here the story is entirely different, and I refuse to let the audit year erase it. Sunita identified, conceived, and wrote the application for a state family-stabilization award that nobody — including me — expected us to win. The award is $600,000 over two years, it arrived in the same year we lost a $340,000 federal grant, and it is the single largest reason Puente's budget for the coming year is stable rather than contracting. The state program officer told our Executive Director that Puente's application was the strongest in the region. Sunita wrote it largely alone, at night, during the same overloaded winter described above. Her grant-writing and funder-relationship skills are genuinely exceptional, and this award is the proof.

**Rating: 5 – Exceptional**

## Self-Assessment

Sunita submitted a self-assessment on July 20, 2026. The following are her words, quoted directly:

> "This was the worst year of my professional life and also, in one way, the best. I own the late reports and I own the monitoring finding — my name is on that function and I won't pretend otherwise. But I want it on the record that I have been a one-person federal compliance office for an $11 million organization, that I asked for help, and that the state award I brought in will pay for the help I asked for several times over. I would ask that the review weigh the whole year, not only the audit."

**Reviewer response:** I have weighed the whole year, and this review reflects both halves of it. Sunita's ownership of the findings is genuine and I credit it. Her account of under-capacity is partly accurate and is addressed in the goals below, including a shared-signature-authority change and a compliance associate position. Where I differ with her self-assessment is on the word "asked": one flagged line in February is not the escalation this situation required. The role demands that when the load becomes unsafe, she says so loudly, in a meeting, with a proposal — not in passing. That is the growth this year demands.

## Overall Rating: 2 – Needs Improvement

The exceptional development work does not offset the failure of the role's core compliance functions; a material weakness and four late federal reports cannot round up to "meets expectations." Neither does the compliance failure erase the $600,000 award, which is why this rating is not lower. This rating comes with a structured improvement plan, not a termination track: I believe Sunita can succeed in this role, and the organization is changing the conditions around it at the same time she is asked to change her practice within it.

## Compensation and the Salary Freeze

Sunita's salary is above the $55,000 freeze threshold, so no adjustment would have been available this cycle regardless of rating; given the rating, a merit increase would not have been recommended in any case. I want to name one thing directly: the freeze exists in part because of the audit finding in her area, and she will hear that from colleagues, fairly or not. My expectation of the staff is professionalism, and my expectation of Sunita is that she let her corrective work — not this year's rating — define her reputation going forward.

## Performance Improvement Plan and Goals for FY 2026–2027

A formal performance improvement plan accompanies this review, with a 90-day check-in on November 10, 2026. The goals below are its substance:

1. **Corrective action plan:** Deliver the subrecipient monitoring corrective action plan to the cognizant agency by October 15, 2026, and execute it fully — risk assessments for all five subrecipients by December 31, and all site visits completed and documented by April 30, 2027.
2. **Reporting timeliness:** 100 percent of quarterly reports submitted on time. Any anticipated delay is escalated to the CPO no fewer than ten business days before the deadline, with a plan.
3. **Signature authority:** Work with finance and the Executive Director to establish a documented backup signature authority by September 30, 2026, ending the single-point-of-failure design.
4. **Responsiveness standard:** Acknowledge all program budget/compliance inquiries within one business day and resolve within five; internal-services survey score of 3.5 or higher.
5. **Team build-out:** Hire and train the grants and compliance associate (funded by the state award's administrative allocation) by November 30, 2026, and delegate report assembly to that role.
6. **Development pipeline:** Maintain the funder cultivation calendar and submit at least two new proposals — but only after goals 1 and 2 are on track, and never at their expense.

# Annual Performance Review

**Employee:** Devon Pritchard
**Title:** Family Case Management Supervisor
**Review Period:** July 1, 2025 – June 30, 2026
**Reviewer:** Renata Osei, Chief Program Officer
**Rating Scale:** 5 – Exceptional | 4 – Exceeds Expectations | 3 – Meets Expectations | 2 – Needs Improvement | 1 – Unsatisfactory

## Context for This Review

Devon was on approved leave for eight weeks during the fall, returning in November. This review evaluates the full year's program outcomes because the program's obligations to families and funders ran for the full year, but my judgment throughout accounts for the reality that Devon was absent for two months, that the organization's coverage plan for his absence was thin, and that he returned to a team already showing strain. Where responsibility is shared between Devon and the organization, I say so. Where it is his, I say that too. Nothing in this review evaluates the leave itself, which was his right and is not a performance matter.

## Performance Against Annual Goals

**Goal 1: Achieve 90 targeted family stabilization placements.**

The team closed 41 of 90 targeted placements — 46 percent of goal. This is a significant shortfall, and I will not soften the number. The trajectory tells a more complicated story: the team was roughly on pace through September, fell to near-zero output during the coverage gap in October and November, and never recovered velocity after Devon's return because by spring the team was down two case managers out of five. A three-person team cannot produce a five-person team's numbers, and the placements-per-active-case-manager figure for the final quarter was actually close to the prior year's rate.

Even so, the shortfall is not fully explained by absence and attrition. In December and January, when Devon was back and the team was still at four, weekly placement activity remained well below the fall pace. Case reviews from that period show families stalled at the same steps for weeks without documented supervisor intervention. Devon has told me he was rebuilding his footing in those months; I believe him, and I also note that he did not ask for the transitional support — a reduced span, temporary co-supervision — that I would have provided had he asked, and that I should have offered without being asked.

**Rating: 2 – Needs Improvement**

**Goal 2: Maintain file documentation compliance at or above the funder-required 85 percent.**

The year-end file documentation rate was 62 percent against a required 85 percent. This is the most serious item in this review, because unlike the placement number, documentation compliance is a funder condition, and the family-services funder has issued a written finding requiring a corrective plan and a re-review in January 2027. Continued non-compliance puts the contract itself at risk, and with it the services of every family on the caseload.

The documentation decline began during the coverage gap — the interim coverage arrangement did not include file review, an organizational failure — but the rate continued to fall after Devon's return, from 71 percent in November to 62 percent by June. Devon's supervision style, which I discuss below, prioritizes clinical conversation about families over administrative verification, and his case managers confirm that file audits effectively stopped this year. His clinical instincts are the best on our staff; but a supervisor's job includes the unglamorous work of checking the files, and that work did not happen.

**Rating: 1 – Unsatisfactory**

**Goal 3: Retain and develop the case management team.**

Two of five case managers resigned in the spring, and both named supervision in their exit interviews. Their specific words matter: one said Devon was "wonderful with families and absent with us," describing canceled supervision sessions and unanswered case questions; the other said she "never knew where I stood" and learned about the documentation finding from a colleague rather than from Devon. Neither described unkindness — both, notably, said they liked him. What they described was inconsistency: supervision that was rich when it happened and unreliable in whether it happened.

The three remaining case managers offered a mixed but not hostile picture in the annual survey. They rated Devon high on clinical guidance and low on structure, follow-through, and communication of expectations. One wrote: "When Devon is present, he's the best supervisor I've had. The problem is you can't count on when that is."

**Rating: 2 – Needs Improvement**

**Goal 4: Maintain high participant satisfaction and family engagement.**

Devon received the highest participant survey ratings of any supervisor in the organization — 4.8 out of 5, with a remarkable 71 percent response rate from his program's families. The written comments are unlike any I have read in eleven years of doing these reviews. Families described a supervisor who showed up at housing court, who remembered children's names months later, who answered the phone on a Saturday when a family was hours from losing a unit. Three separate families credited Devon personally with keeping them together. Whatever else this review must say, it must say this: Devon's connection to the families we serve is the reason this program has the reputation it has, and it is a strength almost no amount of training can produce in someone who lacks it.

**Rating: 5 – Exceptional**

## Audit and Funder Findings

The single audit's subrecipient-monitoring finding does not involve Devon's area. The funder documentation finding described under Goal 2 is the finding that touches his work, and the corrective plan attached to this review is a condition of his continued supervision of the program. The closed $340,000 federal grant did not fund his team.

## Self-Assessment

Devon submitted a self-assessment on July 22, 2026. The following are his words, quoted directly:

> "I'm not going to argue with the numbers. The documentation rate is on me — I know what the funder requires and I let it slide because I always chose the family in front of me over the file behind me, and this year that stopped being a defensible trade. Losing Marisol and Kevin is the thing I think about most. They deserved a steadier supervisor than I was this year. What I'd ask is that the review recognize I came back in November to a team that had been running without real coverage, and I never fully caught up. I'm not asking for a pass. I'm asking for a structure that doesn't depend on me being at my best every single day, because this year taught me I won't always be."

**Reviewer response:** This is one of the most honest self-assessments I have received, and its accuracy makes my job here easier. I accept Devon's account of the coverage gap — that failure belongs to the organization, and I have said so above. I accept his framing of the family-versus-file trade-off as a real tension, while holding, as he now does, that the trade is not his to make when a funder requirement is on the line. His request for structure is exactly right, and the goals below are built to provide it.

## Overall Rating: 2 – Needs Improvement

This rating reflects the failure of two core supervisory functions — documentation compliance and staff retention — in a year when program output reached less than half of target. It is not lower because the participant results are extraordinary, because meaningful parts of the shortfall trace to an organizational coverage failure, and because Devon's ownership of the problems gives me real confidence in correction. This rating is paired with a structured support plan, not a disciplinary track. I want Devon to succeed here, and I believe he can.

## Compensation and the Salary Freeze

Devon's salary falls below the $55,000 threshold, so under the board's action he is eligible for the 4 percent adjustment approved for staff under that line. The adjustment is across-the-board for eligible staff and is not a merit judgment; it will appear in his September pay. I tell him this plainly so there is no confusion: the increase reflects the board's decision about staff below the freeze line, not a signal that this year's performance concerns are minor. They are not, and the corrective plan below is serious.

## Corrective Plan and Goals for FY 2026–2027

A corrective plan with 60- and 120-day check-ins (October 9 and December 9, 2026) accompanies this review:

1. **Documentation recovery:** Raise the file documentation rate to 75 percent by the October check-in and to 85 percent or above before the funder's January 2027 re-review. Devon will personally audit five files per week on a fixed schedule, with results logged and reviewed with the CPO monthly.
2. **Supervision reliability:** Hold weekly individual supervision with every case manager, on the calendar, with no more than one reschedule per staff member per quarter; sessions are documented on the standard supervision form.
3. **Rebuild the team:** Fill both vacant case manager positions by October 31, 2026, with onboarding plans that include documentation standards from day one.
4. **Placements:** 75 targeted placements — a deliberately rebuilt target reflecting the team's staffing reality, to be revisited upward at midyear if the team reaches full strength on schedule.
5. **Structure and self-management:** Working with the CPO, establish the backup structures Devon requested — a documented coverage protocol for any supervisor absence, and a monthly dashboard so that compliance drift is visible within weeks, not quarters. Devon will escalate to the CPO within one week any period in which he cannot sustain his supervisory schedule.
6. **Preserve the strength:** Maintain participant satisfaction at 4.5 or above. Nothing in this plan should distance Devon from families; the point is to make the program as reliable behind the scenes as it is in front of them.

# Reviewer's Closing Memorandum to the Personnel File

**From:** Renata Osei, Chief Program Officer
**To:** Executive Director; Human Resources file
**Re:** Cross-cutting observations from the FY 2025–2026 management review cycle
**Date:** August 9, 2026

This memorandum accompanies the three reviews above and records observations that belong to the organization rather than to any single employee's file. I ask that it be read alongside the reviews, because several of this year's individual failures had organizational causes, and it would be poor practice to document the failures without documenting the causes.

## 1. Single points of failure

Two of the year's most serious problems — the material weakness in subrecipient monitoring and the collapse of documentation compliance in family case management — occurred in functions that depended entirely on one person. Sunita Kapoor held sole signature authority on federal reports and sole responsibility for monitoring five subrecipients. Devon Pritchard's eight-week absence exposed the fact that no one else in the organization reviewed case files or held his team's administrative line. When either individual faltered or was absent, the function simply stopped. The corrective goals in both reviews address this (backup signature authority by September 30; a documented supervisor coverage protocol), but the pattern deserves board-level attention: an $11.4 million organization should not have federally required functions with no redundancy. I recommend a standing inventory of single-point-of-failure functions, reviewed annually by the personnel committee.

## 2. Escalation culture

In all three reviews, the escalation question appears. Sunita flagged an unsafe workload in one line of a February email and then watched the monitoring calendar collapse. Devon returned from leave struggling and did not ask for transitional support. Alonzo, to his credit, raised the acting-duty compensation question twice, clearly — and the organization still took eight months to answer him. The lesson cuts both ways. Staff must learn to escalate loudly and with proposals; leadership, myself included, must demonstrate that escalation produces action rather than silence. I take responsibility for not offering Devon a re-entry plan unprompted and for not resolving Alonzo's compensation question in December when it was first raised. I have noted both failures in the reviews themselves, and I note them again here so that they attach to my file as well as theirs.

## 3. The freeze and its inequities

The board's salary action — a freeze above $55,000 and a 4 percent adjustment below — was a defensible response to the audit finding and the lost grant, but its application to these three managers illustrates its blunt edges. The manager rated Exceptional (Ferrer) receives nothing. The manager rated Needs Improvement on a corrective plan (Pritchard) receives 4 percent, solely because of where his salary sits. The manager whose area generated the finding that prompted the freeze (Kapoor) is frozen, but would have been frozen at any rating. None of these outcomes is a merit judgment, and I have said so explicitly in each review, but staff will draw their own conclusions unless leadership communicates carefully. I recommend the Executive Director address the freeze's rationale and its planned duration at the September all-staff meeting, and that the board be asked to define the conditions under which the freeze lifts — a clean corrective-action closeout, a stabilized budget, or a date certain — so that high performers like Alonzo Ferrer are not asked to wait on an open-ended promise.

## 4. Retention exposure

I assess our retention risk on these three positions as follows. **Ferrer: high and immediate.** He has interviewed with a peer agency, he has set a September decision point, and he is the most marketable of the three. The acting-duty stipend recommendation and the Program Director posting are the only levers available inside the freeze, and both must move before September 1. If we lose him, we lose the year's best program results and the institutional knowledge behind them, and I estimate replacement at six months and a weaker hire. **Kapoor: moderate.** She is bruised by the audit year and aware that colleagues associate her area with the freeze, but her self-assessment suggests she wants to repair the record here rather than leave under a finding. The compliance associate hire will matter greatly to whether she stays. **Pritchard: low to moderate.** His attachment to the families in the program is the strongest retention force in the organization, but a failed January re-review or a punitive reading of his corrective plan could change that. His plan is framed as support, and it must be administered that way.

## 5. Calibration note

For consistency of the rating record: this cycle produced one Exceptional, one Needs Improvement with a performance improvement plan, and one Needs Improvement with a corrective support plan. I am aware that two of three managers rated below Meets Expectations in the same year invites the question of whether the standard moved. It did not. The audit finding and the funder documentation finding are objective external judgments, and the ratings follow them. What the distribution actually reflects is a year in which the organization's administrative infrastructure was thinner than its ambitions, and the individual reviews should be read with that in mind — as should next year's, when the structural corrections now underway will remove the mitigations I extended this cycle. In FY 2026–2027, the coverage protocols, backup authorities, and added staff will exist. The standard next year is therefore clean execution, and all three managers have been told so.
