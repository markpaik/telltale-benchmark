# First-Half Fiscal 2026 Network Operations Review

**Report:** Network Operations Review — Distribution Center Performance, Cost, Quality, Labor, and Second-Half Outlook
**Period Covered:** Fiscal 2026 First Half (October 1, 2025 – March 31, 2026), with April–June 2026 operating data included where noted
**Prepared By:** Sanjay Bhatt, Director of Network Analytics
**Date Issued:** July 29, 2026
**Primary Audience:** Rhonda Piotrowski, Chief Operating Officer; Cesar Villanueva, Vice President of Operations
**Secondary Audience:** Joint Wabash Crossing / Beaumont Home Brands Operations Session (scheduled August 2026)
**Distribution:** Executive Committee; Site General Managers; Beaumont Home Brands Supply Chain Leadership (redacted edition)

## 1. Purpose and Scope

This report presents the consolidated operating results of the Wabash Crossing Logistics distribution network for the first half of fiscal 2026 and establishes the factual basis for two conversations that will follow: the executive committee's second-half planning session and the joint operations review with Beaumont Home Brands.

The scope covers all five distribution centers — Indianapolis, Groveport, Louisville, Carlisle, and Winchester — at the level of shipped volume, cost per unit, service quality, labor condition, and capacity utilization. It further addresses three matters that require decisions rather than observation: the Carlisle performance improvement plan and the associated warehouse management system upgrade delay, the Winchester volume shortfall and its monthly loss, and the network's readiness for peak season.

Two audiences are served by a single document, and the distinction matters. The internal audience needs the unvarnished picture, including the cost of remediation and the internal causes of the Carlisle shortfall. The Beaumont-facing edition, which will be drawn from Sections 4, 5, 6, and 9, needs the same facts without the internal cost commentary. Nothing in this report should be characterized to Beaumont in terms that the underlying data would not support; the company's position in the joint session is stronger for being consistent with what is written here.

---

## 2. Executive Summary

The network shipped strong volume in the first half and held its overall financial commitments, but the quality of that performance is uneven in ways that concentrate risk in exactly the places the company can least afford it. Indianapolis moved 41.2 million units at high accuracy but is losing its workforce at a rate that is now driving cost above budget. Groveport shipped well but missed its service level badly enough to generate $214,000 in customer chargebacks. Louisville is physically full and turning away freight. Carlisle, our largest facility and the site Beaumont placed on a performance improvement plan in April, is shipping on time at 91.3 percent against a 98 percent commitment, and the system upgrade that underpins its recovery has slipped six months. Winchester, ten months old, is receiving barely two-thirds of the volume its business case assumed and is losing approximately $180,000 per month.

Four conclusions follow.

**First, the Carlisle situation is the single largest commercial risk in the portfolio.** Beaumont represents 31 percent of consolidated revenue, roughly $96 million annually. Carlisle is the largest Beaumont site. The improvement plan has a defined runway, and the WMS upgrade that was the centerpiece of the recovery narrative presented to Beaumont in April will not be live until September — after the plan's original review milestone and squarely inside peak. The network must present Beaumont with a recovery path that does not depend on the upgrade to produce results before peak.

**Second, labor instability at Indianapolis has crossed from an HR concern to a cost and quality concern.** Sixty-two percent annualized warehouse turnover is producing a permanently green workforce. The site is holding 99.4 percent accuracy only through supervisory intensity and rework that is showing up as a three-cent-per-unit cost overrun — $1.24 million annualized on Indianapolis volume alone.

**Third, the network has a capacity mismatch, not a capacity shortage.** Louisville is at 96 percent of storage capacity and refused two inbound trailers in June. Winchester is running at 68 percent of contracted volume with unused space and an underloaded fixed cost base. These two facts are 550 miles apart and have not been treated as a single problem. They should be.

**Fourth, the second half carries more risk than the first.** Peak season concentrates volume into the same sites that are already strained, the Carlisle system cutover lands inside the peak window, and the seasonal labor market will be tighter in the markets where retention is already weakest. The mitigations described in Section 9 are not optional refinements; they are the conditions under which the second-half plan is achievable.

## 4. Site-by-Site Operating Review

### 4.1 Indianapolis, Indiana

**Profile:** 610,000 square feet; 41.2 million units shipped in H1; the network's highest-throughput facility on a units-per-square-foot basis at 67.5 units per square foot per half-year.

Indianapolis is operationally the strongest site in the network by output and among the weakest by workforce stability. It shipped 41.2 million units at 99.4 percent order accuracy — comfortably inside the customer requirement — while running warehouse staff turnover at 62 percent annualized.

The cost picture tells the story. Actual cost per unit was $0.42 against a $0.39 budget, a three-cent overrun producing $1,236,000 in unfavorable variance on first-half volume. Analysis of the variance decomposes as follows:

| Variance Component | Cents / Unit | H1 Dollar Impact | Driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Training and onboarding labor | $0.011 | $453,000 | 340 new hires in H1; 80-hour ramp per associate |
| Productivity gap, tenure <90 days | $0.009 | $371,000 | New associates pick at 71% of tenured rate |
| Supervisory overhead | $0.006 | $247,000 | Added 4 floor leads to maintain accuracy |
| Rework and audit | $0.004 | $165,000 | 100% QC on new-associate pick zones |
| **Total** | **$0.030** | **$1,236,000** | |

The critical insight is that the 99.4 percent accuracy figure is not evidence that turnover is under control. It is evidence that the site is spending $412,000 per half-year — the supervisory overhead and rework lines — to *prevent* turnover from becoming an accuracy failure. Indianapolis is buying quality with cost. That is a defensible short-term trade, and General Manager leadership deserves credit for making it deliberately rather than letting accuracy erode. It is not sustainable through a peak season that will add 180 to 220 seasonal associates to a workforce that is already predominantly inexperienced.

**Turnover detail.** Of the 340 separations in H1, 218 occurred within the first 90 days of employment. Exit interview data, available for 61 percent of separations, cites three reasons in rank order: scheduling inflexibility, particularly mandatory Saturday coverage (34 percent); wage competition from two nearby e-commerce fulfillment operations (29 percent); and physical demands of the pick assignment (17 percent). The first two are addressable. The company's Indianapolis starting wage sits $1.35 per hour below the nearest large competing employer as of the most recent market survey.

**Recommendation.** Authorize a targeted wage adjustment for Indianapolis warehouse associates of $1.10 to $1.35 per hour effective before the peak hiring window opens, paired with a shift-bid pilot that converts mandatory Saturday coverage to a premium-paid voluntary bid. The gross labor cost of the wage move on the current Indianapolis warehouse headcount is approximately $1.6 million annualized. If it reduces turnover from 62 percent to 40 percent, the recovered training, productivity, supervisory, and rework cost is approximately $1.7 million annualized on current volume. The move is roughly cost-neutral at the modeled retention improvement and strongly positive at anything better, before counting the reduced peak-season risk.

### 4.2 Groveport, Ohio

**Profile:** 900,000 square feet; 28.7 million units shipped in H1; the network's second-largest facility by footprint.

Groveport shipped volume adequately and failed on quality. Order accuracy came in at 98.1 percent against a contractual 99.5 percent service level — a 1.4-point miss that generated $214,000 in customer chargebacks during the half.

The chargeback figure understates the commercial damage. Chargebacks are the contractual penalty; they do not capture the customer relationship cost, the internal cost of investigating and responding to claims, or the freight cost of expedited replacement shipments, which added an estimated $71,000 not captured in the chargeback line.

**Root cause analysis.** A structured review conducted in May identified three contributing factors, weighted by their share of the accuracy gap:

| Factor | Share of Accuracy Gap | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Slotting integrity | 46% | 2,140 SKUs held slot assignments not refreshed since the 2024 profile; similar-appearing SKUs adjacent in 31% of sampled aisles |
| Pick verification bypass | 33% | Scan-verify step disabled on 6 of 22 pick paths during a March throughput push; not re-enabled until May |
| Inbound putaway errors | 21% | Receiving accuracy at 97.8%; errors propagate to pick face |

The pick verification finding is the most serious item in this report from a controls standpoint. A throughput decision made at the site level disabled a quality control on 27 percent of pick paths for approximately nine weeks, and no network-level control detected it. The accuracy decline was visible in the weekly reporting from mid-March forward; it was attributed to volume mix and not investigated until the customer chargebacks arrived in April.

**Corrective actions taken.** Scan-verify was restored across all 22 pick paths on May 4 and locked at the system-configuration level, requiring VP-of-operations approval to modify. A full slotting reprofile began June 1 and is 58 percent complete as of this report; the remaining 42 percent is scheduled for completion by September 15. Receiving accuracy has been added to the daily site scorecard with a 99.3 percent floor.

**Result to date.** June accuracy recovered to 99.2 percent. This is real improvement but remains three-tenths below the 99.5 percent commitment. The site should not be characterized as recovered until it posts two consecutive months at or above 99.5 percent, which on the current trajectory means August at the earliest.

**Recommendation.** Complete the slotting reprofile ahead of the September 15 target, accelerating to August 31 by adding a second reprofile crew. The incremental cost is approximately $84,000; it protects against entering peak with 42 percent of the pick face on stale slot logic. Additionally, institute a network-wide standing rule that no quality control step may be disabled at site level for throughput reasons, with any exception requiring written VP approval and automatic notification to network analytics.

### 4.3 Louisville, Kentucky

**Profile:** 450,000 square feet; 19.4 million units shipped in H1; the network's smallest operating footprint and its most constrained.

Louisville is running at 96 percent of usable storage capacity. Overtime is at 18 percent of total hours worked. In June, the site refused two inbound trailers because it had no dock space to receive them.

These three facts are one fact. A facility at 96 percent storage utilization has lost the working space that makes efficient operation possible. Travel paths lengthen because product is stored wherever it fits rather than where it belongs. Putaway takes longer. Replenishment competes with picking for aisle access. Dock doors that should be turning inbound freight are occupied by staged outbound and by overflow that has nowhere to go. Overtime at 18 percent is not a labor-planning failure; it is the labor cost of operating a facility that has run out of room.

**Cost impact.** Louisville's $0.44 cost per unit against a $0.40 budget produced $776,000 in unfavorable variance — the second-largest site overrun in the network and the largest on a per-unit basis excluding Winchester. Overtime premium alone accounts for approximately $494,000 of the gap. The remainder is travel-time inefficiency and duplicate handling.

**Refused trailers.** The two June refusals are the most consequential item at this site, not because two trailers matter in a network shipping 132 million units, but because of what they signal. Refusing inbound freight transfers cost and risk to the customer, damages the service narrative, and in most contract warehousing agreements creates exposure regardless of cause. Both refusals were on the same account. Both required the customer to hold freight at origin at their expense. Neither was escalated to the VP level at the time.

**Capacity options evaluated.**

| Option | Capacity Added | Annual Cost | Lead Time | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overflow lease, Louisville metro | ~90,000 sq ft | $610,000 | 45–60 days | Viable; shuttle cost adds $0.02/unit on transferred volume |
| Divert slow-moving SKUs to Winchester | ~110,000 sq ft equiv. | $340,000 net | 60–75 days | Strongest option; addresses two problems |
| Mezzanine / racking densification | ~40,000 sq ft equiv. | $780,000 capital | 120+ days | Too slow for peak; retain for FY27 |
| Reduce inbound receipt windows | N/A | Minimal | Immediate | Palliative only; pushes problem to customer |

**Recommendation.** Pursue the Winchester diversion as the primary action, detailed in Section 6, with an overflow lease as the contingency if diversion cannot be executed before October 1. Louisville must not enter peak season at 96 percent utilization; the operational target is 87 percent or below by October 15. Additionally, establish an immediate escalation protocol: any inbound refusal is reported to the VP of operations within four hours and to network analytics same-day, with a written customer communication issued within 24 hours.

**Profile:** 1,100,000 square feet; 34.9 million units shipped in H1; the network's largest facility; General Manager Tanya Broussard; site placed on Beaumont Home Brands performance improvement plan in April 2026.

Carlisle shipped on time at 91.3 percent against a 98 percent contractual commitment. This is a 6.7-point miss on the network's largest single-customer site, and it is the reason Beaumont initiated a performance improvement plan. Section 5 treats the improvement plan and the WMS upgrade delay in detail; this section covers the operating picture.

**Performance detail by month:**

| Month | Units Shipped (M) | On-Time % | Commitment | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| October 2025 | 6.1 | 94.8% | 98.0% | −3.2 |
| November 2025 | 7.4 | 92.1% | 98.0% | −5.9 |
| December 2025 | 6.8 | 88.6% | 98.0% | −9.4 |
| January 2026 | 4.9 | 90.2% | 98.0% | −7.8 |
| February 2026 | 4.7 | 91.7% | 98.0% | −6.3 |
| March 2026 | 5.0 | 92.4% | 98.0% | −5.6 |
| **H1 Total / Avg** | **34.9** | **91.3%** | **98.0%** | **−6.7** |
| April 2026 | 5.2 | 93.1% | 98.0% | −4.9 |
| May 2026 | 5.4 | 94.0% | 98.0% | −4.0 |
| June 2026 | 5.5 | 94.6% | 98.0% | −3.4 |

The trajectory since the improvement plan began in April is genuinely positive — 3.3 points of recovery in three months. It is also insufficient. At the current rate of improvement, Carlisle reaches 98 percent in approximately February 2027, which is four months past the improvement plan's review date and one full peak season too late.

**Root causes.** Broussard's site analysis, validated by network analytics in May, identifies the following distribution of late shipments:

| Cause | Share of Late Orders | Addressable Without WMS Upgrade? |
|---|---|---|
| Wave planning limitations in legacy WMS | 38% | Partially — manual workarounds available |
| Dock scheduling conflicts / carrier no-shows | 24% | Yes |
| Inventory location accuracy | 19% | Yes |
| Labor coverage on second shift | 12% | Yes |
| Customer order file timing | 7% | Requires Beaumont action |

This distribution is the most important analytical finding in the report. Sixty-two percent of Carlisle's late shipments have causes that do not require the WMS upgrade to fix. The prevailing narrative — inside Wabash Crossing and in the April presentation to Beaumont — has been that Carlisle's recovery is gated on the system upgrade. The data does not support that. The upgrade addresses the largest single cause and does so incompletely; the majority of the gap is dock discipline, inventory accuracy, second-shift staffing, and customer-side file timing.

This is both a problem and an opportunity. The problem is that the company anchored its April commitment to Beaumont on a March-to-September slip that it must now explain. The opportunity is that the company can walk into the joint session with a recovery plan that does not depend on the delayed upgrade and can therefore commit to dates it will actually hit.

**Labor and cost.** Carlisle's cost per unit was $0.41 against a $0.40 budget — a one-cent overrun and the second-best cost performance in the network. Turnover ran at 31 percent, well below Indianapolis. The site's problem is not cost or labor stability. It is execution discipline in scheduling, inventory control, and shift coverage, compounded by a system that makes wave planning harder than it should be. This distinction matters for the joint session: Carlisle is not underperforming because it is under-resourced.

### 4.5 Winchester, Virginia

**Profile:** 520,000 square feet; opened August 2025; 8.1 million units shipped in H1; receiving 68 percent of contracted volume; losing approximately $180,000 per month.

Winchester is ten months old and has never reached the volume its business case assumed. Contracted volume for the first half was 11.9 million units; actual was 8.1 million, a shortfall of 3.8 million units, or 32 percent.

**Financial picture:**

| Line Item | H1 Actual | H1 Plan | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $4,940,000 | $7,259,000 | ($2,319,000) |
| Direct labor | $2,187,000 | $2,834,000 | $647,000 |
| Facility fixed cost (lease, utilities, tax) | $2,610,000 | $2,610,000 | $0 |
| Equipment and systems | $874,000 | $874,000 | $0 |
| Management and overhead | $1,349,000 | $1,278,000 | ($71,000) |
| **Operating result** | **($2,080,000)** | **($337,000)** | **($1,743,000)** |

The monthly loss of approximately $180,000 in the reported figure reflects the run-rate in the later months of the half after startup costs normalized; the full-half loss including startup was $2.08 million.

The structure of the loss is unambiguous. Fixed costs of $3.48 million for the half are being spread across 8.1 million units rather than the 11.9 million assumed, producing $0.43 per unit of fixed absorption against a planned $0.29. That $0.14 gap is the majority of Winchester's $0.18 per-unit overrun. Winchester does not have a productivity problem or a labor problem. It has a volume problem, and volume problems in fixed-cost facilities are not solvable by operating better.

**Why volume is short.** Three causes, in order of magnitude:

The largest is customer ramp deferral. Two of the three anchor accounts committed to Winchester deferred their transition from legacy providers — one by two quarters, one indefinitely pending its own network study. Together these represent 2.6 million of the 3.8 million unit shortfall. Neither deferral reflects Wabash Crossing performance; both reflect customer-side decisions.

The second is seasonal shape. The Winchester business case assumed a volume curve matching the network average. The accounts that did transition run flatter seasonality, delivering less H1 concentration than modeled. This accounts for roughly 0.7 million units.

The third is a smaller account that reduced its commitment by approximately 0.5 million units following an internal SKU rationalization.

**Recommendation.** Winchester's fixed cost base exists whether or not volume arrives. The correct response is to load it — from any source, including internal transfer. The Louisville diversion described in Section 6 is the fastest available action and is developed there. In parallel, the commercial organization should be tasked with a Winchester-specific pipeline review with a target of 4.0 million incremental annualized units identified by October 1 and 2.0 million contracted by January 1. Absent volume improvement, Winchester should be brought to the executive committee for a structural decision — sublease of a portion of the footprint, renegotiation of lease terms, or exit — no later than the FY27 planning cycle.

## 5. Carlisle Performance Improvement Plan and WMS Upgrade Delay

### 5.1 Status of the Improvement Plan

Beaumont Home Brands placed Carlisle on a performance improvement plan in April 2026 following six consecutive months of on-time performance below the 98 percent contractual commitment. The plan as executed contains four elements: a monthly performance review with Beaumont supply chain leadership, a requirement to reach 96 percent on-time by August 2026 and 98 percent by October 2026, a commitment to complete the WMS upgrade, and a reservation of Beaumont's right to reallocate volume if the October milestone is missed.

Current status against these elements:

| Element | Commitment | Status | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly reviews | Ongoing | 3 of 3 completed | On track |
| 96% on-time by August 2026 | 96.0% | 94.6% (June) | At risk |
| 98% on-time by October 2026 | 98.0% | Trajectory implies ~95.5% | Will miss without intervention |
| WMS upgrade complete | March 2026 | Rescheduled to September 2026 | Missed; six-month slip |
| Volume reallocation reserved | — | No action taken by Beaumont | Live risk |

The August milestone is reachable. June closed at 94.6 percent, and the non-WMS causes identified in Section 4.4 — dock scheduling, inventory accuracy, second-shift coverage — represent enough addressable gap to close 1.4 points in two months if pursued with focus. The October milestone at 98 percent is not reachable on the current plan and will not become reachable simply because the WMS goes live in September; a September cutover inside peak season is more likely to cost on-time performance in the near term than to improve it.

### 5.2 The WMS Upgrade Delay

The Carlisle WMS upgrade was scheduled for March 2026 and will now go live in September 2026. The causes of the slip, established through the project post-mortem in May, were: integration testing failures against the Beaumont order interface requiring rework of the mapping layer (approximately ten weeks), vendor resource reallocation following a competing implementation (approximately six weeks), and a decision to expand scope to include labor management modules (approximately eight weeks, partially concurrent).

The scope expansion decision deserves specific attention. It was made in January at the site and IT-director level without escalation, on the reasoning that a single cutover was preferable to two. The reasoning is sound in isolation. It was not sound in the context of a facility already missing its service commitment and about to be placed on an improvement plan. This is a governance failure of the same type as the Groveport scan-verify decision: a locally rational choice made without visibility to the network-level consequences.

**The September cutover risk.** Going live in September places a major system cutover approximately four weeks before peak volume begins to build. Industry experience and our own Groveport cutover in 2023 suggest a six-to-ten-week stabilization period following a WMS go-live, during which throughput typically runs 10 to 20 percent below pre-cutover levels and error rates elevate. Applied to Carlisle, a September 15 go-live would put the stabilization window directly on top of the October and November peak.

**Options:**

| Option | Description | Risk | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Proceed with September go-live as planned | High — stabilization inside peak; on-time likely falls below current 94.6% in Oct/Nov | Not recommended |
| B | Defer go-live to January 2027, post-peak | Moderate — requires explaining a second delay to Beaumont; extends legacy system dependency | **Recommended** |
| C | Phase go-live: core WMS September, labor modules January | Moderate-high — reduces but does not eliminate peak stabilization risk; adds integration complexity | Fallback |

**Recommendation: Option B.** Defer the Carlisle WMS go-live to January 2027 and rebuild the improvement plan commitment around the 62 percent of causes that do not require the upgrade. This is the harder conversation with Beaumont and the better operational decision. The company should not enter peak season on its largest customer's most troubled site with an unstabilized system.

### 5.3 Revised Carlisle Recovery Plan

The following plan is proposed as the basis for the joint session. It is built entirely on non-WMS actions and therefore carries dates that can be committed to without dependency on the vendor.

| Action | Owner | Target Date | Expected On-Time Contribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dock appointment system with carrier scorecards and 2-hour no-show penalty | Broussard / Transportation | September 1, 2026 | +1.6 pts |
| Cycle-count intensification: A-items weekly, B-items monthly | Broussard | August 15, 2026 | +1.2 pts |
| Second-shift staffing to 100% of standard, including 14-person permanent add | Broussard / HR | September 15, 2026 | +0.9 pts |
| Manual wave-planning protocol for top 40 order profiles | Network Analytics / Site | August 1, 2026 | +1.1 pts |
| Beaumont order-file timing agreement (customer action required) | Villanueva / Beaumont | September 30, 2026 | +0.5 pts |
| **Cumulative expected on-time** | | **October 2026** | **~97.5%** |

This plan produces approximately 97.5 percent on-time by October — short of 98 percent but within half a point, achieved without the WMS upgrade, and with a credible path to 98 percent by December as the actions mature. The plan requires one element of Beaumont participation: the order-file timing agreement, which addresses the 7 percent of late orders caused by order files arriving inside the committed processing window. That request should be made directly in the joint session.

**Cost of the recovery plan:** $612,000 annualized, comprising the second-shift staffing addition ($478,000), dock appointment system licensing and implementation ($71,000), and cycle-count labor ($63,000). Against $214,000 in Groveport chargebacks as a reference point and $96 million in annual Beaumont revenue at stake, this is not a close call.

## 6. Louisville–Winchester Volume Rebalance

Louisville is at 96 percent utilization and refusing freight. Winchester is at 68 percent of contracted volume and losing $180,000 monthly on unabsorbed fixed cost. These conditions are complementary and have been managed as separate problems by separate site organizations.

**Proposal.** Transfer slow-moving and seasonal-reserve inventory from Louisville to Winchester, targeting SKUs with turn rates below 4.0 annually and no same-day service requirement. Analysis of the Louisville SKU base identifies approximately 3,100 SKUs meeting these criteria, representing roughly 110,000 square feet of equivalent storage — enough to bring Louisville from 96 percent to approximately 86 percent utilization.

| Element | Value |
|---|---|
| SKUs transferred | ~3,100 |
| Louisville space released | ~110,000 sq ft equiv. |
| Louisville post-transfer utilization | ~86% |
| Winchester incremental volume | ~1.4M units annualized |
| Winchester fixed-cost absorption improvement | ~$0.05 / unit network |
| One-time transfer cost | $290,000 |
| Ongoing shuttle / inventory carrying cost | $340,000 annually |
| Louisville overtime reduction (est.) | ($690,000) annually |
| **Net annual benefit** | **~$350,000** |

The net benefit understates the value. The transfer removes the inbound refusal risk at Louisville, restores operating space before peak, and improves Winchester's absorption without requiring new customer acquisition. It also creates an operating precedent — treating the network as a network rather than five facilities — that this review recommends institutionalizing.

**Required approvals.** Customer consent is required for inventory relocation on two of the affected accounts. Both accounts have contractual language permitting relocation with 30 days' notice provided service levels are maintained. Notice should be issued no later than August 15 to permit an October 1 completion.

## 7. Safety Performance

Network safety results for the first half:

| Metric | H1 FY2026 | H1 FY2025 | Target | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Total Recordable Incident Rate (TRIR) | 3.4 | 3.9 | 3.0 | Improving; below target |
| Lost-time incidents | 11 | 16 | 8 | Improving; above target |
| Days away, restricted, transferred (DART) | 1.9 | 2.4 | 1.6 | Improving; above target |
| Near-miss reports filed | 847 | 512 | 700 | Above target |
| Powered industrial truck incidents | 23 | 19 | 14 | **Deteriorating** |

Network safety is improving on every measure except one, and the exception is the one that correlates most strongly with the operating conditions described in this report. Powered industrial truck incidents rose from 19 to 23 year over year. Distribution by site:

| Site | PIT Incidents | Incidents per 100 Associates |
|---|---|---|
| Indianapolis | 9 | 1.4 |
| Louisville | 7 | 2.1 |
| Carlisle | 4 | 0.5 |
| Groveport | 3 | 0.4 |
| Winchester | 0 | 0.0 |

Indianapolis and Louisville account for 16 of 23 incidents. These are the site with 62 percent turnover and the site at 96 percent storage utilization. Inexperienced operators and congested aisles are the two most reliable predictors of forklift incidents in warehouse operations, and the network is running both conditions simultaneously at these two facilities.

The increase in near-miss reporting from 512 to 847 is a positive signal and should be read as improved reporting culture rather than deteriorating conditions; near-miss volume rising while TRIR falls is the expected pattern in a maturing safety program.

**Recommendation.** Implement a mandatory PIT recertification and supervised-hours requirement at Indianapolis for all operators with under 180 days of tenure, and an aisle-congestion standard at Louisville that halts PIT operation in any aisle where clearance falls below the equipment standard. Both actions should be complete before peak hiring begins. Safety performance at these two sites will worsen under peak conditions unless it is addressed before peak conditions arrive.

Network warehouse turnover at 44 percent is within the range for contract logistics but conceals a 31-point spread between the best site (Carlisle) and the worst (Indianapolis). Carlisle's comparatively strong retention despite its service difficulties reinforces the Section 4.4 finding that Carlisle's problem is process, not people.

Winchester's 44 percent turnover in a facility running at 68 percent of volume is a warning. Underloaded facilities typically have low turnover because work is comfortable; Winchester's elevated rate likely reflects associate perception of the site's uncertain future. Volume improvement addresses this as well.

Overtime at 12 percent network-wide is above the 8 percent planning standard, driven overwhelmingly by Louisville at 18 percent. Resolving the Louisville capacity constraint is the single largest available overtime reduction in the network.
