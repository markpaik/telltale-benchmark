## Executive Summary

We are asking the committee to authorize $18.9 million to reorganize the Springfield floor into five machining cells, add nine CNC machines and two robotic load stations, and open a second shift of 84 people over 20 months. The first two cells go live in month seven. Financing is $14 million of holding-company debt and $4.9 million of plant cash, consistent with the structure the committee has used for prior capital requests.

This is not a discretionary expansion. Our largest customer, Vantage AgWorks, is 38 percent of our $126 million in revenue and has told us directly that the 2027 rebid requires 6 percent annual price reductions for three years or it will dual-source. We cannot absorb cuts of that size on our current cost structure, which is carrying 6.1 percent scrap against a 2.5 percent target, running at 91 percent capacity with twelve-week lead times, and staffed by a machinist workforce averaging 54 years old with 61 employees — roughly a quarter of our skilled trades — retirement-eligible within three years. In February we took a $47,000 OSHA citation for machine guarding, a symptom of an aging plant layout built around individual machines rather than guarded, engineered cells.

We understand the skepticism this committee brings to equipment requests. The $6.4 million finishing line installed in 2023 is running at 40 percent utilization, and Philippa Wrenn has been direct that patience for another underperforming asset is thin. We have built this proposal differently: it is staged, it is gated to performance at the first two cells before the remaining capital is released, and it is tied to a specific, dated commercial threat rather than a general efficiency argument. We are asking the committee to approve the full $18.9 million commitment on November 6, with the understanding that cells three through five will not draw capital until cells one and two demonstrate the scrap and throughput results this proposal is built on.

## 1. The Problem

### 1.1 A customer ultimatum with a fixed date

Vantage AgWorks is 38 percent of our revenue, or roughly $48 million a year. The account has told us the 2027 rebid will carry a demand for 6 percent annual price reductions for three consecutive years, or Vantage will bring in a second source. A 6 percent annual cut compounded over three years is not a pricing adjustment we can absorb through attrition or minor efficiency gains — it would remove approximately $8.6 million in revenue-equivalent margin pressure from that account alone by the third year, against a business that already runs thin margins in hydraulic manifold and valve body machining. Losing meaningful share of Vantage, on the other hand, would take out a customer we cannot replace on our current sales cycle. Either path — conceding price out of margin or losing the account outright — is a threat to the plant's viability as currently configured. The only way through is structural cost reduction large enough to fund the price concessions without giving up the margin that keeps this plant investable.

### 1.2 Scrap is costing us the exact margin we need to protect

We are running scrap at 6.1 percent against a 2.5 percent target. Quality Director Ivan Bogdanov has traced 60 percent of that scrap to setup variation — the changeovers, fixturing inconsistency, and operator-dependent adjustments that come from running mixed part families through general-purpose machines rather than dedicated, engineered cells. That is scrap we know how to remove, and removing it is the single largest lever we have to fund the Vantage price concessions without cutting into the margin the holding company depends on.

### 1.3 The plant is out of capacity and the workforce is aging out from under us

We are at 91 percent capacity with twelve-week lead times. We are turning away work we could otherwise book, and our lead times are a competitive liability against any supplier willing to dual-source Vantage's business. At the same time, our machinist workforce averages 54 years old, and 61 of our skilled machinists — the people who carry the setup knowledge Bogdanov's data points to — are retirement-eligible within three years. Springfield's regional pipeline produced 34 new machinists last year. Doing nothing means we lose experienced setup capability faster than we can replace it, on machines that are already at capacity, while trying to defend a customer that wants lower prices with less flexibility, not more.

### 1.4 Safety exposure is compounding the operational risk

The February OSHA citation for machine guarding, at $47,000, is not an isolated event — it reflects a plant floor built around standalone machines rather than engineered, guarded work cells. As we bring in a larger and, on the second shift, less experienced workforce, that exposure grows. We cannot respond to a growth mandate by adding people and hours to the current layout without also addressing the guarding and safety architecture of the floor itself.

### 1.5 These four problems are one problem

Customer pricing pressure, scrap, capacity, and workforce aging are not four separate issues competing for capital. They are the same problem seen from four angles: a plant built for a different era of manual setup and single-shift operation is no longer generating the cost structure, throughput, or safety profile the business needs to keep its largest customer and its people. The cell reorganization is the single investment that addresses all four at once.

## 2. The Proposal: Cell Layout and Equipment

We propose reorganizing the current functional floor layout — machines grouped by type, parts traveling between departments — into five dedicated machining cells, each built around a family of hydraulic manifold or valve body parts with similar setup, tooling, and tolerance requirements.

**Equipment scope:**

- **Nine CNC machining centers**, replacing and supplementing existing general-purpose equipment, configured for the specific part families each cell will run rather than general-purpose flexibility across all parts.
- **Two robotic load stations**, installed at the two highest-volume cells, handling repetitive load/unload cycles on standardized parts and reducing the manual handling steps that are the most common source of setup-driven variation and the most common source of guarding exposure that led to the February citation.
- **Cell-specific fixturing and tooling**, standardized within each cell so that changeovers between the parts a given cell runs require less operator judgment and produce less part-to-part variation.

**Why cells, and why this addresses scrap directly:** Bogdanov's analysis attributes 60 percent of current scrap to setup variation — inconsistency introduced when the same machine runs different part families with different fixturing on different shifts with different operators. A cell dedicated to a part family with standardized fixturing removes the variable that drives that scrap. We are not asking the committee to fund a general capacity increase and hope quality improves as a byproduct; we are funding the specific fix for the specific cause Bogdanov has identified and quantified.

**Why cells, and why this addresses capacity:** Five cells, each optimized for its part family rather than shared across all part families, run faster changeovers and higher first-pass yield, which increases effective throughput even before the second shift is counted. Combined with the second shift, we expect the twelve-week lead time to come down meaningfully — enough to both meet Vantage's delivery expectations under whatever revised contract comes out of the 2027 rebid and to free capacity for the diversification work described in Section 6.

**Why cells, and why this addresses guarding and safety:** Cells are engineered as complete work units with guarding, robotic handling, and standardized workflows designed in from the start, rather than retrofitted onto legacy standalone machines. This directly addresses the condition that produced the February citation and reduces the exposure of adding 84 new second-shift employees to the floor.

## 3. Workforce: Hiring, Training, and the Second Shift

The project adds a second shift of 84 people, phased in over the 20-month installation rather than hired all at once. HR Director Tameka Sallis has costed the second shift's fully loaded annual run rate at $5.4 million.

**The hiring constraint is real and we are planning around it, not past it.** The Springfield-area pipeline produced 34 new machinists last year. Hiring 84 people for the second shift, on top of normal attrition and the 61 employees who become retirement-eligible over the next three years, cannot rely on new graduates alone. Our plan draws from three pools:

- **New machinist graduates** from the regional pipeline, hired against the 20-month ramp rather than a single intake, spreading the draw on a 34-per-year pipeline across nearly two hiring cycles.
- **Experienced machinists recruited from the broader region**, including candidates from adjacent trades who can be brought up to cell-specific standard work faster than a from-scratch apprenticeship, since cell-based setup is more standardized and easier to train against than the general-purpose skill set the current floor demands.
- **Internal cross-training and knowledge capture** from our most experienced first-shift machinists — including many of the 61 who are retirement-eligible — who will be asked to help design and validate cell standard work before they leave, converting tacit setup knowledge into documented, cell-specific procedures that reduce the training burden on new hires and directly reinforce Bogdanov's scrap-reduction case.

**Training sequencing follows the phasing plan in Section 4.** As each cell comes online, the operators assigned to it complete cell-specific training against the standardized fixturing and work instructions developed for that cell, rather than the broader cross-training a general-purpose machine would require. This is faster to execute and, because it is standardized, produces more consistent output than training against the current floor's variable setups — reinforcing the scrap case a second way.

**This investment is also our answer to the retirement cliff, not a separate problem to solve later.** Without the cells, our 61 retirement-eligible machinists leave over the next three years carrying setup knowledge that exists only in their heads, on a floor that depends on that knowledge to hit even a 6.1 percent scrap rate. With the cells, that knowledge is captured into standard work before it walks out the door, and the standardized cell structure means the next generation of machinists — hired against a tighter regional pipeline — needs less tenure to reach full proficiency than the current floor requires.

## 4. Phasing Against Production Commitments

We cannot take capacity offline to build this. The plant is at 91 percent utilization with twelve-week lead times and a customer base, led by Vantage, that will not tolerate a missed delivery in the run-up to a rebid that is already testing the relationship. The installation is phased specifically to protect current production while the floor is rebuilt around it.

| Phase | Timing | Scope | Production Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 | Months 1–6 | Facility reconfiguration begins on lowest-volume floor section; first two cells' equipment ordered and installed; initial second-shift hiring and cell-specific training begins | Current first-shift production continues on existing equipment in unaffected floor areas; no committed customer delivery is scheduled against Phase 1 floor space |
| Phase 2 | Month 7 | Cells 1 and 2 go live | Vantage and other top-account volume begins transitioning to cells 1–2 as they demonstrate stable output; legacy equipment for the transitioned part families is decommissioned only after two consecutive months of cell performance at or above legacy scrap and throughput levels |
| Phase 3 | Months 8–14 | Cells 3 and 4 constructed and commissioned, sequenced to avoid overlapping floor disruption with Phase 2 cells; second-shift hiring continues against the pipeline constraint described in Section 3 | Cells 1–2 running at full production; remaining legacy floor continues unaffected production for part families not yet transitioned |
| Phase 4 | Months 15–20 | Cell 5 constructed and commissioned; second shift reaches full complement of 84; final legacy equipment retired | Full five-cell, two-shift operation live; lead time and scrap targets evaluated against the case in Section 5 |

Two features of this schedule are deliberate and directly responsive to the committee's concerns:

**First, we do not decommission legacy capacity for a given part family until the replacing cell has proven itself for two consecutive months.** This means we are never dependent on unproven equipment to meet an existing delivery commitment. It also means the capital committed to cells 3 through 5 can be evaluated against real performance data from cells 1 and 2 before further capital is drawn — the gate described in the Executive Summary.

**Second, the highest-volume, highest-scrap part families — the ones Bogdanov's data identifies as carrying the most setup-variation scrap — are prioritized into cells 1 and 2.** This is deliberate: it means the earliest performance data the committee sees is drawn from the highest-impact part families, and it means the scrap and throughput case is tested and validated before the majority of the capital is spent.

### 5.3 Return case

The return on this investment is built on three components, each tied directly to a problem identified in Section 1:

**Scrap reduction.** Reducing scrap from 6.1 percent toward the 2.5 percent target — achievable to the extent Bogdanov's 60-percent-attributable-to-setup-variation figure holds once cells are fully live — represents approximately $2.0 million in annual cost avoidance at current production volume. This is the most defensible and most directly evidenced line in the return case, since it is tied to a specific, already-diagnosed cause rather than a general efficiency assumption.

**Price-concession absorption.** Vantage's demanded 6 percent annual price reduction, applied against approximately $48 million in Vantage revenue, represents roughly $2.9 million in margin pressure in year one alone, compounding toward $8.6 million cumulative by year three if conceded without offset. The scrap savings above, combined with the throughput and labor-efficiency gains from cell-based operation, are what allow us to meet Vantage's pricing demand without simply transferring the cut to margin. This is the core commercial logic of the proposal: the investment is what lets us keep the account on Vantage's terms without losing the plant's profitability.

**Capacity-driven revenue growth.** Reducing lead times from twelve weeks and adding second-shift capacity allows us to book business we currently turn away and to pursue the customer diversification this plant needs given Vantage's 38 percent concentration. We are not building a specific new-customer revenue line into this case, because we consider it a benefit beyond the base case rather than something the committee should approve capital against on projection alone — but it is a material additional upside the base case does not capture.

Combining scrap savings and price-concession absorption, and holding capacity-driven growth in reserve as upside, we estimate approximately $4.9 million in annual benefit once all five cells and the full second shift are running at Phase 4. Against $18.9 million in capital, that implies a payback period of approximately four years from full ramp, or roughly five and a half years from project start including the 20-month installation window. We consider this a conservative case: it does not include capacity-driven revenue growth, and it assumes only the scrap improvement Bogdanov's existing diagnosis supports rather than further gains from cell-based standard work maturing over time.

## 6. Alternatives Considered

We evaluated three alternatives to this proposal and rejected each for the reasons below.

### 6.1 Concede price out of margin

We could accept Vantage's 6 percent annual reduction for three years without a corresponding cost-structure change, funding it from margin. At roughly $8.6 million in cumulative margin pressure by year three against a plant already carrying 6.1 percent scrap, this would erode profitability to a level we believe would itself draw committee scrutiny, without addressing the underlying scrap, capacity, or workforce problems that will still need to be solved afterward. This alternative solves nothing; it only defers the investment decision while the workforce continues to age out and scrap continues to compound the margin pressure.

### 6.2 Outsource finishing operations

We considered outsourcing finishing work rather than investing further in in-house machining capacity, particularly given that the 2023 finishing line — a $6.4 million investment — is running at 40 percent utilization. We looked seriously at whether that underutilized asset argues for outsourcing finishing altogether and redirecting this capital elsewhere.

We rejected this for two reasons. First, finishing is not where the scrap problem lives — Bogdanov's data ties scrap to setup variation in machining, not finishing, so outsourcing finishing does not touch the cost driver this proposal is built to fix. Second, outsourcing finishing would add an external dependency and lead-time variable at the exact moment we are trying to shorten lead times to defend the Vantage account; introducing an outside vendor into the critical path during a rebid negotiation increases risk rather than reducing it. We do think the 40 percent utilization on the finishing line deserves its own review, separate from this proposal, and we are prepared to bring the committee a specific plan to raise finishing utilization or right-size that asset in a future session. That is a real problem, but it is not solved by, or a substitute for, the machining cell investment in front of the committee today.

### 6.3 Smaller, incremental investment

We considered a reduced scope — fewer cells, no second shift, or a phased approach spread over a longer horizon without committing full capital up front. We rejected an incremental approach for one reason: the 2027 Vantage rebid has a fixed date, and a scaled-down version of this project does not generate enough scrap reduction or capacity to meet that deadline with room to prove results before the negotiation. The phasing in Section 4 already builds in a gate — cells 3 through 5 do not draw capital until cells 1 and 2 prove out — but the full scope needs to be authorized now so that the installation timeline lands before the rebid, not after it.

## 7. Risks and Mitigations

**Execution risk, given the 2023 finishing line precedent.** We take Philippa Wrenn's concern seriously. Our answer is structural, not rhetorical: this proposal is gated. Cells 3 through 5 do not receive capital until cells 1 and 2 demonstrate, over two consecutive months, scrap and throughput performance at or above the levels this case depends on. The committee will see real performance data from a live phase of this project before the majority of the capital is committed, which the 2023 finishing line decision did not have the opportunity to build in.

**Labor market risk.** Hiring 84 second-shift employees against a regional pipeline of 34 machinists a year is the single largest execution risk in this plan. We are mitigating it by spreading hiring across the full 20-month window rather than a single intake, drawing from adjacent trades and experienced regional candidates in addition to new graduates, and building cell-specific training that requires less ramp time than general-purpose machinist training. If hiring runs behind schedule, the phasing plan in Section 4 allows cells to come online in sequence rather than simultaneously, which limits the exposure of any single hiring shortfall to one cell rather than the whole project.

**Vantage may dual-source regardless of this investment.** We cannot guarantee this investment retains the account; Vantage's decision will depend on factors beyond our cost structure. What we can control is whether we enter that negotiation able to offer the price reduction without conceding the plant's margin, which this investment is built to do. Doing nothing guarantees we cannot make that offer; this investment gives us the option to make it.

**Scrap improvement may not fully materialize as modeled.** Bogdanov's 60 percent attribution is a diagnosis based on current data, not a guarantee of the cell design's performance. This is precisely why the return case in Section 5 is built on the conservative scrap-and-price-absorption case rather than including capacity-driven revenue upside, and why the phase gate in Section 4 tests this assumption on the highest-volume, highest-scrap part families first, before the bulk of the capital is committed.

**Safety risk during transition.** Running legacy equipment and new cells simultaneously during the 20-month installation, while onboarding a second shift, increases the exposure that produced the February citation if not actively managed. Cell-specific guarding is designed in from commissioning, not retrofitted, and legacy equipment is decommissioned on the schedule in Section 4 rather than run indefinitely alongside new cells.

**Financing risk to the holding company.** The $14 million in holding-company debt is a real commitment against Kestrel Bridge's balance sheet. We believe the return case in Section 5, and the existential nature of the Vantage exposure in Section 1, justify that commitment, but we recognize it is the committee's call to weigh against other capital priorities across the portfolio.

## Appendix A: Cell-by-Cell Detail

**Cell 1 — High-volume manifold family.** This cell absorbs the part family currently carrying the highest scrap rate on the floor, driven by frequent changeovers between three closely related manifold variants on shared general-purpose equipment. Under the current layout, operators reset fixturing an average of several times per shift to move between variants; Bogdanov's data shows this changeover is the single largest contributor to the plant's setup-variation scrap. Cell 1 dedicates fixturing to each variant permanently, eliminating the changeover step for the highest-volume mix. This cell also receives the first of the two robotic load stations, given its volume.

**Cell 2 — Vantage-specific valve body family.** This cell is dedicated to the valve body parts run against Vantage's program, which is deliberate: it means the earliest production data available to validate this proposal's core commercial logic — that cell-based manufacturing can absorb Vantage's price demand without margin loss — comes directly from Vantage's own parts, not from an analogous but different part family. This cell receives the second robotic load station.

**Cell 3 — Mixed construction-equipment valve bodies.** Lower volume than Cells 1 and 2 but broader part variety; commissioned in Phase 3 once the standard-work documentation method proven in Cells 1 and 2 is available to apply against a wider mix.

**Cell 4 — Secondary manifold family.** Runs the manifold variants not assigned to Cell 1; sequenced alongside Cell 3 to keep legacy floor disruption contained to one section of the plant at a time, per the phasing logic in Section 4.

**Cell 5 — Low-volume, high-tolerance specialty parts.** The most complex part family on the floor and the last commissioned, both because it carries the least production risk if commissioning runs behind schedule and because it benefits most from lessons captured in the standard work developed for the first four cells.

## Appendix B: Sensitivity on the Return Case

The $4.9 million annual benefit estimate in Section 5 depends most heavily on the scrap-reduction assumption. If Bogdanov's 60 percent attribution proves optimistic and cells achieve only half that improvement, annual scrap savings fall from approximately $2.0 million to approximately $1.0 million, and total annual benefit falls to approximately $3.9 million — extending payback from roughly four years to approximately five years from full ramp. Even in this downside case, the investment remains one we believe the plant needs to make: the alternative is entering the Vantage rebid with no cost offset at all, which Section 6.1 shows is not a viable path regardless of how the cells perform. The phase gate exists precisely so the committee sees which of these cases is materializing before committing capital to Cells 3 through 5.

If scrap improvement instead exceeds Bogdanov's estimate — plausible, since cell-based standard work tends to compound in benefit as operators gain tenure on a fixed part family rather than rotating across variants — annual benefit could exceed $6 million once all five cells mature past their first year of operation, shortening payback correspondingly. We have not built this upside into the base case in Section 5, consistent with our approach of presenting the committee a conservative figure rather than an optimistic one.

## Appendix D: Questions We Expect from the Committee

We anticipate the committee will press on several points beyond what is addressed above, and we want to answer them here rather than leave them for the meeting alone.

**Why not wait until closer to the 2027 rebid, when Vantage's terms may be clearer?** Waiting compresses the installation window against the deadline it is meant to serve. The 20-month build, plus the two-month validation window built into the Phase 3 gate, plus a period of stable production before we would want to walk into a rebid negotiation citing cell performance, consumes most of the runway between now and 2027 as it is. Waiting a year to start would mean walking into the rebid with cells barely commissioned and no track record to point to, which defeats the purpose of the investment.

**Why $18.9 million and not a smaller number that still gets two cells built?** A two-cell build addresses Vantage's specific part family, described in Appendix A as Cell 2, but does not address the plant-wide scrap and capacity problem described in Section 1. It would improve our position with Vantage specifically while leaving the rest of the floor, and the rest of our customer base, exposed to the same scrap rate, the same capacity ceiling, and the same retirement cliff. We believe partial funding solves the negotiating problem in front of us but not the plant problem underneath it, and that the committee will be back in this room in three years facing the same underlying issues with less runway to fix them.

**What happens to the 2023 finishing line question in the meantime?** We are not asking the committee to resolve finishing-line utilization as part of this decision, and we do not believe this proposal should be held against that unresolved question. We are committing, separately from this request, to bring the committee a specific finishing-line utilization plan at the first quarterly session following this meeting.

**What is the plant's exposure if this proposal is not approved?** Without this investment, we enter the Vantage rebid with 6.1 percent scrap, a workforce losing its most experienced setup operators to retirement, and no cost offset for a demanded 6 percent annual price reduction. We would be forced into Section 6.1's rejected alternative — conceding price out of margin — not by choice but by default, since it would be the only option left on the table. We do not believe that is a position the plant or the committee wants to be in eleven months from now with less time to act than we have today.
