# Predicting Lead: What Fourteen Months of Field Verification Reveal About the Reliability of the Licking Valley Regional Water Authority's Service Line Records

**A Research Brief Prepared for the Board of Trustees**

Prepared by Reginald Boateng, Data Analyst, Engineering Services Division
Commissioned by Alma Restrepo, Deputy Director, at the direction of Sun-Hee Park, Chief Engineer
August 9, 2026

---

## 1. The Question and What Was Found

The Licking Valley Regional Water Authority serves 96,400 connections across Licking, Knox, and Perry counties from its offices in Newark. Under the terms of the 2024 consent order with the Ohio Environmental Protection Agency, the Authority must file a verified lead service line inventory with the state by December 31, 2026. At the start of the verification program, the Authority's records classified 31,200 connections — roughly one in three — as unknown material. Over the past fourteen months, field crews physically verified 4,850 of those unknowns, principally by vacuum-excavation potholing supplemented by interior inspection at the meter where customers granted access.

Before the Board commits the next $19 million tranche of the Authority's $58 million bond authority at its August meeting, Chief Engineer Sun-Hee Park asked this office to answer a straightforward question: **how well did the Authority's records predict what crews actually found in the ground, and what does the answer imply for the December filing and the pace and placement of the remaining replacement program?** Deputy Director Alma Restrepo commissioned the analysis after Heath council member Dale Hutchins alleged publicly that verification crews had been dispatched to wealthier streets first. This brief therefore also examines where the crews went and who declined access, so that the Board can weigh the equity question on evidence rather than impression.

The principal findings are these:

1. **The records substantially understate the lead problem.** Among the 4,850 verified connections that the records classified as unknown, 42 percent — 2,037 services — proved to be lead or galvanized material requiring replacement. The rate ranged from 19 percent in areas platted after 1960 to 68 percent in the Authority's four oldest wards.

2. **Even "known" records are not fully trustworthy.** Where the post-1994 permit database recorded a service as copper, crews still found lead 9 percent of the time. This error rate, if it extends to the roughly 21,400 connections the Authority currently classifies as non-lead on the strength of a single uncorroborated permit entry, places a further 1,900 or so services in doubt.

3. **The verification sample is not representative, and the bias runs in an unwelcome direction.** Seventy-one percent of completed potholes fall in just three of eleven wards, and the ward with the highest verification coverage — Ward 9, the newest and highest-income ward in the service area — is also the ward with the lowest lead-hit rate. Because the remaining unverified stock is concentrated in the old, high-hit wards, the true rate of lead among the 26,350 still-unknown connections is likely *higher* than the pooled 42 percent figure. A stratified reweighting by ward suggests a central estimate near 50 percent, or roughly 13,300 additional lines requiring replacement.

4. **Refusals compound the coverage bias.** Refusal of interior inspection reached 28 percent on majority-renter blocks, against 9 percent on majority-owner blocks. The blocks where crews could not get inside are disproportionately the old rental blocks where the hit rate is highest.

5. **Full physical verification by the December 31, 2026 deadline is arithmetically impossible.** At the program's realized pace of roughly 346 verifications per month, potholing the remaining 26,350 unknowns would take on the order of 76 months. The Authority's December filing must therefore rest on a defensible statistical classification method, and the field capacity remaining between now and December — roughly 1,400 potholes — should be redirected to a stratified random validation sample rather than continued opportunistic coverage.

6. **The financial gap is large.** At the first tranche's realized average unit cost of about $9,480 per full replacement, the $19 million August tranche funds approximately 2,000 replacements. The projected replacement universe — 2,037 confirmed, roughly 13,300 projected among remaining unknowns, and up to 1,900 potentially misclassified "copper" services — implies an eventual program on the order of 15,000 to 17,000 lines and $145 million to $165 million, well beyond the $58 million bond authority.

The remainder of this brief describes the three record sources and their gaps, the verification methods, the results in detail, the coverage and refusal patterns bearing on Mr. Hutchins's allegation, the limitations of the analysis, and the implications for the December filing and the August tranche.

## 2. Background: The Consent Order and the Funding Framework

The 2024 consent order followed the Authority's failure to submit an adequate initial inventory under the federal Lead and Copper Rule Revisions. The order requires a *verified* inventory by December 31, 2026 — a term the order does not define with precision, but which Ohio EPA staff have indicated in correspondence means an inventory whose classifications rest either on physical verification or on a records-and-statistical methodology the agency has reviewed and accepted. The order does not require that every connection be physically inspected by the deadline; it requires that every classification be defensible.

The Board authorized $58 million in revenue bonds for the replacement program in early 2025 and released a first tranche of $12 million, which funded the verification campaign and an initial round of 1,150 full replacements. The second tranche of $19 million is before the Board this month. The Authority employs 214 staff; the verification and replacement work is performed by two in-house crews and three contractors.

## 3. Data and Methods

### 3.1 The three record sources

The Authority's service line knowledge rests on three overlapping sources, each with distinct coverage and distinct failure modes.

**The 1978 tap card file.** Approximately 52,000 paper tap cards document connections installed between roughly 1902 and 1978. The cards record tap date, tap size, street address, and — inconsistently — pipe material. The physical cards were scanned in 2009 and the originals were not retained in accessible form; **6 percent of the scanned cards, about 3,100, are wholly or partially illegible**, with illegibility concentrated among the oldest cards, which were most faded at the time of scanning. Even among legible cards, the material field is blank on a substantial minority, because for much of the file's active life recording material was optional. The cards are the only documentary source for pre-1978 construction, which is precisely the era in which lead and galvanized installation was common. Newark's plumbing practice discouraged lead for new taps after the late 1940s, but galvanized service lines remained common into the 1960s, and lead goosenecks were used at the main connection later still.

**The permit database.** The Authority's electronic permit system begins in 1994. It captures new construction and permitted service replacements from that date forward — about 38,500 connections have at least one entry. It contains no information about any connection that has not been touched by permitted work since 1994, which excludes most of the oldest housing stock. A known weakness, confirmed by this analysis, is that material entries were made by permit technicians from contractor paperwork, and "copper" appears to have functioned as a default entry in some years.

**The meter replacement notes.** During the 2011–2019 automated meter infrastructure changeout, field technicians recorded pipe material observed at the meter setter on paper cards, which clerical staff later keyed by hand into the billing system. About 61,000 connections have such a note. The notes suffer from two limitations: transcription error in the hand-keying, and a fundamental observational one — the technician saw only the pipe segment at the meter, which frequently differs from the material of the buried run to the main. A copper stub at the meter tells us little about the sixty feet of pipe upstream of it.

### 3.2 The classification rule and the unknown pool

For the initial inventory, a connection was classified as known material where at least two sources agreed, or where a single post-1988 permit record documented full replacement. Connections were classified as unknown where the sources conflicted, where all sources were silent, or where the only card was illegible. That rule produced the pool of **31,200 unknowns**. It is worth stating plainly that the rule embeds a judgment: the 21,400 or so connections classified as non-lead on the strength of a single uncorroborated permit "copper" entry sit outside the unknown pool, and the 9 percent error finding reported below bears directly on whether they should.

### 3.3 The verification program

Between June 2025 and July 2026, crews completed **4,850 verifications**, an average of about 346 per month. The standard protocol was vacuum-excavation potholing at two points — near the main and near the curb stop — with interior inspection at the meter where the occupant granted access, so that both the utility-owned and customer-owned portions could be classified. A small number of verifications used closed-circuit camera inspection through the curb stop. Each verification recorded material by segment, presence of a lead gooseneck, and photographs. Galvanized lines were classified as "galvanized requiring replacement" where they were, or may ever have been, downstream of lead, consistent with federal rule definitions.

Site selection was not randomized. Crews were dispatched principally where potholing could share trenches, traffic control, and restoration costs with other scheduled work — a repaving and waterline project in Ward 9, a similar project in Ward 7, and main rehabilitation in Ward 2. The consequences of that operational logic for the representativeness of the sample are examined in Section 5.

### 3.4 Analytical approach

This analysis matched each verified connection to its predictions in all three record sources, computed agreement and error rates by source, and computed hit rates (share of verified unknowns found to be lead or galvanized requiring replacement) by ward and by plat era. To project the composition of the remaining 26,350 unknowns, ward-level hit rates were applied to the remaining unknown counts in each ward — a stratified estimate that partially corrects the coverage bias in the raw pooled rate. Refusal rates were computed at the block level and cross-tabulated with tenure (majority-renter versus majority-owner blocks, from the most recent American Community Survey block-group data allocated to blocks).

## 4. Results

### 4.1 The overall finding

Of the 4,850 verified connections, **2,037 — 42 percent — were lead or galvanized requiring replacement**: 812 full or partial lead lines and 1,225 galvanized lines requiring replacement. An additional 388 services that were otherwise copper carried lead goosenecks at the main, which will be removed in the course of any adjacent main work but which do not, under the applicable definitions, place those services in the replacement-required categories. The 42 percent figure is the headline: the Authority's records, where they confessed ignorance, were concealing a lead-or-galvanized line four times in ten.

### 4.2 Variation by housing age

The hit rate falls steeply and monotonically with construction era. In prose terms: services in areas platted before 1920 came back lead or galvanized roughly three-quarters of the time; the 1920s, about seven in ten; the 1930s, about six in ten; the 1940s, a little under half; the 1950s, roughly three in ten; the 1960s, about one in five; and areas platted after 1970, roughly one in ten. The two summary figures the Board should retain are the ones at the extremes: **68 percent in the four oldest wards (Wards 1 through 4, the Newark core, platted before 1945) and 19 percent in areas platted after 1960**. A bar chart of hit rate by plat decade — which this brief describes rather than draws — would show a staircase descending from left to right with no reversals, the single clearest visual argument that housing age is the dominant predictor available to us.

Second, the coverage column is wildly uneven, a matter taken up in Section 5: Ward 9 has had two-thirds of its unknowns verified while Wards 1 and 3 — the wards with the *highest* hit rates — sit near 3 percent.

### 4.4 How each record source performed

**Tap cards.** Among the 2,880 verified connections with a legible tap card bearing a material entry, the card matched the field finding 61 percent of the time. The card's errors were asymmetric in a useful way: where a card said *lead*, crews confirmed lead or galvanized 89 percent of the time. Cards saying lead are believable; cards saying copper or saying nothing are not exculpatory.

**Permit database.** Among the 1,410 verified connections where the permit database recorded copper (these fell in the unknown pool because another source conflicted or the tap card was illegible), **crews found lead 9 percent of the time — 127 services — and galvanized requiring replacement a further 5 percent of the time**. In total, roughly one in seven "copper-per-permit" services required replacement. Review of a subsample suggests two mechanisms: permits for partial work (a customer-side repair recorded as if it described the whole service) and default data entry in the system's early years.

**Meter notes.** The hand-keyed meter notes matched the field finding 57 percent of the time among the 3,120 verified connections carrying a note — the weakest of the three sources, consistent with both transcription error and the meter-stub observation problem. Notably, 41 percent of services with a copper notation at the meter proved to have lead or galvanized upstream of it.

### 4.5 What the remaining 26,350 unknowns probably contain

The naive projection — 42 percent of 26,350 — yields about 11,070 additional replacement-required lines. But the naive figure is biased *downward*, because the verification sample over-represents Ward 9 and under-represents the old core. Applying each ward's observed hit rate to its remaining unknown count (the stratified estimate) yields approximately **13,300 replacement-required lines among the remaining unknowns, a rate of about 50 percent**. Allowing for sampling uncertainty in the thinly verified wards and for the refusal bias discussed below, a defensible planning range is **11,900 to 14,600**, with the true figure more likely in the upper half of that range than the lower.

To this must be added the shadow cast by the permit-copper error rate. If the 9 percent lead finding extends to the roughly 21,400 connections currently classified non-lead on a single uncorroborated permit entry, on the order of 1,900 additional services are misclassified. That extrapolation carries a caveat discussed in Section 6 — the 1,410 tested permit-copper services were ones where another source disagreed, and may be worse than average — but the state is unlikely to accept records-only non-lead classifications without a validation sample in any case.

The total planning universe, then, is approximately 2,037 confirmed, roughly 13,300 projected among unknowns, and up to 1,900 within the nominally known stock: **on the order of 15,000 to 17,000 service lines**.

## 5. Coverage and Refusal Patterns: The Question Councilmember Hutchins Raised

### 5.1 Where the crews went

Seventy-one percent of completed potholes — 3,444 of 4,850 — fall in three wards: Ward 2 (1,510), Ward 9 (1,205), and Ward 7 (729). The remaining eight wards share 1,406 verifications among them. A map figure — described here rather than drawn — would show three dark patches on an otherwise pale field: the Newark core around the Ward 2 main-rehabilitation corridor, the Ward 7 repaving footprint, and nearly the whole of Ward 9, where verification rode along with the 2025–2026 Heath-area repaving and waterline project and where the principal contractor's staging yard is located.

The operational explanation is cost: a pothole excavated in an open repaving trench costs a fraction of a standalone pothole with its own traffic control and restoration. Program records and dispatch logs show no directive to prioritize by neighborhood income, and Ward 2 — the single largest recipient of verification effort — is among the lowest-income wards in the system.

But the *outcome* Councilmember Hutchins described is real, and the data should be allowed to say so plainly. Ward 9 is the newest and highest-income ward in the service area (median household income approximately $94,200, against roughly $38,700 in Ward 1), and it has had 67 percent of its unknowns verified. Wards 1 and 3 — the oldest, lowest-income, majority-renter wards, with hit rates above 70 percent — have had barely 3 percent of theirs verified. A scatter plot of ward median household income against share of unknowns verified would show a positive relationship (correlation on the order of +0.6), driven overwhelmingly by the Ward 9 point but not erased by its removal. Verification effort per unknown connection was highest where lead was least likely and lowest where it was most likely. The cause appears to be trench-sharing economics rather than direction; the effect is regressive regardless, and it is within the Board's power to reverse it in the remaining program.

### 5.2 Who could not be reached

Interior inspection — necessary to classify the customer-side segment with confidence — requires occupant consent. **Refusal rates reached 28 percent on majority-renter blocks, against 9 percent on majority-owner blocks**, for a program-wide rate of about 14 percent. Field supervisors report the familiar mechanisms: tenants unable to authorize entry, absentee landlords unreachable, and appointment no-shows on blocks with high turnover. Where interior access was refused, crews completed exterior potholing only, leaving the customer-side classification partial for those addresses.

The refusal pattern matters for two reasons. First, it biases the sample further in the same direction as the coverage pattern: majority-renter blocks are concentrated in the old core where hit rates are highest, so the connections we most needed to see are the ones we most often could not. Second, it foreshadows an execution risk for the replacement program itself, since full replacement also requires property access. Mitigations available to the Authority include coordinated outreach through landlord registries with the member municipalities, consent solicitation at lease turnover, and adoption of exterior-verification methods (curb-stop camera inspection) that the state has accepted for customer-side classification elsewhere in Ohio.

## 6. Limitations

Four limitations qualify the findings above and should temper how far the numbers are pressed.

**The sample is opportunistic, not random.** Every projection in Section 4.5 rests on the assumption that verified connections within a ward resemble unverified connections in the same ward. The stratified estimate corrects for *between-ward* imbalance but not for *within-ward* selection: verified addresses cluster along project corridors, and corridor housing may differ systematically from the rest of a ward. The direction of any residual bias is unknown.

**Thin cells are thin.** The hit rates for Wards 1, 3, 4, and 11 rest on between 120 and 240 verifications each. The 72 percent figure for Ward 1 carries a margin of roughly plus or minus eight percentage points. The era-band pattern is robust; individual ward figures are estimates, not measurements.

**The permit-copper error rate may not generalize.** The 9 percent lead finding comes from permit-copper services that landed in the unknown pool precisely because another source disagreed. Uncontested permit-copper records may be more reliable. The 1,900-service figure for potentially misclassified known stock is an upper-range planning number, not a finding, and the validation sample proposed below is designed to replace it with a measurement.

**Refusals truncate the customer-side data.** For roughly one in seven verified addresses — and one in four on majority-renter blocks — the customer-side classification rests on exterior evidence alone. Where the utility side was lead the classification is unaffected (the service requires replacement either way), but the count of customer-side-only galvanized lines is likely an undercount.

None of these limitations weakens the central conclusions — that the records understate the problem, that the understatement is worst in the old wards, and that the sample tilts away from where the lead is — because each identified bias runs toward *understating* the remaining burden rather than overstating it.

## 7. Implications

### 7.1 For the December 31, 2026 filing

The arithmetic admits no ambiguity. Verifying the remaining 26,350 unknowns at 346 per month would take roughly 76 months; even tripling throughput would carry the work into the 2030s. The consent order deadline is not achievable by physical verification, and no plausible tranche of the bond authority changes that. The filing must therefore rest on a **statistical classification methodology submitted to and accepted by Ohio EPA** — a records-plus-predictive model in which plat era, tap card content, and ward carry the classification weight, validated against a random sample. The fourteen months of field data are, in this respect, an asset of exactly the kind such a submission requires: they demonstrate that housing age predicts material with a strong, monotonic gradient, and they quantify the error rates of each record source.

The critical near-term recommendation follows: **the roughly 1,400 potholes the crews can complete between September and December should be redirected from project-corridor opportunism to a stratified random validation sample**, weighted toward the under-verified old wards (1, 3, and 4) and toward a random draw from the uncontested permit-copper stock. That sample would accomplish three things at once: it would give the statistical inventory the validation basis the state will demand; it would replace the extrapolated permit-copper error rate with a measured one; and it would begin correcting the coverage imbalance that Councilmember Hutchins identified. Staff should open discussions with Ohio EPA on the methodology this month rather than after the filing is drafted; the order's "verified" language is the central term to be construed, and the Authority is better served negotiating its meaning in September than litigating it in January. The filing should be expected to classify a substantial number of connections as unknown or as presumed-lead; under the federal framework those connections are treated as lead for replacement-planning purposes, which is uncomfortable but is also, on this evidence, approximately correct.

### 7.2 For the August tranche

The $19 million tranche funds roughly 2,000 full replacements at the first tranche's realized average unit cost of about $9,480. Staff recommend that the Board release the tranche with three conditions attached to its allocation.

**First, prioritize by measured risk, which means prioritizing the old core.** The four oldest wards hold an estimated 9,700 of the projected 13,300 replacement-required lines among remaining unknowns, at hit rates of 65 to 72 percent. Directing the tranche's replacement capacity there maximizes lead removed per dollar and, not incidentally, reverses the regressive coverage pattern of the verification phase. In wards where two of three lines are lead, replacement crews can proceed street by street with verification folded into the replacement excavation itself, collapsing two mobilizations into one.

**Second, fund refusal mitigation explicitly.** A modest allocation — staff estimate under $400,000 — for landlord-registry outreach with the member municipalities, multilingual consent materials, evening appointment capacity, and curb-stop camera equipment would address the 28 percent renter-block refusal rate before it becomes a replacement-phase bottleneck.

**Third, direct staff to bring a full-program finance plan to the Board by November.** The projected universe of 15,000 to 17,000 replacements implies an eventual program cost of $145 million to $165 million at current unit costs. The $58 million bond authority covers roughly a third of that. The gap is addressable — Bipartisan Infrastructure Law lead service line funds through the Ohio EPA's revolving fund carry substantial principal forgiveness for which the Authority's demographics make significant portions of the service area competitive, and the consent order itself strengthens the funding application — but the applications have deadlines of their own, and the November board cycle is the last that comfortably precedes them.

### 7.3 On the allegation

Finally, a note for the Board's public posture. The evidence does not support a finding that crews were *sent* to wealthier streets first; it supports a finding that a cost-driven scheduling logic *delivered* the heaviest verification coverage to the wealthiest ward and the lightest to the poorest, while the refusal pattern further thinned the data on renter blocks. The distinction between intent and effect is real, but it will not, and should not, satisfy residents of Wards 1 and 3, whose service lines are the likeliest in the system to be lead and the least likely to have been examined. The most persuasive answer available to the Authority is not an explanation but an allocation: the August tranche, spent where the lead is, makes the argument that no press release can.

## Appendix B. Design of the Proposed Validation Sample

Section 7.1 recommends redirecting remaining field capacity to a stratified random sample. This appendix specifies the design so that the Board understands what it is approving and Ohio EPA reviewers can evaluate it.

**Sampling frame and strata.** The frame consists of two populations: (a) the 26,350 remaining unknown connections, stratified by ward; and (b) the approximately 21,400 connections classified non-lead solely on an uncorroborated permit "copper" entry, stratified into pre-1994-construction and post-1994-construction substrata (the former being permits for replacement or repair work on older housing, where the misclassification risk concentrates; the latter being new construction, where a lead finding would be both unlikely and alarming).

**Allocation.** Of an assumed 1,400 achievable potholes:

- **900 to the unknown pool**, allocated in proportion to remaining unknown counts but with a floor of 60 per ward, which directs roughly 130 each to Wards 1, 3, and 4 — enough to narrow those wards' hit-rate confidence intervals from roughly ±8 points to roughly ±5, and enough to begin repairing the coverage imbalance visibly.
- **420 to the uncorroborated permit-copper stock**, of which 340 to the pre-1994-construction substratum and 80 to post-1994 construction. A sample of 340 measures the true misclassification rate in the high-risk substratum with a margin of roughly ±3 percentage points, sufficient to either substantiate or retire the 1,900-service planning figure in Section 4.5.
- **80 held in reserve** for re-verification of a random subsample of earlier program potholes as a quality-assurance check on the field classification protocol itself, which the state's inventory guidance rewards.

**Selection mechanics.** Addresses will be drawn by random number within strata by this office, with the draw documented and sealed before dispatch, so that the randomness of the sample is demonstrable to reviewers and to the public. Drawn addresses that prove inaccessible (refusal, vacancy, physical obstruction) will be replaced by the next address on the pre-drawn ordered list within the same stratum, with the substitution logged; the refusal rate within the sample is itself a quantity the filing must report.

**What the sample buys.** Upon completion, the Authority will hold measured, defensible hit rates for every ward at usable precision; a measured error rate for its largest block of records-based non-lead classifications; and a documented QA re-verification rate — the three quantities that, on staff's reading of accepted inventories elsewhere in Ohio, constitute the difference between a statistical filing the state accepts and one it remands.

## Appendix D. Glossary of Classification Terms

For Board members and members of the public reading the inventory filing, the following terms carry defined meanings under the federal rule framework and are used in those senses throughout this brief.

- **Lead service line.** A service line made of lead, in whole or in part, on either the utility-owned or customer-owned side. A line lead on either side is a lead service line in its entirety for inventory purposes.
- **Galvanized requiring replacement (GRR).** A galvanized steel service line that is or ever was downstream of lead, or whose upstream history cannot be documented. Galvanized pipe accumulates lead particulate from upstream lead and releases it unpredictably; the rule therefore treats undocumented galvanized as requiring replacement. Of the 2,037 replacement-required lines found by the program, 1,225 fall in this category.
- **Lead gooseneck.** A short flexible lead connector at the tap to the main, historically used with otherwise non-lead services. Goosenecks alone do not place a service in the replacement-required categories but must be removed when encountered; the program found 388.
- **Unknown.** A service line whose material cannot be classified from records or verification. Unknowns must be reported in the inventory and are treated as lead for replacement-planning and public-notification purposes until reclassified.
- **Verification.** Physical observation of pipe material by excavation, interior inspection, or accepted camera methods, documented photographically. Distinct from records-based classification, which rests on documentary sources, and statistical classification, which rests on a validated predictive method.
- **Hit rate.** As used in this brief, the share of verified previously-unknown connections found to be lead or GRR.

The compression at the center of this chronology is the point on which the brief closes. Between the Board's August meeting and the filing deadline lie twenty weeks, of which perhaps eleven are usable field weeks. Every week the validation sample starts late is a week subtracted from the statistical foundation of the filing, and every pothole spent on corridor convenience rather than the sample is a data point the filing will not have. The Board's August decision is therefore not only a funding decision; it is the last scheduling decision that can still shape what the Authority is able to tell the state, and its residents, in December.
