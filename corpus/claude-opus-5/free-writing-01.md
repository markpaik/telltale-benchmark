# Hapax

In the sixth chapter of Genesis, God instructs Noah to build the ark out of gopher wood. Nobody knows what gopher wood is.

The word appears there and nowhere else — not elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible, not in any surviving inscription, not in the neighboring languages in a form anyone can agree on. The guesses have been going on for two millennia: cypress, cedar, pine, some resinous species now renamed or extinct; or not a species at all but a treatment, planks laminated or pitched or squared in a particular way. Most translators give up and transliterate, which is a way of printing the shrug. The most famous vessel in the history of the world is built out of a material we cannot identify.

The Alexandrian scholars who annotated Homer had a term for this. When a word occurred only once in the poems, they marked it *hapax legomenon* — said once. It was originally a bookkeeping note, a flag for the copyist and the commentator, meaning roughly: careful here, we have no second look.

The Homeric poems are full of them. There is *moly*, the herb Hermes gives Odysseus as protection against Circe: black root, white flower, hard for mortal men to dig, but the gods can do anything. The passage tells you its color and its difficulty and its power and even that its name is the gods' name for it, which implies there is another name, the human one, which we are not given. Botanists have proposed snowdrop, wild garlic, mandrake, rue. What the poet knew, if he knew anything, went into the ground with him.

The one I keep returning to is *epiousios*.

It sits in the middle of the Lord's Prayer — *give us this day our epiousios bread* — and it appears in Matthew and in Luke and, as far as anyone has been able to establish, essentially nowhere else in the whole of Greek. Origen, writing in the third century, when Greek was still the air he breathed, says flatly that the word is not used by any of the Greeks, nor by the philosophers, nor in common speech; it seems, he says, to have been coined by the Evangelists. There was once a papyrus, a household accounts book, in which someone thought they saw it written among the groceries. The papyrus has since been lost, and the reading was never secure, and so that door closed too.

Jerome, translating into Latin, could not decide. In Matthew he rendered it *supersubstantialem* — bread beyond substance, the mystical reading. In Luke, four pages later, he wrote *quotidianum*, daily. The same word, in the same prayer, in the same translator's hand, twice, differently. He did not know either. Nobody has known since. The candidates run from "for tomorrow" to "necessary for existence" to "for the day now coming upon us," and each is grammatically defensible and none is provable, because there is no second instance to check it against.

That is the whole difficulty, and it is worth stating plainly. Firth's old slogan is that you shall know a word by the company it keeps. We learn what words mean by watching where they go and where they refuse to go, what they can be swapped for, what company they will and won't be seen in. Meaning is distributional; it is a matter of habits, and habits require repetition. A word said once has no habits. It has an occasion. You can infer a great deal from an occasion — the syntax will tell you it is an adjective, the context will tell you it modifies bread and is being asked for — but inference of that kind gives you a silhouette, not a face. It is the animal known from a single footprint. You can say it was heavy and it had four toes. You cannot say what it ate, or what sound it made, or whether anyone ever saw it and lived.

So the hapax is the point where philology admits its limit, and for a long time that is all I thought it was: a curiosity, a species of scholarly grief.

Then I learned what Turing did with them.

At Bletchley Park, and afterward in a paper published by I. J. Good in 1953, there was a problem of the following shape. You have a sample — of words, of species, of cipher settings — and you need to estimate the probability that the next thing you draw will be something you have never seen before. This looks impossible. The unseen is by definition unobserved; it leaves no trace in your data; you are being asked to weigh a shadow.

Turing's insight was that the unseen does leave a trace, and the trace is the hapaxes. The things you have seen exactly once are the ones that were nearly not seen at all. They are the population living right at the threshold of your sample, and their abundance tells you how crowded that threshold is. Count them, divide by the size of your sample, and you have an estimate — a good one, provably good — of the total probability mass of everything you have missed.

I find this one of the most beautiful facts I know. The singletons are a measuring instrument pointed at absence. If your data are thick with things seen once, the darkness beyond your data is vast. If almost nothing occurs once, you have very nearly seen it all.

Which changes what a hapax *is*. It stops being a hole in the text and becomes a reading on a gauge.

Take *Beowulf*. Three thousand-odd lines, and by common estimate something like a third of its vocabulary occurs nowhere else in the surviving Old English corpus — a great crowd of compounds and rarities that editors have been arguing about for two centuries. The usual way to describe this is to say that *Beowulf* is a strange and difficult poem. But that is the wrong end of it. *Beowulf* is not strange. *Beowulf* is normal, and we have almost nothing else. Those words were ordinary equipment in a poetic tradition that ran for centuries in the mouths of people who never wrote anything down, or wrote it on materials that burned. The hapaxes are not the poem's eccentricity. They are the poem's report on the size of the library that is gone. Every unique word is a small bright pin marking the place where a whole vanished literature touched the one manuscript that happened to survive the fire at Ashburnham House.

The same arithmetic runs through everything, once you start looking. The reason so much of Sappho is a hapax is that so little of Sappho is anything at all.

And then, because it is impossible not to, you turn the instrument around.

A life is a corpus, and it is a small one. Most of what happens in it happens once. There was a particular afternoon, a particular way someone said your name when you were seven, an expression on a face in a doorway that you have never seen on that face again or on any other. You cannot check these against a second occurrence. You do not know what they meant. You have the silhouette and not the face, and you will not get a second look, and the honest thing to say about most of the contents of a memory is what the lexicographers say about gopher wood: *meaning uncertain; occurs once.*

But Turing's arithmetic holds here too, and it is not consoling exactly, though it is something. The density of unrepeatable things in a life is a measurement. It tells you how much larger the world is than your sample of it. A person who found nothing singular in a year would be reporting, without meaning to, that they had exhausted their world — that the unseen portion had shrunk to nothing. The strangeness is the reading on the gauge, and a high reading means the dark is enormous, and the dark being enormous is, on most days, the good news.

Give us this day our *epiousios* bread.

Two thousand years of saying it, in every language into which it has been dragged, by people utterly certain of what they meant, and the hole is still there in the middle of the sentence, unclosed and unclosable, one word wide. It has not made the prayer harder to say. Nobody has ever stumbled over it. Perhaps that is the finding. You can ask for a thing you cannot name, daily, and be understood — and most of what anyone has ever wanted has been exactly that kind of thing, said once, into a room, and not explained.
