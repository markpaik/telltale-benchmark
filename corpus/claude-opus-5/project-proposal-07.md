## A Capital Proposal to the Kestrel Bridge Holdings Capital Committee

**Submitted by:** Yusuf Abdirahman, Plant Manager; Chelsea Nakagawa, Vice President of Operations
**Facility:** Ozark Precision Components, Springfield, Missouri
**Date of Submission:** July 29, 2026
**Decision Requested By:** November 6, 2026
**Total Capital Request:** $18.9 million

## 1. Executive Summary

We are asking the committee to authorize $18.9 million to reorganize the Springfield machining floor into five product-focused manufacturing cells, add nine CNC machining centers and two robotic load stations, and stand up a second production shift of 84 people over twenty months. Funding would come from $14.0 million of holding-company debt and $4.9 million of plant cash on hand.

The reason to act now is not that the equipment is old. It is that our largest customer has told us what the next contract looks like, and we cannot survive it on the current cost structure.

Vantage AgWorks represents 38 percent of our sales — roughly $47.9 million annually. In the 2027 rebid they will require 6 percent annual price reductions for three years, or they will dual-source the manifold families we currently hold exclusively. Compounded, that is a 16.9 percent reduction in unit price on our largest revenue block by 2030. We currently produce those parts at a gross margin that will not absorb it. If we concede the price out of margin, we lose approximately $6.2 million of annual gross profit by year three on the Vantage business alone. If we refuse and they dual-source, we should assume we lose half the volume within eighteen months of the split and the remainder is repriced downward anyway, because a dual-sourced part is a competitively bid part in perpetuity.

The third option — the one in this proposal — is to take the cost out of the operation so that the price reduction is affordable and the business is defended. Our scrap rate runs 6.1 percent against a 2.5 percent target. Quality Director Ivan Bogdanov's teardown of eighteen months of nonconformance data attributes roughly 60 percent of that scrap to setup variation: parts moved between functionally grouped machines, re-fixtured at each station, with dimensional drift accumulating across four to six handoffs. Cellular flow removes most of those handoffs. Scrap recovery alone is worth $4.4 million annually at current volumes.

We are also out of room. The plant runs at 91 percent capacity on a single shift with twelve-week lead times. We have declined three quote packages in the last nine months because we could not commit to delivery. A second shift converts fixed-asset capacity we already own into revenue we currently turn away.

And we are running out of people. The average machinist on this floor is 54 years old. Sixty-one are retirement-eligible within three years — 27 percent of our direct machining headcount. The local training pipeline produced 34 machinists last year across all employers in the region. We cannot hire our way out of this on the current model, where every machinist is a generalist who must be able to set up any machine on the floor. Cells narrow the skill footprint per station and make it possible to bring a new hire to productive contribution in fourteen weeks instead of the eighteen months a general machinist requires today.

We recognize the committee's position. Operating partner Philippa Wrenn has been direct with us that patience for equipment requests is thin following the 2023 finishing line, a $6.4 million installation now running at 40 percent utilization. We address that history explicitly in Section 8. The short version: that project was justified on a demand forecast that did not materialize and was specified without a committed volume behind it. This project is justified on cost recovery from volume we already have, under contract, with a customer who has told us in writing what they will pay. The two projects are not analogous, and we have built the phasing so that the committee can stop this one at month seven if the first two cells do not perform.

**Financial summary:** $18.9 million capital, $5.4 million annual incremental labor cost for the second shift, against $11.7 million of annual run-rate benefit at full maturity (month 24). Payback of 34 months from first spend. Unlevered IRR of 24 percent over seven years. The project protects $47.9 million of revenue that is otherwise at material risk.

**The decision requested on November 6** is authorization of the full $18.9 million with a hard gate at month seven, at which point the first two cells must demonstrate a scrap rate at or below 3.4 percent and a setup-time reduction of at least 45 percent, or the remaining $11.2 million of uncommitted spend is released back to the committee for reconsideration.

## 2. The Commercial and Operational Problem

### 2.1 The Vantage AgWorks Rebid

Vantage AgWorks has been our customer for nineteen years. We produce fourteen manifold families and six valve body configurations for their compact tractor, skid steer, and telehandler lines. Annual revenue is $47.9 million against total plant revenue of $126 million.

In April, Vantage's supply chain director informed us that the 2027 rebid — covering model years 2028 through 2030 — will be structured around a mandatory 6 percent annual price-down. This is not unusual for the sector; it is what their own OEM customers are imposing on them. What is different is the alternative on offer. In prior cycles, failure to meet the target produced a negotiation. This time we have been told that non-participation triggers dual-sourcing of the manifold families, which are the higher-margin half of the package.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Six percent compounded over three years is a 16.9 percent cumulative reduction. Applied to the Vantage revenue block, that is $8.1 million of annual price erosion by 2030 against today's volume. Our gross margin on this business currently runs 23 percent — approximately $11.0 million. Conceding the full reduction out of margin leaves roughly $2.9 million, before considering that our own input costs will not hold flat over the same period. Realistically, we would be running the largest block of our business near break-even by 2029 and below it by 2030.

Dual-sourcing is worse. In our experience and in the industry generally, a second source does not stay at a token allocation. Vantage would move to a 60/40 split within a year and use each supplier's quote to pressure the other in every subsequent cycle. We would lose approximately $19 million of revenue, the fixed-cost absorption that comes with it, and the pricing power we currently hold. The remaining volume would face the same price-down pressure without the volume base to support it.

We do not believe there is a version of the 2027 rebid in which we hold this business at current cost. The question is whether we take the cost out of the operation or out of the P&L.

### 2.2 Scrap

Our scrap rate is 6.1 percent of material and processing value — approximately $7.6 million annually against a plant standard of 2.5 percent. The gap is $4.4 million.

Ivan Bogdanov's quality organization spent four months building a defect-to-cause map covering 11,400 nonconformance records from January 2025 through April 2026. The finding that matters: 60 percent of scrap value traces to setup variation, not to tooling wear, material defect, program error, or operator skill. In our current functional layout, a valve body moves from saw to rough mill to horizontal machining center to drilling to deburr to finishing, and it is fixtured and re-datumed at nearly every station. Each re-fixturing introduces positional tolerance stack. On the tighter-tolerance manifold families, where port-to-port true position runs at 0.05 millimeters, four re-fixturings are enough to put a part out of print without any single operation being at fault.

The remaining 40 percent of scrap splits between tooling and program issues (roughly 22 percent), incoming material (11 percent), and operator error (7 percent). Those are real but are not what this project is about.

Cellular layout addresses the setup problem structurally. In a cell, the part is fixtured once or twice across the full machining sequence, and the machines are grouped so that the part does not leave the cell until it is complete. Bogdanov's modeling, validated against a pilot we ran on the 4400-series manifold family in Q1, projects scrap on cellularized families falling to between 2.2 and 2.8 percent. The pilot itself — two machines, one operator, one part family, run for six weeks — produced a scrap rate of 2.6 percent against a prior-year baseline of 6.9 percent on the same part number.

### 2.3 Safety and the OSHA Citation

In February, OSHA issued a $47,000 citation for machine-guarding deficiencies across seven machines, principally interlock failures on older vertical mills and inadequate point-of-operation guarding on two manual stations. We abated all cited conditions within the thirty-day window and paid the penalty. There have been no subsequent findings.

We raise it here because it is symptomatic. Our floor layout requires operators to move constantly between machines carrying work in process, crossing forklift aisles, and reaching into machine envelopes to load parts that were designed for a different fixturing approach. We have had four recordable injuries in eighteen months, three of them involving material handling between operations. The citation was the visible outcome of a layout that generates avoidable exposure.

Cellular layout reduces operator travel by an estimated 62 percent based on our spaghetti-diagram analysis. The two robotic load stations remove human hands from the load/unload envelope on the highest-cycle machines entirely. This is not the financial case for the project, but it is a real part of the operational case, and the committee should understand that maintaining the current layout carries regulatory and human cost that will not stay flat.

### 2.4 Capacity and Lead Time

We run at 91 percent of theoretical single-shift capacity. Quoted lead time is twelve weeks; actual delivery performance is 87 percent on-time. In the last nine months we declined to quote three packages — two from existing customers, one from a construction equipment OEM new to us — with a combined annual value of approximately $9.4 million, because we could not commit to a delivery window.

Ninety-one percent utilization on a single shift is not a compliment. It means we have no recovery capacity when a machine goes down, no room to absorb a customer's schedule change, and no ability to pull work forward. It is also why our lead times are twelve weeks against a market norm closer to seven.

A second shift is the cheapest capacity we will ever buy, because the machines are already here and already paid for. The incremental cost is labor, supervision, utilities, and consumables — $5.4 million annually per HR Director Tameka Sallis's costing. Against that, a second shift on the cellularized floor adds approximately 70 percent of first-shift output capacity, or roughly $34 million of annual revenue capability at current mix, of which we conservatively assume we fill $14 million in the first two years.

### 2.5 The Workforce Cliff

The average machinist at Springfield is 54 years old. Sixty-one employees — 27 percent of direct machining headcount — are retirement-eligible within three years. Eleven have already given informal notice of intent to retire in 2027.

The local pipeline produced 34 machinists last year across Ozark Technical Community College, the Springfield Regional Manufacturing Consortium program, and two private programs. That is 34 for every employer in Greene and Christian counties competing for them, including two larger manufacturers with better-known names.

We cannot replace 61 general machinists from a pool of 34 annual graduates shared across the region. But we do not need to. Our current model requires every machinist to be capable of setting up any machine for any part — an eighteen-month competency curve. In a cell, an operator learns one part family across a fixed machine group. Sallis and our training team estimate a fourteen-week curve to independent productive operation, with a further six months to full multi-machine cell competency. That changes the hiring problem from "find experienced machinists" to "find trainable people," which is a much larger pool.

This is, in our judgment, the most durable strategic argument for cellularization and the one least visible on a spreadsheet. The current layout is a bet that we can keep hiring 54-year-old journeymen. That bet has already stopped paying.

## 3. What Is Proposed: Cell Layout and Equipment

### 3.1 Design Principle

The floor is reorganized from six functional departments into five product-family cells, each containing the full machine complement required to take a part from raw casting or billet to finish-ready condition without leaving the cell. Cells are staffed by cross-trained teams operating multiple machines. Work in process within a cell is limited to a single piece between stations where cycle times permit and a maximum of three pieces where they do not.

Cell assignment follows a product-family analysis Bogdanov's team completed in May, grouping our 214 active part numbers by machining sequence similarity, fixture commonality, and tolerance class. The five resulting families cover 91 percent of volume; the residual 9 percent — low-volume, high-mix legacy work — remains in a reduced functional area we are calling the flex bay.

### 3.2 The Five Cells

**Cell 1 — Compact Manifolds (Vantage 4400/4600 series).** The highest-volume, tightest-tolerance work and the core of the Vantage package. Four horizontal machining centers, one of them new, plus one robotic load station serving two of the four. Three-station in-cell washing and CMM gauging. Target staffing: four operators per shift. This is one of the two cells live at month seven.

**Cell 2 — Valve Bodies (Vantage and Ridgeline).** Three new horizontal machining centers and two redeployed vertical centers, one deburr station, in-cell gauging. Target staffing: four operators per shift. Also live at month seven.

**Cell 3 — Large Manifolds (construction equipment).** Two new horizontal centers, two redeployed, one new five-axis center for the complex telehandler bodies. Second robotic load station. Target staffing: five operators per shift. Live month thirteen.

**Cell 4 — Mixed Agricultural.** Three redeployed horizontal centers, two redeployed verticals, one new machining center. Target staffing: four operators per shift. Live month sixteen.

**Cell 5 — Small Components and Subassembly.** Redeployed equipment only, plus new fixturing and material handling. Target staffing: three operators per shift. Live month nineteen.

**Flex bay.** Retains six existing machines for low-volume work, prototype, and overflow. Two operators per shift.

### 3.3 Equipment Detail

Nine new CNC machines:

- Five horizontal machining centers, 500mm pallet, 40-taper, twin-pallet changer (Cells 1, 2, 3)
- One five-axis machining center for telehandler valve bodies (Cell 3)
- Two horizontal machining centers, 630mm pallet (Cells 3, 4)
- One vertical machining center with high-pressure through-spindle coolant (Cell 2)

Two robotic load stations, each a six-axis articulated arm on a linear track serving two machines, with part-presentation conveyors and vision-based part verification. These are placed on the two highest-cycle, most repetitive load operations in Cells 1 and 3.

Twenty-three existing machines are redeployed into cells. Fourteen require re-leveling, foundation work, or controls refresh. Nine older machines — principally manual mills and two 1990s-vintage CNC lathes — are retired and sold; we estimate $310,000 of proceeds, which we have not counted in the return.

### 3.4 Supporting Work

The project includes fixture design and build for all five cells — 74 new fixtures, the largest single non-machine line item at $1.9 million. It includes electrical distribution, compressed air, and coolant delivery reconfiguration; foundation work for the new horizontals; overhead crane rail modification in the Cell 3 area; and a new in-cell gauging program with four coordinate measuring machines relocated from the central quality lab to point-of-use positions.

We are also replacing the shop floor scheduling system. The current MRP push-scheduling logic is incompatible with cellular flow and would actively defeat it. The replacement is a pull-based cell scheduling module integrated to the existing ERP, budgeted at $640,000 including implementation.

## 4. Hiring, Training, and Staffing

### 4.1 The Second Shift

The second shift is 84 people at full staffing in month twenty: 66 direct operators, 8 setup and tooling technicians, 4 quality technicians, 3 material handlers, 2 maintenance technicians, and 1 shift supervisor. Sallis has costed this at $5.4 million annually fully loaded, including a 12 percent shift differential, benefits at 31 percent of base, and a $1,900-per-head annual training allocation.

### 4.2 The Hiring Plan

We do not intend to hire 84 machinists. We intend to hire approximately 90 people (allowing for attrition during training) of whom perhaps 20 will be experienced machinists and the remainder will be trainable candidates from adjacent backgrounds — assembly, warehouse, military, automotive service, and direct-from-high-school through the apprenticeship track.

Recruitment is phased against cell go-live:

| Phase | Months | Hires | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wave 1 | 1–6 | 22 | Cells 1 and 2 second shift, trained before month 7 go-live |
| Wave 2 | 7–12 | 20 | Cell 3 second shift plus first-shift backfill for 2027 retirements |
| Wave 3 | 13–17 | 26 | Cells 4 and 5 second shift |
| Wave 4 | 18–20 | 22 | Completion, flex bay, and retirement backfill |

Sallis has secured a partnership agreement in principle with Ozark Technical Community College for a dedicated cohort of twelve per semester beginning January 2027, in which we fund tuition and pay trainees for classroom hours in exchange for a two-year employment commitment. This does not solve the pipeline problem but it gives us first call on roughly a third of the region's annual output.

We are also proposing a retention structure for the 61 retirement-eligible machinists: a mentorship differential of $3.00 per hour for those who commit to remaining through a defined period and training a named successor. Sallis estimates 30 to 35 will accept. At full uptake this costs approximately $218,000 annually and is the single highest-leverage expenditure in the plan, because it converts departing knowledge into resident knowledge rather than losing it.

### 4.3 Training

Training runs on three tracks.

**Track A — New hire to cell operator.** Fourteen weeks: two weeks safety and shop fundamentals, four weeks classroom and simulator on controls and print reading, eight weeks supervised on-cell with a mentor. Graduates operate one machine type independently within the cell.

**Track B — Cell operator to multi-machine.** Six months following Track A, rotating through the cell's full machine complement. This is what makes the cell work; a cell in which each operator runs one machine is just a functional department in a smaller space.

**Track C — Incumbent conversion.** Existing machinists move from functional to cellular working. Three weeks of structured training on cell principles, standard work, in-cell gauging, and the new scheduling system, plus ongoing coaching. All 224 existing direct employees go through Track C. This is scheduled in waves against cell conversion so we are never training more than 20 percent of a shift at once.

Training cost is $1.4 million across the twenty months, carried within the capital request as a project expense rather than in the $5.4 million annual shift cost.

### 4.4 Supervision and Support Structure

Each cell has a designated cell leader on each shift — a working position, not a supervisor — responsible for standard work adherence, first-off inspection, and escalation. Cell leaders are drawn from existing senior machinists and receive a $2.50 per hour differential.

The second shift requires one shift supervisor, one maintenance technician pair, and quality coverage. We are not duplicating engineering, scheduling, or supply chain; those remain first-shift functions with defined escalation paths.

## 5. Installation Phasing

The phasing constraint that governs everything is that we cannot stop shipping. Vantage takes weekly releases against a firm four-week horizon. Our other major accounts run similar programs. A production interruption during the rebid period would be commercially catastrophic regardless of what the cells eventually deliver.

We therefore convert one area at a time, building each cell in cleared space adjacent to the operating floor before moving production into it, rather than converting in place. This costs more and takes longer than a shutdown conversion but eliminates the delivery risk.

### 5.1 Phase Sequence

**Months 1–3: Preparation.** Detailed layout engineering, machine procurement and order placement (long-lead horizontals carry a 26-week delivery), fixture design, scheduling system configuration, Wave 1 recruitment. Clearing of the northeast bay by consolidating the flex work and retiring nine machines. No production impact.

**Months 4–7: Cells 1 and 2 construction and startup.** Foundation and utility work in the cleared bay. New machine delivery beginning month five. Machine installation and runoff months five through six. Fixture qualification and first-article approval month six. Production transfer month seven, in two steps a week apart. Second shift begins in Cells 1 and 2 at month seven with Wave 1 hires.

**Month 7: Gate review.** See Section 10.

**Months 8–13: Cell 3.** Clearing of the central bay as Cells 1 and 2 vacate their old machine positions. Second robotic load station installed. Cell 3 live at month thirteen.

**Months 14–16: Cell 4.** Predominantly redeployed equipment; shorter cycle. Live month sixteen.

**Months 17–19: Cell 5 and flex bay reconfiguration.** Live month nineteen.

**Month 20: Full second-shift staffing achieved.** Project close, benefit measurement baseline established.

### 5.2 Production Protection

During each transfer we build a two-week finished goods bank on the affected part families. This carries an inventory cost of approximately $1.1 million at peak, which we have included in working capital, not capital expenditure. We have also negotiated with Vantage a written acknowledgment of the conversion schedule and a mutual agreement on expedite handling during transfer weeks — not a waiver of our obligations, but an agreed process.

We have deliberately placed the two cells covering the largest Vantage volume first. If the concept fails, we will know at month seven on the parts that matter most, before $11.2 million of the spend is committed.

## 7. Alternatives Considered

### 7.1 Concede Price Out of Margin

Accept the Vantage terms and absorb the reduction. Zero capital. This preserves the relationship and requires no execution risk.

It also gives away $6.2 million of annual gross profit by 2030 on the Vantage business, with nothing purchased in return. Plant EBITDA falls from $14.8 million to roughly $8.6 million. It does not address scrap, capacity, lead time, safety, or the retirement cliff, all of which continue to degrade. And it establishes with Vantage that price reduction is available on request, which guarantees the same conversation in 2030 from a weaker position. We regard this as the worst available option and recommend against it.

### 7.2 Refuse and Accept Dual-Sourcing

Decline the price-down, retain full margin on reduced volume. We estimate loss of $19 million of revenue within eighteen months and $4.4 million of contribution, with fixed costs unabsorbed and further erosion likely. It also puts a qualified competitor inside the account with our print package. We recommend against it.

### 7.3 Outsource Finishing

Several committee members will reasonably ask whether we should outsource finishing operations — which would relieve capacity, avoid part of the capital, and put the underutilized 2023 finishing line to a decision.

We examined this seriously. Two regional finishing houses quoted the work. The quoted cost is $2.7 million annually against our current internal cost of $2.1 million, so it is $600,000 more expensive on a direct basis. It adds four to six days of transit and queue to every part's cycle, which extends our lead time rather than shortening it. It moves a control point outside the plant on parts where finish quality is a functional requirement for sealing surfaces — a category of defect that is expensive and slow to detect. And it strands the 2023 finishing line entirely, converting a 40-percent-utilized asset into a zero-percent-utilized asset and forcing a write-down of approximately $4.1 million of net book value.

We do not recommend outsourcing finishing. We do recommend, separately from this proposal, that the plant pursue outside finishing work to fill the 2023 line — we have identified three prospective customers and will bring a commercial plan to the operating partner in Q4. That addresses the utilization problem without stranding the asset.

### 7.4 Partial Cellularization

Convert only Cells 1 and 2 — the Vantage-critical families — for approximately $8.6 million, with a smaller second shift of 30 people.

This is the closest competitor to our recommendation and we want the committee to see it clearly. It captures roughly 48 percent of the scrap benefit and about a third of the capacity benefit for 46 percent of the capital. IRR is comparable, at 22 percent. It is a legitimate option.

We do not recommend it, for three reasons. First, it leaves the retirement cliff unaddressed on 60 percent of the floor, where the general-machinist model persists and the hiring problem remains unsolved. Second, a mixed floor — two cells and four functional departments — is operationally harder to run than either pure state, because scheduling, material flow, and supervision must work two ways at once. Third, the fixed elements of the project (utilities, scheduling system, engineering, training infrastructure) are largely incurred in the partial case, so the incremental capital for Cells 3 through 5 buys proportionally more benefit than the first tranche did.

That said, the month-seven gate we propose in Section 10 is functionally an option on exactly this outcome. If the first two cells underperform, the committee can stop and the plant lands in the partial-cellularization state having spent $7.7 million.

### 7.5 Automate Without Reorganizing

Add the nine machines and two robots into the existing functional layout. Capital falls to about $13.4 million by avoiding facility, redeployment, and much of the fixture work.

This is the 2023 finishing line mistake repeated. It adds capacity where the constraint is not capacity — it is setup variation and flow. Faster machines in a layout that re-fixtures parts five times produce scrap faster. We recommend against it and note that it is the version of this proposal the committee should be most skeptical of, which is why we are not proposing it.

## 8. On the 2023 Finishing Line

Philippa Wrenn has told us plainly that the committee's appetite for equipment requests from Springfield is limited, and that the 2023 finishing line is the reason. We think that skepticism is warranted and we want to address it directly rather than hope it goes unmentioned.

The finishing line was approved in 2022 on a forecast that a construction-equipment customer would move a family of large valve bodies to us in 2024, adding roughly $16 million of annual revenue requiring finishing capability we did not have. That program was cancelled by the customer's parent in mid-2023 following a change in their product strategy. The line was specified for that volume, was built for that volume, and has run at 40 percent since because the volume never came.

Three things went wrong that are worth naming. The volume was forecast, not contracted — there was no purchase commitment behind it. The asset was sized for the forecast peak rather than for demonstrated demand with expansion capability. And there was no gate: the full $6.4 million was committed at approval, so when the program cancelled in month nine there was nothing left to stop.

This proposal is constructed differently on all three points.

The volume is not forecast. It exists. We are producing 38 percent of our revenue for Vantage today and the scrap we are proposing to eliminate is scrap we are generating this week. The dominant benefit line — $4.4 million of scrap recovery — requires no new customer, no new program, and no revenue growth whatsoever. It requires only that we make the parts we already make with fewer defects. The second-shift revenue assumption of $14 million is the one genuinely forecast element, it is the smaller half of the benefit, and we have modeled a downside case at $9 million.

The asset is sized to current demonstrated demand, not to a peak. Nine machines is what the five cells require to run today's volume in cellular flow; it is not a bet on growth.

And the spend is gated. $7.7 million at month seven, $11.2 million after, contingent on measured performance on the two cells that carry the most Vantage volume. The committee is not being asked to commit $18.9 million on our projections. It is being asked to commit $7.7 million on our projections and $11.2 million on our results.

We would add one thing. The finishing line's underutilization is a real problem that we have not solved, and we intend to bring a commercial plan for filling it in Q4 independent of this decision. We do not think it is reasonable to ask the committee to fund a new project while an old one sits idle, and we are not asking it to ignore that.

## 9. Risks and Mitigations

**Cells do not deliver projected scrap reduction.** The single largest benefit line. Mitigated by the Q1 pilot, which achieved 2.6 percent on a live part family, and by the month-seven gate, which tests the assumption on $7.7 million rather than $18.9 million. Residual risk: the pilot was one family and two machines; scaling behavior may differ. We have set the gate threshold at 3.4 percent rather than 2.6 percent to allow for scaling degradation while still requiring meaningful improvement.

**We cannot hire 84 people.** The most probable failure mode. The regional pipeline is thin and we are competing for it. Mitigated by the fourteen-week training model that widens the candidate pool beyond experienced machinists, the OTC cohort agreement, the mentorship differential retaining incumbent knowledge, and phased hiring that spreads the requirement across twenty months rather than concentrating it. Contingency: if Wave 3 hiring falls more than 25 percent short, we delay Cell 5 and run the flex bay on one shift, deferring approximately $1.9 million of benefit rather than failing the project.

**Production disruption during transfer.** Mitigated by build-adjacent-then-transfer sequencing, two-week finished goods banking on affected families, transfer scheduled during the lowest-demand weeks of the Vantage release calendar, and written schedule acknowledgment from Vantage. Residual risk is real; a machine that will not run off on schedule delays a transfer. We hold four weeks of schedule float across the twenty months.

**Vantage moves before we finish.** The rebid concludes in 2027; the project completes in 2028. We will be partway through when we have to quote. Mitigated by sequencing Cells 1 and 2 — covering 71 percent of Vantage volume — to complete at month seven, which is before the rebid submission. We will be able to quote year-one pricing against demonstrated cell performance rather than a promise. We have also briefed Vantage's supply chain director on the project in general terms; their response was encouraging and they have asked for a site visit in Q1 2027.

**Cost overrun.** $970,000 of contingency at 5.4 percent is thin for a project of this complexity. Mitigated by fixed-price machine tool contracts (quoted and held to December 15), competitively bid facility work, and a change-control process requiring Nakagawa's approval above $50,000 and committee notification above $250,000. If overrun exceeds contingency we will descope Cell 5 rather than return for supplemental funding.

**Retirements accelerate.** If more than 25 of the 61 eligible retire before month twelve, training capacity becomes the binding constraint. Mitigated by the mentorship differential and by front-loading Track C conversion training for the most at-risk skill areas. Contingency: contract retiree return-to-work arrangements for training delivery, budgeted informally at $200,000.

**Safety incident during construction.** Active construction on an operating floor. Mitigated by physical separation of construction zones, a dedicated project safety coordinator for the duration, contractor pre-qualification, and weekly joint safety walks. Given the February citation we will also invite a voluntary OSHA consultation review at month six.

**Interest rate or holding-company financing conditions change.** Outside our control. The $14 million is subject to Kestrel Bridge's facility terms. If financing terms materially worsen we would recommend the partial-cellularization path at $8.6 million funded with a smaller debt tranche.

## 10. The Decision Requested

We ask the Kestrel Bridge Holdings capital committee, at its meeting of **November 6, 2026**, to approve the following:

**1. Authorization of $18.9 million** in capital for the cellular manufacturing conversion and second-shift startup at Ozark Precision Components, funded by $14.0 million of holding-company debt and $4.9 million of plant cash.

**2. A staged commitment structure.** $7.7 million released at approval for Phase 1 (preparation, Cells 1 and 2, Wave 1 hiring). The remaining $11.2 million released only upon satisfaction of the month-seven gate.

**3. Month-seven gate criteria.** Measured over four consecutive weeks of steady-state production in Cells 1 and 2:
   - Combined scrap rate at or below **3.4 percent** of material and processing value
   - Average setup time reduction of at least **45 percent** against the 2026 baseline for the same part families
   - On-time delivery on cell part families at or above **93 percent**
   - Second-shift staffing in Cells 1 and 2 at or above **80 percent** of plan
   - Zero lost-time injuries within the cells

Failure of any two criteria returns the decision to the committee. Failure of the scrap criterion alone returns the decision to the committee. We propose that the gate review be conducted by Wrenn with Nakagawa, with a written recommendation to the committee within ten business days.

**4. Approval of the second-shift operating cost** of $5.4 million annually, phased against the hiring waves, to be carried in the plant operating budget beginning in FY2027.

**5. Delegation of change authority** to the Vice President of Operations for changes up to $250,000, with committee notification, and reservation to the committee of any change above that threshold.

### Why November 6

The date is not arbitrary. Machine tool quotations are held to December 15; beyond that we requote at prevailing prices with a probable 4 to 7 percent increase and a delivery slot pushed into the following quarter. The long-lead horizontals carry 26-week delivery, which means an order placed in November delivers in May and supports a month-seven go-live in June 2027. That timing is what allows Cells 1 and 2 to be running and measured before we submit the Vantage rebid.

A decision deferred to the following committee meeting costs approximately five months. Five months pushes cell go-live past the rebid submission, which means we quote the 2027 contract on a projection rather than a result. We would rather quote it on a result.
