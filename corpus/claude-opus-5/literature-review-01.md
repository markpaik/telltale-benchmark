**Prepared for the Coronado Health Network Board of Directors**

*Yesenia Alcaraz, MSN, RN, Director of Clinical Effectiveness*
*Grant Peavey, MLIS, Medical Librarian (search strategy and retrieval)*
*Obiageli Nwachukwu, MD, FACC, Chief of Cardiovascular Services (clinical review)*

*July 2026*

## 1. Introduction: The Question and Its Boundaries

Coronado Health Network paid $1.4 million in Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program penalties in the last federal fiscal year. Heart failure accounted for the largest single share of the excess readmission ratio driving that penalty, as it does for most systems of our size and case mix. The board has appropriated $2.2 million for a transitional care program and has asked for an evidence review to inform the design of that program before allocation decisions are made in November.

This review asks a deliberately narrow question: **among adults discharged from acute care with a primary or contributing diagnosis of heart failure, which transitional care interventions reduce readmission within 30 days, and under what conditions?** Three secondary questions follow from it. First, what do these interventions cost, and what do they return? Second, which patient populations does the evidence actually describe, and which does it not? Third, what accounts for the striking disagreement across the literature, and can that disagreement be resolved well enough to support a spending decision?

The scope is bounded in several ways that the board should understand, because each boundary was a choice.

**Population.** Adults 18 and older discharged alive from an acute inpatient stay with heart failure. We included studies of mixed medical populations only where heart failure results were reported separately or where heart failure patients constituted a majority of the sample. We excluded studies restricted to left ventricular assist device recipients, transplant candidates, and patients enrolled in hospice at discharge, on the grounds that these groups are managed through separate pathways at Coronado and would not be served by the program under consideration.

**Intervention.** Any structured activity initiated at or around discharge and intended to bridge the inpatient-to-outpatient transition. This includes but is not limited to nurse-led transitional care, pharmacist medication reconciliation, home visits, telemonitoring, community health worker engagement, structured telephone follow-up, and multicomponent programs combining these elements. We excluded interventions delivered entirely within the inpatient stay with no post-discharge component, and we excluded disease management programs enrolling stable outpatients not tied to a discharge event.

**Comparator.** Usual care, however defined by the study, or an alternative active intervention.

**Outcomes.** Primary: all-cause readmission at 30 days. Secondary: heart-failure-specific readmission, all-cause readmission at 90 and 180 days, emergency department revisits, mortality, patient-reported outcomes including quality of life and self-care confidence, and cost or cost-effectiveness.

**Timeframe.** 2004 through 2025. The lower bound was set at 2004 because the Coleman Care Transitions Intervention and the mature form of the Naylor Transitional Care Model both entered the peer-reviewed literature in that period, and because pre-2004 work predates the payment environment that makes this question actionable.

**Setting.** Any acute care hospital in a high-income health system, with particular attention sought to studies conducted in settings resembling ours: multi-hospital systems, mixed urban-rural catchments, and populations with substantial Hispanic or Latino representation.

The board should be told at the outset that this review does not resolve into a single recommendation supported by uniform evidence. The literature on transitional care is large, methodologically uneven, and internally contradictory in ways that are not incidental. The most useful thing a review can do is explain *why* it contradicts itself, because the pattern of disagreement carries more actionable information than any pooled estimate.

## 2. How the Literature Was Searched

Grant Peavey conducted the searches between March and May 2026. Five sources were queried: PubMed/MEDLINE, CINAHL Complete, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and ClinicalTrials.gov. The trials registry was searched to identify completed but unpublished studies and to assess the scale of potential publication bias.

The search combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms across three concept blocks: heart failure (including MeSH *Heart Failure*, *Cardiomyopathies*, and text variants including "cardiac failure," "CHF," "congestive heart failure"); transitional care (MeSH *Transitional Care*, *Patient Discharge*, *Continuity of Patient Care*, *Aftercare*, plus text terms including "care transition," "discharge planning," "post-discharge," "hospital-to-home," "telemonitoring," "medication reconciliation," "community health worker," "promotora"); and readmission (MeSH *Patient Readmission*, plus "rehospitalization," "readmission," "hospital utilization"). Blocks were combined with AND; terms within blocks with OR. Language was restricted to English and Spanish. No geographic restriction was applied at the search stage.

The strategy was peer-reviewed against the PRESS checklist by a second librarian at the University of Arizona Health Sciences Library. Reference lists of all included syntheses were hand-searched, and forward citation tracking was performed on the eleven included reviews and on the six most-cited trials.

Screening yielded **2,847 unique records** after deduplication. Title and abstract screening was performed in duplicate by Alcaraz and Peavey, with disagreements resolved by discussion; **236 records** advanced to full-text review. Full-text assessment excluded 158 records, most commonly for wrong population (mixed medical cohorts without heart failure subgroup reporting, n=51), wrong intervention (outpatient disease management not tied to discharge, n=38), no comparator (n=29), conference abstract only with no retrievable full report (n=24), and duplicate publication of the same trial cohort (n=16).

**Seventy-eight studies were included**: 31 randomized controlled trials, 22 quasi-experimental evaluations (pre-post, interrupted time series, and non-randomized concurrent control designs), 14 qualitative studies, and 11 syntheses (systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and one realist review).

Risk of bias was assessed using Cochrane RoB 2 for randomized trials, ROBINS-I for quasi-experimental studies, and CASP for qualitative work. Ratings and full extraction — effect sizes with confidence intervals, sample characteristics, intervention components coded against the Taxonomy of Care Coordination Activities, follow-up duration, and funding source — are in the shared folder at `S:\ClinicalEffectiveness\HF_TransCare_Review\Extraction_Master_v7.xlsx`. Studies are cited below by author and year.

Two limitations of the search deserve statement. First, the trials registry search identified nine completed trials with no corresponding publication, seven of which were industry-funded telemonitoring studies. This is a meaningful signal about the telemonitoring literature specifically. Second, our Spanish-language inclusion produced only four additional records, none of which met inclusion criteria — a finding that is itself part of the gap analysis in Section 5.

## 3. Synthesis by Theme

### 3.1 Nurse-Led Transitional Care: The Efficacy-Effectiveness Collapse

No strand of this literature shows a sharper break between its early and late phases.

The foundational trials were small, single-site, and delivered by highly trained advanced practice nurses with low caseloads. Naylor et al. (2004) randomized 239 heart failure patients aged 65 and older at six Philadelphia hospitals to an APN-delivered transitional care model — hospital visits, home visits beginning within 24 hours of discharge, and telephone availability for three months — and reported a hazard ratio for time to first readmission or death of 0.64, with 104 fewer readmissions in the intervention arm over 52 weeks. Coleman et al. (2006), testing a lighter-touch coaching model, found a 30-day readmission rate of 8.3 percent versus 11.9 percent in controls. Stauffer et al. (2011) reported a 48 percent relative reduction in a single-hospital implementation. Across this early cohort, reductions clustered between **25 and 40 percent**, and the consistency was persuasive enough to drive the model into policy: the Community-based Care Transitions Program was built substantially on this evidence base.

Then the pragmatic trials arrived, and the effect vanished.

The three large multi-site pragmatic trials published after 2016 in our included set — **Dhalla et al. (2016)**, **Kripalani et al. (2019)**, and **Voss et al. (2021)** — collectively enrolled more than 6,800 patients and found no statistically significant reduction in 30-day readmission. Dhalla, testing a virtual ward model in Toronto with 1,923 patients, reported an adjusted odds ratio of 0.94 (95% CI 0.79–1.12). Kripalani's pharmacist-inclusive transitional care intervention across four US health systems produced a 30-day readmission rate of 20.1 percent versus 20.9 percent. Voss, in a five-site trial of nurse-led coaching with 2,140 patients, found 18.4 versus 19.2 percent.

Alcaraz and Nwachukwu reviewed these together, and three explanations for the divergence are available. They are not mutually exclusive, and the choice among them has direct budget consequences.

**The dilution explanation.** The early trials used doctoral- or master's-prepared APNs carrying caseloads of 15 to 20 patients. Dhalla's virtual ward nurses carried 40 to 60. Voss's coaches were bachelor's-prepared nurses with a mean caseload of 55, and the protocol permitted the first home visit to occur up to 72 hours post-discharge rather than within 24. Naylor's own later work (Naylor et al., 2018) is explicit on this point: the model's effect appears to depend on visit timing, clinician training level, and caseload, and implementations that relax all three should not be expected to reproduce the original estimates. If this explanation is correct, the pragmatic trials did not test the intervention; they tested a degraded version of it, and the appropriate conclusion is that the intervention works but is expensive to deliver at fidelity.

**The secular trend explanation.** Usual care in 2004 was not usual care in 2019. The Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program took effect in 2012, and hospitals responded. Post-discharge follow-up calls, medication reconciliation, and discharge summary transmission became near-universal. Kripalani's control arm received scheduled follow-up appointments and a discharge medication list as standard practice — components that constituted the *intervention* in several early trials. Under this reading, the effect did not disappear; the comparator caught up, and the marginal benefit of a formal program shrank because much of it was absorbed into baseline care. Bradley et al. (2013), analyzing hospital-level strategy adoption across 599 hospitals, supports the plausibility of this mechanism, finding that hospitals adopting more transitional strategies had lower risk-standardized readmission rates but that the marginal contribution of each additional strategy declined sharply.

**The selection explanation.** Early single-site trials recruited patients who consented, spoke English, had telephones, and lived within a defined home-visit radius. Pragmatic trials used broader eligibility and, in Voss's case, opt-out consent. The pragmatic populations were sicker, poorer, and less connected to primary care. If transitional care works chiefly by activating patients who have the resources to act on activation, then broadening the population dilutes the average effect while preserving benefit in a subgroup. Voss's own prespecified subgroup analysis is suggestive: among patients with a documented primary care provider, the intervention effect was OR 0.78 (95% CI 0.61–0.99); among those without, OR 1.09 (95% CI 0.87–1.36). This interaction was not the primary finding and the trial was not powered for it, but it is consistent with the qualitative literature described in Section 3.6.

Nwachukwu's clinical assessment is that all three mechanisms are operating and that the dilution explanation is the most actionable, because it is the only one Coronado can control. The design implication is that a program staffed at low fidelity should be expected to produce a null result and should not be funded on the assumption that it will not.

### 3.2 Telemonitoring: Two Meta-Analyses, One Evidence Base, Opposite Conclusions

The telemonitoring literature offers an unusually clean natural experiment in how synthesis method determines synthesis conclusion.

**Inglis et al. (2015)**, a Cochrane review of structured telephone support and telemonitoring in heart failure, pooled 41 studies and reported that non-invasive telemonitoring reduced all-cause mortality (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.68–0.94) and heart-failure-related hospitalization (RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.60–0.83). **Nakamura et al. (2022)**, drawing on a substantially overlapping trial set — 33 of 41 studies in common by our extraction — concluded that telemonitoring had no significant effect on all-cause readmission (RR 0.95, 95% CI 0.86–1.05) and that apparent benefits disappeared when analysis was restricted to low-risk-of-bias trials.

The disagreement is not about the data. It is about four analytic decisions.

**Outcome definition.** Inglis pooled heart-failure-related hospitalization as a primary outcome; Nakamura pooled all-cause readmission. Heart-failure-specific readmission is more responsive to a heart-failure-specific intervention and is also more susceptible to ascertainment bias, since intervention patients under active monitoring are more likely to have their decompensation coded as heart failure. Coronado's penalty is driven by all-cause readmission, which makes Nakamura's outcome the operationally relevant one regardless of which is scientifically preferable.

**Risk-of-bias handling.** Nakamura applied a sensitivity analysis restricted to trials at low risk of bias, and the pooled effect moved to the null. Inglis reported risk of bias but did not condition the primary estimate on it. Our own RoB 2 assessment of the shared trials found 18 of 33 at high risk or with some concerns, most commonly for deviations from intended intervention — unsurprising, since telemonitoring cannot be blinded and non-adherence is systematically related to the outcome.

**Trial vintage and the technology problem.** The trials Inglis pooled span 1999 to 2013. Nakamura's extends to 2021. The intervening period saw two large, well-conducted null trials — **Chaudhry et al. (2010)**, the Tele-HF trial, with 1,653 patients and a 30-day readmission rate of 26.7 versus 27.1 percent, and **Ong et al. (2016)**, the BEAT-HF trial, with 1,437 patients and 180-day readmission of 50.8 versus 49.2 percent — that pulled the later pooled estimate toward null. Both trials used remote monitoring with structured telephone follow-up; both reported adherence problems that the earlier, smaller positive trials did not encounter. Chaudhry reported that only 55 percent of intervention patients were still using the system at the end of the study.

**The counterexample.** Against these, **Abraham et al. (2011)**, the CHAMPION trial of implantable pulmonary artery pressure monitoring, reported a 39 percent reduction in heart failure hospitalizations (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.50–0.75) at six months, sustained in open-access follow-up. This is the strongest signal in the telemonitoring literature and it is also the least generalizable to a transitional care program: it requires an implanted device, a cardiology-led titration service, and a patient population with NYHA Class III symptoms and a prior hospitalization. It tells us that hemodynamic data can drive management change; it does not tell us that weight scales and blood pressure cuffs can.

The synthesis that best reconciles this strand is not a pooled estimate but a mechanism claim: **telemonitoring reduces readmission only when the monitored signal reliably precedes decompensation and when a clinician is contractually obligated to act on it within a defined window.** Weight change fails the first condition — Chaudhry and Ong both used weight-based protocols and both were null. Pulmonary artery pressure satisfies it. Programs that transmit data to a nurse who reviews it during business hours satisfy neither.

Finally, the registry search matters here. Seven completed, unpublished, industry-funded telemonitoring trials suggest the published estimate is optimistic even before these adjustments.

### 3.3 Pharmacist-Led Medication Reconciliation: Effective on the Wrong Outcome

This strand is the most methodologically consistent and the least conclusive on the outcome the board cares about.

Pharmacist involvement reliably reduces medication discrepancies and adverse drug events. **Gillespie et al. (2009)** reported a 16 percent reduction in all hospital visits and an 80 percent reduction in medication-related readmissions in an elderly Swedish cohort. **Kwan et al. (2013)**, in a systematic review of 26 studies, found consistent reductions in unintentional discrepancies. **Anderegg et al. (2014)** found that pharmacist-led reconciliation targeted to high-risk patients reduced 30-day readmission from 18.1 to 13.7 percent.

But the largest and best-controlled trials do not find readmission benefit. **Kripalani et al. (2012)**, the PILL-CVD trial with 851 patients, found no difference in clinically important medication errors at 30 days (OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.77–1.10) and no readmission difference. **Farris et al. (2014)**, in a three-arm trial testing minimal versus enhanced pharmacist intervention, found no dose-response relationship on readmission.

The contradiction resolves on **targeting**. Anderegg's positive result came from a pharmacist service restricted to patients scoring high on a readmission risk prediction tool; the intervention reached roughly 30 percent of discharges. Kripalani's and Farris's null results came from unselected populations. **Ravn-Nielsen et al. (2018)**, a Danish trial with 1,467 patients, provides the cleanest evidence for this reading: an extended pharmacist intervention including motivational interviewing and post-discharge follow-up reduced readmission at six months (OR 0.62, 95% CI 0.46–0.84) while a basic reconciliation-only intervention did not (OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.67–1.16). Same trial, same population, two intervention intensities, divergent results.

There is also a plausibility problem the strand rarely addresses directly. Medication error is a mechanism for readmission, but not the dominant one in heart failure. **Dharmarajan et al. (2013)**, analyzing 30-day readmission diagnoses in more than 300,000 Medicare heart failure discharges, found that only 35 percent of readmissions were for heart failure itself and that the remainder were spread across a wide range of causes with no single dominant contributor. An intervention that perfects the medication list addresses a real problem of modest attributable fraction. The effect size ceiling is therefore low, which explains why underpowered trials find nothing and why the positive trials are the ones that either targeted high-risk patients or bundled reconciliation with behavioral components.

Nwachukwu notes a Coronado-specific consideration: our guideline-directed medical therapy optimization rates at discharge are below regional benchmark. A pharmacist service that titrates therapy rather than merely reconciling it addresses a different and possibly larger mechanism, and the trials above largely do not test that.

### 3.4 Home Visits: The Strongest Signal and the Weakest Fit

Home visiting produces the most consistent positive findings in this literature and the most difficult implementation profile.

**Stewart et al. (1999)** reported that a single home visit by a nurse and pharmacist at seven to fourteen days post-discharge reduced unplanned readmissions over six months (36 versus 63 events per patient-year). **Naylor et al. (2004)**, described above, was home-visit-intensive. **Rich et al. (1995)**, an early multidisciplinary intervention including home visits, reported a 44 percent reduction in readmission at 90 days. **Feltner et al. (2014)**, in an AHRQ-commissioned systematic review of 47 trials, concluded that home-visiting programs and multidisciplinary heart failure clinic interventions were the only categories with moderate-strength evidence of reduced all-cause readmission and mortality; structured telephone support reduced mortality but not readmission; telemonitoring reduced neither.

Feltner's conclusion is the single most defensible summary statement available in this literature, and it is worth stating plainly for the board: **among transitional care modalities, home visiting has the best evidence, and it is also the most expensive.**

The contradictions within this strand are mostly about dose and timing rather than direction. **Jaarsma et al. (2008)**, the COACH trial with 1,023 patients in the Netherlands, found no significant difference in readmission or death between basic and intensive nurse-led support, both of which included home contact; if anything, the intensive arm showed a non-significant trend toward more events. **Hansen et al. (2011)**, reviewing 43 studies, noted that no single component was consistently associated with benefit and that multicomponent programs outperformed single-component ones — implying that home visiting may function less as an active ingredient than as a delivery vehicle for whatever else the program does.

The timing evidence is more consistent. Trials with first contact within 48 to 72 hours (Stewart, Naylor, Rich) outperformed those permitting a longer window (Voss, and the basic arm of Ravn-Nielsen). **Hernandez et al. (2010)**, examining 30,136 Medicare heart failure discharges, found that hospitals in the highest quartile for early follow-up (within seven days) had significantly lower 30-day readmission (adjusted HR 0.85), though this is observational and vulnerable to confounding by hospital quality.

The fit problem for Coronado is geographic. Home visiting requires travel time. Our four hospitals draw from a catchment that extends into Pinal and Santa Cruz counties, with patients living more than 60 miles from the nearest clinic. None of the home-visiting trials in our included set enrolled a meaningfully rural population; the median catchment radius where reported was under 25 miles. Section 5 returns to this.

### 3.5 Community Health Workers: Promising, Underpowered, and Nearly Absent in Heart Failure

The community health worker literature is the smallest strand in this review and the one where the gap between promise and evidence is widest.

**Kangovi et al. (2018)**, the IMPaCT trial, is the strongest study: 592 patients with multiple chronic conditions randomized to CHW support, with a reduction in total hospital days (mean difference −155 days per 100 patients) and improved self-rated health, though 30-day readmission itself was not significantly reduced. **Kangovi et al. (2014)**, the earlier trial, found improved post-hospital primary care follow-up (60 versus 48 percent) and reduced 30-day readmission among those with multiple readmissions.

The heart-failure-specific evidence is thin. **Ruiz et al. (2019)** and **Otero-Sabogal et al. (2012)** report promising results in Hispanic and Latino populations, but both are quasi-experimental with pre-post designs and small samples (n=140 and n=176 respectively), and both are at serious risk of bias for confounding and regression to the mean under ROBINS-I. **Hunter et al. (2020)**, an evaluation in a Texas border population, reported a 30-day readmission reduction from 22 to 14 percent but had no concurrent control group.

Set against this, **Coleman et al. (2015)** found no significant effect of a lay navigator model in a large health system implementation, and the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services evaluation of the Community-based Care Transitions Program (Hostetter & Klein, 2015; CMS, 2017) found that most participating sites, several of which used community health worker models, did not achieve the readmission reductions required for continued funding, and the program was not extended.

What the qualitative literature contributes here is a plausible mechanism for why CHW effects might be real but hard to detect. The barriers CHWs address — transportation, food insecurity, housing instability, health literacy, immigration-related fear of institutional contact — are concentrated in a subset of patients. In an unselected trial population, most patients do not have these barriers, and the intervention has nothing to work on for them. The average treatment effect is therefore small even where the effect in the affected subgroup is large. This is the same statistical logic that explains the pharmacist targeting findings in Section 3.3, and it is the most important recurring insight in this review.

### 3.6 Structured Telephone Follow-Up and What Qualitative Work Adds

Structured post-discharge telephone calls are the cheapest intervention in this review and the most widely implemented. The evidence is correspondingly disappointing.

**Inglis et al. (2015)** found structured telephone support reduced heart-failure-related hospitalization (RR 0.85) and mortality (RR 0.87). **Feltner et al. (2014)** found it reduced mortality but not all-cause readmission. **Riegel et al. (2006)** reported reduced heart failure readmission in a telephone case management trial. But **DeWalt et al. (2006)** and **Jaarsma et al. (2008)** found no readmission benefit, and **Harrison et al. (2011)**, in a pragmatic evaluation of a scaled call program across a multi-hospital system, found no effect on 30-day readmission despite 78 percent call completion.

The pattern here mirrors the nurse-led strand: efficacy trials with trained clinicians making substantive calls show effects; effectiveness evaluations of scripted calls made by staff without clinical decision authority do not. **Bahr et al. (2014)** and **Melton et al. (2012)** both found that call outcomes depended almost entirely on whether the caller could resolve the identified problem — schedule an appointment, adjust a diuretic dose, arrange transport — rather than merely document it.

The 14 qualitative studies in this review converge on findings the trial literature does not capture. **Kangovi et al. (2014, qualitative companion)** documented that patients' post-discharge priorities frequently diverged from clinical priorities: housing, income, and caregiving obligations displaced medication adherence. **Retrum et al. (2013)** found that patients readmitted within 30 days often described the readmission as reasonable and necessary rather than as a failure. **Allen et al. (2018)** identified caregiver burden as a determinant of readmission that no included trial measured. **Nair et al. (2020)**, in a study of Spanish-speaking patients in the Southwest, found that discharge instruction comprehension was substantially lower than staff assumed and that professional interpreter use at discharge was inconsistent even where available.

These findings do not overturn the quantitative literature. They explain it. If a large share of readmissions is driven by factors outside the clinical transition — housing, transport, caregiver capacity, comprehension — then interventions confined to the clinical transition have a bounded ceiling, and the trials that find nothing may be measuring accurately.

### 3.7 Cost and Return: A Range Too Wide to Be Useful Unqualified

Reported economic results span from **$2,900 saved per patient** to a net loss.

**Naylor et al. (2004)** reported mean total cost of $7,636 per intervention patient versus $12,481 per control, a difference of roughly $4,845 at 52 weeks. **Stauffer et al. (2011)** reported net savings but also documented that the program did not cover its costs from the hospital's perspective once reduced admission revenue was accounted for. **Kangovi et al. (2020)** reported a return of $2.47 per dollar invested for the IMPaCT CHW model from a payer perspective. Against these, **Ong et al. (2016)** found telemonitoring costs unrecovered, and the CMS evaluation of the Community-based Care Transitions Program found most sites did not generate savings sufficient to sustain funding.

Four factors drive the range, and none of them are incidental:

1. **Perspective.** Societal and payer perspectives generate favorable ratios; hospital perspectives frequently do not, because avoided admissions are avoided revenue. Under a fee-for-service mix, a successful program can worsen a hospital's margin while improving the payer's. Coronado's payer mix — approximately 46 percent Medicare, 22 percent AHCCCS — determines which perspective governs, and the answer differs by hospital.

2. **Whether the penalty is modeled.** The $1.4 million penalty is a fixed cost avoided, not a marginal revenue change, and studies conducted before 2012 or outside the US do not model it at all. This systematically understates the value to Coronado.

3. **Time horizon.** Naylor's favorable ratio depends on 52-week follow-up. At 30 days, the cost of the intervention is fully incurred and only a fraction of the benefit is realized.

4. **Denominator.** Programs costed against all discharges look expensive; programs costed against the targeted high-risk subset look efficient. This is the targeting logic again, now expressed financially.

## 4. Design Differences That Explain the Contradictions

Rather than treat the disagreements strand by strand, five cross-cutting design factors account for most of the variance in this literature.

**Intervention fidelity and intensity.** The single most consistent predictor of a positive result is intervention dose: clinician training level, caseload, timing of first contact, and duration of follow-up. Trials at the high end (Naylor, Rich, Stewart, Ravn-Nielsen extended arm) find effects; trials at the low end (Voss, Harrison, Ravn-Nielsen basic arm) do not. This is not a nuisance finding — it is the finding.

**Comparator drift.** Usual care improved substantially between 2004 and 2020. Effect sizes are measured against a moving baseline, and the apparent decline in efficacy partly reflects an improving control condition rather than a failing intervention.

**Population selection and effect heterogeneity.** Interventions that address a barrier only some patients have will show small average effects in unselected populations. Risk-targeted trials outperform unselected ones consistently across every strand in this review.

**Outcome definition.** All-cause versus disease-specific readmission produces materially different conclusions from the same data, and disease-specific outcomes are vulnerable to ascertainment bias in unblinded designs.

**Analytic and reporting choices at the synthesis level.** The Inglis-Nakamura divergence demonstrates that outcome selection and risk-of-bias conditioning can reverse a conclusion on a shared evidence base. Board members should treat any single meta-analytic estimate in this field with caution.

## 5. What the Evidence Does Not Reach

The gaps are specific and they map directly onto Coronado's population.

**Rural patients.** No included trial enrolled a majority-rural sample. Where catchment was reported, median radius was under 25 miles. Coronado serves patients at distances exceeding 60 miles. Home visiting — the modality with the best evidence — has effectively no evidence base at rural travel distances, and its cost structure changes fundamentally when a visit consumes three hours of travel.

**Spanish-speaking patients.** Approximately 29 percent of Coronado's heart failure admissions involve patients whose preferred language is Spanish. Only six included studies reported outcomes by language preference, and only two (Ruiz et al., 2019; Otero-Sabogal et al., 2012) were designed for Spanish-speaking populations; both are quasi-experimental with serious risk of bias. Our Spanish-language search yielded no additional qualifying studies.

**Patients without transportation.** Transportation appears as a barrier in the qualitative literature repeatedly and as a measured variable in almost no trial. Trials requiring clinic follow-up implicitly excluded patients who could not attend.

**Follow-up beyond 90 days.** Only nine included studies followed patients past 90 days. Whether transitional care shifts readmission timing or prevents it is therefore largely unknown, and the distinction matters for the penalty, which is calculated at 30 days.

**Other unreached areas.** Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction is underrepresented despite constituting roughly half of admissions. Patients with cognitive impairment and those without stable housing were commonly excluded. Caregiver capacity is almost never measured. And the seven unpublished industry-funded telemonitoring trials identified in the registry search suggest the published telemonitoring evidence is favorably selected.

## 6. Implications for a System of This Size

Seven implications follow for Coronado.

**One: fund fidelity, not coverage.** The evidence is clear that low-intensity, high-caseload programs produce null results. A program that reaches 100 percent of heart failure discharges at low intensity is the design most likely to spend $2.2 million and change nothing. The board should prefer a smaller, well-staffed program to a larger, thin one.

**Two: target.** Every strand of this literature shows larger effects in risk-selected populations. Coronado should deploy a validated risk stratification approach and concentrate resources on the highest-risk 25 to 35 percent of heart failure discharges. This roughly triples the affordable per-patient intensity within the same budget.

**Three: prioritize home visiting and early contact.** Feltner's synthesis identifies home visiting and multidisciplinary clinic follow-up as the only modalities with moderate-strength evidence. Contact within 48 to 72 hours is the most consistent timing signal. For patients beyond a feasible visit radius, a hybrid — one early in-person contact where possible, structured clinical follow-up otherwise — is a reasonable adaptation, but the board should understand it as an untested adaptation, not an evidence-based one.

**Four: treat telemonitoring as unproven at this scale.** The evidence does not support general-purpose weight-and-vitals telemonitoring as a readmission intervention. If Coronado pursues remote monitoring, it should be limited to a defined cohort with a clinician contractually obligated to respond within a specified window, and it should be evaluated as a pilot with a comparison group.

**Five: build pharmacist involvement around titration, not reconciliation alone.** Reconciliation-only interventions do not reduce readmission. Extended pharmacist involvement including behavioral components does. Given Coronado's below-benchmark guideline-directed therapy rates at discharge, a titration-focused pharmacist service addresses a mechanism the trial literature has not adequately tested but that clinical reasoning supports.

**Six: build the evidence we lack.** Because no trial describes our rural, Spanish-speaking, transportation-limited population, Coronado's implementation should be designed as an evaluable one — staggered rollout across the four hospitals permitting stepped-wedge or interrupted time series analysis, prespecified subgroup reporting by language preference, distance from facility, and transportation access, and follow-up to 180 days. This costs little at the design stage and is impossible to retrofit.

**Seven: set expectations honestly with the board.** The most probable outcome, based on this literature, is a 15 to 25 percent relative reduction in 30-day readmission within the targeted subgroup — not across all heart failure discharges. Against 41,000 annual admissions with roughly 2,100 heart failure discharges, a targeted program reaching 600 patients at that effect size would prevent approximately 25 to 40 readmissions annually. Whether that recovers a meaningful share of the $1.4 million penalty depends on where Coronado sits relative to the national excess readmission ratio threshold, which is a modeling question the finance office should answer before November.

The board should also be told what this review cannot say. It cannot say that a transitional care program will eliminate the penalty. It cannot say that any specific component is the active ingredient. And it cannot say what happens to patients who live 70 miles from Tucson, speak Spanish, and have no car, because nobody has studied them.
