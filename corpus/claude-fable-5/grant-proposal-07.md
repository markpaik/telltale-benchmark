## Statement of Need

The Cimarron Junction Rail District owns and is responsible for 38 miles of short line railroad in northwest Oklahoma. The line is the only rail connection for nine shippers, including two grain elevators, a gypsum plant, and a wind-tower fabricator whose products cannot practically move any other way. The District is a small public port authority — four employees and a $2.1 million annual budget — and the physical condition of its railroad has now deteriorated past the point where ordinary maintenance can arrest the decline.

**Condition of the line.** Fourteen of the District's 38 miles operate under 10 mile-per-hour slow orders. The affected segments are laid with 85-pound rail installed in 1948, now seventy-eight years in service. Chief Engineer Grigor Sarkisian's 2026 condition assessment, conducted jointly with the District's contract operator, documented the following on the slow-order territory: rail-head wear at or beyond condemning limits on 61 percent of measured locations; 22 broken-rail incidents in the last five years, of which nine occurred in the last eighteen months; defective tie counts averaging 1,100 per mile against a serviceable standard of no more than 500; and surface and cross-level deviations that fail FRA Class 2 standards throughout. Six timber bridges on the line, all built between 1946 and 1953, carry load restrictions. Two of the six have been shored with temporary bents. The Sarkisian assessment concludes that without capital intervention, an additional eight to ten miles will require slow orders within thirty-six months, and that the two shored bridges will require embargo — closure to all traffic — within five years. An embargo at either bridge severs service to shippers beyond it. The full condition assessment, with rail-wear measurements, tie counts, and bridge inspection reports, is submitted as Attachment A.

The line's weight limit compounds the problem. The District's track and bridges are rated for 268,000-pound gross cars, while the North American interchange standard is 286,000 pounds. The two grain elevators on the line must either load cars light — sacrificing roughly six percent of capacity per car and paying full freight anyway — or truck grain to a 286,000-pound-capable elevator sixty miles away. The gypsum plant faces the same penalty. Every year the District remains below the 286,000-pound standard, its shippers pay a competitive tax that their rivals on rehabilitated lines do not.

**Traffic decline.** The consequences are measurable. The District handled 3,400 carloads in 2019. Last year it handled 2,100 — a 38 percent decline in five years. Shipper interviews conducted by the District in preparation for this application attribute the decline principally to transit-time unreliability caused by slow orders, to the car-weight penalty, and to diversion of traffic to truck. Each carload lost puts, on average, 3.5 truckloads onto US-270, a two-lane federal highway that carries school buses, farm equipment, and oilfield traffic through three towns. The 1,300 annual carloads lost since 2019 correspond to approximately 4,550 additional heavy truckloads per year on US-270, with the attendant pavement damage, safety exposure, and emissions.

**The setback the Trust should know about, stated plainly.** The District's traffic projections were dealt a blow in April 2026, when an ethanol producer that had pledged 900 carloads per year withdrew its commitment after its parent company idled the plant for reasons unrelated to rail service. The District has removed every one of those 900 carloads from the projections in this application. The volume case presented here rests entirely on the nine shippers currently on the line, each of which has provided a signed volume commitment letter (Attachment C), and on conservative recapture of traffic those shippers currently divert to truck. The District would rather present the Trust a smaller number it can defend than a larger one it cannot.

**Why now.** The District stands at a decision point that will not remain open. The contract operator's lease expires this year. The two shored bridges are on borrowed time. The shippers who have stayed with the line through five years of slow orders have told the District — through their representative on this application, Manuela Arreola of the elevator cooperative — that they will make their own modal decisions within the next two crop cycles. If the line is rehabilitated, they will commit volume to it. If it is not, they will invest in truck fleets and be gone, and a 38-mile public asset serving a nine-county agricultural economy will follow the path of every deferred-maintenance short line before it: slow orders, embargoes, abandonment. The Short Line Revitalization Program exists for precisely this situation, and the District respectfully submits that no application before the Trust presents a clearer case of a line that is savable now and will not be savable later.

## Project Description

The Cimarron Junction Mainline Rehabilitation and Transload Development Project consists of four integrated elements to be constructed over three years, matching the Program's three-year performance period.

**Element 1: Relay 22 miles of mainline with 115-pound rail.** The District will remove the 1948-vintage 85-pound rail on all 14 slow-order miles plus 8 adjacent miles that the condition assessment identifies as next to fail, and relay those 22 miles with 115-pound relay rail of certified quality, on new and reconditioned ties, fully surfaced and lined to FRA Class 2 standards with the geometry to support future Class 3 qualification. The District has priced 115-pound relay rail from two regional suppliers and holds a written quote valid through the anticipated award date. Tie renewal will install approximately 700 new ties per mile on the relay segments, bringing defective tie counts below the 500-per-mile serviceable standard line-wide. Upon completion, the entire 38-mile railroad will operate at 25 miles per hour with no slow orders, and the relayed segments will be structurally capable of 286,000-pound cars.

**Element 2: Rebuild six timber bridges.** All six load-restricted timber bridges will be rebuilt to a 286,000-pound, Cooper E-60 design standard. Four will be rebuilt as ballast-deck structures on driven steel piles with precast concrete caps; the two currently shored bridges, which are the shortest spans, will be replaced with culvert or box structures where hydraulic analysis permits, which is cheaper to build and nearly maintenance-free thereafter. Chief Engineer Sarkisian has completed 30-percent design on all six structures; hydraulic and geotechnical work is funded in the pre-award budget and will be complete before construction. Bridge reconstruction is sequenced first in the construction schedule because the shored structures represent the nearest-term embargo risk.

**Element 3: Raise line capacity from 268,000 to 286,000 pounds per car.** This element is accomplished through Elements 1 and 2 — the 115-pound rail and rebuilt bridges provide the structural capacity — plus targeted work outside the relay segments: replacement of four turnouts with 115-pound units, joint elimination on two miles of otherwise sound track, and re-rating of the full line following inspection. Upon certification, every shipper on the line gains approximately six percent more payload per car at no additional freight cost, a direct and permanent improvement in their competitive position.

**Element 4: Construct a 14-acre transload facility.** The District will develop a 14-acre transload facility at Cimarron Junction on land the District already owns in fee — a point addressed candidly in the Organizational Capacity section, because the District's failure to secure right-of-way doomed a prior grant, and it has made certain that cannot happen here. The facility will include a 2,400-foot loop-capable siding, a compacted aggregate pad, a permanent loading ramp, scale, and lighting. The facility serves three documented demands: it gives the wind-tower fabricator laydown and project-staging capacity it has formally requested; it allows aggregate, fertilizer, and pipe traffic that currently trucks past the line to convert to rail; and it provides the District a direct revenue stream — transload fees — that funds long-term maintenance, as described under Sustainability.

**Project partners and their roles.** The project is a genuine multi-party undertaking. The county commission will reconstruct the eleven public road crossings within the project limits, performing the roadway approach work with county forces and materials as in-kind match, under an interlocal agreement executed in draft and submitted as Attachment E. The regional economic development district has committed cash match and will lead transload marketing to off-line shippers. Redbud Terminal Railway, the line's contract operator, will contribute cash match and perform force-account track work at audited cost under a new lease described in the Sustainability section. Shipper participation is led by Manuela Arreola, who chairs the District's shipper advisory committee and coordinated the volume commitment letters.

**Schedule.** Year 1 (2027 construction season): bridge reconstruction, all six structures; relay miles 1–7; transload earthwork and siding. Year 2 (2028): relay miles 8–16; crossing reconstruction; transload pad, ramp, and scale. Year 3 (2029): relay miles 17–22; turnouts and joint elimination; 286,000-pound certification; project closeout. The schedule front-loads the embargo-risk bridges and sequences relay work to open slow-order territory to the shippers with the largest committed volumes first, so that carload gains begin accruing in Year 1 rather than waiting for full completion.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

The District has structured its objectives around the five criteria the Trust scores — carloads, slow-order miles eliminated, truck vehicle-miles avoided, shipper retention, and cost per carload — so that the Trust can hold the District accountable in the Trust's own terms. All baselines are calendar-year 2025 actuals unless noted.

**Goal 1: Restore traffic volume.**
- *Objective 1.1:* Increase annual carloads from the 2,100 baseline to 2,300 by the end of Year 1, 2,650 by the end of Year 2, and 3,000 by the end of Year 3, with a Year 5 target of 3,400 — full recovery to the 2019 level. These figures are built shipper-by-shipper from the signed commitment letters in Attachment C and include zero carloads from the withdrawn ethanol plant. Should the ethanol plant reopen, its traffic would be additive to these targets, not necessary to them.
- *Objective 1.2:* Handle at least 150 carloads through the new transload facility in its first full year of operation and 400 by Year 3, from origins and destinations not currently served by direct rail.

**Goal 2: Eliminate slow orders.**
- *Objective 2.1:* Reduce slow-order mileage from 14 miles to no more than 7 by the end of Year 1, no more than 3 by the end of Year 2, and zero by the end of Year 3, verified by the operator's track bulletin record and independent post-construction inspection.
- *Objective 2.2:* Qualify all 38 miles to FRA Class 2 (25 mph) by the end of Year 3, with geometry on the relayed 22 miles meeting Class 3 tolerances.

**Goal 3: Reduce truck traffic on US-270.**
- *Objective 3.1:* Convert the incremental 900 annual carloads (baseline 2,100 to Year 3 target 3,000) from truck to rail, removing approximately 3,150 truckloads per year from US-270 at the established 3.5-truckload-per-carload ratio. At the 62-mile average truck haul documented in the shipper survey (Attachment D), this avoids approximately 195,000 heavy-truck vehicle-miles annually by Year 3, and over one million truck vehicle-miles cumulatively within six years of project completion.

**Goal 4: Retain every shipper.**
- *Objective 4.1:* Retain all nine current shippers through the grant period and for five years after — 100 percent shipper retention, measured annually by active shipping records. The District notes that it has retained all nine through five years of declining service; it does not intend to lose one after conditions improve.
- *Objective 4.2:* Add at least two new rail-served or transload customers by the end of Year 3, with recruitment led by the regional economic development district.

**Goal 5: Deliver defensible cost per carload.**
- *Objective 5.1:* Achieve a program cost per carload of no more than $110, calculated as total project cost ($6.5 million) divided by projected carloads over the improvements' conservative 20-year design life at the Year 3 volume (3,000 × 20 = 60,000 carloads, or $108 per carload). Measured against grant funds alone, the figure is $76 per carload. Measured against incremental carloads only — the strictest possible test — the grant cost is $253 per incremental carload over twenty years, against an avoided truck-freight and pavement cost the District estimates at more than four times that figure.

**Goal 6: Certify 286,000-pound capacity.**
- *Objective 6.1:* Obtain 286,000-pound certification for the full line by the end of Year 3, and document the resulting per-car payload gain for the two grain elevators and the gypsum plant in the final report.

Each objective carries a named owner, a data source, and a reporting date in the evaluation matrix at Attachment F.

## Evaluation Plan

The District will evaluate the project against the objectives above using data it already collects or can collect at negligible cost, reported to the Trust on the Program's semiannual schedule.

**Data sources and methods.** Carload counts come from the operator's interchange records and waybill data, which Redbud Terminal Railway is contractually obligated under the new lease to report to the District monthly; these are hard counts, not estimates, and are reconcilable against the connecting Class I's records. Slow-order mileage comes from the operator's track bulletin file, a regulatory record. Truck vehicle-miles avoided are calculated by applying the fixed 3.5-truckload-per-carload conversion and the 62-mile average haul (re-surveyed in Year 3) to incremental carloads; the methodology is stated in advance in Attachment D so the Trust can audit the arithmetic. Shipper retention is verified annually through shipping records and a written shipper survey administered by Manuela Arreola's advisory committee, which also captures satisfaction, transit-time, and car-supply measures that function as leading indicators of retention risk. Cost per carload is computed from audited project expenditures and actual carloads. Construction-quality outcomes — tie counts, surface, gage, bridge ratings — are verified by an independent inspection firm retained by the District, not by the construction contractor, with final geometry confirmed before closeout.

**Responsibility and governance.** Executive Director Ana-Lucia Ferreira owns the evaluation plan and signs each report to the Trust. Operations Manager Dale Kittredge compiles carload, slow-order, and operating data monthly and maintains the project dashboard, which the board reviews at every meeting under a standing agenda item established by board resolution. Chief Engineer Sarkisian certifies engineering and construction measures. Board Chair Wilbur Hightower reviews and countersigns each semiannual report, so that the governing body — not only staff — is formally accountable to the Trust for results.

**Interim milestones and corrective action.** The plan sets interim tripwires, not just end-state targets: if carloads at any semiannual report fall more than 10 percent below the trajectory to that year's objective, the District will deliver to the Trust, within 60 days, a written variance analysis and corrective-action plan approved by the board. The District adopted this mechanism deliberately, in light of its experience with the withdrawn ethanol commitment: the lesson it took from April 2026 is that projections must be monitored continuously and revised openly, not defended past their expiration.

**Final evaluation.** Within 120 days of project completion, the District will submit a final evaluation report presenting three-year actuals against every objective, the certified cost per carload, before-and-after track charts and bridge ratings, the truck-diversion calculation with worksheets, and a five-year post-grant monitoring commitment under which the District will continue to report carloads, slow-order status, and shipper retention to the Trust annually at its own expense.

## Organizational Capacity

The District is small, and it will not pretend otherwise to the Trust. Four employees and a $2.1 million budget do not build a $6.5 million project through in-house capacity; they build it through disciplined use of contracted expertise, strong partners, and governance that has learned from its mistakes. The Trust's file on this District contains one such mistake, and the District will address it first.

**The 2020 state program funds, and what changed.** In 2020, the District was awarded state program funds for a siding project and returned $260,000 unspent after it failed to close on the necessary right-of-way before the performance deadline. The proximate cause was a title defect discovered late; the actual cause was that the District began the acquisition process after award rather than before it, had no acquisition timeline with decision gates, and had no board-level visibility into the problem until the deadline was unrecoverable. The District does not offer the Trust excuses for this; it offers the corrective record. Since 2022, under policies adopted by the board and administered by Executive Director Ferreira: (1) the District does not apply for capital funds for any project element requiring property interests it does not already control — and accordingly, the 14-acre transload site in this application is owned by the District in fee, with title insurance issued and a current survey on file, both included at Attachment G; (2) every grant-funded project operates under a milestone schedule with 30-day board reporting, adopted by resolution before funds are accepted; (3) the District retains outside grant-administration counsel for all awards above $250,000, budgeted in this application; and (4) the District has since successfully administered and closed out two smaller awards — a state crossing-safety grant and a federal planning grant — on schedule and with clean audits, documented at Attachment H. The District returned that $260,000 rather than spend it badly, and it has spent the years since making sure it never has to return money again.

**Key personnel.** Executive Director Ana-Lucia Ferreira has led the District for six years and previously administered public works capital programs for a county government; she is the authorized official and project director. Chief Engineer Grigor Sarkisian is a licensed professional engineer with twenty-two years in railroad track and structures, including relay and bridge programs on three other short lines; he authored the condition assessment and the 30-percent bridge designs and will serve as engineer of record. Operations Manager Dale Kittredge, a former Class I roadmaster, manages day-to-day coordination with Redbud Terminal Railway and will manage construction-phase train operations, flagging, and outage windows. The District's finance officer, its fourth employee, maintains the District's books, which have received unmodified audit opinions for five consecutive years (Attachment I).

**Governance.** The District is governed by a five-member appointed board chaired by Wilbur Hightower, a retired grain-elevator manager who has served on the board for eleven years. The board meets monthly in public session, and Chair Hightower has established a standing capital-projects agenda item under which this project, if funded, will be reviewed at every meeting against schedule, budget, and the evaluation dashboard. The shipper advisory committee chaired by Manuela Arreola gives the customers whose traffic justifies this investment a formal, minuted voice in project decisions — including construction outage scheduling, which matters enormously to elevators at harvest.

**Procurement and financial management.** Construction will be competitively procured under the District's procurement policy, updated in 2023 to align with federal standards. Rail, ties, and other track material will be procured by sealed bid; the independent inspection firm and grant-administration counsel by qualifications-based selection. Redbud force-account work will be performed only at audited cost under a written force-account agreement with not-to-exceed limits. The District maintains separate funds by grant, and its finance officer will administer Trust funds in a dedicated account with monthly reconciliation reported to the board.

**Partner capacity.** The county commission maintains 640 miles of county road and routinely self-performs the type of crossing approach work committed here. The regional economic development district administers state and federal grants exceeding the District's budget several times over and will provide surge grant-administration support under a memorandum of agreement. Redbud Terminal Railway operates four short lines in the region and has performed relay work of this scale on two of them.

## Sustainability

The Trust's application guidance asks a blunt question, and Board Chair Hightower, Operations Manager Kittredge, and shipper representative Arreola have insisted this section answer it just as bluntly: who maintains this track after the grant, with what money, under what enforceable obligation? The District's answer has four parts — the operator arrangement, a dedicated maintenance reserve, the engineering economics of the rebuilt asset, and the shippers themselves.

**The operator arrangement.** Redbud Terminal Railway's lease expires this year, and Redbud entered renewal negotiations seeking a 22 percent rate increase, driven — by Redbud's own account and Kittredge's independent analysis — substantially by the operating costs the deteriorated track imposes: slow-order transit times that double crew hours, broken-rail delays, and light-loading inefficiency. Rather than treat the lease expiration as a threat, the District has treated it as the opportunity to bind the operator to the rehabilitated railroad. The District and Redbud have negotiated, and their respective boards have approved in principle, a new ten-year lease with a ten-year renewal option, conditioned on award of this grant, with the following terms (term sheet at Attachment J): Redbud contributes $400,000 in cash match to this project; the rate increase is reduced from the requested 22 percent to 8 percent, phased over two years, reflecting the operating-cost savings the rehabilitation delivers to Redbud — a bargain both parties could strike only because the project makes the railroad cheaper to run; Redbud assumes contractual responsibility for routine maintenance of way to FRA Class 2 standard, including surfacing, joint maintenance, vegetation, and crossing surfaces, with an annual minimum maintenance expenditure of $210,000, escalated, verified by annual joint inspection; Redbud pays the District a per-carload lease fee that funds the District's capital reserve, described next; and the lease carries performance standards — service days per week, car-supply coordination, transit-time commitments — with cure provisions and termination rights, so that the shippers whose volumes justify this grant are protected by contract, not goodwill. Should Redbud ever default or depart, the District owns the asset, and a rehabilitated, slow-order-free, 286,000-pound line is one that other regional operators will compete to run — a fact the District confirmed by soliciting, and receiving, two written expressions of interest from alternative operators during lease negotiations. The District negotiated with a stronger hand because it had alternatives, and it will always keep them.

**The maintenance reserve.** By board resolution adopted contingent on award (Attachment K), the District will establish a dedicated, restricted Maintenance-of-Way Capital Reserve funded from three streams: a $45-per-carload assessment built into the Redbud lease fee, generating approximately $135,000 annually at the Year 3 volume of 3,000 carloads; net transload facility revenue, projected at $40,000 to $70,000 annually by Year 3; and an annual District budget transfer of $60,000. Together these fund the reserve at approximately $235,000 to $265,000 per year — deliberately sized against Chief Engineer Sarkisian's twenty-year life-cycle maintenance model for the rebuilt line (Attachment L), which projects average annual capital-renewal needs of $190,000 for programmed tie replacement, surfacing cycles, and turnout components. The reserve may be spent only on track and bridge preservation, only on the Chief Engineer's certification, and its balance and expenditures will be reported in the District's audited financials and in the annual post-grant reports to the Trust. The structure is intentionally self-reinforcing: maintenance funding scales with carloads, and carloads depend on maintained track.

**The engineering economics.** The project replaces the most maintenance-hungry assets on the railroad with the least. Seventy-eight-year-old 85-pound jointed rail generating broken-rail incidents becomes 115-pound rail with decades of remaining life; shored timber bridges requiring annual intervention become steel-and-concrete or culvert structures requiring almost none. Sarkisian's life-cycle model projects that post-project routine maintenance costs per mile fall by more than half, which is precisely what makes the Redbud maintenance covenant and the reserve sufficient. The District is not promising to out-spend deterioration; it is buying a railroad that deteriorates slowly.

**The shippers.** Manuela Arreola's advisory committee will conduct an annual shipper review of track condition and service, reported directly to the board. The people with the largest stake in the railroad's condition will inspect it, formally, every year, and Chair Hightower has committed the board to publishing that review. A public asset stays maintained when its beneficiaries are watching; the District has built the watching into its governance.

**Program income and continuity.** Transload revenues are program income and will be applied first to facility operations and then to the maintenance reserve. The District's four-employee structure is stable and fully funded from operating revenues independent of this grant; no grant-funded position is created that would require continuation funding.

## Budget Narrative

**Total project cost: $6,500,000. Grant request: $4,550,000 (70 percent). Non-federal match: $1,950,000 (30 percent).** All figures derive from Chief Engineer Sarkisian's engineer's estimate (Attachment M), which is based on 30-percent design for the bridges, supplier quotes for rail and other track material, and unit costs from two comparable regional relay projects completed within the last three years.

**Line 1 — Track rehabilitation: $3,960,000 (grant $2,772,000; match $1,188,000).** Relay of 22 miles at an average of $180,000 per mile, comprising: 115-pound certified relay rail, joint bars, and fastenings, $88,000 per mile per current supplier quote; new crossties at 700 per mile installed, $52,000 per mile; ballast, surfacing, and lining to Class 2 with Class 3 geometry, $28,000 per mile; and welding, destressing, and miscellaneous track material, $12,000 per mile. The per-mile figure is within four percent of the two comparable projects cited in Attachment M. Rail and tie prices are the principal cost risk; the rail quote is firm through the anticipated award date, and the contingency in Line 5 is sized against a documented sensitivity analysis.

**Line 2 — Bridge reconstruction: $1,380,000 (grant $966,000; match $414,000).** Six structures at an average of $230,000: four ballast-deck rebuilds on driven steel piles at $265,000 to $295,000 each depending on span and height, and two culvert/box replacements at $95,000 and $110,000, all designed to Cooper E-60 for 286,000-pound service. Estimates reflect 30-percent design quantities. The two shored structures are scheduled first; their replacement with culverts is the least-cost permanent solution and eliminates the project's most acute risk early.

**Line 3 — Transload facility: $840,000 (grant $588,000; match $252,000).** On District-owned land requiring no acquisition: 2,400 feet of siding track including one turnout, $390,000; earthwork, drainage, and 14-acre compacted aggregate pad, $260,000; permanent loading ramp, $75,000; truck scale, $70,000; lighting, fencing, and gate, $45,000. Facility revenues flow to sustainability as described above.

**Line 4 — Engineering, inspection, and grant administration: $260,000 (grant $182,000; match $78,000).** Final design and construction-phase engineering, $145,000; independent construction inspection and post-construction geometry verification, $75,000; grant-administration counsel and audit costs, $40,000. The independent inspection line reflects the District's post-2020 policy that no contractor certifies its own work on a District grant.

**Line 5 — Contingency: $60,000 (grant $42,000; match $18,000).** A deliberately modest contingency, because the largest cost items are supported by firm quotes and recent comparables; any contingency draw requires board approval and prior notice to the Trust, and unspent contingency will be deobligated or, with Trust approval, applied to additional tie renewal.

**The match: $1,950,000, all committed, all documented.** The District does not ask the Trust to fund against speculative match. Sources, with commitment documents at Attachment N: **District capital reserve, $650,000 cash** — accumulated reserves confirmed in the audited financials, appropriated by board resolution contingent on award; this is one-third of the match in the District's own money, because the District believes an applicant's seriousness is measured by its own skin in the game. **County commission, $310,000 in-kind** — reconstruction of eleven crossing approaches with county forces, equipment, and materials, valued at documented county force-account rates under the executed draft interlocal agreement; in-kind valuation worksheets are included. **Regional economic development district, $300,000 cash**, committed by its board and payable in three annual installments matching the construction schedule. **Redbud Terminal Railway, $400,000 cash**, committed under the new lease term sheet and payable at lease execution, which is conditioned on grant award. **Shipper contributions, $290,000** — cash and documented in-kind commitments from five of the nine shippers, including prepaid transload capacity purchases by the wind-tower fabricator and siding-connection cost-shares by the two elevators and the gypsum plant, coordinated by Manuela Arreola and evidenced by signed agreements. No federal funds are included in the match, and no match source is contingent on any pending application elsewhere.

**Cash flow and cost allocation.** Match is scheduled proportionally with grant draws in each project year, so the 30 percent ratio is maintained continuously, not merely at closeout. The District will request reimbursement-basis draws quarterly, supported by invoices and the independent inspector's progress certifications. Costs are allocated to the grant only for the elements described in this narrative; District operating costs, lease revenues, and non-project maintenance are excluded and separately accounted.

**Cost-effectiveness.** At $6.5 million total cost against 60,000 carloads over the improvements' conservative twenty-year design life at the Year 3 volume, the program cost is $108 per carload — $76 per carload in grant funds — while each of those carloads removes 3.5 truckloads from US-270. The District submits that few investments available to the Trust buy as much steel, as much safety, and as much retained rural economy per dollar, and it commits, in the terms set out in its evaluation plan and enforced by its own board, to prove that arithmetic with actuals rather than assert it with projections.
