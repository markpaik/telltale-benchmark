## Abstract

Between 2021 and 2025, districts across Iowa, Nebraska, and South Dakota built tutoring programs at a speed and scale without precedent in the region, financed almost entirely by federal pandemic relief. The Prairie States Education Research Collaborative holds session-level attendance and outcome records for 61,000 students across 118 member districts over that period. This paper reports what those records show and translates the findings into an implementable model.

Three findings drive everything else. First, dosage, not model, is the primary determinant of effect: median attendance in scheduled tutoring was 63 percent, and students below roughly 27 attended sessions showed effects statistically indistinguishable from zero, regardless of who tutored them. Second, tutor consistency — the same adult with the same students across the year — predicted outcomes nearly as strongly as total dosage, which makes the region's 41 percent average annual tutor turnover a first-order instructional problem rather than a human-resources nuisance. Third, cost per student ranged from $1,180 to $4,600, but cost per unit of measured gain converged far more tightly than that range suggests; the least expensive model was not the most cost-effective, and the most expensive was not the least.

When federal relief expired, 74 of 118 districts cut or ended tutoring for 2025-26. A new state grant program of $18 million attracted $71 million in requests. The scarcity is real and will persist. This paper therefore presents a specific implementation and funding model for a 3,400-student district serving approximately 336 tutored students at roughly $2,268 per student, addressing master schedule construction, tutor recruitment and retention, student selection, and fidelity monitoring. It closes by answering the four objections raised most often in the field: instructional time surrendered, equity in selection, union questions about paraprofessional duties, and the reporting burden placed on principals.

Our central recommendation is unwelcome but supported by the data: districts that cannot protect dosage, tutor continuity, and small group size should serve fewer students well rather than more students thinly. Under-dosed tutoring is not a partial benefit. In our records, it is an expenditure with no measurable return.

## 1. The Problem Districts Now Face

### 1.1 A program built on a nonrecurring revenue base

Tutoring arrived in the region's districts as an emergency response and was funded accordingly. Of the 118 districts in our session-level dataset, 111 reported that federal relief — chiefly ESSER II and ARP ESSER — covered 80 percent or more of tutoring costs in at least one year between 2021-22 and 2024-25. Ninety-four reported it covered 100 percent. The programs were built inside a three-year funding window with a hard stop, using staffing structures that assumed continued external revenue.

The stop arrived. For the 2025-26 school year, 74 of the 118 districts either eliminated tutoring or reduced it by more than a third in students served. Twenty-two maintained programs at approximately prior scale, and 22 expanded, in nearly all cases by absorbing costs into general fund or state at-risk allocations. The pattern of survival was not random: districts that had already reduced per-student cost below roughly $2,000 by 2024-25, and districts whose boards had approved multi-year phase-in language before the cliff, were substantially more likely to continue.

### 1.2 Demand exceeds supply by a factor of four

The state grant program that opened in early 2025 with $18 million in available funds received applications totaling $71 million — a 3.9-to-1 oversubscription. Fifty-two of our member districts applied; 19 received awards, at a median 62 percent of requested amount. This tells superintendents and legislators the same thing from opposite directions. Districts read it as confirmation that need is unmet. Legislators read it as evidence that the field cannot yet distinguish between requests likely to produce results and requests unlikely to produce anything. Both readings are defensible, and the second is the more actionable.

### 1.3 The gap between the research literature and field practice

The tutoring research base is unusually strong for an education intervention. Randomized and quasi-experimental evidence has converged on effects in the range of 0.20 to 0.35 standard deviations for high-dosage tutoring delivered three or more times weekly in groups of four or fewer with trained tutors and curriculum-aligned content (Nickow, Oreopoulos, and Quan 2020; Robinson, Kraft, Loeb, and Schueler 2021). That literature describes a treatment that was, in the studies, actually delivered.

Our data describe what happened when 118 districts attempted to deliver it at scale, quickly, with whoever they could hire. The distance between those two things is the subject of this paper. It is not primarily a distance of program design. It is a distance of attendance, staffing stability, group size drift, and scheduling compromise — the operational layer that research protocols hold constant and district practice does not.

## 2. Data and Method

### 2.1 What the collaborative holds

Member districts transmit session-level tutoring records through a common extract specification adopted in 2021 and revised in 2022. A record is one scheduled session for one student and carries: date, subject, scheduled and actual duration, group size as staffed and as attended, tutor identifier and credential class, delivery mode, attendance disposition, and content code linking the session to the district's core curriculum scope and sequence. Records are joined to student demographic, program-participation, and assessment files.

### 2.2 Outcome measures and analytic approach

Primary outcomes are fall-to-spring gains on district interim assessments (NWEA MAP Growth in 84 districts, Renaissance Star in 26, FastBridge in 8), standardized within grade, subject, and assessment vendor to a common metric expressed in student-level standard deviations. Secondary outcomes are state summative proficiency status and, for grades 3–8, year-over-year state scale score change.

Estimates come from student fixed-effects models where two or more years of data permit, and otherwise from propensity-weighted comparisons against untutored students in the same district, grade, and school year, matched on prior-year achievement, attendance, English learner status, IEP status, free/reduced-price lunch eligibility, and mobility. Model-to-model comparisons additionally include district fixed effects, so that the contrast between, for example, paraprofessional-led and vendor virtual tutoring is drawn wherever possible from districts that operated both.

### 2.3 Limits readers should hold in mind

This is not an experiment. Students were selected into tutoring, and districts selected into models, on characteristics that our covariates capture imperfectly. Three specific cautions apply.

Districts choosing teacher-led models had, on average, more general fund capacity and lower staff turnover before tutoring began; some part of that model's advantage in our estimates is plausibly the advantage of being that kind of district. Interim assessments used to select students and to measure outcomes create regression-to-the-mean pressure that we address through prior-two-year achievement controls but cannot eliminate. And attendance is endogenous — students who attend more may differ in ways that also drive achievement — so the dosage-response relationship in Section 3 should be read as an upper bound on the causal effect of attendance.

We report effects to two decimal places because that is the convention. Readers should treat differences smaller than about 0.04 standard deviations as within the noise of this design.

## 3. What Four Years of Session-Level Records Show

### 3.1 Attendance was the binding constraint

The single most consequential number in our dataset is the median: students attended 63 percent of the sessions scheduled for them. That figure was remarkably stable across the four years (61, 64, 63, and 63 percent) and varied more across districts than across models.

The practical implication for scheduling is direct. A program scheduling three 30-minute sessions per week across 28 instructional weeks offers 84 sessions. At the median 63 percent attendance, the typical student attends 53 — comfortably inside the effective band. A program scheduling two sessions per week across 20 weeks offers 40; at 63 percent attendance the typical student attends 25, at the bottom edge of the band, and the lower half of the attendance distribution receives nothing of measurable value. Twenty-nine of our districts operated in that second configuration. Their aggregate estimated effect was +0.05 SD in math.

### 3.3 Tutor consistency rivals dosage

We defined tutor consistency as the share of a student's attended sessions delivered by the modal tutor for that student. The median was 0.74; the interquartile range ran from 0.52 to 0.93.

**Table 3. Independent predictors of tutoring effect, mathematics, grades 2–8**

| Predictor | Specification | Estimated association with gain (SD) |
|---|---|---|
| Attended sessions | Per 10 additional sessions, 15–50 range | +0.042 |
| Tutor consistency | Consistency ≥0.80 vs. <0.60 | +0.081 |
| Group size as attended | Groups of ≤3 vs. 5–8 | +0.063 |
| Curriculum alignment | ≥70% of sessions content-coded to core scope and sequence vs. <40% | +0.057 |
| Session length | 40 min vs. 25 min | +0.019 |
| Time of day | Within school day vs. before/after school | +0.048 |
| Tutor credential class | Certified vs. non-certified, holding above constant | +0.021 |

*Caption: Coefficients from a single model including all listed predictors plus student covariates and district fixed effects. Note that the tutor credential coefficient is small once dosage, consistency, group size, alignment, and scheduling are controlled — a central finding of this analysis.*

Table 3 is the analytic core of this paper. Once we hold constant how often a student came, whether the same adult served them, how many peers were in the room, and whether the content connected to core instruction, the credential of the tutor explains very little. This does not mean credentials are irrelevant; it means credentials operate largely *through* those mechanisms. Certified teachers in our data produced better outcomes chiefly because they were scheduled inside the school day, stayed in the role, and aligned content — not because of some irreducible instructional advantage in a 30-minute small-group setting.

That reframes the staffing question. The task is not to buy the most credentialed tutor affordable. It is to buy the most stable, best-scheduled, best-supported tutor affordable, and then to invest the remaining dollars in alignment and group size.

### 3.4 Turnover

Tutor turnover averaged 41 percent annually across the region, with wide variation: 18 percent among certified staff in dedicated interventionist roles, 44 percent among paraprofessionals, and 63 percent among community college tutor corps members. Turnover has a direct instructional cost through the consistency channel in Table 3, and a direct financial cost. Districts reporting recruitment and onboarding expenditures separately averaged $1,340 per tutor hired, meaning a program with 20 tutors at 41 percent turnover spends roughly $11,000 annually replacing people — approximately five additional students' worth of service.

Exit data from 31 districts (n = 612 departing tutors) identify the leading reasons: insufficient hours or income (34 percent), schedule instability including cancelled sessions (19 percent), lack of belonging or unclear supervision (17 percent), left for a full-time position elsewhere in education (14 percent), coursework or transfer conflict (9 percent, concentrated in the corps model), and other or unknown (7 percent). Half of the top four reasons are within district control at modest cost.

Two features of Table 4 deserve emphasis. First, program management is a nearly fixed cost per student that does not scale down with tutor wages; in the corps model it constitutes 28 percent of total cost. Second, the vendor virtual model's cost is dominated by a per-seat fee that districts paid whether or not the seat was used. In districts where vendor attendance ran below 50 percent, effective cost per *attended hour* exceeded that of teacher-led tutoring.

Three conclusions follow.

The three top-ranked models are not meaningfully distinguishable on cost-effectiveness. A district with a strong paraprofessional labor market should choose the para-led model; a district adjacent to a community college with an education pathway should build a corps; most districts of moderate size should hybridize. The choice is a labor-market question.

Teacher-led tutoring produces the largest effects and should be reserved for the students who need the largest effects. It is the correct model for the top decile of need, not for the top third.

Vendor virtual tutoring, as implemented in our region, failed. This finding requires qualification, because it was implementation and not technology that failed. Within the vendor sample, 1,890 student-years had both a consistently assigned tutor (consistency ≥0.80) and a dedicated in-room adult monitor during sessions. Those students showed a math effect of +0.14 SD and attendance of 68 percent — comparable to para-led tutoring. The remaining vendor student-years, characterized by rotating tutors and unsupervised sessions in library corners and hallways, showed +0.01 SD. Districts purchasing virtual tutoring should treat the in-room monitor and a contractual tutor-consistency guarantee as non-negotiable line items, and should expect the resulting total cost to approach $3,100 per student, at which point the model's cost advantage over para-led tutoring disappears entirely.

## 5. An Implementation Model for a 3,400-Student District

The following model was developed in response to requests from member districts and refined against the operating experience of Cedar Bluffs (2,800 students, four years of tutoring) and six comparably sized member districts. Assume a K–12 district of 3,400 students, roughly 1,880 in grades 2–8, a general fund of approximately $34 million, and no dedicated tutoring revenue beyond state at-risk allocations and Title I-A.

### 5.1 Scope and staffing

**Table 7. Recommended staffing and budget, 3,400-student district, steady state (Year 3+)**

| Element | Configuration | Annual cost |
|---|---|---|

| Paraprofessional tutors | 5 positions at 0.8 FTE, caseload 30 each = 150 students | $188,750 |

| **Total** | **336 students served (17.9% of grades 2–8)** | **$761,750** |

*Caption: Costs in 2026 dollars. Paraprofessional cost assumes $22/hr plus 30 percent benefits at 1,320 hours. Certified cost assumes $63,000 salary plus 33 percent benefits. Total represents 2.24 percent of a $34 million general fund.*

Tiering matters more than totals. The 96 students assigned to certified interventionists are those two or more years below grade level with an additional risk indicator; they receive four 40-minute sessions weekly in groups of three. The 150 paraprofessional-served students are one to two years below level and receive four 30-minute sessions weekly in groups of four using scripted, curriculum-aligned materials. The 90 corps-served students are approaching benchmark and receive three 30-minute sessions weekly in groups of four, functioning as a step-down tier that frees intensive capacity mid-year.

### 5.2 Master schedule construction

Scheduling is where most programs in our dataset failed, and it is the section superintendents should read twice. Table 3 established that in-day tutoring outperformed before- and after-school tutoring by 0.048 SD, chiefly through attendance. The scheduling problem is therefore not whether to schedule inside the day but what the tutoring block displaces.

**Figure 3. Grade-band staggered intervention block, elementary and middle schedules**

```
             8:00   8:30   9:15  10:00  10:45  11:30  12:15   1:00   1:45   2:30
Grades 2–3  [ELA core.............][WIN-A][Math core.....][Lunch][Sci/SS][Spec]
Grades 4–5  [Math core....][WIN-B][ELA core.............][Lunch][Spec][Sci/SS]
Grades 6–8  [Blk 1][Blk 2][WIN-C][Blk 3][Lunch][Blk 4][Blk 5][Blk 6]

WIN-A  9:15–9:55   Grades 2–3   Interventionists ×2, paras ×2, corps ×4

WIN-C 10:05–10:45  Grades 6–8   Interventionists ×1, paras ×1, corps ×4
```
*Caption: Staggering the WIN (What I Need) block across three grade bands allows a single tutor pool of 21 adults to serve 336 students without duplicating staff. Non-overlapping bands also permit certified interventionists to serve two bands where caseload requires.*

Six scheduling rules carry the model.

**No new core instruction occurs during the WIN block.** This is the load-bearing rule. Districts in our dataset that pulled students from core reading or mathematics instruction produced net effects near zero — the tutoring gain was offset by core instruction lost. The WIN block must be a period in which the whole grade band is engaged in differentiated practice, enrichment, or extension, so that tutored students miss practice rather than first instruction.

**The block is 40 minutes, not 30, in the master schedule.** Transitions consume six to nine minutes in our observation data. A 30-minute scheduled block yields 22 minutes of instruction.

**Four days of tutoring, fifth day for makeups and assessment.** Districts operating five-day schedules had no mechanism to recover lost sessions and lost, on average, 11 sessions per student annually to assemblies, field trips, and testing. A four-day schedule with a Friday recovery period produced *higher* total attended sessions than a five-day schedule in the same districts.

**Specials and related-arts rotations are aligned to the WIN block, not against it.** Tutored students disproportionately have IEP or EL service minutes, and those minutes are typically scheduled first. If WIN and specials rotate against each other, tutored students lose specials — which produces both the equity problem and the family-relations problem discussed in Section 7.

**Tutor schedules are fixed for the year and published in August.** Schedule instability was the second-ranked reason tutors quit. Fixed assignments also mechanically raise tutor consistency, the second-strongest predictor in Table 3.

**Middle school tutoring is scheduled as a course with a section number.** Informal middle school pull-out arrangements in our data had median attendance of 44 percent. Scheduled sections had 69 percent.

### 5.3 Tutor recruitment and retention

At 41 percent regional turnover and $1,340 per hire, retention is the highest-leverage available cost reduction. The districts in our dataset that held turnover below 25 percent shared five practices.

They **built positions, not shifts.** Paraprofessional tutoring combined with other duties into 0.8 FTE with benefits eligibility had 26 percent turnover; standalone 12-hour-per-week tutoring positions had 58 percent. Insufficient hours was the top exit reason; the remedy is a real job.

They **paid for training time.** Districts compensating the initial 24 hours of training retained 71 percent of tutors year over year; districts requiring unpaid training retained 49 percent.

They **assigned a named supervisor.** Tutors reporting to "the building" rather than to a specific instructional coach or interventionist left at nearly double the rate. This is a scheduling and organizational-chart matter, not a spending matter.

They **created a wage ladder.** Districts offering a $1.50–$2.50 hourly differential after 200 documented hours and a completed training sequence retained corps and paraprofessional tutors substantially better. Annual cost in the model district is roughly $9,400 — less than the cost of replacing seven tutors.

They **treated the corps as a pipeline.** Six member districts formalized agreements with community colleges under which tutoring hours count toward education program practicum requirements and the district commits to interview corps alumni for paraprofessional and, after licensure, teaching vacancies. Those districts filled 34 percent of their paraprofessional vacancies from corps alumni. The corps model's 63 percent turnover is partly structural — students graduate and transfer — and is tolerable if turnover flows into the district rather than away from it.

For the model district: recruit paraprofessional tutors in March for the following year, not August; recruit corps members through instructor referral rather than job postings, which more than doubled yield in member district experience; and budget $9,400 for the wage ladder and $6,800 for paid training within the $28,000 training line.

### 5.4 Student selection

Selection must be rule-based, documented, and reviewable. The model district uses a three-gate process.

**Gate 1 — Universal screen.** All grade 2–8 students are screened three times annually on the district interim assessment. Students below the 30th percentile in mathematics or reading enter the eligible pool. This is a bright line with no professional judgment.

**Gate 2 — Risk weighting for tier assignment.** Eligible students are ranked by a transparent composite: interim percentile (weight 0.5), prior-year state assessment (0.2), attendance below 90 percent (0.1), two or more school changes in three years (0.1), and multilingual learner status at proficiency level 1–3 (0.1). Tier 1 intensive (certified) goes to the top 96; Tier 2 (paraprofessional) to the next 150; Tier 3 (corps) to the next 90.

**Gate 3 — Structured override.** Teachers may nominate a student outside the composite ranking or request removal, using a one-page form with a required rationale. Overrides are capped at 10 percent of seats and reviewed monthly by the coordinator and a principal. The cap exists because in districts we studied, unlimited teacher referral produced measurable disparities — under-referral of multilingual learners relative to their screen scores in 14 of 19 districts examined, and over-referral of students with behavior incidents relative to their academic need in 11 of 19.

**Exit and entry are continuous.** Students meeting benchmark on two consecutive interim windows step down a tier; the vacated seat goes to the highest-ranked waitlisted student. Districts operating fixed rosters served 8 percent fewer students annually for the same expenditure.

Two design choices matter for equity and are discussed further in Section 7.2. Tutoring is opt-out rather than opt-in — families receive notification with a clear declination mechanism rather than an invitation requiring affirmative response. Opt-in districts in our data enrolled students of college-educated parents at 1.7 times the rate predicted by need. And the eligible-but-unserved list is maintained, reported to the board twice annually with demographic breakdowns, and used as the basis for expansion requests.

### 5.5 Fidelity monitoring

Section 3 identified four fidelity dimensions that predict outcomes. Monitor those four and nothing else.

**Table 8. Fidelity monitoring specification**

| Indicator | Target | Data source | Frequency | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Attended sessions per student | ≥27 by March 1; ≥40 by year end | SIS attendance code, automated | Weekly, automated | Coordinator |
| Tutor consistency | ≥0.80 of sessions with modal tutor | Scheduling module, automated | Monthly, automated | Coordinator |
| Group size as attended | ≤4 in 90% of sessions | Session record, automated | Monthly, automated | Coordinator |
| Curriculum alignment | ≥70% of sessions content-coded to core scope and sequence | Tutor session log, single dropdown | Monthly | Interventionist supervisors |
| Instructional quality | 3 of 4 observed elements present | 12-minute walkthrough protocol | Twice per tutor per year | Principal |

*Caption: Four of five indicators derive from data the district already collects for other purposes and require no additional principal entry. The principal's total obligation is two 12-minute walkthroughs per tutor per year — for a building with seven tutors, approximately 168 minutes annually.*

The escalation logic is as important as the indicators. When a student falls below the mid-year dosage threshold, the coordinator — not the principal — investigates and resolves, most often through a schedule change or a family contact. When a tutor falls below consistency or group-size targets in two consecutive months, the coordinator reports to the principal with a recommended action. Principals receive one dashboard, weekly, and act on exceptions only.

That last comparison is the practical lesson of the funding cliff. Programs entirely financed by external revenue were externally terminable. A general fund commitment of roughly 0.45 percent of the operating budget — $152,000 on $34 million — converted tutoring from a grant activity into a district function. Boards should be asked to make that commitment explicitly, in a multi-year resolution, before the first tutor is hired.

### 6.3 Implications for state policy

The $18 million program that drew $71 million in requests can be made substantially more productive without additional appropriation.

Award multi-year rather than annual grants. Annual awards guarantee Year 1 cost structures, which Table 9 shows are 28 percent less efficient than Year 3 structures, and guarantee high turnover, which the region cannot afford. Three-year awards at declining match would purchase more measured learning per dollar than the current structure.

Permit and require adequate program management. Applications capping indirect and coordination costs at token levels select for the failure mode this dataset documents most clearly: low-wage tutors without supervision. A floor of 12 percent for coordination would improve results.

Fund a shared corps infrastructure with the community college system rather than 52 separate district recruitment operations. Recruitment, screening, and background checks are the corps model's largest non-labor cost and are almost purely duplicative across districts.

Adopt a common session-level reporting standard. The collaborative's extract specification is available without charge and would give the legislature, for the first time, the ability to distinguish funded programs that deliver dosage from those that do not.

## 7. Objections

### 7.1 "Tutoring takes instructional time we cannot spare."

This objection is correct as stated about many programs in our dataset and is answered by scheduling design rather than by rebuttal.

We can quantify the concern. Among districts that scheduled tutoring during core reading or mathematics instruction, the net effect on the tutored subject was +0.21 SD, but the effect on the *displaced* subject was −0.14 SD, for a net across-subject gain of approximately +0.04 SD — a poor return on $2,000 or more per student. Among districts that scheduled tutoring during a differentiated practice or enrichment block, the tutored-subject effect was +0.19 SD with no detectable loss elsewhere.

The instructional-time objection is therefore not an argument against tutoring. It is an argument for the WIN-block architecture in Section 5.2 and against the expedient of pulling students from whatever period is convenient. Districts without a differentiated practice block in the master schedule should build one before they hire tutors; that sequencing is not optional in light of these estimates.

A secondary form of the objection concerns specials, electives, and recess. We take it seriously. Tutoring scheduled against band, art, or physical education produced attendance 14 points lower than tutoring scheduled against academic practice, and generated the large majority of family complaints documented in member district records. The remedy is in the master schedule rules, not in persuasion.

### 7.2 "Selection will not be equitable."

The objection has two distinct forms, and both are empirically supported in our data.

The first is that discretionary referral reproduces bias. It did. Where districts relied on teacher nomination without a screen-based floor, multilingual learners were under-enrolled relative to their screen scores in 14 of 19 districts examined, and students with prior behavior referrals were over-enrolled relative to academic need in 11 of 19. The three-gate process in Section 5.4 exists specifically to constrain this: a bright-line screen determines eligibility, a transparent composite determines tier, and discretion is capped at 10 percent of seats and documented.

The second form is that scarcity itself is inequitable — that serving 336 of 1,880 students means 1,544 students with unmet needs, some of them just below the cut. This is true and cannot be designed away. Three responses are available. Publish the rule, so that families and board members can see exactly why a student was or was not served; districts that published selection criteria received fewer complaints and more accurate expansion advocacy. Maintain and report the eligible-but-unserved list with demographic detail twice annually, which converts a hidden rationing decision into a visible budget question for the board. And use continuous exit and entry, which served 8 percent more students annually at identical cost in our data — the cheapest available equity gain in this entire model.

We also urge districts to resist the pressure that produced the region's most common failure. Facing the same objection, many districts responded by spreading resources across more students at lower dosage. Table 2 shows what that purchases: at fewer than 19 attended sessions, effects are indistinguishable from zero. Serving twice as many students at half the dosage is not a more equitable program. It is an equal distribution of nothing.

### 7.3 "Paraprofessional tutoring raises contract and scope-of-practice questions."

It does, and the questions have answers that have been negotiated successfully in member districts.

The substantive concern is that paraprofessionals will be assigned instructional responsibility properly belonging to licensed teachers. Federal and state frameworks are clear that paraprofessionals may provide instructional support under the direct supervision of a licensed teacher, with the teacher retaining responsibility for planning, assessment, and instructional decisions. The model in Section 5 is built to that standard: paraprofessional tutors deliver scripted, curriculum-aligned lessons that they do not author; a named certified interventionist or instructional coach supervises each paraprofessional tutor; assessment and tier assignment are made by certified staff; and paraprofessionals do not modify IEP services or determine instructional grouping.

The compensation concern is legitimate and should be conceded rather than argued. Paraprofessional tutoring is a distinct assignment requiring training. Member districts that resolved this cleanly did four things: they created a tutoring differential of $1.50 to $2.50 per hour rather than absorbing the duty into existing job descriptions; they paid for all required training hours; they wrote the assignment into a memorandum of understanding with duty descriptions, supervision lines, and a caseload cap; and they included association representatives on the design committee before the model was announced. Districts that announced first and negotiated second lost, on average, most of a school year.

The displacement concern — that paraprofessional tutoring substitutes for certified positions — is answered by non-supplanting language and by the tiering design itself, in which the highest-need students are served by certified interventionists. In practice, tutoring in our member districts created paraprofessional positions rather than eliminating teaching positions; it was one of the few sources of new benefit-eligible classified employment in the region during this period, and 34 percent of paraprofessional vacancies in corps-partnered districts were filled by corps alumni entering the education workforce.

### 7.4 "Principals cannot absorb another reporting requirement."

This objection is the best-founded of the four, and the collaborative's data support the principals rather than the program designers.

We inventoried reporting requirements in 41 member districts. The median tutoring program asked principals for 14 discrete data submissions per week: session attendance rosters, tutor timesheets, room assignments, weekly progress notes, and various grant compliance artifacts. We then tested whether reporting volume predicted fidelity. It did not. Above roughly four principal-facing reporting elements per month, additional requirements showed no association with attended sessions, tutor consistency, group size, or curriculum alignment. What did predicted fidelity was the presence of a coordinator with released time — the $48,000 line in Table 7.

The design conclusion is that fidelity monitoring should be automated and centralized, not distributed to principals. Four of the five indicators in Table 8 derive from data the district already collects: attendance comes from an SIS code applied by the tutor, consistency and group size from the scheduling module, alignment from a single dropdown on the tutor's session log. None requires principal action. The principal's remaining obligation is two 12-minute walkthroughs per tutor per year and weekly review of a one-page exception dashboard — for a seven-tutor building, under three hours annually.

Superintendents should audit this directly. If a proposed tutoring plan asks principals for more than four reporting elements per month, the plan is under-coordinated, and the correct response is to fund coordination rather than to distribute the deficit to buildings.

## 8. Conclusion

The 2021–2025 period gave the region's districts an unusual gift: enough money to attempt high-dosage tutoring at scale, and enough students to learn from. The learning is now more valuable than the money was, because the money is gone and the learning is not.

What 6.41 million session records establish is that tutoring's effects in practice are governed by operational variables that program descriptions rarely mention. Attendance of 63 percent means the median student receives two-thirds of the treatment purchased, and students below roughly 27 attended sessions receive nothing measurable. Tutor turnover of 41 percent means students cycle through adults, and tutor consistency turns out to predict outcomes nearly as strongly as dosage. Group sizes drift upward under scheduling pressure, and curriculum alignment erodes when tutors are unsupervised. Whether the tutor holds a teaching license matters considerably less, once those four variables are held constant, than the field has generally assumed.

This is encouraging news for districts facing the cliff, because it means the choice is not between expensive tutoring and no tutoring. Paraprofessional-led, corps-based, and hybrid models delivered results at cost-effectiveness ratios essentially equivalent to one another and superior to teacher-led tutoring, provided they were scheduled inside the school day against a differentiated practice block, staffed with real positions rather than fragmentary shifts, supervised by a funded coordinator, and monitored on four indicators rather than fourteen.

It is discouraging news for the prevailing response to scarcity. The instinct, when demand exceeds resources by four to one, is to spread thinner. Our data are unambiguous that this instinct destroys the intervention. A district that can fund 336 students at four sessions weekly should not serve 672 at two. The second program costs the same and, on these estimates, returns nothing.

For the 3,400-student district, the model is specific: 336 students, three tiers, $762,000 at steady state, $2,268 per student, roughly 2.2 percent of the operating budget, of which about a fifth must come from the general fund if the program is to survive the next external funding transition. For state legislators, the highest-return changes to the $18 million program require no additional appropriation: award multi-year, fund coordination, share corps infrastructure across districts, and require session-level reporting so that the next four years of investment can be evaluated on evidence rather than on application narratives.

The collaborative will continue to collect session-level records from member districts and will publish updated dosage-response and cost-effectiveness estimates annually. Districts implementing the model in Section 5 are invited to participate in a shared-measurement cohort beginning in the 2026-27 school year.
