# The Weight of Small Rooms

The kettle clicks off before it whistles, which is how you know she's been standing there, waiting, not doing anything else. Just watching the water almost boil.

She pours it anyway. Half a cup, because the mug is her grandmother's and the handle is loose, has been loose for eleven years, glued twice, and she doesn't trust it with more than that.

Outside, someone is teaching a child to ride a bike. You can hear it through the window she keeps cracked even in winter — the particular rhythm of a parent jogging alongside, letting go, grabbing on, letting go again. The kid isn't crying. That's the part that gets her. No crying, just that solemn concentration kids get when they're doing something hard and don't yet know to be afraid of falling.

She used to know how to do that — let go, grab on, let go again. Somewhere along the way she started just holding on, white-knuckled, calling it love or responsibility or whatever word made the grip feel less like fear.

The tea is too hot still. She drinks it anyway, burns her tongue a little, welcomes the small clean pain of it because it's simpler than the other kind.

Three dishes in the sink. A letter on the counter she hasn't opened, though she knows what it says — she wrote most of it herself, months ago, in a different draft, before she lost the nerve.

The bike lesson outside ends in a small triumphant shriek and the particular thud-scrape of a bicycle laid down in grass, not fallen so much as finished with. She smiles without deciding to.

Maybe this is what Tuesday afternoons are for — not resolution, not the letter finally opened, not the mug finally replaced. Just the noticing. The kettle that knew before she did. The tea gone lukewarm in her hand. The neighbor kid who fell, or didn't fall, or fell in the good way that isn't falling at all.

She sets the mug down, handle turned carefully away from the edge.

Tomorrow she'll open the letter. Or she won't. Either way, the water will boil again, and she'll be standing there, waiting, the way she always is, for something that has already, quietly, without her noticing, begun.
