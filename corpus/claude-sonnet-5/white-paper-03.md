# Full Lead Service Line Replacement Within the 2027–2037 Compliance Window: A Program and Financing Model for Mid-Sized Systems

**A White Paper of the Upper Midwest League of Municipal Utilities**

Ravinder Gill, P.E., Policy Director
Consuelo Barrera-Nunez, Senior Fellow
Dr. Halima Bektas, Environmental Economist

Upper Midwest League of Municipal Utilities, Lansing, Michigan

July 2026

---

## Abstract

Federal lead and copper rule revisions require the 412 publicly owned water systems represented by the Upper Midwest League of Municipal Utilities to replace every lead and galvanized service line in a fixed ten-year window running from 2027 through 2037. League members' own inventories put the scope of that mandate at 214,000 confirmed lead or galvanized lines and another 118,000 lines of unknown material embedded within a combined system of 1.06 million service connections across Michigan, Wisconsin, Indiana, and Ohio. At an average cost of $9,100 per line — and as high as $21,000 where street restoration or customer-side work is required — the aggregate exposure for the League's membership approaches $3 billion, against state revolving fund allocations that cover roughly a third of estimated need. This paper sets out the inventory and cost data behind that estimate, compares the five replacement strategies members have already deployed in the field, and builds a detailed implementation and financing model scaled to a representative 22,000-connection system. The model pairs a fixed annual bonding schedule of $2.1 million with regional shared procurement, records-based verification to retire unknown lines faster than physical excavation allows, and a tiered affordability program to blunt rate impact in low-income neighborhoods. The paper closes by addressing the three objections raised most consistently by member utilities — access to private property, a shortage of licensed plumbers to perform customer-side work, and the risk of rate shock — and recommends that members adopt right-of-entry ordinances and opt-out rather than opt-in consent structures before 2027 rather than after.

---

## 1. The Problem

The revised federal lead and copper rule sets a compliance clock that most member utilities have not previously faced: full replacement of every lead and galvanized-requiring-replacement service line, on both the utility and customer sides, within ten years, beginning in 2027 and ending in 2037. Unlike prior rules that allowed indefinite partial replacement or permitted systems to manage lead through corrosion control alone, this rule treats the presence of a lead or galvanized line — not an exceedance of the action level — as the trigger for mandatory replacement. It also closes the loophole that allowed utilities to replace only the publicly owned portion of a line and leave the customer-owned portion in place, a practice that at least one League member is now defending in a class action arising from partial replacements performed in 2018.

For a membership that serves 4.3 million people through 412 separate systems, most of them small to mid-sized municipal utilities without dedicated capital-projects staff, the rule converts a long-deferred infrastructure liability into a fixed, near-term capital obligation. The League's member survey, completed in the spring of 2026, found that most utilities have functioning inventories under the prior rule's mapping requirement but have not built the crew capacity, the financing structure, or the legal authority needed to execute replacement at the pace the rule now demands. Several members reported that their existing capital plans assumed replacement would proceed opportunistically over twenty to thirty years, tied to normal main and paving cycles. That pace is no longer compliant.

The problem this paper addresses is therefore not whether replacement will happen — the rule settles that — but how a mid-sized system replaces on the order of ten thousand or more service lines in a single decade without triggering the three failure modes members have already identified: crews and licensed plumbers insufficient to the physical scope of work, rate increases severe enough to generate political and legal backlash, and access refusal rates high enough to leave a residual population of lead lines in place at the end of the compliance window, in violation of the rule and in continued exposure to residents.

## 2. Inventory and Cost Data

### 2.1 Scope of the Inventory

League members collectively reported 1.06 million service line records under the current inventory requirement. Of these, 214,000 are confirmed lead or galvanized-requiring-replacement lines, and a further 118,000 remain of unknown material — typically because records predate the utility's current mapping system, because the line was installed by a private contractor without utility inspection, or because the connection sits on a parcel that has changed hands multiple times without a corresponding utility-side inspection. Table 1 summarizes the inventory by category.

**Table 1. Member Service Line Inventory by Category (n = 412 systems)**

| Category | Lines | Share of Total |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmed lead or galvanized (requiring replacement) | 214,000 | 20.2% |
| Unknown material | 118,000 | 11.1% |
| Confirmed non-lead (copper, plastic, or other compliant material) | 728,000 | 68.7% |
| **Total inventoried connections** | **1,060,000** | **100%** |

The unknown-material population is functionally as urgent as the confirmed population, because the rule requires systems to treat unknown lines as lead until proven otherwise for purposes of both replacement scheduling and public notification. A system that does not actively retire unknowns through records research or verification digs will, by default, be required to replace them physically within the same ten-year window — at full replacement cost rather than the substantially lower cost of a records-based determination.

### 2.2 Cost per Line

Replacement cost data submitted by members average $9,100 per line but range from $4,200 to $21,000. Table 2 disaggregates the range by the cost drivers members identified most consistently.

**Table 2. Per-Line Replacement Cost Range by Driver**

| Cost Driver | Low-End Scenario | High-End Scenario |
|---|---|---|
| Base excavation and pipe replacement | $4,200 | $6,800 |
| + Street restoration (asphalt, concrete, curb) | $5,800 | $11,500 |
| + Customer-side portion included in scope | $7,400 | $16,200 |
| + Difficult access (mature landscaping, easements, sidewalk/tree lawn conflicts) | $9,100 | $21,000 |

The single largest driver of cost variance is whether the customer-owned portion of the line is included in the utility's scope of work. Systems that replace only the utility-owned segment and leave the customer segment to the homeowner report substantially lower average costs, but also report the highest refusal and non-response rates, and — as the pending litigation illustrates — carry legal exposure if partial replacement is later found to have increased rather than reduced lead exposure through disturbance of the remaining lead segment.

### 2.3 Aggregate Financial Exposure

Applying the $9,100 average across the 214,000 confirmed lines yields a baseline replacement cost of approximately $1.95 billion for the confirmed population alone. If even half of the 118,000 unknown lines are ultimately confirmed as lead or galvanized through excavation rather than retired through records verification, an additional $537 million in replacement cost is added, bringing aggregate member exposure to roughly $2.5 billion under conservative assumptions, and closer to $3 billion if unknown-line confirmation rates run higher or if a larger share of work falls at the high end of the cost range due to street restoration and customer-side inclusion.

### 2.4 Financing Gap

State revolving fund allocations to the four-state region are estimated to cover approximately one-third of aggregate member need under current appropriation levels. The remaining two-thirds must be financed through a combination of local bonding, rate revenue, and — where available — federal Bipartisan Infrastructure Law lead service line replacement grants, which are themselves finite and subject to competitive allocation rather than guaranteed per-system funding. Every member system will need a locally financed capital plan that does not assume state or federal funds will close the gap.

### 2.5 Rate Impact

Modeled against a median residential bill of $52 a month, the rate impact required to finance replacement without full state or federal cost-sharing runs from $6.40 to $18.90 a month, depending on system size, existing debt capacity, and the pace of replacement chosen. That range represents an increase of 12 percent to 36 percent over current bills, concentrated in a population that, in many member service areas, already carries an above-median cost burden for water and sewer service relative to household income.

### 2.6 Field Experience with Access

Members already operating active replacement programs — chiefly through opportunistic or neighborhood-based approaches — report homeowner refusal or non-response rates of 22 to 31 percent. This is the single most consistent operational finding across the member survey and is discussed at length in Sections 3 and 5, because it determines whether a ten-year compliance window is achievable under existing consent frameworks.

## 3. Comparison of Approaches

League members have deployed five distinct approaches to service line replacement, often in combination. None is sufficient alone to meet the ten-year mandate at the scale the inventory data describe; the implementation model in Section 4 combines elements of all five. This section compares them on cost, pace, access performance, and administrative burden.

### 3.1 Neighborhood-by-Neighborhood Full Replacement

Under this approach, the utility selects a contiguous area — typically defined by main age, lead prevalence, or environmental justice criteria — and replaces every service line in the area regardless of individual homeowner request, financed through a bond issue or rate-funded capital program dedicated to that area.

*Advantages:* Predictable unit costs through economies of scale in a concentrated work zone; simplified crew mobilization and traffic control; strong public communication ("your street is scheduled for [month]") that reduces the ambiguity driving refusal; auditable progress against the compliance schedule.

*Disadvantages:* Requires either blanket right-of-entry authority or a high per-neighborhood consent rate; front-loads capital need in early program years; can appear inequitable if neighborhood selection criteria are contested.

*Member experience:* Systems using this approach report the highest per-line completion rate once a neighborhood is initiated, but the slowest initial ramp-up due to the ordinance and financing work required before the first neighborhood begins.

### 3.2 Opportunistic Replacement During Main and Paving Work

Service lines are replaced only when the adjacent water main or street is already scheduled for reconstruction, on the logic that trench costs are shared across projects.

*Advantages:* Lowest marginal cost per line, since excavation and restoration costs are already budgeted to the main or paving project; minimal additional public disruption.

*Disadvantages:* Pace is set by unrelated capital cycles rather than by lead exposure risk or compliance deadlines; systems relying solely on this approach cannot demonstrate a credible path to completing 100 percent of confirmed lines by 2037, because main replacement cycles typically run 40 to 75 years — far longer than the compliance window covers even a single pass through the system.

*Member experience:* Best used as a supplemental strategy layered onto a primary approach, not as a standalone compliance strategy. Every member using this as its sole method reported inventory completion projections extending past 2040.

### 3.3 Homeowner Reimbursement Programs

The utility replaces the public-side line and offers a grant or low-interest loan to homeowners to replace the private-side portion, rather than performing the customer-side work directly.

*Advantages:* Avoids the utility taking on contractual and liability responsibility for work on private property; can be politically popular as a homeowner-empowerment measure; lowest direct utility cost per line.

*Disadvantages:* Depends on homeowner initiative and creditworthiness, producing the weakest completion rates of any approach; disproportionately fails in lower-income neighborhoods, where the population most exposed to lead risk is least able to front reimbursable costs even with a grant; the pending class action against a member city over 2018 partial replacements arose in a program of this type, where a portion of lines was left in place on the customer side for years after the utility-side work was completed, allegedly increasing rather than reducing exposure during that gap.

*Member experience:* No member using reimbursement as a primary strategy has achieved a completion rate above 60 percent of eligible lines, even in programs open for five or more years.

### 3.4 Regional Shared Procurement of Crews and Materials

Multiple neighboring utilities jointly procure replacement crews, pipe materials, and in some cases financing, either through the League or through ad hoc regional consortia, to achieve volume pricing and crew availability that no single mid-sized system could secure alone.

*Advantages:* Directly addresses the licensed-plumber and crew-capacity shortage identified in member objections (Section 5.2); reduces per-line material cost through bulk purchasing; spreads fixed administrative costs (program management, legal review of contracts, GIS system upgrades) across multiple systems.

*Disadvantages:* Requires interlocal agreements and coordinated scheduling across independent governing bodies, which adds administrative lead time; crew allocation across member systems must be negotiated, which can create friction if compliance deadlines are not uniformly urgent across participants.

*Member experience:* The three regional consortia already operating within League territory report per-line costs 8 to 14 percent below the membership average and report the shortest crew-mobilization lag of any approach, because a shared crew pool remains continuously employed across multiple systems' work rather than idled between individual utilities' capital cycles.

### 3.5 Records-Based Verification to Retire Unknowns

Rather than physically excavating every unknown-material line, the utility uses historical permit records, tap card archives, meter installation records, and in some cases predictive modeling to determine material without excavation, retiring lines from the "unknown" category to "confirmed non-lead" without a capital replacement cost.

*Advantages:* By far the lowest cost per line resolved — typically under $150 per record reviewed, compared with $9,100 for physical replacement; directly reduces the denominator of lines requiring replacement, which is the single most effective lever available for reducing total program cost.

*Disadvantages:* Cannot resolve every unknown; industry experience suggests 40 to 60 percent of unknowns can be retired through records work, with the remainder still requiring either excavation or a statistically valid predictive model accepted by the state primacy agency.

*Member experience:* Every member that has invested in a systematic records-verification effort before beginning physical replacement has reduced its physical-replacement workload by a material margin, several by more than 50,000 lines system-wide, at a fraction of excavation cost.

### 3.6 Comparative Summary

**Table 3. Comparison of the Five Approaches**

| Approach | Relative Cost/Line | Pace vs. 2037 Deadline | Access/Consent Performance | Primary Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Neighborhood-by-neighborhood | Moderate-High | Fast once initiated | Best with right-of-entry ordinance | Primary program backbone |
| Opportunistic (main/paving) | Lowest per-line | Too slow alone | Moderate | Supplemental, layered on primary |
| Homeowner reimbursement | Low utility cost, high total cost of delay | Slowest | Weakest (≤60% completion) | Legacy programs; not recommended as primary |
| Regional shared procurement | Reduced 8–14% | Fastest crew mobilization | N/A (procurement, not consent) | Crew and materials strategy |
| Records-based verification | Lowest overall | Immediate reduction in scope | N/A (no excavation) | First-year priority, all systems |

No single approach is sufficient. The implementation model in Section 4 treats neighborhood-by-neighborhood replacement as the programmatic backbone, records-based verification as a mandatory first-year activity to shrink the workload before physical work begins, regional shared procurement as the crew-capacity solution, and opportunistic replacement as a supplemental accelerant layered onto the primary schedule wherever main or paving work coincides with a scheduled neighborhood. Reimbursement is retained only as a narrow option for owners who affirmatively wish to accelerate their own timeline ahead of the scheduled neighborhood pass, not as a substitute for utility-managed replacement.

## 4. Implementation and Financing Model for a 22,000-Connection System

This section builds a program sized to a representative 22,000-connection member system, a size chosen because it approximates the median mid-sized utility in League membership and because it is large enough to require formal financing and crew planning but small enough to lack the in-house capital-projects staff of the League's largest members.

### 4.1 Sizing the Workload

Applying the membership-wide inventory ratios in Table 1 to a 22,000-connection system yields an estimated 4,450 confirmed lead or galvanized lines and 2,440 unknown-material lines, for a total gross workload of approximately 6,890 lines potentially subject to replacement absent records-based reduction.

**Table 4. Estimated Workload for a 22,000-Connection System**

| Category | Estimated Lines |
|---|---|
| Confirmed lead/galvanized | 4,450 |
| Unknown material (pre-verification) | 2,440 |
| Gross replacement workload (unverified) | 6,890 |
| Estimated unknowns retired via records verification (50% of unknown pool) | 1,220 |
| Net physical replacement workload | 5,670 |

At the membership average of $9,100 per line, the net physical replacement workload of approximately 5,670 lines represents a total program cost of roughly $51.6 million over the ten-year window, before financing costs. Records verification of the full unknown-material pool, at an estimated $150 per record, adds approximately $366,000 — a cost recovered many times over by the reduction in physical-replacement scope.

### 4.2 Annual Pace

To complete 5,670 lines over ten years, beginning with a ramp-up year for ordinance adoption, crew procurement, and records verification, the model assumes a graduated pace: a reduced first-year output while program infrastructure is established, a peak sustained output in years three through eight, and a controlled wind-down in the final two years to resolve holdouts and edge cases. Table 5 shows an illustrative annual schedule.

**Table 5. Illustrative Ten-Year Replacement Schedule, 22,000-Connection System**

| Program Year | Calendar Year | Lines Replaced | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2027 | 250 | 250 |
| 2 | 2028 | 500 | 750 |
| 3 | 2029 | 700 | 1,450 |
| 4 | 2030 | 750 | 2,200 |
| 5 | 2031 | 750 | 2,950 |
| 6 | 2032 | 750 | 3,700 |
| 7 | 2033 | 700 | 4,400 |
| 8 | 2034 | 650 | 5,050 |
| 9 | 2035 | 400 | 5,450 |
| 10 | 2036–2037 | 220 | 5,670 |

The compressed final period reflects the expected concentration of access-refusal holdouts and unresolved-access litigation into the closing years of the program, addressed further in Section 5.1.

### 4.3 Crew Capacity

At a sustained pace of roughly 750 lines a year, and using regional shared-procurement crew productivity benchmarks reported by League members (averaging 3 to 4 completed lines per crew per week across a construction season of approximately 34 weeks in the four-state region's climate), the program requires the sustained equivalent of 6 to 7 full-time crews at peak years, most efficiently secured through participation in a regional shared-procurement consortium rather than through a standalone contract, which at this system size would not achieve competitive per-line pricing.

### 4.4 Financing Structure: $2.1 Million Annual Bonding Schedule

The model finances the program through a fixed $2.1 million annual bonding schedule rather than a single lump-sum issuance, for three reasons: it matches debt issuance to the graduated pace of physical work, it allows the system to adjust the schedule in later years as records verification results and actual per-line costs become known, and it distributes rate impact evenly across the compliance window rather than concentrating it in the early years when program infrastructure, not physical replacement, is the dominant activity.

Over ten years, a $2.1 million annual bonding schedule generates $21 million in principal — sufficient, combined with the one-third share reasonably expected from state revolving fund allocation (approximately $17 million against the $51.6 million total program cost) and a modest allocation of competitive federal lead service line replacement grant funds, to close the financing gap without requiring rate increases at the high end of the modeled range.

**Table 6. Illustrative Financing Sources, 22,000-Connection System ($51.6M total program cost)**

| Source | Estimated Contribution | Share of Total |
|---|---|---|
| Local bonding ($2.1M/year × 10 years) | $21.0M | 40.7% |
| State revolving fund allocation | $17.0M | 33.0% |
| Federal competitive grant (assumed partial award) | $6.0M | 11.6% |
| Rate-funded pay-as-you-go / reserve draw | $7.6M | 14.7% |

### 4.5 Rate Impact Under the Model

Financed under this blended structure, the modeled rate impact for the 22,000-connection system falls in the lower half of the League-wide range identified in Section 2.5 — an estimated $7.20 to $11.50 a month above the $52 median bill, depending on the actual state revolving fund award and the pace at which records verification reduces the physical workload. Systems that fail to secure state revolving fund allocation at the assumed one-third share, or that fail to invest in records verification, should expect rate impact to move toward the upper end of the League-wide $6.40–$18.90 range.

### 4.6 Right-of-Entry Ordinances

The neighborhood-by-neighborhood backbone of this model depends on a right-of-entry ordinance adopted before the first scheduled neighborhood begins. The League recommends an ordinance structure built on three elements: (1) advance written notice to affected property owners of no less than 60 days before scheduled work, including the specific date range and a clear statement that the replacement is being performed at no direct cost to the homeowner; (2) a default opt-out rather than opt-in consent structure, under which the utility proceeds with replacement of both the utility-side and customer-side portions of the line unless the owner affirmatively declines within the notice period; and (3) a documented, narrowly defined enforcement pathway for a residual population of owners who decline and do not respond, which may include municipal code enforcement tied to the health hazard finding underlying the federal rule, applied only after multiple documented outreach attempts.

### 4.7 Affordability Protections

To address the rate impact identified in Section 4.5, the model builds in three affordability protections funded from within the program budget rather than as an unfunded mandate on the rate base: a tiered low-income rate discount scaled to the same income bands used for the system's existing low-income water assistance program, if one exists, or to a new tier established concurrently with the bonding ordinance; a waiver of any customer-side cost-share for owner-occupied households below 200 percent of the federal poverty line, fully absorbing that cost into the bonded program rather than the reimbursement structure described in Section 3.3; and a rate-smoothing reserve, funded from the pay-as-you-go allocation in Table 6, that caps the year-over-year rate increase attributable to the program at a fixed percentage even if bonding or grant timing creates a temporary funding gap.

## 5. Answering the Objections

Member feedback on program design has centered on three objections. Each is addressed in turn, drawing on the field experience summarized in Sections 2 and 3.

### 5.1 Private Property Access

The objection: replacing the customer-side portion of a service line requires entering private property, and homeowners are not obligated to grant access; refusal and non-response rates of 22 to 31 percent among members with active programs suggest the ten-year window is unachievable if the utility must obtain affirmative consent from every owner.

The response: the refusal and non-response rates members report were generated predominantly under opt-in consent structures, in which the burden falls on the homeowner to initiate contact and schedule work. The right-of-entry ordinance structure recommended in Section 4.6 shifts to a default opt-out structure, which League members' own legal review and comparable programs outside the region indicate is a defensible exercise of municipal police power given the documented health hazard finding underlying the federal rule, provided adequate notice and a genuine opportunity to decline are built into the ordinance. This does not eliminate refusal, but it converts the baseline population from "must affirmatively agree" to "must affirmatively object," which field data from systems that have already made this shift show reduces effective non-completion to a single-digit residual population rather than the 22-to-31 percent baseline. That residual population is addressed through the documented, staged enforcement pathway in Section 4.6, applied only after the compliance window's early years have demonstrated the ordinance's workability and after outreach — not as a first response.

### 5.2 Shortage of Licensed Plumbers

The objection: the physical scope of work, particularly the customer-side portion, requires licensed plumbers, and the four-state region does not currently have workforce capacity to support a decade of concurrent replacement across 412 systems without driving up labor cost or extending project timelines.

The response: this is the primary rationale for the regional shared-procurement strategy described in Section 3.4. A single 22,000-connection system attempting to secure dedicated licensed-plumber capacity on its own competes against every neighboring utility for the same limited pool and will pay a premium, if it can secure crews at all, particularly in peak construction seasons. Regional consortia already operating within League territory report both lower per-line cost and shorter mobilization lag because they maintain crews in continuous employment across multiple systems' overlapping but staggered schedules, rather than each system separately trying to secure short-term crew capacity for its own peak years. The League further recommends that members support state-level licensing reciprocity and apprenticeship-acceleration measures specific to service line replacement work, which several members have already raised with state licensing boards, to expand the regional labor pool over the life of the program rather than relying solely on redistribution of the existing pool.

### 5.3 Rate Shock in Low-Income Neighborhoods

The objection: the modeled rate impact of $6.40 to $18.90 a month falls hardest, in relative terms, on the same low-income households that are statistically most likely to occupy older housing stock with a higher prevalence of lead service lines, creating a program that funds its own remediation disproportionately from the population it is intended to protect.

The response: the financing model in Section 4 is structured specifically to avoid this outcome, through three mechanisms working together rather than any single fix. First, the blended financing structure in Table 6 keeps the bonded share below half of total program cost, which keeps the rate-funded portion — the piece that falls directly on the residential bill — below the level a bond-only financing structure would require. Second, the affordability protections in Section 4.7 fund the customer-side cost share for low-income owner-occupants directly from the bonded program budget rather than through a reimbursement structure that requires the homeowner to front costs, which is the mechanism most directly responsible for the inequitable outcomes members have observed under reimbursement-based programs (Section 3.3). Third, the neighborhood-by-neighborhood backbone allows systems to sequence early program years toward the highest-lead-prevalence, typically older and lower-income, neighborhoods, which concentrates the direct benefit of replacement — not just the cost — in the same population bearing the rate impact, and allows the system to demonstrate the affordability protections in practice before the program reaches full scale.

## 6. Conclusion

The ten-year compliance window set by the revised federal lead and copper rule converts a long-deferred capital liability into a fixed, near-term obligation for the 412 systems the League represents. The inventory data are unambiguous: 214,000 confirmed lead or galvanized lines, another 118,000 of unknown material, and per-line costs that can reach $21,000 in the most difficult cases mean that no member system will meet the 2037 deadline through opportunistic replacement or homeowner-initiated reimbursement alone. The program model developed in this paper — records-based verification to shrink the workload before excavation begins, a neighborhood-by-neighborhood backbone financed through a fixed annual bonding schedule, regional shared procurement to solve the crew and licensed-plumber constraint, and affordability protections funded directly into the bonded program rather than left to reimbursement — is designed to bring a representative 22,000-connection system to full compliance within the mandated window at a rate impact toward the lower half of the League-wide range. The League recommends that members begin records verification and right-of-entry ordinance adoption in the current fiscal year, well ahead of the 2027 start of the compliance clock, because the lead time required to establish financing, crew contracts, and legal authority is itself a significant share of the ten years available.

The estimated aggregate replacement cost in Table 7 applies the membership-wide average of $9,100 per line to the confirmed population in each state and assumes a 50 percent records-verification retirement rate applied to each state's unknown-material population, consistent with the assumption used in the 22,000-connection model in Section 4.1. It does not net out state revolving fund allocation, which varies materially by state appropriation cycle and is addressed separately below.

State revolving fund capitalization grants and state match commitments differ enough across the four states that the one-third coverage estimate used in Section 2.4 should be treated as a membership-wide average rather than a state-specific planning figure. Michigan and Ohio members reported SRF allocations covering closer to 38 and 35 percent of estimated need, respectively, reflecting larger recent state appropriations tied to lead line replacement specifically, while Wisconsin and Indiana members reported allocations closer to 26 and 22 percent, reflecting SRF programs that continue to fund lead replacement within a broader, non-earmarked water infrastructure allocation. Systems in Wisconsin and Indiana should therefore weight the financing model in Section 4.4 toward a larger local bonding share, a larger pay-as-you-go reserve draw, or a longer initial ramp-up period to build reserve capacity before peak-year construction begins.

Right-of-entry ordinance authority also varies by state enabling statute. Michigan and Ohio members report that existing municipal police power and public-health-nuisance statutes provide reasonably clear authority for the opt-out consent structure recommended in Section 4.6, and several members in both states have already adopted such ordinances without significant legal challenge. Wisconsin members report a more contested legal landscape, with at least one pending state-level challenge to a municipal right-of-entry ordinance on private-property-rights grounds; Wisconsin members are advised to coordinate ordinance language through the League's legal affairs committee before adoption rather than adapting Michigan or Ohio ordinance language directly. Indiana members report the least developed case law on this question and are encouraged to pursue the interlocal, regional-consortium approach described in Section 3.4 partly as a means of pooling legal costs for a coordinated test ordinance rather than each system bearing that cost individually.

Member systems adapting the ten-year schedule in Table 5 to their own connection count and inventory profile should treat the following as sequential prerequisites rather than parallel, independent tasks. League technical assistance staff are available to member systems working through this sequence.

**Table 8. Pre-2027 Readiness Milestones**

The League's strong recommendation, restated from Section 6, is that every member system treat 2026 as an unofficial eleventh year of the program rather than as preparation time separate from it.
