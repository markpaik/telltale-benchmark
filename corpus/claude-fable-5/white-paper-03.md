**A White Paper of the Upper Midwest League of Municipal Utilities**

Lansing, Michigan | August 2026

**Authors:**

## Abstract

The federal Lead and Copper Rule Improvements (LCRI) require water systems to replace all lead and galvanized-requiring-replacement service lines within ten years, beginning in 2027 and concluding in 2037. Inventories filed by the 412 member systems of the Upper Midwest League of Municipal Utilities cover 1.06 million service lines, of which 214,000 are confirmed lead or galvanized requiring replacement and 118,000 remain of unknown material. At an average replacement cost of $9,100 per line — with a range of $4,200 to $21,000 — the confirmed backlog alone represents a capital obligation approaching $2 billion across the membership, while state revolving fund allocations cover roughly one-third of estimated need. This paper presents the inventory and cost data, evaluates the five program approaches members have adopted to date, and builds a complete implementation and financing model sized to a representative 22,000-connection system: an annual replacement schedule, crew capacity plan, a $2.1 million annual bonding program, a model right-of-entry ordinance, and affordability protections designed to hold rate impacts within the modeled range of $6.40 to $18.90 on a median monthly bill of $52. The paper closes by answering the three objections members raise most often — private property access, the licensed plumber shortage, and rate shock in low-income neighborhoods — and finds that each is manageable with tools already proven in member systems and peer utilities. The central conclusion is that a hybrid program anchored in neighborhood-by-neighborhood full replacement, accelerated by opportunistic coordination with capital work, supported by regional shared procurement, and preceded by aggressive records-based verification of unknowns, is achievable within the compliance window at rate impacts most communities can absorb with targeted assistance.

## 1. The Problem: A Ten-Year Clock on a Century-Old Liability

Lead service lines are the largest remaining source of lead in drinking water in the United States. They were installed lawfully — in many Upper Midwest cities they were required by plumbing code well into the mid-twentieth century — and they sit in a legal gray zone, typically split between a utility-owned portion in the right-of-way and a customer-owned portion running from the curb stop to the meter. That split ownership, more than any engineering difficulty, has stalled replacement for decades.

The regulatory environment has now changed decisively. The Lead and Copper Rule Improvements finalized by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency in 2024 require water systems to replace all lead service lines and galvanized service lines requiring replacement, regardless of ownership, at a mandatory minimum annual pace, with the replacement obligation beginning in 2027 and full completion required by 2037 (U.S. EPA, 2024). Partial replacements — replacing only the utility-owned side while leaving lead in the ground on the customer side — are effectively prohibited except in narrow emergency circumstances, a restriction grounded in well-documented evidence that partials can temporarily elevate lead levels at the tap.

The compliance clock is running against three hard constraints:

1. **Money.** The confirmed replacement backlog across League members implies capital need on the order of $1.9 billion at average unit costs, before resolution of 118,000 unknowns. State revolving fund (SRF) allocations, including Bipartisan Infrastructure Law lead service line set-asides, cover roughly one-third of estimated member need.
2. **Labor.** Every member state reports shortages of licensed plumbers and experienced utility construction crews. A ten-year, region-wide replacement surge will tighten that market further.
3. **Access.** Because the customer-side portion sits on private property, systems cannot replace lines without owner consent or legal authority to enter. Members already running programs report homeowner refusal or non-response rates between 22 and 31 percent.

One member city is currently defending a class action arising from partial replacements performed in 2018 — a reminder that the cost of doing this work incompletely can exceed the cost of doing it right. The question before city managers, utility boards, and finance officers is no longer *whether* to run a full replacement program, but *how* to run one that finishes on time, survives legal scrutiny, and does not price low-income households out of water service.

This paper answers that question in five steps: it presents the member inventory and cost data (Section 3), compares the five program approaches members have adopted (Section 4), builds a complete implementation and financing model for a representative 22,000-connection system (Section 5), and answers the principal objections (Section 6), before closing with recommendations.

## 2. Regulatory Context in Brief

Four features of the LCRI drive program design for League members:

- **Universal replacement mandate.** All lead and galvanized-requiring-replacement service lines must be replaced within ten years of the 2027 compliance date, at a minimum average annual rate generally equal to one-tenth of the system's replaceable inventory.
- **Full replacement standard.** Utility-side-only (partial) replacements do not count toward the mandate and are restricted. Systems must make a good-faith, documented effort to replace the customer-owned portion.
- **Inventory and unknowns.** Systems must maintain a validated service line inventory and resolve lines of unknown material. Unknowns are treated conservatively — they remain in the denominator of the replacement obligation until verified non-lead — which makes verification one of the highest-return activities a system can undertake.
- **Documented refusals.** A system that makes and documents required outreach attempts and is refused access may, under defined conditions, count the line as addressed for compliance purposes, though the line remains in the inventory and the public health liability remains in the ground.

Michigan systems face an additional constraint: the state's 2018 Lead and Copper Rule already required full replacement at utility expense, established a 5 percent annual replacement pace, and generally barred charging customers directly for the customer-side portion. The program model in Section 5 is designed to satisfy both the federal rule and the strictest state overlay in the League's footprint.

Two facts in Table 1 should shape every member's program. First, one in five service lines in the region is a confirmed replacement obligation — this is not a boutique problem confined to a few legacy cities. Second, the unknowns are large enough to swing total program cost by hundreds of millions of dollars region-wide. Every unknown line retired by records verification at $85–$140 per line is a line that does not have to be excavated at $9,100. No other early program activity has a comparable return.

### 3.2 What Replacement Costs and Why It Varies

Member-reported unit costs average $9,100 per line but range from $4,200 to $21,000. Table 2 decomposes the drivers.

**Table 2. Reported Unit Cost Range and Principal Cost Drivers (member data, 2023–2026)**

| Cost Component / Driver | Low End (~$4,200) | High End (~$21,000) |
|---|---|---|
| Replacement method | Trenchless pull-through or directional bore | Open trench full length |
| Customer-side portion in scope | Short run, accessible meter | Long run, basement penetration, interior restoration |
| Street and surface restoration | Coordinated with paving; restoration shared with road project | Stand-alone excavation in recently paved arterial; full-width mill and overlay required by local ordinance |
| Mobilization | Neighborhood-scale contract, 200+ lines per package | Scattered single-address work orders |
| Site conditions | Sandy soils, shallow frost-free depth achievable | Rock, congested utility corridors, contaminated soils handling |
| Labor market | Regional shared crews, multi-year unit-price contract | Spot-market contracting in tight trades market |

The spread in Table 2 is the strategic heart of this paper: **unit cost is not fixed; it is a function of program design.** Systems that batch work geographically, coordinate with paving, use trenchless methods, and procure regionally consistently report costs in the $6,500–$8,200 range. Systems doing scattered, reactive, address-by-address work report costs at or above the average, and in hard conditions well above it. Over a 5,000-line program, the difference between $7,500 and $10,500 per line is $15 million.

### 3.3 The Funding Gap and the Rate Consequence

State revolving fund allocations — including the federal infrastructure law's lead service line set-asides, a portion of which flows as principal forgiveness to disadvantaged communities — cover roughly one-third of estimated member need. The remaining two-thirds must come from utility revenue bonds, general obligation bonds, rates, and local or philanthropic sources.

League modeling of the resulting rate impact on a median residential bill of $52 per month produces a range of **$6.40 to $18.90 per month** at full program build-out, depending on unit cost achieved, SRF share captured, borrowing terms, and inventory size relative to the rate base. The low end is a manageable 12 percent increase phased over a decade; the high end is a 36 percent increase that will generate genuine hardship in low-income neighborhoods without deliberate affordability design. Section 5.6 addresses that design directly.

## 4. Five Approaches in Member Practice: A Comparative Assessment

Members have organized their programs around five approaches. They are not mutually exclusive — indeed, the model in Section 5 combines four of them — but each has a distinct cost, pace, and risk profile. Table 3 compares them.

**Table 3. Comparative Assessment of the Five Program Approaches**

| Approach | Typical Unit Cost | Annual Pace Achievable | Refusal / Non-Response Exposure | Equity Performance | Administrative Burden | Best Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Neighborhood-by-neighborhood full replacement | $6,500–$8,500 | High — sets its own pace | Lowest (22% → low teens with block-level outreach) | Strong when sequenced by risk and demographics | Moderate–High | **Program backbone** |
| 2. Opportunistic replacement during main and paving work | $4,200–$6,500 marginal | Limited — tied to CIP schedule | Low (work is visibly "already happening") | Neutral; follows CIP geography | Low incremental | **Cost accelerator** |
| 3. Homeowner reimbursement | $7,000–$12,000 (owner-procured) | Low and unpredictable | Highest — requires owner initiative and up-front cash | Weak; uptake skews to higher-income owners | High per line (applications, inspections, quality control) | Supplement for owner-driven remodels only |
| 4. Regional shared procurement of crews and materials | 8–15% savings on any approach | Enabler, not a pace-setter | n/a | Neutral–positive (extends capacity to small systems) | Moderate (joint agreements) | **Force multiplier** |
| 5. Records-based verification of unknowns | $85–$140 per line resolved | 2,000–10,000 lines/year per analyst-team | n/a | Positive (prevents unnecessary excavation on private property) | Low–Moderate | **First move, every system** |

### 4.1 Neighborhood-by-Neighborhood Full Replacement

Systems that designate contiguous replacement zones — typically 150–400 lines per construction package — and replace every confirmed line in the zone, both sides, achieve the best combination of unit cost, pace, and consent rates. Mobilization is amortized across the block; restoration is done once; and the social dynamics of consent change fundamentally. Members report that when a crew is visibly working the street and neighbors are signing, non-response falls from the 22–31 percent range observed in scattered programs to the low teens, and below 10 percent with door-to-door canvassing in the two weeks before construction. This mirrors the experience of Newark, New Jersey, which replaced more than 23,000 lines in under three years using zone-based construction and an ordinance-backed access framework (Newark DWSU, 2022).

The weakness is capital intensity: the approach requires bonding capacity and multi-year contracting authority up front. It is the approach the financing model in Section 5 is built to fund.

### 4.2 Opportunistic Replacement During Main and Paving Work

When a water main is being replaced or a street repaved, the marginal cost of pulling the service lines is dramatically lower — restoration is shared, traffic control is already in place, and the excavation is open. Members report marginal costs of $4,200–$6,500. Every system should adopt a **"dig once" policy**: no main replacement or reconstruction-depth paving project proceeds without replacing all lead and galvanized services in the project limits, and unknowns in the limits are verified during construction.

The limitation is arithmetic. A typical member replaces 0.8–1.5 percent of its mains annually; even a doubled CIP touches only a fraction of the lead inventory within the compliance window. Opportunistic work is a cost accelerator, not a compliance strategy.

### 4.3 Homeowner Reimbursement

Several members offer reimbursement (typically $3,000–$6,000) to owners who hire their own plumber to replace the customer side. In practice, uptake has been low, skewed toward higher-income owners with cash to front, and administratively expensive per line — each job requires application processing, permit verification, and post-construction inspection, and quality varies. The approach also consumes exactly the licensed-plumber capacity that is the region's binding labor constraint, at retail rather than production-crew efficiency. The League recommends retaining reimbursement only as a narrow supplement for owners undertaking renovations on their own schedule, not as a program pillar.

### 4.4 Regional Shared Procurement

Small and mid-size systems individually lack the volume to attract competitive bids from specialized trenchless crews or to secure materials pricing. Members that have formed joint purchasing agreements — shared multi-year unit-price contracts, pooled pipe and fitting orders, and traveling crews scheduled across systems — report savings of 8–15 percent and, more importantly, guaranteed crew availability. The League operates a model joint powers agreement and a qualified-contractor pool for this purpose. Shared procurement is especially critical for systems under 10,000 connections, which cannot sustain a dedicated crew year-round.

### 4.5 Records-Based Verification of Unknowns

Verification campaigns combine tap cards, plumbing permits, meter records, water main installation dates, and statistical or machine-learning prediction, validated by targeted potholing or in-home inspections, to reclassify unknowns. Primacy agencies in all four League states have accepted statistically validated records methods. Members completing campaigns have retired 40–75 percent of unknowns as non-lead without excavation, at $85–$140 per line — roughly one to two percent of the cost of replacement. Because unknowns remain in the compliance denominator until resolved, verification directly reduces both the mandated annual replacement pace and total program cost. **It is the first move for every system, without exception.**

### 4.6 Synthesis

The evidence supports a hybrid: verification first to shrink the denominator; neighborhood-based full replacement as the backbone; a dig-once policy to capture opportunistic savings; regional procurement to control cost and secure labor; and reimbursement retained only at the margin. Section 5 assembles that hybrid into a complete, financeable program.

## 5. The Program Model: A 22,000-Connection System

This section builds the model the League's members requested: a full implementation and financing plan sized to a representative 22,000-connection system, reflecting League-average inventory shares. Every element scales approximately linearly for systems from 8,000 to 60,000 connections.

### 5.1 Scope, Verification, and Schedule

Applying League-average shares, the model system's inventory is:

- **Confirmed lead / galvanized requiring replacement:** 4,440 lines (20.2%)
- **Unknown material:** 2,440 lines (11.1%)
- **Confirmed non-lead:** 15,120 lines

**Year 0–1 (2026–2027): Verification campaign.** A two-person records team, supported by the League's predictive model and a field-verification contract (potholing at approximately 5 percent of predicted-non-lead addresses for statistical validation), resolves the 2,440 unknowns within 18 months at a cost of approximately $290,000. Based on member experience, an estimated 30 percent — roughly 730 lines — resolve as lead or galvanized requiring replacement.

**Program scope: approximately 5,200 replacements** (4,440 confirmed plus ~730 from unknowns), completed 2027–2036, one year ahead of the 2037 deadline to provide schedule contingency. Figure 1 shows the ramp.

Replacement zones are sequenced by a published prioritization matrix: childhood blood-lead surveillance data, presence of schools and child-care facilities, housing age, share of households below 200 percent of the federal poverty level, and coordination opportunities with the paving and main replacement CIP. Publishing the matrix in advance is essential to defending the sequence against both political pressure and equity challenge.

### 5.2 Crew Capacity and Workforce Plan

Production assumptions, drawn from member experience with trenchless (pull-through and directional bore) methods:

- A standard replacement crew — equipment operator, two laborers, one licensed plumber for the meter connection and interior work, plus shared inspection — completes **1.8–2.2 lines per working day** in zone-based production.
- At approximately 185 effective field days per season (April–November, weather-adjusted), **one crew delivers 330–400 lines per year.**

The full-production pace of 560 lines per year therefore requires **1.5 crew-equivalents**: one dedicated crew under a multi-year unit-price contract, plus a half-time share of a **regional crew procured through the League's joint purchasing pool** and scheduled across two or three neighboring systems. This structure is deliberate — it gives the system guaranteed capacity without carrying idle crew cost, and it gives small neighboring systems access to production-priced work.

Plumber capacity is the binding constraint and is addressed three ways in the model: trenchless methods concentrate the licensed-plumber scope to roughly 2–3 hours per line (meter reconnection and interior tie-in) rather than a full-day excavation job; the unit-price contract permits apprentices working under a master plumber's supervision consistent with state code, paired with the community-college pre-apprenticeship pipeline described in Section 6.2; and interior work is batched so one plumber services both crews. On these assumptions, the full program requires the equivalent of **2.0–2.5 licensed plumber FTEs** — a demand the regional pool can supply even in the current market.

Program administration requires a program manager (may be shared with the CIP), one inspector per crew, a full-time community outreach coordinator, and 0.5 FTE for records, permitting, and LCRI reporting: approximately **$520,000 per year in program delivery costs**, included in the financing plan below.

### 5.5 The Right-of-Entry Ordinance

The customer-side portion cannot lawfully be replaced without access, and access cannot be left to ad hoc persuasion at 5,200 doors. The model program is built on a municipal ordinance, adapted from the League's model code (and from ordinances upheld in Newark, Benton Harbor, and several Wisconsin municipalities), with these elements:

1. **Declaration and mandate.** Lead and galvanized service lines are declared a public health hazard; replacement of both portions is declared a public purpose performed at utility expense, with no special assessment to the property owner.
2. **Consent process.** The utility must attempt contact at least four times through at least two channels (mail, door hanger, phone/text, in-person canvass), in the property's predominant languages, with a standard consent form granting temporary right of entry and releasing the utility for ordinary restoration.
3. **Access requirement.** Owners and occupants are required to grant reasonable access for replacement scheduled with not less than 30 days' notice; refusal is documented on a standard form that satisfies LCRI documentation requirements.
4. **Enforcement ladder.** Documented refusal triggers, in sequence: certified-mail notice of the health risk and of continued lead exposure; a requirement to disclose the lead line upon sale or lease under the city's existing disclosure code; eligibility conditions (properties refusing access are ineligible for future street-degradation fee waivers and certain permits); and, as a last resort for vacant or unresponsive properties, administrative warrant procedures. The League does **not** recommend water shutoff as an enforcement tool.
5. **Rental property provisions.** Landlord consent may be given for a portfolio; replacement is added to rental certification checklists on the certification renewal cycle, which member cities report is the single most effective lever for absentee-owner properties.
6. **Restoration standard.** The ordinance codifies like-for-like exterior restoration and workmanlike interior restoration, which materially reduces refusal driven by fear of property damage.

Counsel review under each state's constitution is required — the League's model ordinance file includes state-specific annotations — but the core structure has survived challenge where adopted.

### 5.6 Affordability Protections

The program is designed so that **no household pays a direct charge for replacement of its line**; the affordability question is therefore about the rate ramp, not job-cost assessments. The model includes four protections, budgeted within the program:

- **Percentage-of-income cap.** Households at or below 200 percent of the federal poverty level enrolled in the utility's assistance program receive a bill credit ensuring total water charges do not exceed 3 percent of household income, funded by an affordability rider of approximately $0.60 per month across the rate base (included within the peak impact figures above).
- **Targeted principal forgiveness capture.** SRF applications are structured around disadvantaged-community census tracts to maximize forgiveness, which flows through as a reduction in the rate-funded share attributable to those zones.
- **Arrearage protection during construction.** No shutoff for nonpayment at any address within an active replacement zone, and payment-plan enrollment offered at the consent doorstep.
- **Fixture and filter support.** Every replaced household receives a certified filter and six months of cartridges (an LCRI requirement) plus free aerator cleaning and a high-risk-household faucet replacement program funded at $60,000 per year.

Finance officers should note that these protections cost roughly 2 percent of program value while directly answering the rate-shock objection addressed in Section 6.3 — an inexpensive purchase of both equity and political durability.

## 6. Answering the Objections

### 6.1 "You can't make people let you onto their property."

Correct — and the program does not depend on making them. The evidence from member systems and national peers is that refusal is mostly a function of program design, not fixed public sentiment. Scattered, letter-based programs see the 22–31 percent non-response rates our members report. Zone-based programs with door-to-door canvassing, visible neighborhood construction, trusted-messenger partnerships (block clubs, congregations, community health workers), multilingual materials, and a no-cost, restoration-guaranteed offer consistently drive non-consent below 10 percent; Newark finished below 3 percent. The ordinance in Section 5.5 converts the residual into either lawful access (rental certification, administrative warrant for vacant properties) or LCRI-compliant documented refusal. And the pending class action against a member city over 2018 partial replacements is the decisive counterpoint to any suggestion that systems simply replace their own side and move on: the legal and public-health exposure created by partials now exceeds the cost of doing the outreach properly. The model program performs **no partial replacements**, full stop.

### 6.2 "There aren't enough licensed plumbers to do this work."

The shortage is real, but the arithmetic is more forgiving than the objection assumes, for three reasons. First, method choice: trenchless replacement confines the licensed-plumber scope to the meter connection and interior tie-in — two to three hours per line — so the model system's full-production pace requires only 2.0–2.5 plumber FTEs, not the dozens a trench-and-repipe mental model suggests. Second, regional pooling: the League's shared procurement program lets one master plumber and supervised apprentices serve multiple small systems on a scheduled circuit, which is precisely how the region's rural electric cooperatives have long managed scarce line-worker capacity. Third, pipeline building: a ten-year, regionally coordinated program is exactly the demand signal community colleges and joint apprenticeship committees need to expand cohorts; members in Wisconsin and Michigan have seeded pre-apprenticeship programs with utility-funded stipends at a cost of under $4,000 per graduate, hiring locally from the very neighborhoods being served. The workforce constraint argues for early, coordinated, multi-year contracting — not for delay, which would concentrate all 412 members' demand into a shorter, more expensive scramble at the end of the compliance window.

### 6.3 "The rates will crush low-income neighborhoods."

Rate impact is real and must be engineered, not waved away. Three points answer the objection. First, magnitude and phasing: the model's peak impact of $13.50 per month arrives in $1.35 annual steps over a decade — significant, but far from the single-year shock the objection imagines, and within the League's modeled range. Second, incidence: the households facing the largest rate burden are disproportionately the same households drinking through lead lines today; the status quo is not free — it is paid in blood-lead levels, special education costs, and property devaluation, borne most heavily in the very neighborhoods the objection invokes. Dr. Bektas's review of the health-economics literature places the monetized benefit of full replacement at several multiples of its cost, driven by avoided cognitive harm to children (see also EPA's LCRI economic analysis, which reached the same conclusion at national scale). Third, protection: the percentage-of-income cap, targeted SRF forgiveness, shutoff moratorium, and zero direct charge to homeowners in Section 5.6 mean that the lowest-income households are shielded from the marginal increase while receiving the replacement first under the risk-based sequencing matrix. A program that replaces lines in the highest-risk neighborhoods early, at no direct cost, while capping bills at 3 percent of income, is an anti-rate-shock program by construction.

## 7. Recommendations

The League recommends that every member system, regardless of size:

1. **Complete records-based verification of all unknowns by the end of 2027.** It is the cheapest capital avoidance available and directly reduces the mandated pace.
2. **Adopt a dig-once ordinance immediately** requiring full service line replacement within the limits of every main and reconstruction-depth paving project.
3. **Structure the program around neighborhood zones** sequenced by a published, risk-and-equity-based matrix, with door-to-door consent operations preceding each zone.
4. **Adopt the model right-of-entry ordinance** with rental-certification integration, and prohibit partial replacements by policy.
5. **Finance through annual bond tranches matched to construction draw**, layered with maximum SRF and principal-forgiveness capture, on the pattern of the $2.1 million schedule in Section 5.4.
6. **Join the League's regional procurement pool** for crews, materials, and predictive-model services; systems under 10,000 connections should not attempt standalone contracting.
7. **Fund affordability protections inside the program budget** — a percentage-of-income cap, construction-zone shutoff moratorium, and filter support — at roughly 2 percent of program value.
8. **Start in 2026, not 2027.** Verification, ordinance adoption, SRF applications, and first contracting all precede the compliance clock; systems that use the remaining pre-compliance period will buy their lines at the bottom of the regional cost curve.

## 8. Conclusion

The ten-year federal mandate converts a liability our region has deferred for a century into a scheduled, financeable public works program. The member data presented here support a clear conclusion: the difference between a $6.40 program and an $18.90 program — and between a program that finishes in 2036 and one that faces enforcement in 2037 — is design, not destiny. Verification shrinks the problem before a shovel turns. Neighborhood-scale construction cuts unit costs by a quarter and cuts refusals by half or more. Annual-tranche bonding turns a $50 million obligation into a decade of modest, predictable rate steps. Regional procurement turns 412 small labor markets into one workable one. And affordability protections costing 2 percent of program value answer the equity objection before it is raised. The systems that move in 2026 will replace their lines with the region's cheapest crews, its most patient capital, and its most willing homeowners.
