# Stable Scheduling in Hourly Retail Work: A Review of the Evidence on Turnover, Sales, Cost, and Worker Outcomes

**Prepared for Pinal Ridge Markets, Phoenix, Arizona**
**Hyun-Woo Chang, Ph.D., Labor Economics Research Group, Tempe, Arizona**
**Internal data support: Camille Deschamps-Iyer, Pinal Ridge Markets**
**August 2026**

---

## 1. Introduction: The Question and Its Scope

Pinal Ridge Markets operates 61 grocery stores across Arizona and southern Nevada with 8,400 employees, of whom 61 percent work part time. Front-end clerk turnover reached 87 percent in the most recent fiscal year, with per-replacement costs estimated internally at approximately $2,100. The company's chief people officer has proposed a twelve-store pilot of stable scheduling — schedules posted two weeks in advance, a guaranteed weekly core of hours, and elimination of on-call shifts — while the chief financial officer projects an incremental labor cost of $1.9 million annually if the practices were extended chainwide. The question this review addresses is narrow and practical: **what does the empirical literature say about whether stable scheduling practices of this kind reduce turnover, affect sales, cost what proponents or skeptics claim, and improve worker outcomes — and what does that evidence imply for a regional grocery chain of this size?**

The scope requires careful definition, because "stable scheduling" is not a single intervention in the literature. The research base spans at least four distinct practice bundles: advance notice of schedules (typically 10 to 14 days), guaranteed minimum hours or "core hours," elimination of on-call and clopening shifts, and worker input into schedule construction. Some studies evaluate one component; some evaluate all four bundled; municipal ordinance studies evaluate whatever mix a city council enacted, often with predictability-pay penalties that no voluntary employer program includes. Failure to distinguish these bundles is, as this review will argue, one of the principal reasons the literature appears contradictory. A second scope condition matters equally: retail is not homogeneous. Grocery, with its perishable inventory, relatively predictable demand curves, and union density higher than apparel or general merchandise, behaves differently in the data than fashion retail or food service, and findings from one segment transfer imperfectly to another (Lambert, Fugiel & Henly 2014; Kesavan et al. 2022).

This review therefore proceeds in five parts. Section 2 describes the search. Section 3 synthesizes the evidence thematically — turnover and retention, sales and productivity, labor cost, and worker outcomes — setting studies against one another rather than cataloguing them. Section 4 explains which methodological differences account for the divergent findings. Section 5 identifies what has not been studied. Section 6 states what the evidence, on balance, supports for a chain of Pinal Ridge's size and composition, including how the pilot should be structured so that the company learns something from it.

A candid orientation is warranted at the outset. The literature does not deliver a clean verdict. It contains one well-executed field experiment showing meaningful sales gains under stable scheduling in one national apparel retailer; a set of ordinance evaluations showing that mandated notice improves workers' schedule predictability and sleep but has mixed or null effects on turnover; administrative-data studies suggesting some employers respond to scheduling mandates by trimming total hours; and cost estimates that differ by an order of magnitude depending on what is counted. The task of this review is not to average these findings into a false consensus but to explain when and why each result obtains, and to locate Pinal Ridge's situation within that map.

## 2. Search Strategy and Corpus

The search covered EconLit, Business Source Complete, PsycINFO, and the working-paper series of four labor research centers (the Shift Project at Harvard, the Employment Instability Researchers Network at the University of Chicago, the W.E. Upjohn Institute, and the UC Berkeley Center for Labor Research and Education) for the period 1998 through 2026. Search terms combined scheduling vocabulary (advance notice, secure scheduling, fair workweek, on-call, schedule stability, hours guarantee) with outcome vocabulary (turnover, retention, quits, sales, productivity, labor cost, well-being, sleep, income volatility) and setting vocabulary (retail, grocery, supermarket, hourly, part-time).

The search returned 1,120 unique records after deduplication. Title-and-abstract screening excluded studies of salaried scheduling, healthcare shift work without retail relevance, and purely theoretical models without empirical content, leaving 217 records read in full. Fifty-nine studies met inclusion criteria: empirical evaluation of at least one stable-scheduling practice or its absence, in a retail, grocery, or comparable hourly service setting, with a measured outcome in one of the four domains above. The corpus comprises nine field experiments, 21 quasi-experimental evaluations of municipal or state scheduling ordinances, 16 administrative-data studies drawing on payroll, point-of-sale, or matched employer-employee records, and seven qualitative studies. Effect sizes, outcome definitions, sample characteristics, and firm sizes are recorded in the extraction file accompanying this review and are cited here by author and year. Where the review draws on Pinal Ridge internal data — turnover rates, replacement cost, part-time share — those figures were compiled by Camille Deschamps-Iyer from company HRIS and payroll records and are identified as such.

Two limitations of the corpus should be flagged before synthesis. First, publication bias runs in both directions: advocacy-adjacent research centers have incentives to publish favorable findings, while employer-funded studies emphasizing cost have their own selection pressures; the review weights pre-registered experiments and studies with transparent administrative data most heavily. Second, the modal study is now five to eight years old and predates both the post-pandemic tightening of hourly labor markets and the diffusion of AI-assisted scheduling software, both of which plausibly change the cost-benefit arithmetic in ways the older evidence cannot capture.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Turnover and Retention

The turnover evidence divides sharply by study design, and the division is instructive rather than merely frustrating.

The strongest experimental evidence comes from the Gap stable-scheduling field experiment (Williams et al. 2018; Kesavan et al. 2022), a cluster-randomized trial across 28 stores in two metropolitan areas that bundled two-week advance notice, elimination of on-call shifts, part-time hour targets, and shift-swapping technology. The experiment found a statistically significant reduction in turnover among the most experienced sales associates — the workers whose replacement is most expensive — though effects on turnover among the newest hires were weaker and less precise. The mechanism the authors propose is that schedule stability functions as a form of compensation for workers with outside obligations, and that its retention value is concentrated among workers who have already accumulated firm-specific skills and would otherwise be pushed out by schedule chaos rather than pulled out by better offers. A smaller field experiment in a quick-service restaurant chain (Choper, Schneider & Harknett 2022, drawing on Shift Project survey-to-turnover linkages rather than randomization in the strict sense, but with strong panel identification) found that workers experiencing on-call shifts and last-minute cancellations were roughly twice as likely to quit within the following year as observationally similar coworkers with stable schedules, and that schedule instability predicted turnover more strongly than wage level within the observed wage range.

Set against these results are the ordinance evaluations, which are considerably less encouraging on turnover specifically. Evaluations of Seattle's Secure Scheduling Ordinance (Harknett, Schneider & Irwin 2021; Romich et al. 2020) found clear improvements in advance notice and schedule predictability but no statistically detectable reduction in turnover in the first two years. The Emeryville Fair Workweek evaluation (Pollack et al. 2021) similarly reported compliance gains and worker-reported predictability improvements without measurable retention effects. An evaluation of Oregon's statewide law (Marquez & Ostrander 2023) found null turnover effects in retail overall, with a suggestive but imprecise reduction in grocery specifically.

How should these be reconciled? Three differences do most of the work. First, *treatment intensity*: the Gap experiment implemented a full bundle including guaranteed hour targets, whereas the ordinances mandate notice and predictability pay but generally do not guarantee weekly hours; the qualitative literature (Henly & Lambert 2014; Carrillo et al. 2017) consistently identifies hour insufficiency and volatility of *total hours* — not merely timing unpredictability — as the proximate driver of quits among part-time workers with caregiving obligations. An ordinance that stabilizes when you work but not how much you work addresses only half the problem. Second, *compliance*: audit studies of ordinance implementation (Schneider & Harknett 2019a; Ananat, Gassman-Pines & Fitz-Henley 2022) document substantial noncompliance in the early years, meaning intent-to-treat estimates from ordinance evaluations dilute the effect of actually receiving stable schedules. Third, *horizon*: turnover is a lagging outcome; the ordinance evaluations mostly cover 12 to 24 months post-enactment, and the one longer-horizon study (Harknett & Schneider 2024, extending the Seattle panel to five years) finds turnover effects emerging in years three through five, concentrated among tenured workers — a pattern strikingly consistent with the Gap experiment's finding that retention gains accrue among experienced staff.

For Pinal Ridge, the relevant reading is this: the evidence does not support the claim that posting schedules two weeks out will, by itself, move an 87 percent front-end turnover rate. It does support the claim that the *full bundle* — notice plus a guaranteed weekly core plus no on-call — reduces quits among workers past their first six months, and that the effect takes more than a year to appear in the data. Given internal figures showing that a large share of Pinal Ridge's front-end turnover occurs within the first 90 days (Deschamps-Iyer, internal analysis 2026), the company should expect stable scheduling to compress turnover among the retained core rather than to fix early attrition, which the literature attributes more to hiring, onboarding, and job-preview factors (Ton 2014; Batt & Colvin 2011).

### 3.2 Sales and Productivity

The sales evidence rests heavily on a single well-identified result, and honesty requires saying so. The Gap experiment reported sales increases of approximately 7 percent in treated stores and labor productivity gains of about 5 percent (Kesavan et al. 2022; Williams et al. 2018). The proposed mechanisms — better staffing-to-traffic alignment once managers planned further ahead, higher effort and lower absenteeism among workers with predictable lives, and retention of experienced sellers — are plausible and partially supported by the experiment's internal evidence. But the result comes from apparel, where the sales-per-labor-hour production function differs materially from grocery: apparel selling is consultative and conversion-sensitive to floor coverage, whereas grocery front-end labor is largely throughput labor whose contribution to sales operates through queue length, shrink control, and customer defection rather than persuasion.

The grocery-specific evidence is thinner but not absent. Ton's work on retail operations (Ton 2014; Ton & Huckman 2008) links employee turnover to conformance quality — phantom stockouts, misplaced inventory, pricing errors — using data from a large grocery-adjacent retailer, and finds that turnover degrades operational execution in ways that depress sales with a lag; the implication is that scheduling practices affect sales through retention rather than directly. An administrative-data study of a European supermarket chain (van der Berg & Lindqvist 2020) found that stores with more stable schedules had lower shrink and fewer scan errors, though the study is observational and stable schedules may proxy for better store management generally. A quasi-experimental study exploiting staggered rollout of scheduling software in a U.S. grocery chain (Feldman & Okafor 2023) found small positive effects on customer satisfaction scores and null effects on same-store sales over 18 months — a result that should temper extrapolation of the 7 percent apparel figure.

On the negative side of the ledger, one strand of the operations literature argues that scheduling flexibility is itself a productivity tool: just-in-time scheduling exists because it lets managers match labor to demand, and constraining it should mechanically raise labor cost per unit of sales unless offset by the effort and retention channels (Kamalahmadi, Yu & Zhou 2021, modeling and simulation evidence; Bell & Marchetti 2019, a consultancy-adjacent analysis included with caution given its funding). The empirical question is whether the offsets exceed the matching loss. The Gap experiment says yes, in apparel, with demand that is genuinely hard to forecast. Grocery's demand is *more* forecastable — weekly cycles, promotional calendars, and weather effects are well captured by modern forecasting tools — which cuts in two directions for Pinal Ridge: the productivity loss from committing to schedules two weeks out is smaller than in apparel (because two-week-ahead forecasts are good), but the sales upside from better matching is also smaller (because current matching is already decent). The net expected sales effect in grocery is therefore probably positive but modest — likely low single digits or null on same-store sales, with more reliable gains appearing in operational metrics (shrink, scan accuracy, queue abandonment) than in top-line revenue.

### 3.3 Labor Cost

Cost estimates in the literature differ by an order of magnitude, and the divergence is almost entirely definitional rather than empirical. Three cost concepts circulate under the same label.

The first is *mechanical scheduling cost*: the wage bill increase from guaranteed hours that would not otherwise have been scheduled, predictability-pay penalties, and overstaffing during demand troughs. Employer-side estimates in the ordinance debates (Bell & Marchetti 2019; National Retail Federation comment analyses summarized in Marquez & Ostrander 2023) put this at 2 to 4 percent of front-line labor cost, and Pinal Ridge's CFO projection of $1.9 million annually — roughly consistent with a low-single-digit percentage of front-end labor spend — sits within this range and appears methodologically reasonable *as a gross figure*.

The second concept is *net cost after behavioral response*. The ordinance evaluations consistently find that mechanical costs overstate realized costs because employers adapt: they improve forecasting, cross-train staff, build voluntary-standby lists, and reduce their reliance on the on-call shifts that generated penalties (Romich et al. 2020; Pollack et al. 2021). The Gap experiment found the intervention roughly cost-neutral on the wage bill before counting sales effects, because eliminating on-call shifts removed paid-but-unproductive labor and better planning reduced overtime (Williams et al. 2018).

The third concept is *fully loaded net cost*, which subtracts turnover savings and any sales or shrink effects. Here the arithmetic becomes sensitive to assumptions but is worth stating explicitly for Pinal Ridge. Internal data indicate roughly 87 percent annual turnover among front-end clerks at $2,100 per replacement (Deschamps-Iyer, internal analysis 2026). Across the front-end population, total annual replacement cost plausibly runs in the low millions of dollars. If the full stable-scheduling bundle reduced front-end turnover by 15 to 25 percent — the range spanned by the Gap experiment's tenured-worker effects and the long-horizon Seattle estimates (Kesavan et al. 2022; Harknett & Schneider 2024) — the turnover savings alone would offset a substantial fraction, though probably not all, of the projected $1.9 million gross cost. Whether the program is net-positive then turns on the operational and sales channel, which Section 3.2 characterized as modestly positive but uncertain in grocery. Notably, the $2,100 internal replacement-cost figure is conservative relative to the literature: fully loaded estimates that include vacancy productivity loss, supervisor time, and error rates during ramp-up run $3,000 to $5,000 for grocery front-end roles (Boushey & Glynn 2012; Ton 2014). If the true figure is nearer the literature's, the offset arithmetic improves accordingly.

One cost-side finding deserves separate emphasis because it is the literature's most consistent warning: **several studies document employers responding to scheduling mandates by cutting total hours.** Ananat, Gassman-Pines & Fitz-Henley (2022) found hour reductions among some covered workers after ordinance enactment; the Oregon evaluation found modest workweek compression in food service (Marquez & Ostrander 2023); and Shift Project survey data show that in ordinance cities a minority of workers report both more predictable and *fewer* hours (Schneider & Harknett 2019a; Harknett, Schneider & Irwin 2021). The mechanism is straightforward: when hours become contractually stickier, risk-averse managers schedule to the low end of expected demand. This is a managed risk rather than a fatal objection — it arises chiefly where guarantees attach penalties to hour reductions, and voluntary programs can design around it — but any Pinal Ridge pilot should track total hours per worker as a primary outcome, because a program that stabilizes schedules while shrinking paychecks will fail on both retention and worker-welfare grounds.

### 3.4 Worker Outcomes: Health, Income Volatility, and Caregiving

The worker-outcome literature is the most internally consistent of the four domains, and it is consistently favorable to stable scheduling. Survey-based studies linking schedule characteristics to well-being find that unpredictable and on-call scheduling is associated with psychological distress, poor sleep quality, and work-family conflict at magnitudes comparable to or exceeding the associations with low wages (Schneider & Harknett 2019b; Harknett, Schneider & Luhr 2021). The Seattle evaluation found causal improvements in sleep quality and overall well-being attributable to the ordinance (Harknett, Schneider & Irwin 2021), one of the few worker-health outcomes in this literature with quasi-experimental identification. Studies of income volatility (Morduch & Schneider 2017; Finnigan 2018) document that week-to-week earnings swings among hourly retail workers are large — commonly 30 percent or more month to month — and that hour volatility, not wage rates, drives most of it, with downstream effects on food insecurity, missed bill payments, and childcare instability (Ananat & Gassman-Pines 2021, which found last-minute schedule changes predicted same-week child behavioral problems among service workers' children).

Part-time workers with caregiving obligations — a group that describes a large share of Pinal Ridge's 61-percent part-time workforce — appear almost exclusively in the qualitative literature, and this is a significant evidentiary gap discussed in Section 5. What the qualitative work shows is nonetheless coherent across sites and authors: caregivers describe schedule unpredictability as incompatible with childcare arrangements that require booking days or weeks ahead, describe on-call shifts as forcing a choice between forfeiting income and forfeiting childcare reliability, and describe quitting not as preference but as the collapse of an unsustainable logistics problem (Henly & Lambert 2014; Carrillo et al. 2017; Luhr, Schneider & Harknett 2022). Two qualitative studies in grocery specifically (Ochoa & Bettencourt 2019; Ramirez-Whitfield 2021) add a detail relevant to program design: workers valued a guaranteed hour floor more than advance notice per se, because a predictable-but-small paycheck still forced second jobs whose own schedules then collided with the first — a finding that supports including the guaranteed weekly core in the Pinal Ridge pilot rather than piloting notice alone.

The worker-outcome evidence matters to the business case in two ways beyond its intrinsic importance. First, the well-being effects are the most plausible mechanism behind the lagged retention effects documented in Section 3.1; the health and sleep improvements are the leading indicator, turnover the lagging one. Second, in a labor market where Pinal Ridge competes for hourly workers against warehouses, fulfillment centers, and gig platforms, schedule quality is a recruitment differentiator with documented value: stated-preference experiments find hourly workers willing to accept wage discounts of roughly 20 percent to avoid employer-driven schedule variability (Mas & Pallais 2017), implying that stable scheduling functions as compensation the company can offer at a cost below its value to workers.

## 4. Why the Literature Splits: A Methodological Accounting

The apparent contradictions in this literature resolve substantially once five methodological fault lines are made explicit.

**Bundle versus component.** Field experiments evaluate full bundles chosen and implemented by a motivated employer; ordinance studies evaluate the specific components a legislature enacted, minus noncompliance. The Gap experiment's bundle included hour targets; Seattle's ordinance emphasized notice and predictability pay. Since the qualitative evidence identifies hour adequacy as the binding constraint for caregivers, bundles including hour guarantees should — and do — outperform notice-only interventions on retention. Studies are not measuring the same treatment.

**Intent-to-treat versus treatment-on-treated.** Ordinance evaluations estimate the effect of a law, diluted by documented noncompliance rates of 30 to 60 percent in early years (Schneider & Harknett 2019a). Field experiments estimate the effect of actually receiving stable schedules. A voluntary corporate pilot with committed store management resembles the latter; ordinance null results therefore understate what Pinal Ridge should expect from a well-implemented program, though they fairly warn about implementation drift across 61 stores.

**Outcome horizon.** Turnover effects appear at 24 to 60 months in the studies that follow workers long enough (Harknett & Schneider 2024; Kesavan et al. 2022); most ordinance evaluations stop at 24 months. Sleep and well-being effects appear within months. Studies with short windows find worker-outcome effects and null turnover effects; this is a horizon artifact, not a substantive contradiction.

**Sector composition.** Pooled retail samples mix grocery with apparel and food service. Where studies disaggregate, grocery shows smaller sales effects (demand already forecastable), smaller mechanical costs (fewer on-call practices to begin with in many chains), and — suggestively — larger retention effects, possibly because grocery's workforce skews older and more caregiving-constrained (Lambert, Fugiel & Henly 2014; Marquez & Ostrander 2023; Feldman & Okafor 2023).

**Selection into treatment.** Administrative-data studies comparing stable-schedule stores to unstable ones within chains confound scheduling with management quality: good managers produce both stable schedules and good outcomes. Studies without design-based identification (van der Berg & Lindqvist 2020, and much of the operations literature) should be read as upper bounds. Conversely, employer-funded cost projections that count mechanical cost while ignoring behavioral adaptation and turnover offsets (Bell & Marchetti 2019) should be read as upper bounds on cost.

Once these fault lines are applied, a reasonably coherent picture emerges: full-bundle stable scheduling, well implemented, in a grocery-like setting, over a horizon of two or more years, produces meaningful retention gains among tenured workers, near-term worker well-being gains, modest or null same-store sales effects, and net costs well below mechanical projections — with a real but manageable risk of hours compression if managers over-hedge against guarantee obligations.

## 5. Gaps in the Evidence

Five gaps bear directly on Pinal Ridge's decision and should temper confidence in any projection.

**No randomized evaluation exists in grocery.** The single retail field experiment is in apparel. Everything grocery-specific is quasi-experimental or observational. The 7 percent sales figure that circulates in advocacy for stable scheduling should not be transported to grocery without heavy discounting.

**Part-time caregivers are studied qualitatively but almost never quantitatively.** The population most theoretically responsive to stable scheduling — and the majority of Pinal Ridge's workforce — has no experimental effect-size estimates attached to it. Existing quantitative samples skew toward full-time and mixed-status workers.

**Small and mid-sized regional chains are absent.** The experimental evidence comes from a national retailer with sophisticated corporate infrastructure; the ordinance evidence covers all covered employers but rarely disaggregates by firm size. Whether a 61-store chain's scheduling, forecasting, and management capacity can replicate large-firm implementation is unstudied. Franchise and small-employer evidence hints that implementation capacity is a binding constraint (Pollack et al. 2021).

**Interaction with tight labor markets and modern scheduling software is unstudied.** Most of the corpus reflects 2015–2022 labor markets and earlier-generation workforce-management tools. Whether AI-assisted forecasting shrinks the cost of two-week commitments — plausibly a large effect — has not been evaluated.

**Long-run general-equilibrium effects are unknown.** No study convincingly addresses whether stable scheduling changes who applies — compositional improvements in applicant pools are theorized (Mas & Pallais 2017) but not measured in field settings — or whether early adopters gain a durable recruiting advantage that erodes if practices diffuse.

## 6. Implications for Pinal Ridge Markets

Read as a whole, the evidence supports proceeding with the twelve-store pilot, with five design conditions that the literature indicates will determine whether the pilot succeeds and whether the company learns anything from it.

**First, pilot the full bundle, not notice alone.** The retention evidence favors bundles including a guaranteed weekly core; the qualitative grocery evidence identifies hour adequacy as the binding constraint for the part-time caregivers who make up most of the workforce. The proposed design — two-week posting, guaranteed core, no on-call — matches the treatment that produced positive results, and should not be diluted to reduce cost, because a diluted treatment is precisely what the null ordinance results evaluate.

**Second, plan and budget on a two-to-three-year horizon.** The literature's turnover effects are lagging; a pilot judged at twelve months will likely show well-being and operational gains but not yet turnover gains, and killing it on that basis would repeat the horizon error that makes the ordinance literature look more negative than it is. The January capital meeting should approve the pilot with pre-committed evaluation points at 12, 24, and 36 months and pre-specified outcomes at each.

**Third, treat total hours per worker as a guarded primary metric.** The most credible adverse finding in the literature is hours compression. Store managers should be given explicit guidance that the guarantee is a floor to schedule *toward*, not a liability to schedule *away from*, and district-level review should flag any store where average part-time hours fall post-implementation.

**Fourth, evaluate the pilot as an experiment, not a rollout preview.** With 61 stores, Pinal Ridge can match or randomize the twelve pilot stores against comparable controls on volume, turnover history, and market. The extraction file's most consistent lesson is that observational comparisons confound scheduling with management quality; the company should not build a $1.9 million chainwide decision on a confounded pilot when clean design is nearly free. Primary outcomes should be 90-day and 12-month retention (separately — the literature predicts effects on the latter, not the former), total hours per worker, labor cost per store, shrink and scan accuracy, and a short quarterly worker survey covering schedule predictability, sleep, and work-family conflict, mirroring validated Shift Project instruments so results are benchmarkable.

**Fifth, hold the cost projection to a net standard.** The $1.9 million figure is a defensible gross mechanical estimate, but the literature indicates realized net cost will be materially lower after behavioral adaptation and turnover offsets — plausibly by half or more — while conceding that the sales upside documented in apparel should not be assumed in grocery. The honest financial framing for the capital meeting is: gross cost near the CFO's figure; probable net cost substantially lower; principal quantified benefit through retention of tenured staff; sales effect uncertain and likely modest; worker well-being effect well established and strategically valuable in recruitment.

The literature does not guarantee this program will pay for itself. It does establish that the practices are operationally feasible, that their costs are routinely overestimated ex ante, that their benefits are real but slow and concentrated among exactly the experienced workers Pinal Ridge most needs to keep, and that a well-designed pilot is the only way to resolve the grocery-specific, firm-size-specific uncertainties the published evidence cannot. On that basis, the pilot is supported.
