## 1. Purpose and Approach of This Report

This report presents the agency's third-quarter results across all five program lines, the consolidated financial position at three quarters, the agency-wide staffing picture, the status of the state corrective action plan at the Petersburg site, and the outlook for the fourth quarter and the federal grant reduction that takes effect October 1.

At the direction of the Chief Executive and the Chief Financial Officer, this quarter's report is presented in full rather than by exception. The prior quarter's report followed an exception format, surfacing only variances that crossed a materiality threshold. Several trustees noted afterward that the format left them without context for judging whether a flagged variance was serious or routine, and without early visibility into conditions that had been developing for some time before they crossed a threshold. Two program lines that appear in this report with significant problems — behavioral health counseling and emergency housing — showed early indicators in the second quarter that did not meet the exception threshold and therefore did not reach the committee.

The full-presentation format means the committee will read about program lines that are performing as expected alongside those that are not. Early literacy met every target this quarter and is reported here at the same level of detail as youth workforce, which did not. This is deliberate. A line that meets its targets provides the comparison point that makes an underperforming line legible, and in early literacy's case the line that is performing well is also the line facing the largest external threat.

Each program section below follows a common structure: results against target, the explanation for those results, and the implications the committee should carry into the fourth quarter. Financial data appears in tables where tabular presentation is clearer. Causal explanation appears in prose, because the causes this quarter are not reducible to line items.

## 3. Youth Workforce

### 3.1 Results

Youth workforce served 1,340 participants against a full-year target of 1,800, placing the line at 74 percent of target with one quarter remaining. Prorated against the fiscal calendar, the line should have reached approximately 1,350 participants by the close of the third quarter to remain on pace, so enrollment volume is marginally behind pace rather than substantially so. The line will not reach 1,800 without a fourth-quarter enrollment substantially above any quarter recorded this year, and the working expectation is a year-end figure between 1,650 and 1,720.

The more significant result is the placement rate. Forty-seven percent of participants who completed the program entered employment, down from 58 percent in the comparable period a year ago — a decline of 11 percentage points, or roughly a fifth of the prior rate.

| Youth Workforce Measure | FY2026 Q3 YTD | FY2025 Q3 YTD | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Participants enrolled | 1,340 | 1,412 | −72 |
| Full-year enrollment target | 1,800 | 1,800 | — |
| Percent of target | 74% | 78% | −4 pts |
| Job placement rate | 47% | 58% | −11 pts |
| Participants placed | 630 | 819 | −189 |

The absolute number of young people who entered employment through this program fell from 819 to 630, a decline of 189 placements. That figure — not the percentage — is the one worth carrying, because it represents the practical difference in outcome between this year and last.

### 3.2 Explanation

The decline in placement rate is attributable to three factors that operate together.

**Employer partner attrition in the hospitality and retail sectors.** The program's placement pipeline has historically depended on a concentrated set of employer partners, with the largest six accounting for slightly more than half of all placements. Two of those six substantially reduced entry-level hiring during the second and third quarters, and a third, a regional hospitality operator, closed two Hampton Roads locations in February. The program's placement staff have been rebuilding the employer base since January, but new employer relationships have a lag of roughly one to two quarters between first contact and first placement, so the rebuilding work done this quarter will show in results next fiscal year rather than this one.

**A shift in participant profile.** Referral sources changed composition this year, with a larger share arriving from justice-involved and foster-transition pathways than in prior years. These participants have historically placed at lower rates and required longer service durations, and the program's service model was not adjusted to reflect the change. This is a planning failure rather than a participant failure: the program accepted a harder-to-place population without extending the service window or adding the supports that population requires.

**Reduced placement staff capacity.** The program lost one of four placement specialists in November and did not refill the position until March, operating at three-quarters of designed placement capacity for the bulk of the quarter. Placement specialists carry the employer relationships, and a vacant specialist position means dormant employer accounts.

### 3.3 Implications for the Fourth Quarter

The placement rate will not recover materially by June 30. Placements completing in the fourth quarter are drawn from a cohort already enrolled and already working with the employer base as it currently stands. Management's expectation is a full-year placement rate between 47 and 50 percent.

The program has begun three corrective steps: an employer development push targeting healthcare support, warehousing, and skilled trades to reduce dependence on hospitality and retail; a revised service model that extends the standard service window from 90 to 150 days for participants entering through justice-involved and foster-transition pathways; and a redesign of the enrollment forecast to reflect realistic conversion rather than historical assumption. None of these will change fiscal 2026 results. All three are relevant to the fiscal 2027 budget the committee will consider in June.

The committee should expect a proposed downward revision to the fiscal 2027 youth workforce enrollment target. Holding 1,800 as a target when the program has not exceeded 1,450 in two years produces the appearance of failure where the real problem is a target set without reference to capacity.

## 4. Adult Reentry Employment

### 4.1 Results

Adult reentry employment served 410 participants against a target of 400 and held its job placement rate at 63 percent, unchanged from the prior year. On the face of the numbers this is the agency's steadiest performing line: volume slightly above target, outcome flat, no variance requiring explanation.

The numbers conceal a staffing situation that makes the results fragile.

| Adult Reentry Measure | FY2026 Q3 YTD | FY2025 Q3 YTD | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Participants served | 410 | 396 | +14 |
| Job placement rate | 63% | 63% | — |
| Case manager FTE (end of period) | 4 | 6 | −2 |
| Average caseload per manager | 71 | 47 | +24 |
| Senior case managers | 0 | 2 | −2 |

### 4.2 Explanation

Both senior case managers resigned in February, within three weeks of each other. One left for a state agency position with a salary approximately 18 percent above the agency's rate for the role; the other cited caseload volume and the absence of a clinical supervision structure. Neither position has been filled. The remaining four case managers, all with less than two years of tenure in the role, absorbed the departing caseloads.

Average caseload now stands at 71 participants per case manager. The program's own service model specifies a caseload of 45, and the standard used by comparable reentry programs regionally falls between 40 and 50. The program is operating at approximately 155 percent of its designed caseload with a staff whose average experience is materially below what it was six months ago.

The reason placement outcomes have not yet declined is that placements recorded in the third quarter overwhelmingly reflect relationships and case plans established in the first and second quarters, when the senior case managers were still in place. Reentry placement has a long lag; the work that produces a March placement typically began in October or November. The third-quarter placement rate is therefore a measure of first-half staffing, not of current staffing.

This is the clearest example in this report of why the exception-based format was inadequate. Adult reentry would not have appeared in an exception report this quarter. Every reported metric is at or above target. The condition that will produce next quarter's problem is invisible in this quarter's outcome data.

### 4.3 Implications for the Fourth Quarter

Management expects the placement rate to decline in the fourth quarter and more sharply in the first quarter of fiscal 2027, as the cohort served under 71-participant caseloads reaches its placement window. A decline to the mid-fifties by the close of the first quarter of fiscal 2027 is the planning assumption.

Three actions are underway. Both senior case manager positions have been reposted at a salary band raised approximately 12 percent, which the Chief Financial Officer has approved within existing program salary savings from the vacancy period. A contract case manager has been engaged through June to reduce average caseload to approximately 57 for the fourth quarter. And the program has triaged its caseload into intensive and maintenance tiers so that the highest-risk participants retain contact frequency at the cost of reduced contact for lower-risk participants — a defensible allocation under the circumstances, but one that will show up as reduced service intensity in the program's own quality metrics.

The committee should understand the salary band adjustment as a structural finding rather than a one-time fix. The agency lost these staff to a state agency and to caseload burden. The first is a compensation problem and the second is a staffing-ratio problem, and both will recur in fiscal 2027 unless addressed in the budget rather than in vacancy savings.

## 5. Emergency Housing

### 5.1 Results

Emergency housing has expended $4.9 million against a full-year budget of $4.2 million, an overrun of $700,000, or 16.7 percent, with one quarter remaining. This is the agency's largest single financial variance and the principal driver of the consolidated position described in Section 8.

| Emergency Housing Measure | FY2026 Q3 YTD | Budget / Prior Year | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total expenditure | $4.9M | $4.2M (full-year budget) | +$700K / +16.7% |
| Motel voucher unit cost | +34% vs. prior year | — | +34% |
| Average length of stay (nights) | 66 | 41 | +25 nights / +61% |
| Implied capacity effect | — | — | −38% throughput |

Two operational drivers produced the overrun, and they compound rather than merely add.

### 5.2 Explanation

**Motel voucher costs rose 34 percent.** The regional motel market tightened considerably over the past year. Two properties the program used routinely converted to extended-stay operations at higher nightly rates, and a third stopped accepting voucher placements entirely. Remaining properties raised rates in response to reduced regional supply. The program has no contractual rate protection; placements are made at prevailing nightly rates, which means the agency absorbs market movement in full and immediately.

**Average length of stay rose from 41 nights to 66 nights.** This is a 61 percent increase and is the larger of the two drivers. Length of stay is governed by exit availability — how quickly a sheltered household can move into permanent or transitional housing. Regional permanent supportive housing units have not expanded, the local rapid rehousing program reduced its subsidy depth in the fall, and market-rate rents in the service area continued to rise. Households that would have exited in six weeks a year ago now remain in shelter for more than nine.

The compounding effect matters and should be stated plainly. A 34 percent increase in nightly cost applied to a 61 percent increase in nights produces a per-household cost increase of approximately 116 percent — the two multiply. The program is spending more than twice as much to serve a household to exit as it did a year ago. It has held total expenditure to a 16.7 percent overrun only by serving substantially fewer households, since each occupied unit is tied up for two-thirds longer. The overrun understates the operational strain, because the budget line absorbed part of the pressure by reducing throughput rather than by spending more.

The households not served because of reduced throughput do not appear anywhere in this report's figures. They are the unmeasured cost of the variance, and the committee should hold that in mind when weighing the $700,000 against the alternative.

### 5.3 Implications for the Fourth Quarter

Fourth-quarter expenditure at the current run rate would add approximately $1.6 million, producing a full-year total near $6.5 million against a $4.2 million budget — an overrun approaching $2.3 million. Management does not intend to allow that outcome and is pursuing four measures.

First, negotiated block-rate agreements with two motel operators, in discussion since March, would provide rate certainty for approximately 40 percent of placement volume at roughly 12 percent below current prevailing rates. Second, a housing navigation position redeployed from vacancy savings elsewhere is dedicated exclusively to exit planning for households past 45 nights, targeting reduction of average stay toward 55 nights by fiscal year end. Third, the program has instituted a weekly length-of-stay review for households past 60 nights, with the program director personally reviewing each case. Fourth, the agency has opened discussions with the regional Continuum of Care and two municipal partners regarding cost-sharing for the extended-stay population, since the exit bottleneck is a system-level condition rather than an agency-level one.

Even with all four measures effective, management projects full-year expenditure between $6.0 and $6.2 million, an overrun of $1.8 to $2.0 million. The committee should treat that as the planning figure. Section 8 addresses how it is absorbed.

The strategic question for the committee is not whether emergency housing overspent. It is whether a program whose cost per household has doubled because of conditions entirely outside the agency's control should continue at current scale, at reduced scale with a defined budget cap, or at current scale with a deliberate board decision to fund the overrun from reserves as a response to regional conditions. Management does not recommend that this question be resolved on May 12, but recommends it be framed then and resolved in the fiscal 2027 budget discussion in June.

## 6. Behavioral Health Counseling

### 6.1 Results

Behavioral health counseling billed $2.1 million in Medicaid revenue against $2.8 million projected, a shortfall of $700,000, or 25 percent.

| Behavioral Health Measure | FY2026 Q3 YTD | Projected / Prior Year | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medicaid revenue billed | $2.1M | $2.8M | −$700K / −25% |
| Clinician positions, budgeted | 11 | 11 | — |
| Clinician positions, filled | 8 | 11 | −3 |
| Positions vacant, full quarter | 3 | 0 | +3 |
| Revenue per filled clinician | $263K | $255K | +$8K |

The final row is the one that carries the explanation. Revenue per filled clinician rose slightly year over year. The eight clinicians in place are billing at or above expected productivity. The shortfall is not a productivity problem or a billing problem; it is a headcount problem, and it is close to arithmetically complete as an explanation.

Three vacancies out of eleven represent 27 percent of clinical capacity. The revenue shortfall is 25 percent. The two figures track closely enough that the vacancies account for essentially the entire variance.

### 6.2 Explanation

All three positions were vacant for the full quarter, not partially. Two became vacant in the first quarter and one in December, and none has been filled as of this report's issue date. The agency has conducted searches continuously since November.

The recruitment difficulty is regional and structural. Licensed clinical social workers and licensed professional counselors in Hampton Roads are in demand from hospital systems, private group practices, and telehealth platforms, all of which pay above the agency's range. Private practice and telehealth also offer schedule autonomy the agency's service model cannot match, since agency clinicians serve clients with complex needs requiring coordination, documentation, and no-show absorption that a private caseload does not.

A secondary factor is licensure supervision. The agency has capacity to supervise pre-licensure clinicians, which would open a much larger candidate pool, but supervision capacity currently sits with the clinical director, whose administrative load has grown as vacancies increased. This produces a self-reinforcing cycle: vacancies raise the clinical director's administrative burden, which reduces supervision capacity, which narrows the candidate pool, which extends the vacancies.

### 6.3 Implications for the Fourth Quarter

If all three positions were filled on July 1 — an optimistic assumption given search history — the revenue effect would not appear until the second quarter of fiscal 2027, since new clinicians require six to ten weeks to build a full caseload and Medicaid billing lags service delivery by four to six weeks. Management's assumption is that fourth-quarter revenue will remain near the third-quarter run rate, producing full-year Medicaid revenue between $2.7 and $2.8 million against approximately $3.7 million projected.

Three actions are underway. The agency has engaged a specialized behavioral health recruiter on contingency, at a cost the Chief Financial Officer has approved against the salary savings the vacancies themselves have generated. It has proposed a licensure supervision stipend and protected supervision time for the clinical director, funded within the program line, which would allow recruitment of pre-licensure candidates at a lower salary point with a defined path to full licensure. And it has commissioned a compensation benchmarking study for clinical roles, to report in advance of the fiscal 2027 budget.

The committee should understand that the salary savings from these vacancies partially offset the revenue shortfall. Three unfilled clinician positions represent approximately $210,000 in unspent salary and benefits over three quarters. The net effect on the agency's bottom line is therefore closer to $490,000 than to $700,000. This does not make the shortfall acceptable — the missing revenue represents clients not served — but it is material to the consolidated financial picture and is reflected in Section 8.

## 7. Early Literacy

### 7.1 Results

Early literacy enrolled 620 children against a target of 600 and met every program target for the quarter: enrollment, attendance, assessment completion, family engagement contacts, and the two developmental outcome measures the funder requires. It is the smallest program line at $1.6 million, approximately 5 percent of agency budget, and operates within budget.

| Early Literacy Measure | FY2026 Q3 YTD | Target | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Children enrolled | 620 | 600 | Met (+3%) |
| Attendance rate | Above threshold | Funder threshold | Met |
| Assessment completion | Above threshold | Funder threshold | Met |
| Family engagement contacts | Above threshold | Funder threshold | Met |
| Developmental outcome measures (2) | Both above threshold | Funder thresholds | Met |
| Budget performance | Within budget | $1.6M | Met |

### 7.2 Explanation

The line performs because of three conditions that are worth naming, since they are the conditions the agency will need to protect.

The program has the agency's most stable staff. Turnover in early literacy over the past twelve months was approximately 9 percent, against 29 percent agency-wide. The site coordinator has been in place four years and the lead educators average more than three years' tenure. Program continuity in early childhood work depends heavily on relationships with families, and stable staff produce stable enrollment.

Its service model is well matched to its funding. The federal grant supports a defined model with defined ratios, and the program operates that model as designed rather than stretching it to serve more children than it is funded to serve. This is a discipline the agency has not maintained uniformly across other lines.

Its outcome measures are proximate to its service. Early literacy measures things that happen inside the program — attendance, assessment completion, developmental gains observed by program staff. It does not depend on external labor markets, external housing supply, or external payer behavior. Youth workforce depends on employers hiring. Emergency housing depends on housing existing. Behavioral health depends on Medicaid reimbursement and a clinical labor market. Early literacy depends principally on the agency doing its own work well.

The committee should draw a limited lesson from this. Early literacy is not better managed than the other lines. It is less exposed. The comparison is useful for understanding what conditions produce reliable performance, not for assigning credit.

### 7.3 The October 1 Grant Reduction

The federal grant supporting this line drops by $600,000 effective October 1, 2026 — the start of the second quarter of fiscal 2027. Against a $1.6 million program budget, this is a 37.5 percent reduction.

The reduction results from a formula change at the federal level, not from any performance issue. The agency's performance is strong and its reporting is current. This is not a finding and cannot be appealed on programmatic grounds.

The consequences are not proportional to the reduction. Early literacy's cost structure is heavily fixed: classroom space, ratio-driven staffing, and a required curriculum and assessment package. Approximately 70 percent of the budget is personnel operating at funder-mandated ratios that cannot be relaxed without losing compliance. Reducing the budget by 37.5 percent cannot be accomplished by trimming; it requires removing whole units of service.

Management has modeled three scenarios for the committee's consideration in June.

**Scenario A — Proportional service reduction.** Reduce enrollment from 620 to approximately 390 children, closing two classroom units and reducing staff accordingly. Preserves model fidelity and compliance for remaining children. Eliminates service for roughly 230 children and reduces staffing by approximately five positions. Fully closes the gap.

**Scenario B — Partial backfill with diversified funding.** Reduce enrollment to approximately 500, closing one classroom unit, and raise $250,000 to $300,000 from local philanthropic and municipal sources to preserve the remainder. Requires successful fundraising on a timeline of roughly four months, with a defined fallback to Scenario A if targets are not met by August 15. Preserves more service; carries execution risk.

**Scenario C — Cross-subsidy from agency unrestricted funds.** Maintain current enrollment and absorb the reduction from agency unrestricted resources. Preserves all service. Requires $600,000 annually from a fund balance already under pressure from the emergency housing overrun, and is not sustainable beyond one year without a permanent revenue solution.

Management's preliminary recommendation is Scenario B with a firm fallback date, on the reasoning that the program's performance record makes it a credible fundraising case and that a defined decision point prevents open-ended exposure. The Chief Executive has asked that the committee treat this as a framing discussion on May 12 and a decision in June, since the fundraising timeline requires a decision no later than mid-June for Scenario B to remain viable.

The committee should note the timing interaction. This decision must be made while the emergency housing overrun is still being absorbed. The agency's strongest program line requires investment at precisely the moment its weakest financial position constrains capacity to invest. That collision is the central strategic tension in this report.

The agency's $2 million line of credit remains undrawn. Management does not currently project a need to draw on it, but the Chief Financial Officer has recommended, and the Chief Executive has agreed, that the committee be informed now rather than at the point of drawing. Whether the committee wishes to formally recognize the emergency housing overrun through a budget amendment at the June meeting, or to carry it as a variance through year end. Whether the committee wishes to set a spending cap on emergency housing for the fourth quarter, and if so at what level, recognizing that a cap translates directly into households not sheltered.

Whether the committee wishes management to prepare the fiscal 2027 budget on the assumption that reserves absorb the fiscal 2026 deficit, or on the assumption that fiscal 2027 must include partial replenishment. This choice materially affects every program line's fiscal 2027 target.

## 9. Staffing

### 9.1 The Agency-Wide Picture

Agency-wide staff turnover ran 29 percent annualized in the third quarter. Against 214 staff, this implies approximately 62 separations over a twelve-month period at the current rate. The agency's five-year average is 19 percent, and the regional nonprofit benchmark for comparable human services organizations is 22 to 25 percent.

Turnover is not evenly distributed.

| Program Line / Function | Approx. Headcount | Annualized Turnover | vs. Agency Avg. |
|---|---|---|---|
| Behavioral Health Counseling | 34 | 38% | +9 pts |
| Adult Reentry Employment | 26 | 35% | +6 pts |
| Emergency Housing | 41 | 33% | +4 pts |
| Youth Workforce | 48 | 27% | −2 pts |
| Administration & Shared Services | 33 | 21% | −8 pts |
| Early Literacy | 32 | 9% | −20 pts |

The pattern is legible. Turnover is highest in lines with the highest caseloads, the greatest exposure to client crisis, and the largest gap between agency compensation and external market compensation for the same credential. It is lowest in the line with stable ratios, a defined model, and predictable working conditions.

### 9.2 Causes

Exit interview data from the past three quarters identifies four recurring themes, in descending order of frequency.

**Compensation.** Cited in 61 percent of exit interviews. Clinical and case management roles are the most exposed, with departing staff in these roles frequently moving to state agencies, hospital systems, or private practice at 12 to 20 percent higher salary. Compensation was cited in only 22 percent of early literacy separations, which are predominantly relocations and retirements.

**Caseload and workload.** Cited in 47 percent. This theme is concentrated in adult reentry, emergency housing, and behavioral health, and correlates directly with the caseload conditions described in Sections 4, 5, and 6.

**Supervision and support.** Cited in 33 percent. Departing staff describe supervisors stretched across too many direct reports, infrequent supervision sessions, and limited access to clinical consultation. This theme has grown as vacancies at the supervisory level increased.

**Advancement.** Cited in 24 percent. Staff describe limited internal mobility, particularly in programs with flat structures where the only advancement path is into the single supervisory role.

These causes reinforce one another. A departure raises caseloads for those remaining, which increases workload strain, which raises the probability of further departure. Supervisory vacancies reduce support, which accelerates line-staff turnover, which increases supervisory workload. Both adult reentry and behavioral health are currently inside this cycle.

### 9.3 Response

The agency has taken four actions.

A compensation benchmarking study covering clinical, case management, and supervisory roles is underway and will report before the fiscal 2027 budget. Preliminary findings suggest the agency is competitive at entry level and progressively less competitive at experienced and supervisory levels — consistent with the observed pattern of losing senior staff while retaining junior staff.

Caseload standards have been formally documented for every direct service role, with a monthly report to the Chief Executive showing actual against standard by program. This does not by itself reduce caseloads but ensures the condition is visible before it produces outcome effects — a direct response to the adult reentry situation described in Section 4.

Supervisory span of control has been capped at eight direct reports, with two programs currently in exception and required to submit remediation plans by June 30.

An internal advancement framework defining lead-worker and specialist tracks in each program is in development, to allow advancement without requiring movement into supervision.

Management does not represent these as sufficient. The compensation gap is the dominant driver and cannot be closed within the current expense structure while the agency runs an operating deficit. The committee will face a genuine tradeoff in June: closing the compensation gap materially would cost an estimated $900,000 to $1.2 million annually, which the agency does not currently have. Continuing at 29 percent turnover carries its own costs — recruitment, training, lost productivity, and the outcome degradation now becoming visible in adult reentry — which management estimates conservatively at $700,000 to $900,000 annually and which are largely invisible because they are distributed across program budgets rather than reported as a line item.

### 10.2 Findings and Causes

The 22 files represented approximately 14 percent of open cases at the site at the time of the visit. The deficiencies were documentation deficiencies rather than service deficiencies: missing signatures on service plans, assessments completed but not uploaded within the required window, and incomplete eligibility re-verification documentation. The state's report did not identify any instance in which a client received inappropriate service or in which service was delivered without a corresponding plan.

This distinction is important for the committee's understanding, but it should not be used to minimize the finding. Documentation failures are compliance failures, they place funding at risk, and repeat findings escalate consequences substantially. The state's letter noted that a second finding on the same standard would trigger enhanced monitoring and could affect contract renewal.

The site's own root cause analysis, which the state accepted, identified three factors. The site experienced 40 percent turnover in the twelve months preceding the visit, well above even the elevated agency average, meaning a substantial share of files were held by staff with less than six months' tenure. The site operated without a permanent supervisor for four months in the fall, during which file review did not occur. And the agency's case management system does not generate automated alerts for documentation deadlines, so compliance depended entirely on individual staff tracking and supervisory review — a design that fails predictably when either is disrupted.

The third factor is agency-wide, not Petersburg-specific. The same system limitation exists at every site. Petersburg was found because Petersburg was monitored.

### 10.3 Ms. Pham's Response and Management's Assessment

Ms. Pham assumed direction of the site in November, approximately eight weeks before the monitoring visit, inheriting the conditions that produced the findings. Her response has been the strongest element of this situation. She completed a full audit of all open files rather than only the 22 cited, identifying and remediating an additional 14 files with deficiencies the state had not reached. She implemented a weekly supervisory file review before the corrective action plan required it. And she requested, and the agency approved, a temporary quality assurance position through September to sustain compliance while the site's staffing stabilizes.

Management's assessment is that Petersburg's compliance risk is now materially lower than it was in January, and lower than at two other sites that have not been recently monitored. Management has therefore extended Petersburg's file review protocol agency-wide effective May 1, and has moved a case management system enhancement adding automated documentation deadline alerts into the fiscal 2027 technology request at an estimated cost of $45,000.

### 10.4 What the Committee Should Watch

The state follow-up visit is expected in June or July. Management's expectation is release from the plan in the first quarter of fiscal 2027, but this is not assured and depends on findings at follow-up.

The larger risk is not Petersburg. It is that the conditions producing the Petersburg findings — high turnover, supervisory gaps, and a case management system without automated compliance safeguards — exist across the agency. The agency-wide file review protocol addresses the third factor partially and the second not at all. A monitoring visit at another site during a supervisory vacancy would likely produce comparable findings.

### 11.2 The Three Things the Committee Should Carry Forward

**The fiscal 2026 deficit is real and will consume roughly a third of reserves.** A $2.2 to $2.3 million operating deficit is the working projection, driven primarily by emergency housing cost inflation and secondarily by behavioral health revenue shortfall. Reserves remain above the board floor, but the erosion is substantial and the recovery period is multi-year.

**The staffing situation is the underlying condition beneath most of what appears in this report.** The behavioral health revenue shortfall is a vacancy problem. The adult reentry risk is a caseload problem. The youth workforce placement decline is partly a placement staff capacity problem. The Petersburg findings are a turnover and supervision problem. These present as four separate issues and are substantially one issue. Addressing them line by line will produce four partial responses to a single underlying condition.

**The October 1 grant reduction requires a decision in June, not in September.** Scenario B — the partial backfill option — requires a fundraising campaign launched by late June to have any prospect of reaching target by the August fallback date. A decision deferred to the September meeting is a decision for Scenario A by default, since the alternatives will no longer be executable. Management asks that the committee treat the May discussion as preparation for a June decision and identify at the May meeting what information it requires to decide.

### 11.3 A Note on This Report's Format

This report presented all five program lines in full, including the line that met every target and the line whose reported metrics show no variance at all. The value of that choice is visible in Section 4. Adult reentry would not have appeared in an exception-based report this quarter, because every metric it reports is at or above target. The condition that will produce its fourth-quarter and first-quarter decline — 71-participant caseloads carried by four junior staff — is invisible in outcome data and will remain invisible until the outcomes arrive.

Management recommends that the full-presentation format continue for the fourth-quarter and year-end reports, and that the committee consider adopting it as standing practice. The additional length is real. The alternative is a report that reliably describes the previous quarter's problems and reliably misses the next quarter's.
