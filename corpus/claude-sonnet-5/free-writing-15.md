# On the Weight of Small Rooms

There is a particular quality of light that occurs only in the last twenty minutes before sunset in late summer, when the sun has dropped low enough to slide beneath the awnings and strike the undersides of things — the bottoms of leaves, the underneaths of chins, the backs of hands held up to shield the eyes. It turns everyone briefly gold and briefly young. I have been trying for years to name this light and have failed, so instead I have started collecting the rooms it enters.

There is a kitchen in a house that no longer belongs to my family, where at 7:40 in July this light would come through the window over the sink and land on a particular tile, the third one from the left, chipped in the shape of a country that does not exist. My mother would be standing there not washing dishes, just standing, her hands wet, watching the light do this thing it did. I did not understand then that she was memorizing it. I understand now because I am doing the same thing in a kitchen of my own, in a city she has never seen, to a window that faces the wrong direction for that light to ever reach it.

This is the trouble with small rooms: they hold on to you longer than you hold on to them. You move out, you sell the house, you box up the dishes and give away the piano, and still — some Tuesday, decades later, doing something unrelated, like reaching into a bag for change — a room will surface. Not the whole room. Never the whole room. Just a corner of it, or a sound it used to make, or the particular resistance of a door that stuck in humidity.

I think about my grandmother's bedroom, which I visited perhaps a dozen times in my life and which I could still draw from memory with more accuracy than I could draw my own bedroom from three years ago. The wallpaper had small blue flowers that were not flowers if you looked closely — they were shaped like flowers but the pattern repeated with a kind of error, a stutter every seventh row, as though the person who designed it had lost count and started again slightly wrong. I found this stutter more interesting than anything else in that house. I would lie on the floor and trace the wallpaper with my eyes until I found where the pattern broke, and there was something almost holy in locating the mistake, in knowing that even wallpaper, printed by machines in some factory decades before I was born, contained a flaw that only I seemed to notice or care about.

She has been dead eleven years. The house was sold to people who, I assume, repapered the walls, because why would they keep it — a stranger's grandmother's taste in florals, imperfect ones at that. And yet the flaw in the seventh row exists somewhere still, doesn't it? Not physically. But as information, as a fact that was once true about the world and that I alone, perhaps, still carry: that in a bedroom in a house that no longer holds that wallpaper, the pattern used to stutter.

I don't know what to do with facts like this. They accumulate. I am thirty-something years old and I am becoming, slowly, a warehouse of very small, very specific, entirely unverifiable truths about rooms that no longer exist in the configuration I remember them. The sound the icebox made in my aunt's kitchen — a particular shudder every eleven minutes, I timed it once, bored, at nine years old — is gone, because the icebox is gone, because my aunt is gone, because time did what time does. But I have it. I have the eleven minutes. It sits somewhere in me unused, unusable, taking up a kind of space I cannot name.

Maybe this is what a self is — not memory in the grand sense, not the big scenes, the graduations and the funerals, but this. The chipped tile shaped like a country that doesn't exist. The stutter in the seventh row. Eleven minutes between shudders. A hundred small rooms, each holding a piece of light that will never enter them again in quite that way, each waiting patiently inside you for a Tuesday, for a bag of change, for a reason to surface that has nothing to do with meaning and everything to do with the strange persistence of the specific.

I used to think I would forget these things as I got older. I find instead that they sharpen. The big scenes blur — I could not tell you now, with certainty, what was said at either of my grandmothers' funerals, or in what order the years of my twenties actually occurred. But the tile. The wallpaper. The icebox. These I could swear to in court, though there is no court, though there is no case, though there is no one left to testify to but myself, standing in a kitchen that faces the wrong way, waiting for a light that isn't coming, holding a country that never existed in the shape of a chip in a tile in a house I do not own in a city I no longer live in, gone, gone, and entirely, uselessly, mine.
