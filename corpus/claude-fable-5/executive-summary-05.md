# Executive Summary: Tupelo Preventive Maintenance Overhaul

**Tallapoosa Precision Components — Capital and Continuous Improvement Review Office**
**Prepared by:** Sanjay Mirchandani, Director
**For:** Ekaterina Novikova, VP of Manufacturing, and the Operations Committee
**Date:** September 19, 2025 | **Committee Meeting:** October 3, 2025

## Finding and Recommendation

The Tupelo preventive maintenance overhaul is the strongest capital result in this year's portfolio and one of the strongest this office has reviewed in the last five fiscal years. On an investment of $2.2 million, the program cut unplanned downtime from 11.4 percent to 4.1 percent, raised overall equipment effectiveness from 62 to 78, and removed $3.1 million from annual scrap cost. The program has already returned more than its full cost in a single year, and the returns recur.

This office recommends that the Operations Committee take three actions: formally close the Tupelo program as complete and successful; approve an annual sustainment budget of approximately $600,000 to hold the gains; and direct this office and Plant Manager Dwayne Lockhart to prepare a replication plan for the Dothan, Columbus, and Gadsden plants, with a funding request of roughly $2.4 million per plant to come before the Committee in the first quarter of fiscal 2026. The decision before the Committee is not whether the program worked — it did — but whether Tallapoosa will treat it as a local success or as a template.

## What the Program Was and What It Cost

The overhaul, sponsored by Mr. Lockhart and executed over eighteen months, replaced Tupelo's run-to-failure maintenance culture with a scheduled preventive and predictive regime. The $2.2 million covered condition-monitoring sensors on critical stamping and machining assets, a computerized maintenance management system, spare-parts inventory rationalization, dedicated planner and reliability technician positions, and roughly 4,100 hours of technician training. The program came in on budget and on schedule, which is itself worth noting in a portfolio where two of four programs did neither.

The design was deliberately unglamorous. Rather than pursue a plant-wide transformation at once, Mr. Lockhart's team ranked assets by downtime cost and worked down the list, converting the top thirty machines first, proving the method, and then extending it. That sequencing meant the program was generating measurable savings within its first six months, and it meant that when problems arose — early sensor calibration failures on two press lines, resistance from senior technicians accustomed to reactive work — they were caught and corrected on a small footprint before the approach scaled.

## The Measured Results

The results are verified against production system data, finance-validated scrap records, and customer quality reports, not self-reported program metrics. Three numbers carry the case.

First, unplanned downtime fell from 11.4 percent of scheduled production time to 4.1 percent. At Tupelo's loading, that recovered the equivalent of roughly 5,600 machine hours annually — capacity the plant used to absorb a volume increase from two customers without capital expansion or sustained overtime. Second, overall equipment effectiveness rose from 62 to 78, a sixteen-point gain driven mainly by the availability component but supported by quality and performance improvements as machines stopped drifting out of specification between breakdowns. Third, annual scrap cost fell by $3.1 million, most of it from the elimination of the out-of-tolerance runs that had previously followed unplanned stoppages and hurried restarts.

The financial return is straightforward. Against $2.2 million invested, the plant is generating $3.1 million in annual scrap savings alone, before counting recovered capacity, reduced overtime, and reduced premium freight from missed shipments, which finance estimates conservatively at a further $900,000 to $1.2 million per year. The simple payback was under nine months. No other program in this year's review approaches that performance; the Dothan weld cell, reviewed separately, has consumed more than three times the money and returned negative value.

Two secondary effects deserve the Committee's attention because they bear on replication. Maintenance overtime at Tupelo fell 38 percent, and voluntary turnover among maintenance technicians fell from 19 percent to 8 percent — technicians consistently told our reviewers that planned work is simply better work than perpetual firefighting. Separately, Tupelo's customer quality metrics improved measurably: parts-per-million defect rates on the two largest programs fell by roughly half, which strengthens Tallapoosa's position in upcoming sourcing decisions in a way the scrap number alone does not capture.

## Attribution and Caveats

This office has tested whether the gains belong to the program or to circumstance. They belong to the program. Tupelo's product mix and volumes were broadly stable across the period; no major equipment was replaced outside program scope; and the improvement curve tracks the asset-by-asset conversion schedule closely — machines improved when they were converted, in the order they were converted. The comparison with the other three plants is also instructive: over the same period, downtime at Columbus and Gadsden was flat, and Dothan's worsened.

Two caveats temper, without undermining, the finding. The gains are not self-sustaining. Preventive maintenance regimes decay when planner positions are absorbed into production headcount, when PM schedules are deferred to chase shipments, and when sensor networks go uncalibrated. Industry experience and our own review of Tallapoosa's prior improvement initiatives suggest that without dedicated sustainment funding, roughly a third of the gain erodes within two years. Second, some fraction of the scrap reduction — we estimate no more than $300,000 of the $3.1 million — reflects the concurrent retirement of one chronically failing press that would likely have been replaced regardless. Adjusting for both caveats still leaves the program comfortably the best return in the portfolio.

## The Decision Before the Committee

The Committee faces a continue-or-stop decision with an unusual shape: the original investment is complete, so the question is what it costs to protect and extend the result versus what it costs to walk away from it.

The cost to continue is modest and comes in two parts. Sustainment at Tupelo requires approximately $600,000 annually — the reliability headcount, sensor maintenance and calibration, CMMS licensing, and continued training. Replication at the other three plants would require roughly $2.4 million per plant, slightly above Tupelo's cost because Dothan and Gadsden carry older asset bases; a full three-plant rollout over fiscal 2026 and 2027 would therefore total approximately $7.2 million. If the other plants achieve even 70 percent of Tupelo's result — a conservative planning assumption given that Tupelo started from a worse baseline than Columbus — the enterprise-wide recurring benefit would be in the range of $6 million to $7 million annually, with payback inside eighteen months at each site.

The cost to stop is the quiet erosion of a $3.1 million annual gain. There is no write-off and no visible cancellation; there is simply a plant that drifts back toward 11 percent downtime over two to three years as positions are reabsorbed and schedules slip, at an eventual cost of $1 million or more per year in returned scrap and lost capacity. Declining to replicate carries a further opportunity cost: the three untreated plants continue to run at OEE levels in the low-to-mid 60s while carrying the same customer pricing pressure Tupelo faces.

This office's judgment is that the sustainment funding is not optional if the Committee wishes to keep what it has paid for, and that replication is the single highest-confidence use of improvement capital available to the company in fiscal 2026. We recommend the Committee approve the sustainment budget at the October 3 meeting and direct preparation of the replication proposal, with Dothan sequenced first for reasons set out in the weld cell summary that follows.

# Executive Summary: Dothan Robotic Weld Cell

**Tallapoosa Precision Components — Capital and Continuous Improvement Review Office**
**Prepared by:** Sanjay Mirchandani, Director
**For:** Ekaterina Novikova, VP of Manufacturing, and the Operations Committee
**Date:** September 19, 2025 | **Committee Meeting:** October 3, 2025

## Finding and Recommendation

The robotic weld cell at the Dothan, Alabama plant has failed. After $7.4 million of investment — against an original approval of $5.6 million — the cell runs a 61-second cycle against a 42-second specification, holds 81 percent first-pass yield against the 96 percent achieved by the manual line it was meant to replace, and has failed customer production part approval twice. It is slower and less capable than the process it replaced, and it is currently sustaining production only through 100 percent manual inspection and rework that costs the plant an estimated $85,000 per month in containment labor and premium freight.

This office recommends that the Operations Committee halt all further capital spending on the cell effective immediately; direct the restoration of the manual weld line to full capacity, at an estimated cost of $700,000, to secure customer supply and end containment costs; and authorize management to pursue recovery from the systems integrator under the performance provisions of the contract while evaluating redeployment of the cell's robots to a less demanding application. We do not recommend a third attempt to bring the cell to specification on its current program. The evidence, set out below, is that the cell's failures are rooted in the original process design, not in commissioning problems that further money will cure.

## What the Program Was and What It Has Cost

The cell was approved in fiscal 2023 at $5.6 million to automate high-volume structural welding for one of Tallapoosa's largest customer programs. The business case rested on a 42-second cycle — matching customer takt with margin — a projected first-pass yield of 97 percent, and labor savings of eleven positions across three shifts, worth roughly $1.1 million annually. On those assumptions, payback was projected at just over four years.

Actual spending stands at $7.4 million, 32 percent over approval, with the overage consumed by two rework campaigns: a fixture redesign and re-tooling effort in 2024 after the first PPAP failure, and a weld-parameter and vision-system overhaul in early 2025 after the second. Neither campaign closed the gap. Beyond the capital, the plant carries the containment cost noted above — roughly $1 million on an annualized basis — plus the cost of retaining a partial manual crew as backup, which erases most of the labor saving the business case promised. The all-in economic position is that Tallapoosa has spent $7.4 million to make its welding operation slower, lower-yielding, and more expensive to run.

## The Measured Results

Three verified facts define the cell's condition, and each has resisted two rounds of corrective investment.

First, cycle time. The cell runs at 61 seconds against a 42-second specification, a 45 percent shortfall. The gap is structural: the cell's part-handling sequence requires reorientation moves the original simulation did not model accurately, and the integrator's own revised analysis, obtained by this office, concedes that the current architecture cannot reach 42 seconds without a fixture-and-layout redesign amounting to a substantial rebuild. At 61 seconds the cell cannot meet customer takt on its own; the manual line covers the shortfall, which is why the promised headcount reduction has not materialized.

Second, quality. First-pass yield stands at 81 percent against the manual line's 96 percent. The dominant defect modes — inconsistent penetration on two joint configurations and positional variation traceable to fixture repeatability — are the same modes identified after the first PPAP failure, which is the strongest single indicator that the corrective campaigns have treated symptoms rather than causes. Every part shipped from the cell passes through manual inspection; the customer has been kept whole, but at Tallapoosa's expense and on Tallapoosa's risk.

Third, customer approval. The cell has failed PPAP twice, in March 2024 and June 2025. The customer's supplier quality organization has advised, in writing, that a third submission will not be scheduled until Tallapoosa demonstrates sustained capability over a sixty-day production run — a standard the cell is not close to meeting. More consequentially, the customer has raised the cell's status in its supplier risk review, and Dothan's performance rating has fallen two tiers over the period. The commercial exposure of that decline, ahead of sourcing decisions on the successor program in 2027, is difficult to quantify but is plausibly larger than the entire capital sum at issue.

## Why This Office Does Not Recommend a Third Attempt

The natural instinct after $7.4 million is to protect the investment with one more push. The Committee should resist it, for three reasons. The money already spent is sunk and is irrelevant to the choice ahead; only future costs and future returns matter. The integrator's estimate for the redesign required to reach specification is $1.8 million to $2.6 million and six to nine months — during which containment costs continue — and the integrator will not warrant the outcome, meaning Tallapoosa would be funding a third experiment, not buying a result. And the failure pattern is diagnostic: when the same defect modes survive two engineered corrective campaigns, the defect is in the concept, not the execution. Independent review commissioned by this office concurs that the joint geometry on this part family is at the edge of what the chosen process can hold at the specified rate.

## The Decision Before the Committee

The Committee faces a genuine stop-or-continue decision, and both paths carry real cost. They should be weighed explicitly.

The cost to continue is $1.8 million to $2.6 million in redesign capital, six to nine further months of containment at roughly $85,000 per month, continued customer risk exposure, and no warranty of success — a total forward commitment approaching $3.4 million at the upper bound for an uncertain outcome, on top of the $7.4 million already spent.

The cost to stop is substantial and should be stated without flinching. Restoring the manual line to full standalone capacity requires approximately $700,000 in equipment refurbishment and staffing. Of the $7.4 million invested, we estimate $1.2 million to $1.5 million is recoverable through redeployment of the robots and controls to a lower-demand application under evaluation at Gadsden, leaving an unrecoverable write-off in the range of $5.9 million to $6.2 million. Contract recovery from the integrator is possible — the performance provisions are reasonably strong — but should be treated as upside, not plan. Stopping also means forgoing, for now, the labor savings the automation was meant to deliver, though those savings never materialized in practice.

Weighed against each other, stopping costs roughly $700,000 in new cash and an accounting recognition of money already gone; continuing costs up to $3.4 million in new cash for an outcome two prior campaigns have failed to achieve, while extending the customer exposure that is the program's most dangerous feature. This office's judgment is that the write-off is the price of a decision made in 2023, not of the decision before the Committee now, and that the Committee's obligation is to stop the ongoing loss. We recommend halting the program at the October 3 meeting, funding the manual line restoration immediately, and directing that any future automation proposal for this part family come forward only with a demonstrated process capability study — a discipline whose absence is the root lesson of this program.

# Executive Summary: Apprenticeship Pipeline with Northeast Mississippi Community College

**Tallapoosa Precision Components — Capital and Continuous Improvement Review Office**
**Prepared by:** Sanjay Mirchandani, Director
**For:** Ekaterina Novikova, VP of Manufacturing, and the Operations Committee
**Date:** September 19, 2025 | **Committee Meeting:** October 3, 2025

## Finding and Recommendation

The apprenticeship pipeline with Northeast Mississippi Community College is a program that works as a school and leaks as a pipeline. Over three years and approximately $1.9 million of company investment, it has enrolled 64 apprentices and graduated 41 — a completion rate of 64 percent that compares well with peer programs — but 15 of those 41 graduates, more than a third, left Tallapoosa within a year of finishing. Skilled-trade vacancies fell from 17 percent to 12 percent over the program's life, but the regional hiring market loosened over the same period, and this office cannot attribute the improvement cleanly to the program. The economics are the problem: at 26 retained graduates, Tallapoosa's effective cost is roughly $73,000 per skilled employee actually kept, and a meaningful share of the program's output is now working for competitors who paid nothing to train them.

This office recommends the Committee continue the program, but conditionally: approve one further two-year funding cycle of approximately $1.3 million, contingent on management implementing a retention package — wage progression, service commitments, and mentorship changes detailed below — with an explicit performance gate. If twelve-month post-graduation retention does not reach 80 percent by the fiscal 2027 review, the program should be wound down at the end of the college's academic commitments. The decision before the Committee is not whether the training is good; it is whether Tallapoosa can keep what it trains.

## What the Program Is and What It Costs

The pipeline, launched in fiscal 2022 in partnership with NEMCC, is a registered apprenticeship covering machining, industrial maintenance, and tool-and-die trades. Apprentices split time between paid production work at the Tupelo and Columbus plants and classroom instruction at the college, earning journeyman credentials over roughly two years. Tallapoosa's investment — about $650,000 per year, $1.9 million to date — covers apprentice wages during classroom hours, tuition and instructional cost-sharing with the college, mentor time on the floor, and program administration. The college contributes instructors, facilities, and a share of curriculum development, supported in part by state workforce funds; that leverage is real and would not survive a Tallapoosa withdrawal.

The program was conceived as a response to a genuine crisis. In 2021, skilled-trade vacancy at Tallapoosa stood at 17 percent, overtime among incumbent tradespeople was chronic, and the average age of the maintenance workforce was 51, with a wave of retirements visible five years out. The pipeline was designed to build labor the market was not supplying. That retirement wave has not gone away; it is the strongest argument for continuation and it should frame the Committee's reading of everything below.

## The Measured Results

The enrollment and completion numbers are sound. Sixty-four apprentices enrolled across four cohorts; 41 graduated; 8 remain in progress; 15 withdrew before completion, most in the first six months and most for reasons — relocation, pay competition from unskilled work paying comparable early-stage wages — that the program's design partly invites. A 64 percent completion rate exceeds the national registered-apprenticeship average, and supervisor evaluations of graduates are consistently strong: graduates reach independent-work proficiency roughly a year faster than external hires at comparable wage grades, and two graduates contributed materially to the Tupelo preventive maintenance program reviewed separately, which is exactly the compounding benefit the program was designed to produce.

The retention number is not sound. Fifteen of 41 graduates — 37 percent — left within twelve months of completing the program. Exit data gathered by this office is unambiguous about why: newly credentialed journeymen received offers from other regional manufacturers at $4 to $7 per hour above Tallapoosa's post-graduation rate, and Tallapoosa's wage progression required two further years to close that gap. The company has, in effect, been operating a training service for the regional labor market. Of the 15 leavers, at least 9 went to identifiable competitor or adjacent manufacturers within fifty miles.

The vacancy improvement — 17 percent to 12 percent — is real but only partly the program's. Over the same period, two regional plant closures released skilled labor into the market and Tallapoosa's own attrition slowed. Our best estimate, netting out market effects by comparing trades the program covers against trades it does not, is that the pipeline accounts for roughly half the improvement — about two and a half points of vacancy, or on the order of 20 positions. That is a genuine contribution. It is also about half of what the funding requests projected.

The unit economics follow from the retention leak. Spread over 41 graduates, program cost is about $46,000 each — defensible against external recruitment and ramp-up costs for skilled trades, which finance estimates at $35,000 to $50,000 per hire when premium wages, search fees, and productivity ramp are counted. Spread over the 26 graduates Tallapoosa actually kept, cost rises to roughly $73,000, and the comparison turns unfavorable. Retention is therefore not a soft-side concern; it is the variable on which the entire business case turns. Moving twelve-month retention from 63 percent to 85 percent would, by itself, bring cost per retained graduate below the external-hire benchmark with no other change to the program.

## The Decision Before the Committee

The Committee faces a three-way choice — continue as is, continue with conditions, or stop — and the costs of each should be explicit.

Continuing as is costs approximately $650,000 per year and, on current retention performance, buys skilled labor at a premium to market hiring while subsidizing competitors. This office does not recommend it.

Continuing with conditions costs approximately $1.3 million over two years, plus an estimated $180,000 annually in retention measures: a compressed post-graduation wage progression that closes the market gap within six months rather than two years, a two-year service commitment with pro-rated tuition repayment for early leavers (structured to comply with state law), and assigned-mentor continuity through the first post-graduation year, where our exit interviews show attachment is weakest. With those measures, and with cohort size trimmed from 18 to 14 to match realistic absorption, the program's economics clear the external-hire benchmark under any retention outcome above roughly 78 percent. The performance gate should be written into the approval: 80 percent twelve-month retention by the fiscal 2027 review, or wind-down.

Stopping costs little in cash — an estimated $150,000 to honor commitments to the 8 apprentices in progress and to the college through the current academic year — but its real costs arrive later. The retirement wave in maintenance and tooling has not moved; roughly 60 skilled employees reach retirement eligibility by 2030, and the loosened hiring market that flattered this program's vacancy numbers is cyclical, not permanent. Rebuilding a college partnership and state funding relationship after a withdrawal would take two to three years — time the retirement curve does not grant. Stopping also forfeits the state cost-share and the recruiting presence the program gives Tallapoosa in a labor market where it competes with larger employers.

This office's judgment is that the program's flaw is specific, identified, and fixable, and that the demographic problem it addresses is neither. We recommend conditional continuation with the retention package and the 2027 gate, approved at the October 3 meeting.

# Executive Summary: Manufacturing Execution System Integration

**Tallapoosa Precision Components — Capital and Continuous Improvement Review Office**
**Prepared by:** Sanjay Mirchandani, Director
**For:** Ekaterina Novikova, VP of Manufacturing, and the Operations Committee
**Date:** September 19, 2025 | **Committee Meeting:** October 3, 2025

## Finding and Recommendation

The manufacturing execution system integration, overseen by Quality Director Fatoumata Camara, is 71 percent over its approved budget, a year behind on its final site, and short of its central performance target. Approved at $5.2 million, the program has spent $8.9 million; it is live at three of four plants; inventory accuracy at the live sites stands at 87 percent against a 98 percent target; and the fourth plant's go-live has slipped from January to September 2025 and, in this office's assessment, will slip again — a realistic completion date is the second quarter of fiscal 2026. This is a troubled program. It is not, however, a program the company can cleanly abandon: three plants now run on it, the legacy systems they left behind are partially decommissioned, and two customers' traceability requirements are contractually tied to capabilities only the MES provides.

This office recommends that the Committee neither continue the program on its current terms nor stop it, but rebaseline it: authorize an estimated $2.3 million to complete the fourth plant and remediate accuracy at the first three, bringing total program cost to approximately $11.2 million; impose new governance, including independent stage-gate review by this office and a freeze on scope additions; and gate the fourth plant's go-live on the first three sites reaching 96 percent inventory accuracy, so that the company stops replicating a configuration that has not yet worked anywhere. The decision before the Committee is how to finish, and under whose discipline — not whether to finish.

## What the Program Was Approved to Do and What It Has Cost

The MES integration was approved in fiscal 2022 at $5.2 million to give all four plants a common real-time system for production tracking, quality data capture, lot traceability, and inventory control, replacing a patchwork of legacy applications and paper travelers. The business case promised 98 percent inventory accuracy (from a baseline near 82 percent), full lot traceability to meet tightening customer requirements, a $1.4 million annual reduction in inventory carrying and expedite costs, and elimination of the manual data transcription that consumed an estimated 11,000 labor hours per year across the plants.

Spending stands at $8.9 million. The overrun decomposes into three roughly equal parts, and the decomposition matters because it tells the Committee which risks remain live. About a third is integration cost with the ERP system that the original estimate — prepared substantially by the software vendor — understated badly; that work is now largely complete and does not recur. About a third is scope growth: seventeen approved change requests added customer-specific labeling, additional quality modules, and plant-requested customizations, each individually defensible and collectively unaffordable; this risk is fully live and is the reason we recommend a scope freeze. The final third is the cost of the schedule itself — extended consultant and contractor presence as go-lives slipped, which will continue to accrue at roughly $130,000 per month until the program closes. Every month of further delay has a price, and the Committee should hold that number in mind when weighing the options below.

## The Measured Results

At the three live plants — Tupelo, Columbus, and Gadsden — the system is functioning but underperforming. Inventory accuracy stands at 87 percent against the 98 percent target. That eleven-point gap is not an abstraction: below roughly 95 percent, planners do not trust the system, and this office's plant reviews found exactly the predicted consequence — shadow spreadsheets, manual cycle-count workarounds, and buffer stock quietly rebuilt to compensate, which together erode most of the $1.4 million annual saving the business case promised. Our root-cause review attributes the gap primarily to incomplete barcode scanning discipline at receiving and floor-transfer points, and to data-migration errors from the legacy systems that were never fully reconciled — problems of implementation rigor rather than software capability, which is why we judge them remediable at bounded cost.

The program's successes should also be on the record, because they define what stopping would forfeit. Lot traceability is live and performing at all three sites, and it has already been exercised in earnest: a supplier material issue in April 2025 was contained in four hours using MES trace data, a task that under the legacy systems took days and had previously forced over-quarantine of good stock. Two customers have audited and accepted the traceability capability against contractual requirements that the legacy environment could not have met. Manual transcription hours are down roughly 60 percent at the live plants. The system's foundation, in short, is sound; its inventory function was implemented without sufficient discipline, and its budget was governed without sufficient skepticism.

The fourth plant, Dothan, has moved from January to September 2025 and will not make September. The slip reflects both the program team's diversion to accuracy remediation at the live sites and Dothan's own condition — the plant is simultaneously managing the weld cell failure reviewed separately, and its legacy inventory data is the dirtiest of the four sites. A realistic go-live, properly gated, is the second quarter of fiscal 2026.

## The Decision Before the Committee

The Committee's options are three, and their costs differ sharply in kind.

Continuing on current terms — same governance, same open scope — is the path of least resistance and the worst option. The program's history is the evidence: seventeen scope changes and a 71 percent overrun occurred under the existing governance, and there is no reason to expect the fourth plant to behave differently under the same regime. Estimated cost: $2.3 million if nothing further goes wrong, with the program's own record arguing that something will.

Stopping now would cap capital spending but is far more expensive than it appears. The three live plants cannot revert: their legacy systems are partially decommissioned, licenses lapsed, and the institutional knowledge to run them substantially dispersed. Stopping would strand Dothan permanently on its legacy environment, forcing dual-system operation — two sets of processes, reporting, and interfaces — at an estimated ongoing cost of $400,000 to $500,000 per year with no end date. It would leave the accuracy gap at the live plants unremediated, forfeiting the $1.4 million annual benefit indefinitely. Most seriously, Dothan serves one of the two customers whose traceability requirements are contractually tied to MES capability; stopping puts Tallapoosa out of conformance at that site by 2027, at a plant whose customer standing is already damaged by the weld cell. Stopping, in short, converts an $8.9 million overspend into a permanent structural cost and a compliance exposure.

Rebaselining — this office's recommendation — costs an estimated $2.3 million: roughly $800,000 for accuracy remediation at the live sites (scanning infrastructure, data reconciliation, and cycle-count discipline), $1.2 million for the Dothan implementation, and $300,000 of contingency held at the Committee level, not the program level. Its conditions are the substance of the recommendation: a hard scope freeze with any change routed to this office and the CFO; monthly stage-gate reviews independent of the program team; and the accuracy gate — no Dothan go-live until the three live plants sustain 96 percent for sixty days — so that the final site inherits a proven configuration rather than a flawed one. On those terms, total program cost closes at approximately $11.2 million against a recurring benefit this office now estimates at $1.6 million to $1.9 million annually once accuracy is remediated, counting inventory savings, transcription labor, and avoided dual-system cost. That is a payback the original $5.2 million approval would have celebrated and the $11.2 million actual must simply accept.

The honest summary for the Committee is this: the money already spent bought a system that mostly works, governed by a process that did not. We recommend approving the rebaseline at the October 3 meeting, with the governance conditions attached not as formality but as the actual remedy — and we note for the record that the estimating and change-control failures documented here should inform the Committee's review standards for every capital program in fiscal 2026, beginning with the preventive maintenance replication proposal this office has recommended elsewhere in this set.
