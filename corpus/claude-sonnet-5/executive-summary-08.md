# Executive Summary: Small-Format Urban Store Pilot (FY2023–FY2025)

**Prepared by the Strategy and Performance Office | Director Sun-Hee Park**
**Date: February 20, 2025 | For the March 2025 Capital Meeting**

The small-format urban pilot has outperformed the chain on every productivity measure that matters at the store level, and the Strategy and Performance Office recommends that the capital committee authorize expansion of the format beyond the two sites already committed for fiscal 2026, with a formal three-year real estate pipeline attached to the recommendation rather than a site-by-site approval process.

The four stores opened in calendar 2023 — in downtown Raleigh, midtown Charlotte, the Cary transit corridor, and the Fan District in Richmond — average 9,400 square feet against a chain-wide average format of roughly 44,000 square feet. On that footprint they generated $912 in sales per square foot in fiscal 2025, against a company average of $640, a 42 percent premium that held across all four locations rather than being carried by a single outlier store. Cary posted the highest figure, at $1,040 per square foot, driven by lunch-hour and evening basket frequency near the transit stop; the weakest of the four, the Fan District store, still cleared $840, comfortably above the chain average. Each store reached store-level operating breakeven within 14 months of opening, well inside the 24-to-30-month window the finance office typically models for new conventional-format stores, and inside the 18-month hurdle Mr. Andrakis's office set for the pilot at approval.

The economics behind that speed are structural, not incidental. Build cost per store averaged $3.1 million, roughly a third of a standard new-store build, because the format uses existing retail shells rather than ground-up construction and carries a narrower SKU count — approximately 6,800 items against 34,000 in a full-size store, concentrated in fresh, prepared foods, and convenience center-store categories. Labor cost as a percentage of sales came in at 11.2 percent, above the 9.8 percent chain average, reflecting the fixed-cost floor of running a store on any footprint, but this was more than offset by gross margin rates roughly 260 basis points above the chain average, driven by the prepared-foods and grab-and-go mix that anchors the format. Basket size is 34 percent smaller than the chain average, at $18.40 versus $27.90, but trip frequency is roughly 2.3 times higher per square foot of trade area, which is the mechanism behind the sales-per-square-foot outperformance and is consistent with the urban, walk-in customer base the format targets.

The pilot was not without friction. Inventory shrink in the two stores without secured back-of-house space ran 90 basis points above the chain average in the first six months, before operational fixes brought it into line by month nine. Delivery frequency from the distribution center had to be increased from three times weekly to daily for two of the four stores to prevent out-of-stocks in the narrower assortment, which added an estimated $40,000 in annual logistics cost per store that was not in the original pilot model. Both issues are now understood and have been built into the site-selection and operating standards for the format going forward.

Two additional sites — one in South End Charlotte and one in the Church Hill neighborhood of Richmond — are already committed for fiscal 2026 openings, with capital of approximately $4.5 million allocated across the pair, including lease costs already executed. That commitment predates this review and is not before the capital committee for reconsideration.

What we are recommending the committee approve is the next tier: a formal pipeline of six to eight additional small-format sites over fiscal years 2027 through 2029, concentrated in the Triangle, Charlotte, and Hampton Roads metros where the chain's real estate team has already identified qualifying urban infill sites. We recommend the committee direct real estate and operations to convert the pilot's operating model into a standardized prototype — fixed assortment logic, a defined back-of-house minimum square footage requirement to address the shrink issue observed in the first two stores, and a daily-delivery logistics standard built into the site pro forma from the outset rather than added after opening. Formalizing the format now, while the evidence is strong and before urban real estate costs in these three metros continue to rise, is the basis of our recommendation.

# Executive Summary: Third-Party Delivery Partnership (FY2023–FY2025)

**Prepared by the Strategy and Performance Office | Director Sun-Hee Park**
**Date: February 20, 2025 | For the March 2025 Capital Meeting**

The third-party delivery partnership has produced negative unit economics and a deteriorating customer experience over its three-year run, and the Strategy and Performance Office recommends the capital committee restrict the program to the scaled-back, twelve-store scope already committed for fiscal 2026 and set a firm decision date of the fiscal 2026 midyear review on whether to renew, restructure, or exit the relationship entirely.

The partnership processed 380,000 orders across the pilot's three fiscal years, growing from roughly 60,000 orders in the first partial year to over 190,000 in fiscal 2025 alone. Despite that volume growth, the program carried a cumulative negative contribution margin of $1.9 million once commission fees, pick-and-pack labor, packaging, and refund costs are netted against incremental sales. On a per-order basis, the program loses approximately $5.00 in contribution margin, a figure that has not improved meaningfully as volume has scaled, which indicates the economics are structural rather than a fixed-cost problem that resolves with growth. The commission rate paid to the third-party platform, 28 percent of order value, is the single largest driver: at current average order values, the commission alone exceeds the gross margin the chain earns on the basket.

Basket composition compounds the margin problem. Average delivery baskets ran 22 percent smaller than in-store baskets, at $46.10 versus $59.20, concentrated in the loss of high-margin, high-touch categories — fresh meat, seafood, and deli — where customers are evidently reluctant to delegate selection to a picker. The substitution rate across the program averaged 31 percent of line items, well above the 12 percent internal benchmark the operations team uses for its own pickup service, and substitutions were heavily weighted toward produce and center-store private label items, the two categories the chain has invested in most heavily over the same period. The substitution rate is the direct driver of the 1,100 refund claims logged over the pilot period, which cost the chain approximately $61,000 in direct refunds and, more significantly, generated a measurable decline in repeat order rate among customers who filed a claim — repeat rate for that group fell to 34 percent within 90 days, against 58 percent for customers who did not experience a substitution issue.

Store-level operational strain is the third factor behind the recommendation. Pick times average 26 minutes per order against an 18-minute target, and the labor drawn to fulfill delivery orders during peak hours has been cited in four of the twelve participating stores' manager feedback as a contributor to slower checkout times for in-store shoppers during the same windows. This is a real, if harder to quantify, cost to the core business that the original pilot model did not account for.

For fiscal 2026, the chain has already committed to a reduced twelve-store trial under a renegotiated one-year contract extension, which lowers the commission rate to 24 percent and introduces a substitution-rate penalty clause the platform must meet or forfeit a portion of its fee. That commitment is in place and is not before the committee for reversal.

What is before the committee is the decision framework for what follows. We recommend the committee direct the merchandising and operations teams to use the twelve-store trial to test two structural fixes — restricting delivery-eligible SKUs to categories with acceptable substitution performance, and piloting an owned-picker model in two stores as an internal cost comparison against the third-party rate — and to bring a formal renew-or-exit recommendation to the fiscal 2026 midyear capital meeting. Absent a clear path to positive contribution margin by that review, our office's view is that the chain should not renew the partnership past its current term.

# Executive Summary: Private Label Expansion (FY2023–FY2025)

**Prepared by the Strategy and Performance Office, in coordination with SVP of Operations Jamal Whitfield**
**Date: February 20, 2025 | For the March 2025 Capital Meeting**

The private label expansion has delivered on its two primary financial targets — penetration and gross margin — but the gains are uneven across categories, and the Strategy and Performance Office recommends the capital committee approve continued expansion into the categories already committed for fiscal 2026 while directing a pause on new private label introductions in dairy and frozen until the velocity and assortment issues in those categories are resolved.

Private label penetration rose from 18 percent of units sold at the start of the pilot to 24 percent by the close of fiscal 2025, a six-point gain against a three-year target of five points, and the fastest expansion the chain has recorded in this category. Gross margin on private label units improved 140 basis points over the period, and because the mix shift toward private label was itself a driver of margin, the blended effect across all grocery categories added an estimated $6.2 million to gross profit in fiscal 2025 alone relative to a flat-penetration scenario. Penetration gains were strongest in shelf-stable center-store categories — canned goods, snacks, and baking — where private label now represents 31 percent of units, and in health and beauty care, a category Mr. Whitfield's team added to the program in the pilot's second year and which reached 19 percent penetration within eighteen months of launch.

The exception to this performance is dairy and frozen, where unit velocity fell over the course of the pilot even as the SKU count and shelf space allocated to private label increased. Private label dairy velocity declined 8 percent over the three-year period on a per-store, per-week basis, and frozen declined 11 percent, against a backdrop of flat-to-slightly-positive velocity for national brands in the same categories over the same window. Nine private label items — six in frozen, three in dairy — were discontinued within twelve months of launch, each carrying markdown and write-off costs that together totaled approximately $410,000. Category review attributes the underperformance to two causes: private label packaging in frozen was redesigned twice during the pilot period in response to consumer research, which created supply disruption and shelf-tag confusion during transition windows, and private label dairy was introduced at a price point only 6 percent below national brand, compared with a 15 to 20 percent gap in the categories where private label is succeeding, which gave shoppers in a highly brand-loyal category insufficient reason to switch.

The nine discontinuations, while a small share of the roughly 340 private label items launched during the pilot, are concentrated enough in two categories to warrant a deliberate pause rather than a program-wide course correction. Every other category in the program is performing at or above target, and the overall penetration and margin numbers reflect a program that is, on balance, working.

For fiscal 2026, the chain has already committed an incremental $2.8 million in private label development funding, earmarked primarily for expansion into two new categories — pet supplies and a private label coffee and tea line — both of which Mr. Whitfield's team has in supplier development now. That commitment stands.

The recommendation before the committee is narrower: direct that no new private label SKUs launch in dairy or frozen during fiscal 2026 until pricing has been reset to the 15 to 20 percent gap that is working elsewhere in the program and packaging has stabilized for two consecutive quarters, and require that the fiscal 2026 development funding already committed be reported against category-level velocity targets at each quarterly business review rather than against penetration alone, so that a category performing on volume but not on penetration, or vice versa, is caught earlier than it was in dairy and frozen.

# Executive Summary: Store Remodel Program (FY2023–FY2025)

**Prepared by the Strategy and Performance Office | Director Sun-Hee Park**
**Date: February 20, 2025 | For the March 2025 Capital Meeting**

The remodel program has materially underperformed its underwriting on both cost and return, and the Strategy and Performance Office recommends the capital committee halt authorization of new remodels beyond the fiscal 2026 commitments already in place, formally suspend the four paused projects pending a value-engineering review, and require a revised underwriting model before any further capital is approved for this program.

The program was approved for 11 stores at a budgeted $2.3 million per remodel, a total authorized capital commitment of $25.3 million. Actual average cost across the stores completed to date is $3.4 million, a 48 percent overage that has pushed total capital deployed on the program toward $37.4 million against the original $25.3 million authorization, a variance of roughly $12 million that was absorbed without a return to this committee at the time the overages occurred. The overage is broadly distributed rather than concentrated in one or two outlier stores: nine of the eleven projects came in above budget, with the range running from 22 percent over at the best-performing site to 71 percent over at the worst, a Fayetteville location where structural issues discovered mid-project required an unbudgeted refrigeration system replacement.

The return side of the program has underperformed by a comparable margin. The program was underwritten on an assumption of an 11 percent comparable sales lift in the twelve months following reopening, a figure drawn from the chain's historical remodel performance in the prior program cycle. Actual comparable sales lift across the completed remodels averages 6.1 percent, roughly 55 percent of the underwritten assumption. Combined with the cost overage, the payback period on the program has extended from an underwritten 3.4 years to a current estimate of 7.1 years, which puts the program's return meaningfully below the chain's cost of capital over the relevant holding period.

Store-level results vary enough to be instructive. The three highest-performing remodels, all in stores with above-average pre-remodel traffic, delivered comparable sales lifts of 9 to 10 percent, close to the original underwriting, suggesting the format investment itself is sound when applied to the right store profile. The four lowest-performing remodels, all in stores that were underperforming the chain average before the remodel, delivered lifts of 2 to 4 percent, indicating that the program's site-selection criteria — which prioritized stores most in need of physical investment rather than stores with the traffic base to convert that investment into sales — may have been inverted relative to what actually drives return.

Four remodels have been paused since January, pending this review, representing approximately $9.8 million in capital not yet deployed. We recommend these remain paused rather than resuming on the current design and cost specification. A preliminary review by the real estate and construction team attributes roughly 60 percent of the cost overage to scope additions made mid-project — expanded prepared foods sections and equipment upgrades beyond the original remodel specification — that were approved at the regional level without returning to this committee, and 40 percent to underestimated base construction costs in the original underwriting, a gap that should be corrected in any revised model.

Three of the eleven original remodels are already committed for completion in fiscal 2026, with capital allocated under the original program authorization; that commitment is not in dispute. What we recommend the committee decide now is whether to release the $9.8 million for the four paused projects under a revised, tighter scope specification with a hard cap on mid-project change orders, or to reallocate that capital elsewhere until the underwriting model itself has been rebuilt using the actual cost and return data from this pilot rather than the prior program cycle. Our office's recommendation is the latter: hold the paused capital, rebuild the underwriting model using the traffic-based site criteria suggested by the variance between high- and low-performing stores, and bring a revised program back to this committee before any of the four paused sites resume construction.

Taken together, the four reviews point to a common thread the capital committee should weigh as a set rather than in isolation: the pilots that succeeded did so where the underlying unit economics were tested against a narrow, well-understood customer behavior before scale was added, and the pilots that struggled did so where scale or capital commitment outran the evidence. The small-format stores were expanded only after four sites proved the model over eighteen months; the remodel program authorized eleven stores against underwriting drawn from a prior cycle and absorbed a 48 percent cost overage before the pattern was visible to this committee. The private label program shows the same lesson inside a single portfolio — categories where pricing and packaging were tested and held stable before wide rollout are outperforming, and the two categories where mid-course changes were made during expansion are the two that are underperforming. The delivery partnership is the clearest case of scale preceding proof: volume nearly tripled year over year while per-order economics stayed flat and negative.

For the March meeting, our office recommends the four items be sequenced as follows. The small-format pipeline request should be taken first, since it is the one program asking for expansion beyond what is already committed and carries no open cost or performance concerns. The private label pause in dairy and frozen should be taken as an operating decision rather than a capital one, requiring Mr. Whitfield's confirmation of the revised pricing approach rather than a committee vote. The remodel program's paused capital should be held pending the revised underwriting model, with a target of returning a rebuilt program proposal to the May capital meeting. The delivery partnership should be treated as monitor-and-decide, with the twelve-store trial running under its current terms through the fiscal 2026 midyear review, at which point our office will bring a renew-or-exit recommendation supported by the contribution-margin trend from the reduced-scope trial.

We are prepared to walk through any of the four in greater detail with Ms. Halloran, Mr. Andrakis, or the operating partner ahead of or during the meeting, and to make the underlying store-level and order-level data available on request.

## Note on Methodology

Sales-per-square-foot, comparable sales, and contribution margin figures throughout this packet are drawn from the finance office's fiscal 2025 close, reconciled as of February 14, 2025. Store-level figures for the small-format and remodel programs reflect trailing-twelve-month performance as of each store's most recent full period; stores opened or reopened after August 2024 are shown on a partial-year, annualized basis and are footnoted individually in the underlying store-level file. Delivery partnership figures are drawn from the third-party platform's settlement reporting, cross-checked against internal POS records for order count and refund claims; contribution margin nets platform commission, incremental pick-and-pack labor, packaging, and refund cost against incremental basket revenue, and does not assume any cannibalization or halo effect on in-store sales, a limitation the finance office flagged during the review and recommends testing directly in the fiscal 2026 trial. Private label velocity figures are unit sales per store per week, indexed against the same store set for the full pilot period to control for store openings and closings elsewhere in the chain.
