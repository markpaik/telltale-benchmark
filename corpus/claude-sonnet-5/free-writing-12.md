# The Weight of Small Sundays

My grandmother used to save the twist ties from bread bags in a jar on the windowsill, sorted by color though she never told anyone why. When she died, I found four hundred and twelve of them, and I sat on her kitchen floor for an hour trying to understand what kind of mind keeps a jar like that — what kind of hope, or fear, or simple animal habit of *not yet, not yet, don't throw it away yet*.

I am thirty-four now, older than she was when I was born, and I have started noticing my own jars. The rubber bands from the asparagus. The single chopsticks I keep because someday I'll need only one. The voicemail from my father, seven seconds long, saying only *call me back, it's not important* — three years old now, and I have never once let my phone auto-delete it.

There is a particular kind of Sunday that belongs only to adulthood, a Sunday with no plans and no guilt about having no plans, when the light comes through the kitchen window at four in the afternoon and lands on the table in a shape you recognize from a hundred other Sundays, and you understand, all at once and without sadness, that this is simply what a life is made of. Not the weddings. Not the funerals. The twist ties. The seven-second voicemails. The light at four o'clock, arriving the same way it always arrives, indifferent and exact.

I used to think meaning was a thing you built, like a house, with a foundation and a plan. Now I think it's more like sediment — it just settles, in jars, in drawers, in the bottom of your chest, and one day someone sits on your kitchen floor and tries to read you in the pattern of what you couldn't let go of.

I don't know what I'm keeping the chopstick for. I don't know why the color-sorting mattered to her, or if it mattered at all, or if some hopes never asked to be understood — they only asked to be kept.

But I keep it anyway. The single chopstick. The seven seconds. The particular gold of four o'clock, which I have started, without meaning to, saving up.
