# Access Denied: The Two-Year Struggle to Fix Primary Care at Ogeechee Valley Health

## A System Under Strain

Ogeechee Valley Health sits astride the pine flats and farm country around Valdosta, Georgia, a 146-bed hospital anchored by six outlying clinics that serve a service area of roughly ninety thousand people spread across four counties. It is not a large system by national standards — 1,300 employees, $210 million in annual revenue — but it is the only hospital of consequence for forty miles in any direction, and for thousands of families it is also the only source of primary care. That dual identity, safety-net hospital and de facto medical home, is the thread that runs through everything that happened at Ogeechee Valley between 2023 and 2025.

By the close of 2023, the strain on that identity had become impossible to ignore. The system's operating margin for the year came in at minus 4.1 percent, the third consecutive year of losses, driven in large part by a primary care network that was failing at the most basic level: getting patients in the door. Nine of the system's thirty-one primary care physician positions sat vacant, nearly a third of the establishment. Patients calling to become new patients were told to expect a forty-seven day wait for the first available appointment. Of the patients who did get appointments, better than one in five — 24 percent — simply didn't show.

The downstream effect of that access failure showed up in the emergency department, where internal analysis found that 38 percent of visits were for conditions that a primary care office, urgent care clinic, or nurse line could have handled just as well, at a fraction of the cost and without contributing to the boarding and throughput problems that were themselves eating into hospital margins. Chief Executive Beatrice Nwachukwu had been in her role for two years by the time the 2023 numbers came in, and she later described the emergency department data as the moment the board's tone changed. "We had been talking about the ED as a capacity problem," she said in an interview conducted for this case study. "It was actually an access problem wearing a capacity problem's clothes."

Chief Medical Officer Vikas Deshpande, a family medicine physician by training who had joined the system in 2019 after a decade in a multi-specialty group in Macon, had been raising the primary care vacancy numbers at every medical staff meeting since early 2022, with limited traction. The pandemic-era exodus of primary care physicians from small and rural systems had hit Ogeechee Valley harder than most; recruiters were telling Deshpande that candidates who might once have considered a rural Georgia posting were now choosing employed positions in Atlanta suburbs or telehealth-only arrangements that let them live wherever they wanted. Two open positions had gone unfilled for over eighteen months despite six figures in signing bonus commitments. "We were competing for a shrinking pool with a compensation package we couldn't move much on," Deshpande said, "and losing."

The 2023 numbers turned that slow-burn staffing problem into a board-level financial emergency. A margin of minus 4.1 percent on $210 million in revenue meant the system had lost close to $8.6 million for the year, and the finance committee's own projections showed the trajectory worsening in 2024 absent some structural change, both because supplemental Medicaid payments were expected to soften and because the volume the system was losing to non-emergent ED utilization was reimbursed, when it was reimbursed at all, at rates far below what the same visit would have generated in a primary care setting. The math was not subtle: every patient who used the ED instead of a clinic appointment cost the system money twice over, once in the immediate reimbursement gap and once in the opportunity cost of a primary care panel that never got the visit it needed to stay financially viable.

## The Decision

Nwachukwu and Deshpande brought a proposal to the board in December 2023 and received approval in January 2024 for $5.4 million in capital and operating funding to open two access-focused clinics, one in Hahira and one in a second community roughly fifteen miles from the main hospital campus, both staffed primarily by nurse practitioners rather than physicians and both built around a same-day scheduling model rather than the traditional advance-booking system used at the system's existing primary care sites.

The logic behind the plan rested on four assumptions, each of which seemed reasonable at the time and each of which would be tested hard in the first year.

The first assumption was that the access problem was primarily a supply problem, and that adding capacity in underserved geographies would generate its own demand rapidly, since so much unmet need was presumably sitting untreated in the community. Deshpande's planning memo to the board cited the forty-seven day new patient wait and the ED misuse rate as evidence that demand already existed; the clinics simply needed to open their doors.

The second assumption was that nurse practitioners could be recruited and onboarded faster than physicians, closing the staffing gap on a timeline measured in months rather than the eighteen-to-twenty-four months the system had already spent failing to fill physician vacancies. Nurse practitioner training programs in the region were producing graduates at a healthy clip, and the compensation gap between NP and physician salaries meant the system could staff two clinics for less than the cost of recruiting two more physicians into the existing model.

The third assumption was that Halcyon Clinical Staffing, a regional locum tenens and temporary staffing agency the system had used previously for hospitalist coverage, could bridge the gap between the clinics' opening date and the date permanent nurse practitioner hires were in place, at a cost that would taper off within a matter of months as permanent staff came aboard.

The fourth assumption, less explicit in the planning documents but evident in how the budget was constructed, was that the operational and billing infrastructure that supported the system's existing clinics — scheduling, credentialing, claims processing — could simply be extended to the two new sites without material modification, since the new clinics were seen as smaller, simpler versions of an established model rather than as a fundamentally different care delivery approach requiring its own build-out.

Each of these four assumptions would prove only partially correct, and the gap between assumption and reality is where the first year of the program's history lives.

## Year One: What Went Wrong

The Hahira clinic opened its doors in April 2024, roughly three months after board approval, a fast timeline that itself reflected pressure from the finance committee to show results before the next fiscal year review. The clinic had been built out in a medical office park adjacent to a since-closed urgent care center, chosen primarily because the space was available on short notice and required minimal renovation.

The results in the opening months were not what the planning memo had projected. Against a target of twenty-six patients a day, the daily average settled at six. The gap was not for lack of demand in the abstract — community surveys conducted the previous year had shown real appetite for a same-day option — but for a mismatch between where that demand actually lived and where the clinic had been sited. The medical office park was a reasonable location by the logic of clinical real estate, close to physician offices and easy for referral patterns, but it was not a location patients passed on their way to anywhere else, and it was not the kind of retail-adjacent, low-friction site that same-day, walk-in-oriented care tends to require. Patients calling the clinic said, in exit interviews conducted later, that they hadn't known it was there, or hadn't realized it accepted walk-ins, or had assumed same-day availability was a marketing claim rather than an operational reality given how empty the waiting room usually looked.

The staffing side fared no better in year one. Halcyon Clinical Staffing had committed to five nurse practitioner placements to bridge the two clinics until permanent hires could be recruited; over the first seven months of the program, it filled three of the five. The two unfilled slots meant the clinics were operating below even their reduced patient volume capacity for stretches of the year, and the three locum nurse practitioners who were placed billed at $312 an hour, a rate that reflected both the premium locum staffing agencies charge for short-notice, no-benefits coverage and the scarcity value of nurse practitioners willing to take temporary rural assignments. Over seven months, that arrangement consumed $1.4 million, a run rate that, if it had continued at that pace for the full fiscal year the program was budgeted against, would have absorbed more than half of the entire $5.4 million line the board had approved for two clinics that were together seeing barely a dozen patients a day.

The financial picture darkened further in the fall of 2024, when Emiliano Cortez, the system's Revenue Cycle Director, discovered during a routine payer audit that a credentialing error had caused four months of claims from the new clinics to be denied outright. The nurse practitioners staffing the sites had not been properly enrolled with several of the major payers in the system's contracted network before they began seeing patients, a lapse that traced back to the compressed opening timeline: credentialing, which under normal circumstances runs sixty to ninety days per provider per payer, had been treated as a parallel-track item to be finished after clinical operations began rather than a gating requirement before they did. By the time Cortez's team identified the scope of the problem, $780,000 in claims had been denied, almost all of it unrecoverable through simple resubmission because the timely filing windows on many of the claims had already lapsed or were about to.

Cortez, who had joined the system in 2021 after a career in revenue cycle roles at larger Georgia systems, described the discovery process in blunt terms. "We were reconciling a denial report that looked unusual for two sites that small, and when we pulled the detail, it wasn't a coding issue or a documentation issue, it was that the providers weren't in the payer systems as recognized rendering providers at all. That's not a claim you appeal. That's a claim you either catch before it goes out the door or you eat." His team spent the following months triaging the denials, distinguishing claims that might still be salvageable through payer-specific appeal processes from those that were simply lost, a process that would not conclude until well into 2025.

By the close of calendar year 2024, the program that had been approved as a solution to the system's access and financial crisis had become, in the words of one board member who asked not to be named, "a second crisis sitting on top of the first one." The clinics were seeing a fraction of their target volume, the locum staffing bridge had cost nearly as much as the entire program budget while filling only three of five positions, and a credentialing failure had erased three-quarters of a million dollars in revenue the clinics had actually earned but would likely never collect. Nwachukwu would later describe November 2024 as the low point of the entire initiative, the month in which the board discussed, seriously, whether to close both clinics rather than continue funding them.

## The Correction

Instead of closing the clinics, the board and executive team made a leadership change. In November 2024, Ambulatory Director Latrice Bonnaire, who had spent the prior three years running the system's largest existing primary care site and had a reputation internally for operational discipline, was given direct authority over the access clinic program, reporting jointly to Nwachukwu and Deshpande with a mandate to either turn the program around within two quarters or recommend its closure.

Bonnaire's first move, undertaken within weeks of taking the assignment, was to relocate the second clinic, which had not yet opened to the public, out of the medical office park site originally selected and into a storefront in a grocery-anchored retail plaza on the main commercial corridor of its target community. The logic was straightforward and, in retrospect, obvious: same-day, walk-in-friendly care needed to be sited where people already were, doing their errands, rather than where clinical planners assumed patients would go once they knew to look for it. The relocated clinic opened in January 2025 with visible signage from the parking lot shared with the grocery anchor, a location choice that Bonnaire credited directly with the volume increases that followed.

On staffing, Bonnaire moved to convert the two functioning locum roles that Halcyon had filled into employed nurse practitioner positions, negotiating directly with the two clinicians already on assignment rather than restarting a national search from scratch. Both accepted, drawn in part by loan repayment terms and a scheduling structure that gave them more predictable hours than the locum arrangement had, and in part, according to Bonnaire, by a simple appeal to continuity: they had already built relationships with the small patient panels the clinics had managed to see, and neither wanted to abandon those patients to another rotating temp. The conversion ended the $312-an-hour billing rate for those two positions immediately and gave the clinics their first stable clinical staffing since opening.

Bonnaire's operational changes extended beyond the location and staffing decisions. She added evening hours at both sites, reasoning that a same-day access model built entirely around a nine-to-five schedule was solving only half the access problem, since many of the patients driving ED non-emergent visits worked daytime shifts themselves and could not easily leave work for a same-day appointment offered only during business hours. She also implemented an automated text reminder system for scheduled appointments, a low-cost intervention that addressed the no-show problem directly rather than assuming, as the original planning documents implicitly had, that same-day scheduling alone would solve it. The reminder system sent messages forty-eight hours and again two hours before each appointment, with a simple reply mechanism for patients to confirm or cancel, freeing the slot for another patient rather than letting it sit empty.

On the billing side, Cortez's team, working alongside Bonnaire once she took over the program, undertook a systematic rework of the denied claims backlog. Of the original $780,000 in denials, the team was able to recover $520,000 through a combination of retroactive credentialing effective-date negotiations with several payers, formal appeals on claims still within filing windows, and, in a small number of cases, direct negotiation with payer medical directors who agreed to treat the claims as an administrative rather than a coverage issue. The remaining roughly $260,000 was written off as unrecoverable, a permanent loss attributable directly to the credentialing lapse.

## Before and After

The results of these changes, measured across the seven months from Bonnaire's arrival in November 2024 to June 2025, were substantial enough to change the tenor of board discussion about the program from whether to close it to how to expand it.

Daily patient volume across the two clinics rose from the six-patients-a-day figure recorded at Hahira in its opening months to 31 patients a day by June 2025, a figure that exceeded the original 26-patient-per-day target the program had been built around. The relocation of the second clinic to a higher-visibility, higher-foot-traffic location, combined with the addition of evening hours, appears to have been the primary driver, according to Bonnaire's own account, though she was careful to note that stable staffing — patients seeing the same nurse practitioner on a return visit rather than a rotating cast of locum clinicians — likely mattered as much as location in converting first-time visitors into a durable patient panel.

New patient wait times, which had stood at forty-seven days system-wide in 2023, fell to twelve days by June 2025 for the access clinics specifically, a reduction that reflected both the added capacity of two functioning sites and the more efficient scheduling practices Bonnaire's team put in place once the reminder system reduced the number of slots lost to no-shows.

The no-show rate itself fell from the system-wide 24 percent baseline to 13 percent at the access clinics, a decline that both Bonnaire and Deshpande attributed primarily to the text reminder system, though the evening hours option — which allowed patients to book appointments around work schedules rather than in conflict with them — likely also played a role in reducing the share of scheduled visits that patients simply could not keep.

The emergency department effect, which had been the original financial rationale for the entire program, also moved in the intended direction, though more modestly than the clinic-level operational numbers might suggest. Non-emergent emergency department visits, which had constituted 38 percent of total ED volume in 2023, fell to 29 percent by June 2025. Deshpande cautioned against attributing the full nine-point decline to the access clinics alone, noting that a concurrent nurse triage line expansion and a regional urgent care chain's entry into the market in early 2025 likely contributed as well, but he characterized the access clinics as a material and probably necessary contributor to the shift, particularly in the Hahira service area where the clinic's own patient panel overlapped heavily with historical high-utilizer ED patients.

Financially, the access clinic program itself moved from a pure cost center to a modest net contributor, generating $210,000 in contribution margin in the second quarter of 2025, the first quarter in the program's history in which revenue from patient visits and recovered claims exceeded the direct operating costs of running the two sites. That figure does not erase the losses of 2024, but it marks the point at which the program's trajectory inverted.

At the system level, Ogeechee Valley Health's overall operating margin improved from minus 4.1 percent in 2023 to minus 0.6 percent by the trailing period ending in June 2025, an improvement that reflected a combination of factors well beyond the access clinic program alone, including a broader cost reduction initiative in supply chain and a renegotiated managed care contract that took effect in early 2025. Nwachukwu was careful in interviews to avoid overstating the access clinic program's individual contribution to that system-wide margin recovery, but she and the finance committee both pointed to the reduction in non-emergent ED utilization, and the associated reduction in uncompensated and under-reimbursed ED volume, as a meaningful piece of the larger financial turnaround.

## The Full Accounting

Any honest accounting of the access clinic program has to hold the recovery numbers alongside the cost of getting there, because the path from the $5.4 million initial investment to the $210,000 quarterly contribution margin recorded in mid-2025 was neither cheap nor fast, and a narrower reading of the June 2025 numbers alone would understate what the system actually paid to arrive at them.

The $5.4 million board authorization from January 2024 was intended to cover both capital costs, principally clinic build-out and equipment, and roughly a year of operating losses anticipated during the ramp-up period before the clinics reached break-even volume. Actual spending against that authorization ran higher than budgeted, driven substantially by the Halcyon staffing costs, which consumed $1.4 million over seven months against an original staffing budget that had assumed the locum bridge would be a short-term, low-cost stopgap rather than the primary source of clinical staffing for most of the first year. The $780,000 in denied claims, even after the $520,000 recovery effort, left a permanent $260,000 write-off that also has to be counted as a direct cost of the credentialing failure, a cost that would not have been incurred at all had the original opening timeline allowed for standard credentialing lead times before clinical operations began.

Taken together, the locum staffing overrun and the unrecovered claims write-off represent roughly $1.66 million in costs directly attributable to the execution failures of the program's first year, costs that sat on top of, rather than within, the originally budgeted $5.4 million, and that were only partially offset by the second-quarter 2025 contribution margin the reorganized program eventually produced. Bonnaire's own assessment, offered candidly in a system town hall in the summer of 2025, was that the program's underlying concept had been sound but that its first-year execution had cost the system close to a full extra year of losses that a better initial site selection process, a more conservative staffing bridge plan, and a credentialing timeline built into the critical path rather than run in parallel to it, could have avoided.

## What the System Would Do Differently

In debriefs conducted after the June 2025 results were reported to the board, three lessons emerged consistently from the people most directly involved in both the original planning and the eventual turnaround, and each points to a specific structural change the system has since adopted for future service line expansions.

The first lesson concerned site selection. The original Hahira location and the initial site chosen for the second clinic had both been selected using criteria appropriate to a traditional, appointment-driven primary care practice — proximity to referring physicians, availability of clinical space, ease of build-out — rather than criteria appropriate to a walk-in, same-day access model, which depends on visibility, foot traffic, and low-friction proximity to where people already conduct their daily errands. Deshpande, reflecting on the decision in 2025, said the planning team had essentially built a retail-style access product using a real estate selection process designed for a referral-based specialty practice, and that the mismatch was foreseeable in hindsight even though it had not been flagged by anyone during the original planning process. The system has since adopted a formal site-selection checklist for any new ambulatory access point that explicitly weighs retail visibility and walk-in convenience alongside the traditional clinical siting criteria, and requires sign-off from someone with retail or consumer-access experience, not solely clinical operations leadership, before a lease is signed.

The second lesson concerned the staffing bridge. The plan had treated Halcyon's locum placements as a short-term stopgap expected to taper quickly as permanent hires landed, but had not built in a contingency for what would happen if the agency filled only a fraction of the committed positions or if permanent recruitment took longer than the optimistic several-month timeline the original plan assumed. Nwachukwu said the system now builds staffing bridge plans with an explicit maximum-duration and maximum-spend cap, beyond which the program automatically triggers a leadership review rather than continuing to draw down locum coverage on an open-ended basis, a change directly responsive to the seven-month, $1.4 million run-up that occurred at Hahira and its sister site before anyone with authority over the budget was forced to formally reassess the arrangement.

The third lesson concerned the sequencing of credentialing relative to clinical opening. The compressed three-month timeline from board approval to clinic opening had treated credentialing as a task to be completed alongside clinical operations rather than as a prerequisite gating them, a decision that Cortez said reflected understandable pressure to show the board rapid results but that ended up costing the system far more, in denied and unrecoverable claims, than the few months of delay a proper credentialing-first sequence would have cost in delayed opening. The system's revenue cycle department now holds formal sign-off authority over any new clinical site's opening date, with credentialing completion for all major contracted payers required before the site may begin scheduling patients, a change that adds lead time to future openings but that Cortez and the finance committee both regard as considerably cheaper than the alternative.

Beyond these three specific structural changes, Nwachukwu and Deshpande both pointed to a broader cultural lesson about the relationship between an ambitious access-and-margin goal and the operational discipline required to hit it without incurring avoidable losses along the way. The original diagnosis — that Ogeechee Valley's financial crisis was substantially an access crisis, and that the ED misuse and primary care vacancy numbers pointed toward a genuine unmet need in the community that the right kind of clinic could capture — turned out to be correct. The June 2025 volume, wait time, and no-show numbers, all of which moved decisively in the intended direction once the clinics were properly sited, staffed, and scheduled, vindicate that original diagnosis. But the diagnosis being correct did not make the execution easy, and the system paid roughly a year of avoidable extra losses for treating site selection, staffing bridges, and credentialing as secondary details to be worked out during implementation rather than as first-order risks requiring the same rigor as the original financial and clinical case for the program.

## What a Reader in a Similar Position Should Take From This

For an executive team at another rural or safety-net system looking at similar numbers — a persistent operating margin deficit, a high rate of non-emergent emergency department utilization, chronic primary care vacancies, and long new-patient waits paired with high no-show rates — the Ogeechee Valley experience offers a specific and somewhat counterintuitive lesson. The strategic diagnosis that access constraints are driving both the clinical and financial problems, and that a same-day, nurse-practitioner-staffed access model can address both simultaneously, is very plausible and, in this case, was ultimately borne out by the data. A reader facing similar numbers should not conclude from Ogeechee Valley's rocky first year that the underlying strategy was flawed.

What should give a reader pause is the ease with which a sound strategic concept can be undermined by execution decisions that seem minor at the time: a site chosen for clinical rather than retail logic, a staffing bridge treated as open-ended rather than capped and time-limited, a credentialing process run in parallel rather than as a gate. None of these three failures reflected a flaw in the original access-clinic concept itself; each reflected an implementation shortcut taken under time pressure, and each proved expensive to correct after the fact. The $1.66 million in avoidable costs the program incurred in its first year — nearly a third again on top of the original $5.4 million authorization — is best read not as the price of trying something ambitious, but as the price of skipping ordinary diligence steps in service of showing the board fast results.

A reader should also take note of the leadership change that turned the program around. Bonnaire's success came not from a different strategic vision than the one Nwachukwu and Deshpande had originally approved, but from operational execution of that same vision: better real estate, stabilized staffing, extended hours, and a simple reminder system addressing a well-known driver of no-shows. The lesson is not that the original executive leadership lacked the right instincts, but that programs of this kind benefit from a dedicated operational owner, with clear authority and a defined review timeline, once the initial launch phase reveals gaps between plan and reality — and that the earlier such a review and reassignment happens, the less expensive the correction tends to be. Ogeechee Valley took roughly seven months to make that leadership change after the clinics opened; the system's own retrospective judgment, delivered candidly by its own executives, is that had the review happened sooner, several hundred thousand dollars of the first year's losses would likely have been avoidable.
