# High-Dosage Tutoring After the Fiscal Cliff: Evidence, Delivery Models, and a Sustainable Implementation Framework for Midwestern School Districts

**A White Paper of the Prairie States Education Research Collaborative**
Iowa City, Iowa

**Authors:**
Lourdes Sandoval-Whitmer, Ed.D., Executive Director
Tobias Njoroge, Director of Analytics
Hana Al-Sabbagh, Senior Research Associate
Corey Lindenbaum, Superintendent, Harlan Creek Community School District

## Abstract

Between 2021 and 2025, 118 school districts in Iowa, Nebraska, and South Dakota operated tutoring programs at scale, generating session-level records for 61,000 students that now constitute one of the largest regional tutoring datasets in the country. This white paper draws on those four years of data to answer the question now confronting district leaders: with federal relief funds expired and state grant capacity covering roughly a quarter of demand, which tutoring models are worth sustaining, and how should a mid-sized district build and fund one? The evidence points to a clear pattern. Delivery model matters less than three operational variables — attendance in scheduled sessions, tutor continuity, and integration with the school-day master schedule — but delivery model drives cost, and cost now determines survival. Teacher-led small-group tutoring produced the strongest results but at $4,600 per student is fiscally unsustainable for most districts; paraprofessional-led instruction with scripted materials and community college tutor corps models achieved 70 to 85 percent of the teacher-led effect at 26 to 40 percent of the cost. We present a complete implementation model for a representative 3,400-student district, including master schedule design, recruitment and retention strategy for a workforce with 41 percent annual turnover, transparent student selection criteria, and a fidelity monitoring system that does not overload principals. We conclude by addressing the four objections most frequently raised in the field: lost instructional time, selection equity, paraprofessional working conditions, and administrative burden. Tutoring in the post-relief era is achievable, but only for districts willing to treat it as a core scheduling and staffing problem rather than a grant-funded add-on.

## 1. The Problem: A Proven Intervention Meets a Funding Wall

### 1.1 What districts built, and what they are losing

Between 2021 and 2025, federal relief funding allowed districts across Iowa, Nebraska, and South Dakota to build tutoring programs at a scale the region had never attempted. The 118 districts contributing data to the Prairie States Education Research Collaborative served 61,000 students in structured tutoring over four years. For many of these districts — a majority of them rural, with enrollments under 1,500 — this was the first systematic academic intervention infrastructure they had ever operated outside of special education.

The programs worked, with important caveats detailed in Section 2. But the funding that built them is gone. When federal relief money expired, 74 of the 118 districts in our dataset — 63 percent — cut or eliminated tutoring for the 2025-26 school year. The state grant program created to soften the cliff was funded at $18 million and drew $71 million in requests, a nearly four-to-one oversubscription that tells district leaders everything they need to know about relying on competitive grants as a sustaining mechanism.

The result is a familiar and frustrating pattern in education: an intervention is validated at scale, and then dismantled at the moment its evidence base matures. The purpose of this paper is to prevent that outcome where prevention is possible — by identifying which models deliver acceptable results at sustainable cost, and by giving district leaders a concrete implementation and funding pathway.

### 1.2 The operating environment

Three features of the regional context shape everything that follows.

**Labor scarcity.** Tutor turnover across our dataset averaged 41 percent annually. Districts competing for certified teachers at $52 per hour are drawing from the same depleted pool that staffs their classrooms. Paraprofessionals at $22 per hour and community college tutor corps members at $17 per hour are more available but require training, scripted materials, and supervision to be effective.

**Attendance friction.** Median attendance in scheduled tutoring sessions across all models and years was 63 percent. A student scheduled for three sessions per week received, at the median, fewer than two. This single number explains more variation in outcomes than model choice does, and it is the central design constraint for any implementation plan.

**Fiscal reality.** Annual cost per student ranged from $1,180 to $4,600 depending on staffing model. A district that built its program at the top of that range with federal dollars cannot sustain it with general fund dollars. The question is not whether to cut costs but how to cut them without cutting effectiveness proportionally.

### 1.3 The question this paper answers

For a superintendent, chief academic officer, or board member, the practical question is: *Given what four years of regional data show, what tutoring model should my district run in 2026-27, how should it be scheduled and staffed, who should be selected, how will fidelity be monitored, and how will it be paid for?* Sections 2 through 5 answer that question in order. Section 6 addresses the objections we hear most often from the field. For legislators, Section 5.3 addresses what the state grant program's oversubscription implies for appropriations policy.

## 2. Four Years of Evidence: What 61,000 Student Records Show

### 2.1 The dataset

The Collaborative holds session-level tutoring records for 61,000 students in 118 districts across three states, spanning school years 2021-22 through 2024-25. Records include scheduled and attended sessions, tutor identity and credential type, delivery model, subject, grade level, and linked assessment outcomes where districts provided them. Participating districts range from under 300 students to over 30,000, with a median enrollment near 1,600 — meaning the dataset reflects the small and mid-sized districts that make up most of the region, not just its urban systems.

### 2.2 Attendance is the first-order variable

The single most consequential finding in our data is not about tutoring model. It is about attendance.

Median attendance in scheduled sessions was 63 percent, but the distribution around that median is wide and structured. Programs delivered *inside the school day, during a dedicated intervention block* achieved median attendance of 88 percent. Programs delivered *before or after school* achieved median attendance of 44 percent. Pull-out models during elective or specials periods fell in between, near 71 percent, with attendance eroding over the course of the year as pull-out fatigue set in among students and scheduling conflicts accumulated.

The outcome implications are direct. Across models, students who attended at least 80 percent of scheduled sessions showed learning gains roughly two and a half times those of students attending below 50 percent, controlling for prior achievement, grade, and district. A mediocre model with strong attendance outperformed a strong model with weak attendance in nearly every district-level comparison we ran.

**The implication for leaders is blunt: if your tutoring plan depends on students showing up outside the school day, plan for roughly half your dosage to evaporate.** The master schedule is the delivery mechanism. Section 4 treats it accordingly.

### 2.3 Tutor continuity is the second-order variable

Annual tutor turnover across the dataset averaged 41 percent, but its effects operate within the school year as well as across years. Students who kept the same tutor for a full year of sessions gained measurably more than students whose tutor changed mid-year, even at identical attendance levels. Mid-year tutor changes were most common in the vendor virtual model (where tutor assignment was often not stable by design) and the community college tutor corps (where academic calendars and course loads pulled tutors away).

Districts that stabilized their tutor workforce — through retention stipends, semester-length commitments with completion bonuses, or assignment of tutoring duties to existing staff — saw both continuity and outcomes improve. The cost of a retention stipend is small against the cost of a lost half-year of relationship and instructional coherence.

### 2.4 Dosage, group size, and materials

Three additional findings from the four-year record inform model design:

- **Dosage.** Three sessions per week of 30 to 45 minutes was the modal design and the point of diminishing returns. Programs scheduling five sessions per week did not outperform three-session programs once attendance was accounted for, largely because attendance decayed faster under heavier schedules.
- **Group size.** Groups of one to four students outperformed groups of five to eight by a meaningful margin in reading; in mathematics the penalty for groups of five to six was smaller. Groups above eight performed indistinguishably from ordinary small-class instruction and should not be labeled tutoring.
- **Materials.** For non-certified tutors, scripted, sequenced materials were not optional. Paraprofessional-led programs using structured curricula produced results approaching teacher-led programs; paraprofessional-led programs asked to improvise produced results near zero. Certified teachers showed effectiveness with or without scripts, which is part of what districts pay $52 per hour for.

### 2.5 Who benefited

Gains were largest for students starting between the 10th and 35th percentiles of prior achievement — students behind grade level but within reach of it. Students in the lowest decile benefited but typically needed tutoring layered with, not substituted for, more intensive services. Students already near grade level showed small gains that rarely justified the cost. These patterns drive the selection criteria in Section 4.3.

## 3. Comparing the Five Delivery Models on Cost and Results

### 3.1 The models

The 118 districts operated five broad delivery models:

1. **Teacher-led small group (in-day):** Certified teachers tutoring groups of one to four during a dedicated intervention block.
2. **Paraprofessional-led with scripted materials:** Trained paraprofessionals delivering structured, sequenced curricula, typically in groups of two to five, supervised by a certified interventionist.
3. **Community college tutor corps:** Education and liberal arts students from regional community colleges, trained and placed by partnership agreements, usually in groups of one to three.
4. **Vendor virtual tutoring:** Contracted online tutoring, one-to-one or one-to-two, delivered on district devices during or outside the school day.
5. **Hybrid models:** Combinations of the above, most commonly paraprofessional-led delivery with certified-teacher planning and progress monitoring.

### 3.2 Cost and outcome comparison

Table 1 summarizes the four-year comparison. Effectiveness is expressed as a relative index with teacher-led small group set at 100, based on assessment-linked gains adjusted for prior achievement, attendance, and grade.

**Table 1. Delivery model comparison, 2021-2025 (Prairie States Education Research Collaborative dataset)**

| Model | Hourly labor rate | Annual cost per student | Median session attendance | Effectiveness index (teacher-led = 100) | Cost per effectiveness point |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Teacher-led small group (in-day) | $52 | $3,900–$4,600 | 87% | 100 | ~$44 |
| Hybrid (para delivery, teacher oversight) | $22–$52 blended | $2,100–$2,700 | 84% | 85 | ~$28 |
| Paraprofessional-led, scripted | $22 | $1,600–$2,000 | 82% | 78 | ~$23 |
| Community college tutor corps | $17 | $1,180–$1,500 | 76% | 71 | ~$19 |
| Vendor virtual | $38 | $2,400–$3,100 | 51% | 52 | ~$53 |

*Note: Cost ranges reflect variation in dosage, group size, and overhead across districts. Attendance figures reflect the modal scheduling arrangement for each model; in-day scheduling raises attendance for any model.*

### 3.3 Reading the table honestly

Four conclusions follow, and leaders should hold all four at once.

**First, teacher-led tutoring is the most effective model and the least sustainable one.** At $3,900 to $4,600 per student, a district tutoring 15 percent of a 3,400-student enrollment would spend roughly $2 million to $2.3 million annually — an amount that was plausible under federal relief and is implausible from a general fund. Districts should reserve certified-teacher tutoring for the students with the most complex needs, not deploy it as the default.

**Second, the paraprofessional and tutor corps models are the value frontier.** At 70 to 85 percent of teacher-led effectiveness for 26 to 45 percent of the cost, these models deliver the most learning per dollar in the dataset — *provided* they are run with scripted materials, in-day scheduling, and active supervision. Stripped of those supports, their effectiveness collapses. The cost advantage is real, but it is conditional.

**Third, the hybrid model is the strongest all-around performer for a mid-sized district.** Paraprofessional delivery with certified-teacher planning and progress monitoring captured most of the teacher-led effect while cutting cost nearly in half. It also answers, structurally, the union concerns discussed in Section 6.3 by keeping instructional design in certified hands.

**Fourth, vendor virtual tutoring underperformed on both cost and results in our region.** Its effectiveness index of 52 is driven substantially by its 51 percent attendance — much of it was scheduled outside the school day or delivered in loosely supervised settings — and by unstable tutor assignment. Districts that ran virtual tutoring inside the school day with an adult monitor in the room did better, but at that point the model's supposed convenience advantage largely disappears while its $38 hourly rate does not. Virtual tutoring retains a legitimate niche: low-incidence subjects, very small districts that cannot recruit locally, and homebound students. As a primary model, the regional evidence does not support it.

### 3.4 The turnover tax

Every model pays the turnover tax, but at different rates. Certified teachers assigned tutoring duties within their contracts had the lowest turnover; tutor corps members the highest, driven by graduation and course conflicts rather than dissatisfaction. Districts should budget explicitly for turnover — recruitment, onboarding, and training for roughly 40 percent of the tutor workforce annually — and should treat retention spending (stipends, completion bonuses, schedule stability) as instructional spending, because in this dataset it functions as such.

## 4. An Implementation Model for a 3,400-Student District

This section responds directly to member districts' request for a worked implementation model. We use a representative district of 3,400 students — two elementary schools, one middle school, one high school — but every element scales up or down.

### 4.1 Program architecture and scope

**Target population:** 15 percent of enrollment, approximately 510 students, concentrated in grades K-8 with a smaller grade 9 credit-recovery-prevention cohort. Priority subjects: reading in K-5, mathematics in 6-9.

**Model:** Hybrid — paraprofessional and tutor corps delivery using scripted materials, with certified interventionists (one per building, drawn from or added to existing Title I / MTSS staffing) responsible for grouping, progress monitoring, and tutor coaching. A small certified-teacher-led strand (roughly 60 students) serves the highest-need cases.

**Dosage:** Three 35-minute sessions per week, groups of three to four, delivered inside the school day. Minimum program length of 20 weeks; students exit or continue based on progress data at trimester checkpoints.

**Staffing requirement:** Approximately 16 to 18 tutors (paraprofessionals and tutor corps members combined, most part-time), four building interventionists (partially reallocated from existing roles), and 0.5 FTE district coordination.

**Budget:** Approximately $950,000 to $1.1 million annually — roughly $1,900 to $2,150 per served student, or about 2 to 2.5 percent of a typical general fund budget for a district this size. Section 5 addresses how to assemble that amount.

### 4.2 Master schedule: the load-bearing wall

Because in-day scheduling is worth roughly 40 attendance percentage points, the master schedule is where implementation succeeds or fails. Three design patterns from high-attendance districts in our dataset:

**Elementary: the dedicated intervention block ("WIN time").** Each grade level receives a 35-minute "What I Need" block during which no new core instruction occurs anywhere in the grade. Tutored students attend tutoring; other students receive enrichment, extension, or independent practice. Because no student misses new content, the lost-instruction objection (Section 6.1) is structurally neutralized. Staggering blocks across grade levels lets a single tutor serve four to five groups daily, which is essential to the cost model.

**Middle school: the flex period.** A daily flex/advisory period of 30 to 40 minutes, built into the bell schedule, serves the same function. Districts without an existing flex period can create one by trimming three to four minutes from each of eight periods — a change requiring board and, where applicable, association consultation, but not additional minutes in the school day.

**High school (grade 9 strand):** Tutoring is scheduled as a support lab paired with Algebra I or English 9, appearing on the student's schedule like any course. This avoids the pull-out stigma and attendance decay documented in our high school data.

**Scheduling rules from the data:** never schedule tutoring against lunch or the only recess (attendance collapses); never rotate tutoring through different periods week to week (tutors and teachers cannot plan); protect the block from assemblies and picture day with the same rigor applied to state testing windows.

### 4.3 Tutor recruitment and retention against 41 percent turnover

Recruitment channels, in order of yield in our member districts:

1. **Existing paraprofessionals** offered extended hours or reassigned time, with a tutoring differential above base para pay. These staff already know the students, the building, and the calendar. This is the highest-retention channel in the dataset.
2. **Community college partnership** with the regional community college, structured as a formal agreement: the college provides candidates from education pathways, the district provides paid placement hours that count toward practicum requirements where the college allows. Semester-length commitments with a completion bonus (we recommend $250–$400) measurably reduced mid-semester attrition among member districts using them.
3. **Community members** — retired teachers, degreed adults seeking part-time work — hired at para-equivalent rates for tutoring-only roles.

**Retention design:** (a) pay at or slightly above the regional market ($22–$24 for paras in a tutoring role, $17–$19 for corps members); (b) guarantee schedule stability — same students, same times, all semester; (c) provide real training (two days pre-service, monthly one-hour coaching from the building interventionist) because tutors who feel effective stay; (d) build tutor continuity into the budget as a line item, not a hope. Districts implementing this package reported turnover in the mid-20s rather than the low 40s.

### 4.4 Student selection: transparent criteria, reviewed for equity

Selection should be criterion-based, documented, and audited. The recommended sequence:

1. **Screen universally** using the district's existing benchmark assessment (FastBridge, MAP, or state interim), fall administration.
2. **Prioritize the 10th–35th percentile band** in the target subject — the range where the four-year data show the largest gains — plus any student flagged by the building MTSS team regardless of score.
3. **Layer, don't displace:** students below the 10th percentile receive tutoring *in addition to* Tier 3 or special education services where appropriate, per IEP teams; tutoring never substitutes for specially designed instruction.
4. **Audit the roster** each trimester against building demographics — by race/ethnicity, IEP status, EL status, and free/reduced eligibility — and require the interventionist to document any material divergence between the eligible pool and the served roster. Section 6.2 explains why this step is non-negotiable.
5. **Exit on evidence:** students meeting benchmark at a trimester checkpoint exit with a two-week transition monitor; open seats are filled from the standing eligibility list so capacity is never idle.

### 4.5 Fidelity monitoring without burying principals

The reporting-burden objection (Section 6.4) is legitimate: several early member districts built monitoring systems that consumed hours of principal time weekly and were quietly abandoned by December. The durable systems in our dataset share three features:

- **Automate attendance.** Tutors log session attendance in the student information system or a simple shared form at the point of delivery — 60 seconds per session. Attendance is the single best fidelity proxy in the entire dataset; if a district monitors only one thing, monitor this.
- **Push monitoring down to the interventionist.** The building interventionist, not the principal, reviews attendance weekly, observes each tutor monthly against a one-page look-for checklist, and reviews progress data at trimester checkpoints. The principal receives a one-page dashboard monthly: attendance rate, students on/off track, staffing status, flags requiring principal action.
- **Set intervention triggers, not just reports.** A student below 80 percent attendance for two consecutive weeks triggers a family contact and schedule review. A group showing no progress at a checkpoint triggers an observation and materials check. Monitoring exists to trigger action, not to accumulate files.

Total principal time under this design: under one hour per month. Total interventionist time: three to four hours per week, which is why the interventionist role is budgeted rather than assumed.

## 5. Paying for It: A Post-Relief Funding Model

### 5.1 The braided-funding structure

No single post-relief source covers a $1 million program; four braided sources can. For the 3,400-student model district:

**Table 2. Illustrative funding braid, 3,400-student district (~$1.05M annual program cost)**

| Source | Approximate share | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Title I, Part A (schoolwide programs) | 30–35% | Tutoring is an allowable, evidence-aligned use; requires plan amendment |
| General fund (reallocation, not new money) | 25–30% | Primarily reallocated interventionist FTE and para hours already budgeted |
| State grant program | 15–25% | Treat as variable, not base, funding given oversubscription (Sec. 5.3) |
| At-risk/dropout prevention allowable growth or state categorical funds | 10–15% | Where state finance rules permit (Iowa SBRC at-risk mechanism, comparable NE/SD categories) |
| Partnerships (community college cost-share, local foundations) | 5–10% | Corps stipends and completion bonuses are attractive philanthropic asks |

*Note: Shares are illustrative; districts should map against their own Title I allocation and state finance rules with their AEA/ESU and business office.*

### 5.2 Design rules for fiscal durability

Districts that survived the cliff in our dataset followed three rules. **First, base funding covers base program:** the core hybrid model must be sustainable on Title I, general fund, and categorical sources alone. Grant funds expand capacity (more students, the certified-teacher strand); they never carry the core. **Second, staff with people you would employ anyway where possible:** extending existing paraprofessional hours is fiscally and organizationally cheaper than net-new hiring. **Third, publish the cost-per-outcome math to the board annually.** At roughly $2,000 per served student, tutoring compares favorably with retention, summer credit recovery, and remedial course sequences — but only boards shown the comparison will protect the line item in a tight budget year.

### 5.3 A note for legislators

The state grant program drew $71 million in requests against $18 million in funding. That 4:1 ratio is not evidence of district opportunism; the requests in our dataset map closely onto documented, previously-operating programs facing termination. Three policy observations follow from the regional data:

1. **Oversubscription at this scale converts a grant program into a lottery,** which pushes districts toward the rational decision not to build durable programs on it. If the state's intent is sustained tutoring capacity, a formula-based or multi-year allocation serving the paraprofessional/tutor-corps cost tier (~$1,200–$2,000 per student) would fund three to four times the students per dollar that teacher-led programs would.
2. **Conditioning funds on in-day delivery and attendance reporting** would be the single highest-leverage quality requirement the state could impose, at essentially zero cost, given the attendance findings in Section 2.2.
3. **A modest state investment in the community college pipeline** — practicum credit agreements, corps stipend matching — would address the labor constraint that no district can solve alone.

## 6. Answering the Field's Objections

### 6.1 "Tutoring steals instructional time."

This objection is correct about badly scheduled tutoring and incorrect about well-scheduled tutoring. Pull-out models that remove students from core instruction do trade one form of instruction for another, and our data show the trade is often a poor one — particularly when students miss new content and fall further behind in class while catching up in tutoring.

The dedicated intervention block eliminates the trade. When no new core instruction occurs during the block anywhere in the grade level, tutored students give up enrichment or independent practice time, not core content. The relevant comparison is then between 105 minutes per week of targeted small-group instruction and 105 minutes of independent practice — a comparison the tutoring data win decisively for students below benchmark. Districts unwilling or unable to build a protected block should be candid that they are choosing a lower-attendance, higher-tradeoff version of the program, and should size their expectations accordingly.

### 6.2 "Selection isn't equitable."

The concern takes two forms, and both appear in our data. In some districts, tutoring seats flowed disproportionately to students whose families advocated effectively — often not the students furthest behind. In others, tutoring rosters over-identified certain student groups relative to the eligible pool, raising stigma and disproportionality concerns.

Both failure modes are failures of process, not of tutoring, and both are addressed by the selection design in Section 4.3: universal screening (so no one needs an advocate to be seen), criterion-based eligibility (so seats follow data, not squeaky wheels), and a trimester roster audit against building demographics with documented reconciliation. We recommend districts report roster demographics to the board annually alongside outcomes. Transparency is the equity mechanism; districts that published their selection criteria reported markedly fewer parent complaints than districts that treated selection as an internal black box.

One further equity point deserves emphasis: the largest inequity in the current moment is between districts that sustain tutoring and districts that cannot. The 74 districts that cut programs serve students no less behind than the 44 that kept them. This is the strongest argument for the state-level formula funding discussed in Section 5.3.

### 6.3 "Paraprofessional tutoring raises union and working-conditions questions."

Associations in several member districts have raised two legitimate questions: whether paraprofessionals delivering instruction are performing certified work, and whether tutoring duties are being added to para workloads without compensation.

The hybrid model answers the first question structurally. Instructional design, student grouping, progress monitoring, and diagnostic decisions remain with certified staff; paraprofessionals deliver scripted, teacher-supervised instruction — a division of labor consistent with how paras already function in Title I and special education settings across all three states. Districts should document this division in writing and review it with association leadership before launch, not after a grievance.

The second question should be answered with money and schedule, not argument: tutoring hours are additional paid hours (or reassigned hours, by agreement) at a differential above base para pay, with training provided on paid time. In our dataset, districts that negotiated the tutoring role openly with their associations reported both smoother launches and better para retention. The 41 percent turnover problem and the union question have the same solution: treat the tutoring role as a real job with real pay, training, and stability.

### 6.4 "Principals can't absorb another reporting burden."

They cannot, and the monitoring system in Section 4.5 is designed so they do not have to. The design principles bear repeating because they generalize beyond tutoring: automate data capture at the point of delivery; assign routine monitoring to a designated role (the interventionist) rather than the building leader; give the principal a one-page exception dashboard monthly; and define triggers so that data review produces action rather than filing. Districts implementing this design reported principal time costs under one hour monthly. Districts that instead asked principals to compile weekly narrative reports abandoned monitoring by mid-year — and unmonitored programs in our dataset drifted measurably on attendance and group size within a semester. The choice is not between burden and no burden; it is between a small, well-placed burden and an unmonitored program that quietly stops working.

## 7. Conclusion

Four years and 61,000 student records yield a conclusion that is neither triumphant nor despairing. Tutoring works in this region when — and largely only when — it is delivered inside the school day, by tutors who stay, in small groups, with structured materials and someone watching the attendance data. Delivery model matters chiefly through cost, and cost now decides which programs live. The teacher-led model that federal dollars made possible is, for most districts, over; the hybrid and paraprofessional models that capture most of its effect at half its cost are not, and the community college pipeline offers the region a labor solution suited to its geography.

The 74 districts that cut tutoring for 2025-26 did not conclude it failed. They concluded they could not pay for the version they had built. This paper's argument is that a different version — deliberately scheduled, honestly staffed, transparently governed, and braided into base funding — is within reach of an ordinary 3,400-student district at roughly two percent of its budget. Whether the region sustains that capacity is now a matter of local scheduling and staffing decisions, and of a state funding structure that stops asking four dollars of documented need to compete for one dollar of grant. The evidence has done its part. The rest is implementation.
