# Larkspur Markets, Inc. — Internal Memorandum Packet

---

**TO:** All Store Directors; Corporate Department Heads
**FROM:** Deshawn Whitfield, President
**DATE:** August 10, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Launch of the Larkspur Digital Loyalty and Targeted-Pricing Program; Closure of Four Stores; Reformat of North Adams and Bellows Falls

This memo formally opens the program we have been building toward for eighteen months, and it also carries news that will be hard for some of the people receiving it. I want both parts stated plainly and in one place, because they are parts of a single decision about how this company survives the next five years.

First, the program. Effective this week, Larkspur is retiring the weekly paper circular as our primary promotional vehicle and moving to a digital loyalty and targeted-pricing platform across all continuing stores. Enrollment kiosks, app onboarding materials, and shelf-tag conversions will roll out on the schedule that Renata Kovalenko's team distributed to store directors on Friday, beginning with the Pioneer Valley stores in September and completing the chain by mid-November. The circular does not disappear overnight; it will run in reduced form through the end of the calendar year and be discontinued in January. Yuki Tanabe-Ortiz's status memo on the six-store pilot is attached to this packet, and I encourage every director to read it carefully, because the pilot results are the reason we are doing this. Average basket in the pilot stores rose 4.1 percent against control, and the customers who enrolled in the loyalty program shopped us more often and traded up more consistently than anything the circular ever produced. There are complications in the vendor funding picture, which Padraig Malloy addresses in his memo, and I am not going to pretend the economics are simple. They are not. But the direction is right.

I want to be honest about why the direction matters so much. Our gross margin has fallen from 26.8 percent to 24.1 percent in two years. That is not a rounding problem; on our volume it is roughly $16 million of annual gross profit that we no longer have. Some of that erosion came from cost inflation we could not fully pass through, but a meaningful share came from the circular model itself — deep, undifferentiated promotions that gave our best discounts to customers who would have shopped us anyway, and that trained everyone else to cherry-pick. The targeted model lets us put the right price in front of the right household and stop giving away margin indiscriminately. We are not the first regional grocer to make this move, but we are making it later than we should have, and the delay has narrowed our room for error. The vendor implementation slipped from March to August, and the capital estimate has risen from $5.4 million to $7.9 million. Padraig's memo explains both, and explains how we are absorbing them.

Second, the closures. After extended review by the executive team and the board, we will close four stores: Athol, Ware, North Adams, and Bellows Falls. All four lost money in every quarter of 2026, and the losses were not marginal or seasonal. We examined every alternative we could construct — remodel scenarios, labor-model changes, assortment resets — and none of them produced a credible path to profitability at the current footprints. The Athol and Ware closures are permanent. North Adams and Bellows Falls will close in their current buildings but reopen in 2027 as 12,000-square-foot small-format stores, a concept built around fresh, prepared foods, and a curated center store, supported by the digital pricing platform. We believe those two communities can support Larkspur at the right size and cost structure, and we intend to prove it.

The closures affect 310 employees. Every one of them will have recall rights at other Larkspur stores, and Estelle Nwachukwu's memo in this packet sets out the transfer, severance, and scheduling policy in full. I ask store directors at receiving stores to treat incoming transfers as colleagues joining the team, not as a staffing problem being handed to you. Many of these people have been with us for a decade or more. How we handle the next ninety days will be remembered inside this company for a long time, and it will also be watched closely by UFCW Local 1459, whose contract covering 2,400 of our employees reopens in January. I have communicated the closure decision to the Local's leadership, and I expect there will be disagreement about process and timing. That is their right and their job. Ours is to execute this transition with as much decency as the situation allows.

Closure timing: Athol and Ware will hold final sale days on October 17. North Adams and Bellows Falls will close November 7, with reformat construction beginning immediately thereafter. Renata will run the wind-down playbook — inventory drawdown, transfer logistics, fixture disposition — and store directors at the four locations will hear from her office within forty-eight hours of this memo.

I will close with this. Larkspur has operated in these valleys since 1961, and nobody in this building takes lightly the decision to leave two towns and shrink our presence in two others. But a 24.1 percent gross margin cannot fund 3,100 jobs indefinitely, and four stores losing money every quarter cannot be carried by the other twenty-four forever. The digital program, the closures, and the small-format reopenings are one plan, not three. I am asking every director and every department head to execute it as one plan.

**TO:** Deshawn Whitfield, President; Executive Committee; Board Finance Committee
**FROM:** Padraig Malloy, Chief Financial Officer
**DATE:** August 14, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Revised Capital Plan for the Digital Program; FY28 Margin Forecast Incorporating the Overrun and Vendor Funding Reduction

This memo revises the capital plan approved by the board in November 2025 and restates the FY28 margin forecast to reflect two developments: the $2.5 million capital overrun on the digital platform, and the reduction in vendor promotional funding identified during the pilot. It also incorporates the closure-related charges, including the lease termination demands at Athol and Ware. My intent is to give the committee one document that shows the full financial shape of the program as it now stands, rather than continuing to track these items in separate updates.

**Capital plan.** The original estimate for the digital loyalty and targeted-pricing platform was $5.4 million: $2.9 million in vendor licensing, integration, and implementation; $1.6 million in store-level hardware, including electronic shelf labels for high-velocity sections, kiosks, and network upgrades; and $0.9 million in internal IT and contingency. The revised estimate is $7.9 million. The increase breaks down as follows. Approximately $1.1 million reflects the vendor implementation slip from March to August — extended integration work on our point-of-sale environment, which proved substantially more customized than the vendor's discovery phase captured, plus five additional months of parallel-systems costs and consultant time. Approximately $0.8 million reflects scope that should have been in the original estimate and was not: chiefly, the data warehouse work required to make household-level targeting function against our legacy transaction logs. The remaining $0.6 million is expanded shelf-label coverage, which the pilot demonstrated is necessary rather than optional; stores running targeted prices on paper tags generated pricing-integrity errors at a rate we cannot accept from either a customer-trust or a weights-and-measures standpoint. I want to be direct with the committee: the original estimate was too thin, and the vendor's slip compounded a problem that began in our own scoping. I have restructured the remaining spend under a fixed-fee amendment with the vendor, with $400,000 held back against milestone completion, and I am confident in the $7.9 million figure. We are funding the increase from the revolver; year-end leverage rises to 2.6x EBITDA from the planned 2.3x, still inside covenant.

**Vendor promotional funding.** The pilot surfaced a structural issue we had modeled too optimistically. A substantial portion of our promotional income — historically about $14 million a year — is tied contractually to circular placement, display commitments, and ad-block features. Under the targeted model, fewer items qualify for those placements, and several of our largest CPG partners have declined to shift funding into targeted offers at equivalent levels, at least in year one. The pilot experience, annualized across the chain, implies a reduction of $1.2 million per year in vendor funding. Yuki's team is negotiating replacement structures — data-sharing agreements and targeted-offer co-funding — and the industry trajectory suggests vendors will follow shoppers into digital over time. But I am not booking recovery we have not contracted, so the forecast below carries the full $1.2 million reduction in FY27 and FY28.

**Closure economics.** The four closing stores generated a combined operating loss of $3.4 million on an annualized basis. Exiting them removes that loss but triggers one-time charges: severance and transition costs of approximately $1.9 million per Estelle Nwachukwu's policy memo; inventory markdown and wind-down costs of roughly $0.7 million; and lease exposure. The Athol and Ware landlords have demanded termination payments totaling $2.1 million against a combined remaining obligation of $4.8 million. Counsel believes both demands are negotiable and I have authorized settlement authority up to $1.5 million combined; the forecast conservatively carries the full $2.1 million. The North Adams and Bellows Falls reformats require an additional $3.6 million of capital in FY27, which was in the November plan and is unchanged. Total one-time charges of approximately $4.7 million will be taken in Q4 FY26.

**FY28 forecast.** The November plan projected FY28 gross margin of 26.3 percent, recovering roughly 220 basis points from the current 24.1 percent, driven by targeted-pricing margin capture, shrink reduction from the closures, and mix improvement. The revised forecast is 25.7 percent. The reduction reflects the vendor funding loss (approximately 20 basis points on a smaller revenue base), a more conservative ramp of targeted-pricing benefits given the compressed rollout timeline (approximately 30 basis points deferred into FY29), and the revenue base itself shrinking to approximately $585 million with the closures net of the small-format reopenings and expected transfer of sales to nearby Larkspur stores, which we model at 22 percent recapture for Athol and Ware. FY28 EBITDA under the revised forecast is $19.8 million versus $23.1 million in the November plan. I want the committee to see clearly that the program still works: even at 25.7 percent, we recover roughly $9 million of annual gross profit against doing nothing, and doing nothing was never a stable option, because the margin decline of the past two years shows no sign of arresting on its own. But the cushion has thinned, and the January contract reopener with UFCW Local 1459 now sits closer to our covenant headroom than I would like. I will present labor-cost scenarios to the committee in October.

I recommend the committee approve the revised capital authorization of $7.9 million and the settlement authority described above at the August 26 meeting.

**TO:** All Store Directors; District Managers; Human Resources Staff
**FROM:** Estelle Nwachukwu, Vice President of Human Resources
**DATE:** August 17, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Transfer, Severance, and Scheduling Policy for Employees of the Athol, Ware, North Adams, and Bellows Falls Stores

This memo sets company policy for the 310 employees affected by the store closures announced in President Whitfield's memo of August 10. It applies to both union-represented and non-represented employees; where the collective bargaining agreement with UFCW Local 1459 provides greater rights, the agreement governs. I have reviewed this policy with the Local's business agents, and while the union has reserved its position on the adequacy of the notice period — a matter President Whitfield addresses separately — the mechanics described here reflect our contractual obligations and, in several respects, exceed them. I expect every director and manager receiving this memo to apply it consistently. Affected employees will receive individual meetings and a written summary in the two weeks following this memo; no employee should learn the details of their own situation from rumor or from a bulletin board.

**Transfer rights and process.** Every affected employee has the right to transfer to an open position at another Larkspur store for which they are qualified. We currently have approximately 240 open or soon-to-open positions across the continuing 24 stores, concentrated in the Pioneer Valley and Berkshire districts, and we will hold routine external hiring at receiving stores through November 30 so that transfers get first access. Transfer selection will follow contractual seniority within classification for represented employees. Employees transferring to a store more than fifteen miles from their current store will receive a one-time transition payment of $750 for full-time and $400 for part-time employees. Transferring employees keep their seniority, their accrued vacation and sick time, their current wage rate, and their benefits without interruption. There is no probationary period at the receiving store. Employees at North Adams and Bellows Falls may elect to transfer now or to take layoff with priority recall rights to the small-format stores when they reopen in 2027; those stores will employ roughly 35 people each, against current staffs of about 70, so I want to be honest that recall to the reformatted stores cannot be guaranteed for everyone who waits, and HR counselors will walk each employee through that choice individually.

**Severance.** Employees who do not transfer — whether because no suitable position exists within a reasonable distance, or because they decline transfer — will receive severance of one week of pay per full year of service, with a minimum of four weeks for full-time employees and two weeks for part-time employees, plus payout of accrued vacation. Employees with ten or more years of service receive an additional two weeks. The company will pay the employer share of health coverage for three months following separation for employees enrolled in our plan, and we have engaged MassHire and Vermont Department of Labor rapid-response teams to run job-search and retraining sessions at all four stores in September and October. Severance for represented employees will be paid per the contract where the contract applies, and per this policy where this policy is more generous. Acceptance of severance does not extinguish recall rights, which run for eighteen months under the contract; an employee recalled within that window who accepts the recall will not be required to repay severance, a term we agreed to with the Local and which goes beyond the contract's language.

**Scheduling during the wind-down.** Between now and the closure dates, the four stores must remain fully operational, and customers and employees alike deserve stores that do not feel abandoned. Directors at the closing stores will maintain schedules at current average hours through the final two weeks of operation; we will not wind hours down early, and any employee who stays through their store's final day receives a stay bonus of $500 for full-time and $250 for part-time employees. I recognize the tension in asking people to give their best effort to a store that is closing, and the stay bonus is a concrete acknowledgment of that. Employees who secure outside employment before the closure date and resign with at least one week of notice will still receive their severance; we will not penalize people for looking after themselves. At receiving stores, directors must integrate transferred employees into schedules per their seniority from their first week — transferred employees are not to be slotted into leftover shifts behind existing staff of lower seniority. District managers will audit receiving-store schedules through December, and I will treat violations of this provision as a serious performance matter.

**Points of contact and conduct.** HR business partners have been assigned to each closing store and will be on site at least three days a week through closure: Marisol Beauvais for Athol and Ware, Tomasz Sikora for North Adams and Bellows Falls. All media inquiries go to the corporate communications office; all inquiries from the Local's representatives should be handled cooperatively and referred to me or to labor counsel where they concern the notice-period dispute. Finally, a word to managers everywhere in the chain: 310 people are absorbing hard news, and roughly 2,800 more are watching how we treat them. Answer questions honestly, escalate what you cannot answer, and do not speculate about matters — including the January contract reopener — that are not yours to characterize. The measure of this policy will not be the memo; it will be how it is carried out in each store, one conversation at a time.

**TO:** Deshawn Whitfield, President; Executive Committee
**FROM:** Yuki Tanabe-Ortiz, Senior Vice President of Merchandising
**DATE:** July 31, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Digital Loyalty and Targeted-Pricing Pilot — Final Results, Six Stores, February–July 2026

This memo reports final results from the six-store pilot of the digital loyalty and targeted-pricing platform, covering the twenty-two weeks from February 16 through July 18. The pilot stores were Greenfield, Northampton, Amherst, Holyoke, Pittsfield, and Brattleboro, selected to represent our volume tiers and both states. Results were measured against a matched control group of six stores adjusted for weather, holiday shift, and district-level trend. My summary judgment up front: the platform works, the customer response exceeded our plan, and the program should proceed to chainwide rollout — but the vendor funding problem it exposed is real, structural, and should be built into the financial plan rather than treated as a negotiation to be won later.

The headline result is basket. Average basket across the six pilot stores rose 4.1 percent against control, worth approximately $2.6 million in annualized sales across the six stores and, extrapolated with appropriate haircuts for store mix, roughly $9 to $11 million chainwide. The gain was not uniform. Greenfield and Northampton, our strongest loyalty-enrollment stores, ran above 5 percent; Holyoke ran at 2.3 percent, and the difference tracks enrollment almost exactly. That is the most important operational finding of the pilot: the program's value is a direct function of enrollment, because unenrolled customers see neither the targeted offers nor the personalized pricing, and the fading circular gives them progressively less reason to shop us. Enrollment across the six stores reached 61 percent of transactions by week twenty-two, against a plan of 50 percent, but the ramp took a full twelve weeks and required sustained front-end effort — cashier prompts, kiosk staffing on weekends, and a $5 enrollment credit that we should carry into the chainwide rollout despite its cost. Where store leadership treated enrollment as a daily operational metric, it moved; where it was treated as a marketing program running in the background, it stalled. Renata Kovalenko and I have agreed that enrollment rate will appear on the store scorecard from the first week of each store's rollout.

Margin performance in the pilot stores was encouraging but partly masked. Gross margin in the six stores ran 90 basis points above control, driven by three mechanisms: reduced blanket promotional depth, since targeted offers went to price-sensitive households rather than to everyone; improved mix, as the personalization engine proved effective at moving enrolled households into private label and into perishable categories, where fresh department sales rose 6.8 percent; and a measurable reduction in cherry-picking, with the share of transactions consisting predominantly of deal items falling by roughly a third. Offsetting these gains is the vendor funding issue, which I want to describe precisely because it will otherwise be summarized in ways that make it sound either smaller or more fixable than it is. Our promotional income agreements with major suppliers are built on the circular: page placement, feature blocks, and display performance tied to advertised items. In the pilot stores, the number of items receiving qualifying circular placement fell by roughly 60 percent, and the associated funding fell with it. Annualized and extended chainwide, the reduction is $1.2 million per year. I have now had direct conversations with our eight largest suppliers. Three are prepared to shift meaningful dollars into targeted-offer co-funding and audience-data agreements in 2027; two are willing but constrained by national trade-spend structures that treat digital regional programs as an exception requiring headquarters approval; three are, candidly, using the transition as an opportunity to reduce total spend with us. I believe we recover half to two-thirds of the lost funding within two to three years as digital co-funding matures, and the audience data we now hold gives us something to sell that we never had before. But Padraig is right to carry the full reduction in the forecast, and I have told him so.

Two operational findings deserve the committee's attention before rollout. First, pricing integrity. In the weeks before electronic shelf labels were fully deployed, the pilot stores ran targeted prices against paper tags, and mismatches between shelf price and register price generated customer complaints and two weights-and-measures inquiries in Massachusetts. Full shelf-label coverage in high-velocity sections is a prerequisite, not an enhancement, and the additional capital Padraig has included for expanded coverage reflects my strong recommendation. Second, the older-customer segment. Roughly 15 percent of pilot-store customers, skewing older, neither enrolled nor adapted, and their trip frequency declined 3 percent. We mitigated this with an in-store "everyone price" program on 150 staple items — good everyday prices requiring no enrollment, no app, and no card — and frequency in that segment stabilized after its introduction in week fourteen. The everyone-price program should launch chainwide simultaneously with the loyalty rollout, not after, and it should feature in our public communication about the transition, particularly in communities where the circular has been a fixture for decades.

On the closures, one merchandising note: none of the four closing stores was a pilot store, and I want the record clear that the closure decision rests on their sustained operating losses, not on any pilot outcome. The small-format concept for North Adams and Bellows Falls will, however, be built natively on the digital platform, with no circular legacy at all, and I regard those two stores as the purest test of where this company's merchandising model is heading.

My recommendation is to proceed to chainwide rollout on the schedule Renata's team has published, with the enrollment credit, the everyone-price program, and full shelf-label coverage as non-negotiable elements, and with vendor funding recovery pursued vigorously but planned conservatively.

**TO:** Deshawn Whitfield, President; File
**FROM:** Deshawn Whitfield, President
**DATE:** September 4, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Response to UFCW Local 1459 Formal Objection Regarding the Closure Notice Period

*The following is the text of my response, sent this date to the Local's president, with copies to Estelle Nwachukwu and labor counsel. It is included in this packet so that the executive team has the company's position in full.*

Dear President Arceneaux:

I write in response to the Local's formal objection of August 24 regarding the notice period for the closures of the Athol, Ware, North Adams, and Bellows Falls stores. I have read the objection carefully, discussed it with Estelle Nwachukwu and with counsel, and I want to respond to its substance directly rather than through a purely legal reply, because the relationship between this company and Local 1459 is fifty years old and deserves a direct answer.

The objection makes three arguments: that the interval between the August 10 announcement and the October 17 closures of Athol and Ware falls short of the ninety days the Local views as the appropriate standard; that the company failed to engage in effects bargaining before announcing the closures; and that the compressed timeline prejudices members' ability to evaluate transfer options before severance elections come due. I will take each in turn.

On the notice period. The Athol and Ware closures provide sixty-eight days of notice; North Adams and Bellows Falls provide eighty-nine. Our collective bargaining agreement, at Article 22, requires sixty days' notice of store closure, and the federal WARN Act does not apply to any of the four stores individually, as none reaches the hundred-employee threshold, though we have chosen to follow WARN-style practices in our notifications to the state rapid-response agencies regardless. We are therefore in compliance with, and beyond, every obligation we have. I recognize that compliance is not the whole of the Local's argument — the objection contends that ninety days is the standard a responsible employer should meet. I will tell you honestly why we did not extend the Athol and Ware dates. Those two stores are losing money at a rate that worsens each additional month they operate, and — more importantly from your members' standpoint — the open positions at receiving stores are available now and will not remain open indefinitely. We are holding external hiring at receiving stores through November 30 to protect transfer access. Stretching the closure timeline to ninety days at Athol and Ware would have pushed transfer decisions into a period when fewer positions remain and would have run the wind-down into the holiday season, which serves no one. The timeline was set with the transfer window in mind, not in disregard of it.

On effects bargaining. The decision to close the stores is, under settled law and under our agreement's management-rights clause, a decision the company is entitled to make. The effects of that decision on your members are a mandatory subject of bargaining, and we do not dispute that for a moment. Estelle Nwachukwu met with your business agents on August 12 and 15, before the transfer and severance policy issued, and several provisions of that policy — including the agreement that recalled employees will not repay severance, and the transition payments for long-distance transfers — came directly from those discussions and exceed the contract. If the Local's position is that those sessions did not constitute adequate effects bargaining, we are prepared to meet formally on effects at any time, beginning next week, and I have instructed Estelle to make herself and counsel available on the Local's schedule. What we cannot do is treat effects bargaining as a mechanism to reopen the closure decision itself, and I want to be candid that we will resist any framing that conflates the two.

On the prejudice argument, I think it is the strongest of the three, and we are prepared to act on it. The concern, as I understand it, is that members at Athol and Ware must elect between transfer and severance without sufficient time to evaluate commute, scheduling, and family circumstances. In response, the company will extend the severance election deadline for Athol and Ware employees from September 26 to October 10, and will further permit any employee who transfers and then concludes within thirty days that the transfer is not workable to revert to layoff with full severance as though they had elected it originally. No member should feel forced into an irrevocable choice on incomplete information, and this modification removes that pressure. Estelle will issue the revised terms to affected members this week.

I want to say one more thing, outside the four corners of the objection. The Local has noted, and your objection implies, that these closures arrive four months before the contract reopener. I understand the suspicion that the timing is tactical. It is not. The four stores lost money in every quarter of this year; the decision was driven by that arithmetic and by the capital demands of the digital program, and delaying it into the bargaining period would have made the closures a bargaining chip, which would have been worse for everyone. The company I intend to bring to the table in January is one that has been straight with the Local all year — about the closures, about the margin position, and about the investments we are making to secure the other twenty-four stores and the 2,800 jobs in them. The reformatted North Adams and Bellows Falls stores will be union stores, and recall rights to them will be honored in full.

We are prepared to meet on effects bargaining the week of September 8. Estelle's office will contact yours Monday to schedule.

Sincerely,

Deshawn Whitfield
President, Larkspur Markets, Inc.
