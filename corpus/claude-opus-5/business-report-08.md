## 1. Purpose and Scope

This report reviews manufacturing performance across Vandalia Industrial Group's four production sites for the first half of fiscal 2026 and identifies the operating and financial exposures the company carries into the second half and into fiscal 2027. It responds to the Chief Executive's 12 June request for plant-level detail rather than consolidated reporting, and it is submitted to the Audit Committee as well as the Executive Committee because of the Occupational Safety and Health Administration inspection conducted at the Springfield, Ohio plant in March 2026 and the proposed penalty arising from it.

The report covers five subjects the Executive Committee asked to see addressed directly: the operating performance and principal problem at each of the four plants; the March inspection findings and their implications for the company as a whole; input cost and staffing pressure; the loss of an appliance customer at Muncie, Indiana; and the delayed startup and yield shortfall of the new coating line at Chambersburg, Pennsylvania.

The scope is manufacturing operations. Commercial strategy, product development, and capital structure appear only where they bear on the operating picture. Financial figures are unaudited management figures drawn from the June close and are stated in U.S. dollars.

## 2. Executive Summary

Vandalia shipped $271.4 million in the first half of fiscal 2026 against $259.0 million in the prior-year half, a 4.8 percent increase carried almost entirely by price and mix rather than volume. Gross margin fell from 21.4 percent to 17.2 percent and operating income fell from $23.9 million to $14.6 million. The decline is not the result of a single event. It is the accumulation of four plant-level problems occurring at the same time as a step change in resin cost and a labor market that will not fill 121 open production positions.

Huntington, the largest plant and the site attached to headquarters, ran overall equipment effectiveness of 61 percent against a 72 percent target. A rebuild of Press 14 that was scoped at nine weeks took twenty, and the plant absorbed 340 hours of unplanned downtime tied to that project and its aftermath. Scrap reached 4.9 percent against a 3.0 percent standard. Huntington is the company's largest single earnings variance at $6.3 million unfavorable to plan.

Springfield drew three serious citations and an $84,600 proposed penalty from a March 3–6 inspection and is running a recordable injury rate of 4.8 against a company rate of 2.1. The citations concern hazardous energy control, machine guarding, and respiratory protection. The finding that matters most is not the penalty. A corporate audit conducted in April at Malik Ferguson's direction found that the same energy-control documentation deficiency exists in some form at Huntington and Muncie, which converts a single-site enforcement matter into a company-wide exposure and, on a future inspection at any site, a repeat-violation risk carrying penalties an order of magnitude higher.

Muncie learned on 14 May that an appliance original equipment manufacturer accounting for $28.0 million of annual revenue will move its business to a competing supplier effective January 2027. The customer confirmed that the decision was made on piece price against a Mexican-sourced quote and not on quality or delivery; Muncie is the company's best-performing plant on both measures. Because the affected parts are long-cycle and low value density, they consume 26 percent of Muncie's machine hours while producing 19 percent of its revenue. Absent replacement work, plant utilization falls from 84 percent to 63 percent in January 2027.

Chambersburg commissioned a $22.0 million coil coating line five months behind schedule and is holding first-pass yield at 78 percent against a 94 percent design target. The line has consumed $24.6 million of capital against a $22.0 million authorization and produced $2.9 million of cost of poor quality in five months of operation. The yield gap is not a mystery; three defect families account for 73 percent of it, and two of the three are traceable to commissioning steps that were compressed to recover schedule.

Across the company, resin cost rose 19 percent year over year, of which we recovered less than half through contractual pass-through. Overtime reached 14.0 percent of direct hours against a 6.5 percent plan. Annualized hourly turnover is 31 percent.

The second half will not correct itself. This report recommends a defined program of thirteen actions with named owners and dates, summarized in Section 11, and it flags four risks in Section 12 that require Executive Committee decisions before the September capital review.

### 4.2 The Press 14 rebuild

Press 14 is a 1,650-ton hydraulic press installed in 2004 and dedicated to five high-volume heat exchanger housings representing $19 million of annual revenue. The rebuild was authorized in August 2025 at $1.85 million, scoped at nine weeks, and scheduled to run from 3 November 2025 through 5 January 2026. The press returned to qualified production on 19 March 2026, twenty weeks after teardown and eleven weeks late.

Three causes account for the overrun, and all three were foreseeable.

First, teardown in week two revealed circumferential cracking in the moving platen that had not been detected by the pre-rebuild inspection, which was a visual and ultrasonic survey performed without removing the platen. Replacement fabrication added seven weeks of lead time. Second, the controls package specified in the authorization was obsolete at the vendor, a fact confirmed only after the purchase order was placed; substitution of a current-generation controller required re-engineering of the hydraulic interface and added three weeks. Third, no contingency production plan existed. Tooling for four of the five part numbers had never been qualified on an alternate press, and the qualification work performed under duress in December and January consumed capacity on Presses 9, 22, and 31 and produced the elevated scrap discussed below.

Of the 1,412 unplanned downtime hours the plant recorded, 340 are directly attributable to the rebuild and its aftermath — 186 hours of the extended outage that fell outside the approved window and could not be backfilled, and 154 hours of interruption on the three presses used for emergency qualification runs.

### 4.3 Scrap

Scrap at 4.9 percent against a 3.0 percent standard cost $4.1 million in the half against a $2.5 million standard, an unfavorable variance of $1.6 million. Sixty-one percent of the excess is attributable to the emergency tool transfers. Moving a tool between presses of different tonnage, tie-bar spacing, and control architecture requires a validated process window; the January transfers were performed on engineering judgment and short-run capability studies rather than full validation, and dimensional drift on two housings produced three containment events and one customer sort at a heating equipment assembler in Ohio.

The remaining 39 percent is a longer-running condition. Huntington's scrap rate has drifted upward for six consecutive quarters, from 3.1 percent in Q1 FY2024 to the present 4.9 percent, and the drift correlates with hourly turnover and with the reduction of the process technician headcount from fourteen to nine during the fiscal 2024 cost program. The plant currently operates with seven process technicians against a staffing model of fourteen and is attempting to cover forty-one presses on three shifts.

### 4.4 Assessment

Huntington's problem is not the press. It is that a large, single-point-of-failure asset was taken out of service without a validated alternate route, without a platen inspection adequate to the age of the machine, and without confirmation that the specified controls were available. The financial consequence is $6.3 million unfavorable to plan. The organizational consequence is that the plant spent the half in recovery mode and did not advance any of its four committed continuous improvement projects.

Delia Marchetti, plant manager, has been directed to complete alternate-press validation for all forty-one A-classified part numbers by 31 December 2026 and to restore process technician headcount to twelve by 30 September 2026. A capital asset criticality review covering all four plants is proposed in Section 11.

## 6. Safety: The March Inspection and Its Company-Wide Meaning

*Prepared by Malik Ferguson, Director, Environmental Health & Safety*

### 6.1 The inspection

On 3 March 2026 a compliance safety and health officer from the Cincinnati Area Office arrived unannounced at the Springfield plant. The inspection was programmed under the Site-Specific Targeting program, for which Springfield qualified on the basis of its fiscal 2024 Form 300A data. It was not initiated by a complaint or a referral. The inspection ran four days, included a walkaround of the coil coating and slitting areas, records review covering three years, and twelve employee interviews. A closing conference was held on 6 March.

The citation was issued 21 April 2026 and comprises three serious citations containing seven instances, with a total proposed penalty of $84,600.

| Citation | Standard | Subject | Instances | Proposed penalty | Abatement date |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 29 CFR 1910.147(c)(4)(ii) | Machine-specific hazardous energy control procedures absent or inadequate for the Line 2 uncoiler, recoiler, and three slitter stations | 3 | $34,200 | 5 June 2026 |
| 2 | 29 CFR 1910.212(a)(1) | Point-of-operation guarding removed or bypassed on two 200-ton presses in the stamping department | 2 | $28,900 | 22 May 2026 |
| 3 | 29 CFR 1910.134(c)(1) and (f)(2) | Written respiratory protection program not implemented for coating room solvent exposure; fit testing not current for eleven employees | 2 | $21,500 | 5 June 2026 |

We did not contest. An informal conference was held with the Area Director on 14 April 2026, at which we presented abatement evidence for Citations 2 and 3 and a corrective plan for Citation 1. The Area Office has offered an informal settlement agreement reducing the penalty to $59,220 and reclassifying no items. The settlement has not been executed as of the date of this report; counsel expects execution in August 2026. Abatement is complete and certified for all seven instances, verified by my office on 11 June.

The proposed penalty is not financially material. The two findings that follow are.

### 6.2 Finding one: the deficiency is not confined to Springfield

Following the closing conference I directed a corporate audit of hazardous energy control programs at all four plants, executed between 30 March and 24 April using the same protocol the compliance officer applied at Springfield. The audit covered machine-specific procedure existence, procedure accuracy against the installed equipment, annual periodic inspection documentation, and authorized-employee training records.

| Site | Machines requiring procedures | Procedures present | Procedures accurate on verification | Periodic inspections current | Authorized-employee training current |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Huntington | 214 | 191 (89%) | 148 (69%) | 62% | 81% |
| Springfield | 96 | 74 (77%) | 51 (53%) | 44% | 73% |
| Muncie | 173 | 168 (97%) | 141 (82%) | 79% | 94% |
| Chambersburg | 88 | 88 (100%) | 71 (81%) | 58% | 88% |
| **Company** | **571** | **521 (91%)** | **411 (72%)** | **64%** | **85%** |

Of 571 machines requiring machine-specific procedures, 160 either lack a procedure or carry a procedure that does not match the installed configuration. Huntington alone accounts for 66 of those. The most common failure mode is not absence but obsolescence: a procedure written at installation and never revised through subsequent controls upgrades, guard modifications, or auxiliary equipment additions. Periodic inspection documentation, which OSHA requires annually for each energy control procedure, is current for 64 percent of machines company-wide.

The practical meaning is that any of the four plants inspected tomorrow under the same protocol would likely receive a citation under 1910.147. Springfield's inspection was programmed by its injury rate. Huntington's fiscal 2025 rate does not place it in the targeting program, but complaint-driven and referral-driven inspections are not rate-dependent, and a serious injury at any site triggers a reporting obligation and an inspection.

### 6.3 Finding two: repeat-violation exposure

A citation issued at a second facility for substantially the same standard within five years of a final order may be classified as repeat. The maximum penalty for a repeat violation is ten times the serious maximum. Once the Springfield settlement becomes a final order, every Vandalia plant carries repeat exposure on 1910.147, 1910.212, and 1910.134 through calendar 2031. On the audit data above, a repeat citation at Huntington for energy control procedures is a realistic outcome, and a multi-instance repeat citation could carry a proposed penalty in excess of $500,000.

This is the reason the Audit Committee is receiving this section. The exposure is not the $59,220 settlement. It is a five-year period during which our documented, self-identified, company-wide deficiency in a program we have now been cited for is discoverable by any inspector, and would be evidence of knowledge in any enforcement or civil proceeding.

### 6.4 Company safety performance

| Measure | H1 FY2026 | H1 FY2025 | FY2026 goal |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total recordable incident rate | 2.10 | 2.38 | 1.50 |
| DART rate | 1.31 | 1.44 | 0.80 |
| Recordable injuries | 19 | 21 | — |
| Days away from work | 118 | 96 | — |
| Near-miss reports per 100 employees | 3.1 | 4.4 | 12.0 |
| Serious-injury-potential events | 7 | 4 | — |
| Contractor recordable incidents | 3 | 1 | — |
| Safety training hours per employee | 6.2 | 9.8 | 14.0 |

The headline rate improved. Three underlying indicators moved the wrong way. Days away from work rose 23 percent, meaning our injuries are more severe even as they are slightly less frequent. Near-miss reporting fell 30 percent, which in my experience signals reduced reporting confidence rather than reduced hazard. Serious-injury-potential events — high-energy near misses that could have produced a fatality or life-altering injury — rose from four to seven, with three at Huntington associated with the Press 14 work and two at Chambersburg associated with commissioning.

Safety training hours per employee fell 37 percent. That decline is a direct consequence of overtime and open positions: training is the first thing a supervisor cancels when the line must run. Sections 7 and 8 describe the same pressure from a cost perspective. It is the same pressure.

### 6.5 Recommended safety actions

1. Complete rewrite and verification of all 571 machine-specific energy control procedures, with photographic verification against installed configuration, by 31 January 2027. Estimated cost $640,000, of which $410,000 is contract engineering support. Not in the current plan.
2. Bring all periodic inspections current by 31 October 2026 and establish a rolling schedule with corporate audit sampling quarterly.
3. Add one dedicated EHS coordinator at Springfield and one at Chambersburg, effective 1 October 2026. Annualized cost $196,000.
4. Restore the safety training standard to fourteen hours per employee per year and protect it as a fixed, non-cancellable schedule element, with compliance reported monthly to this committee.
5. Extend the Springfield abatement measures — guard interlock verification, coating room exposure monitoring, and fit-test recall — to all four plants regardless of citation scope, by 31 December 2026.

## 7. Muncie, Indiana

Muncie contributed $1.9 million of favorable operating variance in the half and leads the company on every quality and delivery measure. It is also the plant that will lose 19 percent of its revenue in January 2027.

### 7.2 The customer loss

On 14 May 2026, the customer's director of global sourcing notified our vice president of sales, Gwen Sattler, and Mr. Alcantara that eleven part numbers across two cooking platforms will transition to an alternate supplier beginning January 2027, with full transition complete by April 2027. The business represents $28.0 million of annual revenue, 19 percent of Muncie's annual sales, and 5.4 percent of company revenue.

The customer was explicit about the basis for the decision. A Monterrey-based supplier quoted an average piece price 11 percent below ours on a like-for-like basis, and the customer's dual-source policy, adopted in 2024, required that at least one North American source outside the United States be qualified on each cooking platform by 2027. Our quality and delivery performance on the account was rated "exceeds" in the customer's most recent supplier scorecard, dated February 2026. We were not displaced for performance.

We requested and received a rebid opportunity on 2 June. Our best sustainable offer closed 6 of the 11 points of price gap and was declined on 26 June. Closing the remaining gap would have required pricing below variable cost on seven of eleven parts.

### 7.3 Capacity and financial consequence

The eleven parts are large, thin-walled, long-cycle moldings run on eight presses between 800 and 1,400 tons. They are low value density: they consume machine hours out of proportion to the revenue they generate.

| Measure | Affected business | Muncie total | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual revenue | $28.0M | $146.4M | 19.1% |
| Annual machine hours | 62,400 | 240,000 | 26.0% |
| Annual variable contribution | $6.7M | $41.0M | 16.3% |
| Direct headcount | 71 | 402 | 17.7% |
| Dedicated tooling assets (net book value) | $2.1M | — | — |

The revenue exits in FY2027, not FY2026. Shipments continue at plan through December 2026 and taper through Q1 FY2027. The FY2027 impact, absent replacement, is $28.0 million of revenue, $6.7 million of variable contribution, and approximately $4.9 million of unabsorbed fixed cost at current cost structure — a total operating income impact of roughly $11.6 million on a full-year basis, before mitigation.

Utilization falls from 84 percent to 63 percent. That figure assumes no replacement volume and no capacity removal, and it is the number the Executive Committee should treat as the unmitigated case.

### 7.4 Mitigation

Four workstreams are underway.

**Commercial replacement.** Mr. Alcantara and Ms. Sattler have assembled a Muncie-specific quote pipeline of $41.3 million in annualized revenue across nineteen opportunities, of which $14.2 million is probability-weighted and $8.9 million would award before January 2027. Three opportunities in the pipeline are with heating equipment customers currently served from Huntington, which would represent internal transfer rather than growth; those are excluded from the $14.2 million.

**Insourcing.** We currently purchase $6.4 million annually of molded components from external suppliers for assembly at Huntington and Springfield. Preliminary review indicates $3.8 million of that spend could be produced at Muncie at a cost advantage of 8 to 14 percent once freight is considered. A make-versus-buy analysis is due 30 September 2026.

**Capacity right-sizing.** Eight presses become available. Three are candidates for transfer to Huntington, where the Press 14 experience demonstrated inadequate redundancy in the 800- to 1,400-ton range. Two are candidates for sale. Three should be retained for pipeline conversion. A recommendation is due 31 October 2026.

**Cost structure.** Direct headcount of 71 is affected. Muncie's annualized turnover of 22 percent generates approximately 88 separations per year, which means that if a hiring freeze on affected classifications begins 1 September 2026 the reduction can be substantially achieved through attrition by April 2027. This is the preferred path and should be decided before the September capital review, because the freeze must begin well in advance of the volume decline to work.

### 8.2 The coating line project

The Line 3 wide-web coil coating line was authorized in March 2024 at $22.0 million, with mechanical completion scheduled for July 2025 and commercial production for September 2025. First qualified commercial coil ran on 9 February 2026, five months late. Capital spend through June 2026 is $24.6 million, 11.8 percent over authorization, with an additional $2.4 million supplemental appropriation now requested for corrective work described below.

| Delay driver | Weeks | Cost impact |
|---|---|---|
| Air permit modification — curing oven NOx limits | 9 | $0.4M |

The air permit issue was the earliest and the most avoidable. The authorization assumed the oven would qualify as a minor modification under the existing permit. The state agency determined in April 2025 that the projected NOx increase required a modification review, which took nine weeks and required a low-NOx burner substitution. The determination could have been sought in 2024 during design.

### 8.3 The yield gap

First-pass yield of 78.0 percent against 94.0 percent design is the plant's central problem. The defect Pareto for the five months of operation is unambiguous:

| Defect family | Share of rejected footage | Root cause status |
|---|---|---|
| Coating thickness variation, cross-web | 34% | Identified — roll coater gap control loop not tuned; tuning was deferred to recover schedule |
| Edge build and blistering | 22% | Identified — oven zone 3 temperature profile deviates from design by up to 18°C |
| Color and gloss out of specification | 17% | Partially identified — related to zone 3; also inadequate operator SPC discipline |
| Substrate handling scratches | 14% | Identified — entry accumulator roll surface finish |
| Other / unclassified | 13% | Under investigation |

Seventy-three percent of the loss comes from three causes, two of which trace to commissioning steps compressed in the effort to recover the schedule. The coater gap control loop was accepted on the vendor's default parameters rather than tuned to our substrate range. The oven profile was validated at one width and one line speed rather than the nine-point design matrix specified in the commissioning protocol.

### 8.4 Recovery plan

A corrective program was approved by Ms. Charbonneau on 6 July and is led by plant manager Errol Nwosu with corporate process engineering support from Huntington.

| Action | Owner | Complete by | Yield gain (est.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Roll coater gap control loop tuning with substrate-specific recipes | Corporate process engineering | 30 Sept 2026 | +6 pts |
| Oven zone 3 burner and baffle modification | Nwosu / OEM | 15 Nov 2026 | +5 pts |
| Full nine-point commissioning matrix re-execution | Corporate process engineering | 31 Dec 2026 | +2 pts |
| Accumulator roll replacement | Nwosu | 31 Oct 2026 | +3 pts |
| Operator SPC certification, all three shifts | Nwosu / corporate quality | 31 Jan 2027 | +2 pts |

The plan supports 88 percent first-pass yield by December 2026 and 94 percent by March 2027. It requires the $2.4 million supplemental appropriation, of which $1.5 million is the oven modification. The appropriation should be judged against a cost of poor quality currently running at $7.0 million annualized.

### 8.5 Staffing

The plant competes with three distribution centers opened within twenty miles since 2023 and pays a starting wage $2.15 below the local market median for warehouse work with less demanding conditions. A wage adjustment recommendation is in Section 9.

### 9.1 Resin

| Resin family | H1 FY2026 spend | Price change vs. H1 FY2025 | Share of spend |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glass-filled polypropylene | $23.1M | +22% | 35.6% |
| Nylon 6/6 | $18.4M | +24% | 28.4% |
| Polycarbonate/ABS | $11.9M | +14% | 18.4% |
| Thermoset compounds | $7.2M | +11% | 11.1% |
| Other | $4.2M | +9% | 6.5% |
| **Total** | **$64.8M** | **+19%** | **100%** |

Resin spend rose $10.3 million on price at constant volume. Contractual pass-through mechanisms recovered $4.1 million, leaving $6.2 million unrecovered. The recovery shortfall has two structural causes. First, 38 percent of resin-consuming revenue sits under contracts with no index mechanism, concentrated in the appliance portfolio at Muncie. Second, indexed contracts carry an average lag of 4.6 months between index movement and price effectivity, which in a rising market is a permanent one-time loss and in a sustained rise is a recurring one.

Two recommendations follow. Every contract renewal from 1 August 2026 forward must carry a resin index clause with a lag not exceeding 90 days; exceptions require the Chief Executive's approval. And the sourcing organization should complete a resin substitution qualification program targeting the nylon 6/6 portfolio, where a qualified alternate grade would address the fastest-rising input.

The distribution is instructive. Muncie, the best-performing plant, has the lowest turnover and the shortest time to fill. Chambersburg, the worst-performing, has the highest of both. Whether stability produces performance or performance produces stability, the two travel together, and the two plants at the extremes have the same relationship between them on safety, yield, and delivery.

A competitive wage review completed by human resources on 30 June found Vandalia's entry production wage below the local market median at Chambersburg by $2.15 per hour and at Springfield by $0.85, at median at Huntington, and above median at Muncie by $0.60. The recommendation before the Executive Committee is a targeted adjustment at Chambersburg and Springfield effective 1 October 2026, with an annualized cost of $1.94 million, against an overtime premium and contract labor spend at those two sites currently running $5.1 million annualized. The adjustment is not a cost increase. It is a substitution of a cheaper cost for a more expensive one, and it carries the safety and quality benefits that overtime reduction brings.

## 12. Risks Requiring Committee Attention

**Repeat-violation exposure.** Once the Springfield settlement becomes final, all four plants carry repeat exposure on three standards through 2031, against a documented company-wide deficiency in one of them. Mitigation is Action 4, and its schedule is the binding constraint.

**Muncie replacement shortfall.** The probability-weighted pipeline of $14.2 million covers half the lost revenue. If commercial replacement underperforms and capacity is not removed, Muncie moves from the company's best-performing plant to a structurally unprofitable one within four quarters.

**Chambersburg yield plan slippage.** The recovery plan depends on an OEM oven modification scheduled for November. The same OEM was responsible for five weeks of the original delay. A contingency plan and an alternate contractor should be identified by 30 September.

**Concentration of unresolved condition at Huntington.** Huntington carries the largest earnings variance, 66 of the company's 160 deficient energy control procedures, 41 open positions, seven process technicians against a model of fourteen, and three of the company's seven serious-injury-potential events. It is the site where the next significant loss is most likely to originate, and it should receive the first capital asset criticality review under Action 3.

## Appendix — Definitions

**OEE (overall equipment effectiveness):** availability × performance × quality, measured against scheduled production time.
**First-pass yield:** units or footage conforming at first presentation, without rework, as a share of total produced.
**Utilization:** demonstrated capacity consumed as a share of demonstrated capacity available at current staffing and shift pattern.
**Recordable injury rate (TRIR):** OSHA recordable cases × 200,000 ÷ hours worked.
**DART rate:** cases involving days away, restricted duty, or transfer × 200,000 ÷ hours worked.
**Serious-injury-potential event:** an incident or near miss in which a high-energy hazard was present and a direct control was absent, failed, or ineffective.
**Cost of poor quality:** internal scrap and rework, external sort and containment, warranty, and premium freight attributable to quality failure.
