## 1. Purpose and Framing

This report covers the third quarter of fiscal 2026 across all six reporting service lines, the revenue cycle disruption that followed the April electronic health record conversion, the status of the commercial payer contract that expires September 30, and the practice's operating position entering the fourth quarter.

The quarter does not resolve into a single verdict. Brandywine's procedural lines performed at or above plan. Orthopedics in particular had its strongest quarter on record. At the same time, the practice is carrying three simultaneous problems that compound one another: a revenue cycle that has not recovered from the April conversion and is holding $19 million of cash it should not be holding; a cardiology division that lost a quarter of its physician capacity on August 1 and will not replace it inside this fiscal year; and an unsigned contract with the payer that represents the largest single share of the practice's commercial book, sixty days from expiration at the time this report was drafted and twenty-one days from expiration at the time the committees meet.

Each of these is manageable in isolation. Together they narrow the practice's margin for error in the fourth quarter considerably, because the same finance and operations staff who would ordinarily be running denial remediation are also modeling contract scenarios, and because the cash the practice would ordinarily draw on to absorb a soft contract outcome is sitting in accounts receivable.

The committees are asked to review the service line results, take note of the revenue cycle recovery trajectory and the assumptions underneath it, and provide direction on the payer negotiation posture described in Section 8.

## 3. Primary Care

### 3.1 Volume, Panel, and Access

Primary care is the practice's largest service line by provider count and by encounter volume, and it is the line whose performance is least well described by its own profit and loss statement.

Ninety-eight providers handled 412,000 visits on a trailing twelve-month basis. Panels average 2,180 patients per provider. The third next available appointment — the access measure the quality committee has tracked since fiscal 2023 — stands at 22 days.

| Primary care measure | Q3 FY26 | Q2 FY26 | Q3 FY25 | Target |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Providers (physician + APP) | 98 | 97 | 95 | — |
| Visits (TTM) | 412,000 | 408,500 | 396,000 | — |
| Visits per provider (annualized) | 4,204 | 4,211 | 4,168 | 4,300 |
| Average panel size | 2,180 | 2,155 | 2,090 | 2,000 |
| Third next available (days) | 22 | 19 | 16 | 10 |
| Same-day fill rate | 71% | 74% | 78% | 85% |
| Contribution before referral credit | ($4.2M) | ($3.9M) | ($3.1M) | — |

The 22-day figure is the number that should concern the quality committee most, and it deserves interpretation rather than simple presentation. Twenty-two days to the third next available appointment means that a patient calling a Brandywine primary care office today for a routine visit will, on average, be offered a slot three weeks out. That is more than double the practice's stated target of 10 days and represents a six-day deterioration in a single quarter.

The deterioration has two drivers, and they pull in opposite directions for purposes of remediation. The first is panel growth: average panels rose from 2,090 to 2,180 over four quarters, an increase of 90 patients per provider, or roughly 8,800 additional attributed patients across the line without a proportionate increase in provider capacity. Provider count grew by three over the same period. The second is a decline in same-day fill rate, from 78 percent to 71 percent, which means that capacity that exists is going unused at the margin even as scheduled access lengthens. That combination — panels too large and same-day slots going unfilled — is characteristic of a scheduling and template problem layered on top of a genuine capacity shortfall.

The same-day decline correlates in timing with the April conversion. Front-desk staff report that the new scheduling module requires additional steps to identify and release held slots, and that the previous system's automated release of unbooked same-day capacity at a fixed hour has not been reproduced. Practice operations has this on the conversion remediation list, but it currently sits below the claims and eligibility items described in Section 7.

### 3.2 Financial Result and the Referral Question

Primary care lost $4.2 million before referral contribution, against $3.9 million in the prior quarter and $3.1 million in the prior year quarter. The trend is unfavorable and is expected to continue absent intervention, because the underlying drivers — compensation guarantees for recently recruited providers, payer mix shift toward Medicare Advantage, and the reduced visit throughput described above — are all still in motion.

The referral contribution question recurs in every quarterly discussion and is worth stating precisely for the committees. Primary care generates downstream volume for orthopedics, cardiology, imaging, and the ambulatory surgery center. Brandywine's internal referral attribution methodology, adopted in fiscal 2024, credits primary care with a share of the contribution margin on downstream services when the referring provider is a Brandywine primary care physician or advanced practice provider and the service occurs within 90 days. On that basis, primary care's fiscal 2026 year-to-date referral credit is approximately $17.6 million, which converts the standalone loss into a substantial positive.

Two cautions belong with that number. First, the attribution methodology credits primary care with the full downstream contribution rather than an incremental share, which overstates the marginal value of an additional primary care provider, because some portion of that downstream volume would reach Brandywine specialists through other channels. Second, and more immediately relevant, the referral credit is only as good as the specialty capacity available to absorb the referral. Cardiology's situation, described in Section 5, means that a meaningful share of the primary care referral stream in the fourth quarter will encounter a 31-day echocardiogram backlog and reduced physician availability. Referrals that leak to outside cardiology groups because of wait times do not generate referral credit and, more consequentially, do not return.

### 3.3 Recommended Actions

Practice operations recommends three actions for the fourth quarter, in priority order. First, elevate the same-day slot release defect in the conversion remediation queue; the fix is understood and the estimated effort is modest relative to its access impact. Second, cap panel growth at current levels for the twelve providers whose panels exceed 2,400, closing them to new attribution until panel size normalizes. Third, complete the advanced practice provider recruitment authorized in the fiscal 2026 budget, of which four of six positions remain open.

The migration to the ambulatory surgery center is the dominant financial story. ASC share rose from 48 percent to 61 percent over four quarters, moving roughly 790 cases per year out of the hospital setting. Contribution per case rose $900 over the same period, and the majority of that increase is attributable to site of service rather than to case mix or rate improvement.

The migration is not finished. The clinical criteria committee's current view is that approximately 68 to 70 percent of Brandywine's orthopedic case mix is ASC-appropriate under present anesthesia and recovery protocols, which implies remaining runway of seven to nine points, or roughly 430 to 550 additional cases annually. Reaching that ceiling would add an estimated $3.5 million to $4.5 million in annual contribution at current per-case economics.

### 4.2 Constraints

Two constraints govern how quickly the remaining migration can occur. The first is ASC block time. The center currently operates four rooms across ten sessions per week at 84 percent utilization. At present growth rates the center reaches practical capacity — which the operations team places at 88 to 90 percent utilization, above which turnover delays compound — during the second quarter of fiscal 2027. A fifth room is included in the fiscal 2027 capital request at an estimated $2.4 million, and the payback analysis, which practice operations has provided to the finance committee separately, shows recovery inside twenty-six months at current case growth.

The second constraint is the payer contract discussed in Section 8. The current agreement's ASC facility rates are favorable relative to the market and were negotiated in fiscal 2023 when Brandywine's ASC volume was roughly half its current level. The payer's opening position in the current negotiation includes a site-of-service rate adjustment that would reduce ASC facility reimbursement. Section 8 quantifies the exposure. It is the single largest financial variable in the negotiation and it lands squarely on the practice's best-performing service line.

## 5. Cardiology

### 5.1 The Departures

Cardiology lost three physicians effective August 1, 2026 — after the close of the reporting quarter but before this report was drafted, and material enough to the practice's forward position that it is treated here as a quarter event rather than a subsequent event.

The three departures reduce the division from 32 physicians to 29, a 9.4 percent reduction in headcount. The operational impact is larger than the headcount suggests, because two of the three departing physicians were among the division's higher-volume interventional practitioners and because the departures concentrate in the division's Newark and Middletown coverage.

Division chair Stefan Marek attributes the departures to call coverage. That attribution deserves to be recorded accurately rather than summarized, because it is the practice's principal available signal about whether further departures should be expected. Dr. Marek's position, as stated to practice operations and to the chief executive, is that the division's call burden — currently one in seven for general cardiology and one in five for interventional — has been unfavorable relative to the regional market since the fiscal 2025 consolidation of the Wilmington and Newark call pools, and that the three departing physicians each cited call in exit conversations. He has further stated that he does not consider the current call structure sustainable at 29 physicians and that the burden will worsen to approximately one in six and one in four respectively, which he characterizes as a level at which additional departures become likely.

Practice operations does not have an independent basis to confirm or dispute the causal attribution. What can be stated from the data is that cardiology physician turnover over the trailing three years is 14.2 percent annualized against a practice-wide average of 8.1 percent, and that exit interview call-related citations in cardiology run substantially above other procedural divisions. The attribution is consistent with the available evidence.

### 5.2 Volume Projection

Cardiology projects a 14 percent volume decline for the fourth quarter and into the first quarter of fiscal 2027.

| Cardiology measure | Q3 FY26 actual | Q4 FY26 projected | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physicians | 32 | 29 | (9.4%) |
| Office visits (quarterly) | 21,400 | 18,400 | (14.0%) |
| Diagnostic catheterizations | 610 | 525 | (13.9%) |
| Interventional procedures | 284 | 238 | (16.2%) |
| Echocardiograms performed | 8,900 | 8,100 | (9.0%) |
| Echo backlog (days) | 31 | 34–38 | Unfavorable |
| Contribution margin | $12.8M | $10.6M–$11.2M | (12%–17%) |

The 14 percent projection assumes no further departures, retention of the existing advanced practice provider complement, and no reduction in referral volume from primary care or from outside referring physicians. Each of those assumptions carries risk in the same direction. If referral sources begin routing to competing groups in response to access delays — which is the ordinary pattern when a division's wait times lengthen — the decline will exceed 14 percent and will persist beyond the point at which physician capacity is restored, because referral relationships re-form slowly.

### 5.3 The Echocardiogram Backlog

The echocardiogram backlog stands at 31 days and is the division's most visible access failure. It is also, in the committees' likely assessment, the item with the clearest quality implication, because echocardiography sits on the diagnostic pathway for heart failure, valvular disease, and post-infarction assessment, and a 31-day wait for a study ordered as part of a symptomatic workup is clinically consequential.

The backlog has three contributing causes. First, technologist staffing: the division carries four sonographer vacancies against an authorized complement of 22, and two additional sonographers are on extended leave. Second, reading capacity: the physician departures reduce interpretation capacity proportionally, and the division has not yet redistributed reading assignments. Third, the conversion: order-entry defects in the cardiology module produced a period of duplicate and incorrectly-routed orders in April and May that required manual reconciliation and consumed scheduling staff time.

Practice operations recommends three near-term measures. Contract sonographer coverage for two full-time equivalents through the fourth quarter, at an estimated cost of $148,000, would restore approximately 1,100 studies of quarterly capacity. Redistribution of reading assignments to include the two non-invasive cardiologists currently reading at reduced volume would add interpretation capacity without new cost. Triage protocol implementation — separating urgent from routine studies with a two-tier scheduling template — would reduce the clinically relevant portion of the backlog even if the aggregate figure moves slowly.

### 5.4 Recruitment and Retention

Two cardiology searches are active. Neither is expected to produce a start date inside fiscal 2026. Interventional cardiology recruitment in the Philadelphia–Wilmington market currently runs nine to fourteen months from search initiation to start date, and Brandywine's two searches were initiated in June and August respectively.

The more consequential question before the committees is retention rather than recruitment. If Dr. Marek's assessment is correct, the practice has a window of one to two quarters in which to alter the call structure before further departures occur, and each further departure both worsens the call burden for those remaining and extends the recovery timeline. Practice operations and the chief executive have discussed three options: a call stipend structure at an estimated annual cost of $640,000 to $780,000; recruitment of two nocturnist-model advanced practice providers to absorb first-call at an estimated $420,000 annually; and a partial outsourcing of overnight coverage to a regional teleCardiology arrangement at an estimated $510,000 annually. None of these is fully modeled. The committees may wish to direct that a recommendation be brought forward within thirty days rather than at the next quarterly cycle, given the timeline described.

## 6. Behavioral Health and Imaging

### 6.1 Behavioral Health

Behavioral health operates 41 clinicians against an authorized complement of 50, leaving 9 vacancies — an 18 percent vacancy rate that has persisted above 15 percent for six consecutive quarters.

| Behavioral health measure | Q3 FY26 | Q2 FY26 | Q3 FY25 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clinicians (filled) | 41 | 42 | 40 |
| Authorized positions | 50 | 50 | 47 |
| Vacancy rate | 18.0% | 16.0% | 14.9% |
| Visits (quarterly) | 24,800 | 25,600 | 23,900 |
| Video share of visits | 68% | 65% | 58% |
| No-show rate | 24% | 22% | 19% |
| Effective visits per clinician per day | 6.8 | 7.1 | 7.4 |
| Contribution margin | $0.4M | $0.6M | $0.9M |

Two figures interact here and the interaction is the whole of the behavioral health problem. Video delivery reached 68 percent of visits, up ten points year over year, and video has been the mechanism by which the line has maintained volume despite a persistent vacancy rate. But the no-show rate rose to 24 percent over the same period, up five points, and the no-show rate on video visits runs materially above the in-person rate — 27 percent against 18 percent in the quarter's detail.

The practical consequence is that a clinician scheduled for nine visits delivers 6.8. The line's contribution margin has fallen from $0.9 million to $0.4 million over four quarters, and essentially all of that decline is attributable to unrecovered no-show capacity rather than to rate, mix, or cost.

Three interventions are available and practice operations recommends all three. Reminder sequencing — the current single reminder at 24 hours should become a three-touch sequence at 72 hours, 24 hours, and 2 hours, with the final touch by text — is the highest-yield and lowest-cost change; comparable practices report six to nine point no-show reductions. Overbooking at a 15 percent rate on video templates would recover capacity at the cost of occasional clinician overrun and should be piloted with volunteer clinicians before general implementation. Same-day waitlist activation, which the new EHR supports but which has not been configured, would fill released slots.

The vacancy rate itself is a market condition more than a Brandywine condition. Licensed clinical social worker and psychologist recruitment in the region is constrained, compensation benchmarks have moved 11 percent over eighteen months, and Brandywine's current bands sit at approximately the 45th percentile. A compensation review is scheduled for the fiscal 2027 budget cycle. The committees should understand that the vacancy rate is unlikely to improve materially before that review concludes.

|---|---|---|---|
| Total studies (TTM) | 218,000 | 214,300 | 205,600 |

Magnetic resonance utilization fell five points to 74 percent, and the cause is specific: the Middletown scanner has been down six weeks on a helium compressor failure. The unit is a 2018-model 1.5T system. The compressor failed on May 19. The replacement part carried a manufacturer lead time of four weeks, and installation and requalification consumed a further two.

The financial impact of the outage is approximately $610,000 in foregone contribution across the six weeks, calculated on the Middletown unit's trailing volume and average contribution per study. The more durable cost is the referral leakage figure. Outbound magnetic resonance leakage rose from 6.1 percent to 9.4 percent during the outage, as referring physicians — including Brandywine's own primary care providers — routed patients to competing imaging centers rather than to Brandywine's Wilmington and Newark units. Some portion of that leakage will persist after the Middletown unit returns to service.

The service interruption also exposes a maintenance posture question the finance committee should consider. Brandywine's imaging equipment is covered under time-and-materials service agreements rather than full-service contracts, a decision made in fiscal 2022 on the basis that the practice's equipment was then relatively new. The Middletown unit is now eight years old, and three of the practice's eleven magnetic resonance and computed tomography units are at or beyond seven years. A full-service contract on the aging units would cost an estimated $340,000 annually across the three and would have converted the six-week outage into an estimated ten-day outage under guaranteed-response terms. Practice operations recommends the finance committee direct a formal analysis for the fiscal 2027 capital and operating cycle.

The Middletown unit returned to service June 30. July utilization recovered to 77 percent. Leakage in July measured 8.1 percent, still above the pre-outage baseline.

## 7. Revenue Cycle: The Conversion Impact

### 7.1 What Happened

The practice converted to a new electronic health record on April 4, 2026, across all 26 sites in a single cutover. The conversion moved scheduling, registration, clinical documentation, charge capture, and claims generation onto the new platform simultaneously.

Two measures deteriorated immediately and have not recovered to baseline.

| Revenue cycle measure | Pre-conversion (March) | April | May | June | July | August |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Initial claim denial rate | 6.9% | 15.1% | 14.2% | 12.8% | 11.6% | 10.4% |
| Days in accounts receivable | 41 | 47 | 54 | 58 | 57 | 55 |
| Clean claim rate | 93.1% | 84.9% | 85.8% | 87.2% | 88.4% | 89.6% |
| Cash collections vs. plan | 101% | 78% | 82% | 87% | 91% | 94% |
| Excess A/R above baseline | — | $6.2M | $13.8M | $19.0M | $18.4M | $16.9M |

The initial claim denial rate nearly doubled, from 6.9 percent to a peak of 15.1 percent in April, closing the quarter at 12.8 percent. Days in accounts receivable rose from 41 to 58. The gap between actual accounts receivable and the level implied by the pre-conversion 41-day performance was $19.0 million at quarter close.

That $19 million is the number the finance committee should hold. It is cash the practice has earned, for services delivered, that is sitting in claims that have been denied, are pending rework, or are in aged buckets awaiting follow-up. It is not written-off revenue. The great majority of it is collectible. But it is not available, and it is not available at a moment when the practice would benefit considerably from having it available.

### 7.2 Denial Composition

Understanding the composition matters because the remediation approach differs by category.

| Denial category | Share of denials | Pre-conversion share | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Registration / eligibility | 34% | 19% | Conversion-driven |
| Coding / documentation | 23% | 26% | Baseline |
| Authorization | 19% | 21% | Baseline |
| Charge capture / missing charge | 14% | 8% | Conversion-driven |
| Timely filing | 6% | 2% | Conversion-driven, escalating |
| Other | 4% | 24% | Reclassification |

Roughly 54 percent of current denials fall into categories that were not significant contributors before the conversion. Registration and eligibility denials are the largest single block at 34 percent, and they trace to two specific defects: the real-time eligibility verification interface to the clearinghouse was not configured with the same payer-specific field mappings as the legacy system, and the registration workflow permits users to advance past incomplete insurance fields without a hard stop. Both are correctable. The field mapping remediation is 80 percent complete as of late August; the hard-stop configuration is scheduled for the September 12 release.

Charge capture denials at 14 percent reflect a different problem: charges generated in the new system's procedure module do not consistently carry the modifiers that the legacy system appended automatically. This has affected orthopedics and cardiology disproportionately.

The timely filing category at 6 percent, up from 2 percent, is the one that requires immediate attention regardless of the others. Timely filing denials are, in most payer contracts, unappealable. Every claim that ages past the filing deadline while sitting in a work queue converts from collectible to written off. The escalating share indicates that the April and May denial backlog is beginning to reach filing limits. Practice operations has quantified approximately $1.4 million of claims currently within thirty days of a filing deadline and recommends that these be worked ahead of all other queue items.

### 7.3 Remediation Status and Trajectory

The revenue cycle recovery plan comprises four workstreams.

**Configuration remediation.** Thirty-one distinct defects were identified between April and July. Nineteen are closed, seven are in the September release, and five remain in analysis. The five in analysis are the most complex and include the modifier logic and the professional-technical split billing for imaging.

**Backlog workdown.** The practice engaged an outside revenue cycle vendor on June 15 for supplemental denial follow-up capacity, at a contracted cost of $780,000 through December. The vendor is working the aged bucket while internal staff work current claims. Backlog volume peaked at 41,200 claims in late June and stood at 28,600 at the end of August.

**Front-end prevention.** Registration staff at all 26 sites completed refresher training in July and August. Registration-driven denials fell from 38 percent of total denials in May to 34 percent in the quarter and to an estimated 30 percent in August.

**Monitoring.** A daily revenue cycle dashboard was implemented July 1, reporting denial rate, clean claim rate, days in accounts receivable, and cash collections against plan by site and by service line.

The trajectory is favorable but slower than the practice projected in May. The original recovery model assumed return to a 7.5 percent denial rate and 44 days in accounts receivable by September 30. Current performance — 10.4 percent and 55 days in August — will not reach those figures by that date. The revised projection places denial rate at 8.5 to 9.0 percent and days in accounts receivable at 48 to 50 by December 31, with return to pre-conversion baseline in the second quarter of fiscal 2027.

| Recovery milestone | Original projection | Revised projection |
|---|---|---|
| Denial rate below 10% | July 31 | September 30 |
| Denial rate below 8% | September 30 | January 31, 2027 |
| Days in A/R below 50 | August 31 | December 31 |
| Days in A/R at 41–43 baseline | September 30 | March 31, 2027 |
| Excess A/R fully released | September 30 | March 31, 2027 |

The finance committee should plan on the revised figures. The practice will recover approximately $9 million to $11 million of the $19 million excess during the fourth quarter, with the remainder releasing across the first and second quarters of fiscal 2027.

## 8. Payer Contract Status

### 8.1 Position

The contract with the state's largest commercial payer expires September 30, 2026. As of the drafting of this report it remains unsigned.

The agreement covers approximately 31 percent of Brandywine's commercial volume and approximately 22 percent of total net patient revenue, or roughly $107 million annually. It is the single largest payer relationship the practice holds.

### 8.2 Negotiation History

Negotiations opened April 14. Six sessions have occurred. The parties have exchanged three proposal rounds.

| Element | Payer position | Brandywine position | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Professional fee schedule | +1.5% year one, +1.5% year two | +4.0% / +3.5% | Open, converging |
| ASC facility rates | Site-neutral adjustment, est. (11%) | Current rates + 2% | Open, principal dispute |
| Term | 3 years | 2 years | Open |
| Quality incentive pool | $1.4M at risk | $2.2M, upside-weighted | Near agreement |
| Prior authorization | Status quo | Gold-card for 14 procedures | Open |
| Timely filing | 90 days | 180 days | Brandywine priority |

The professional fee schedule gap has narrowed across rounds and practice operations expects settlement in the 2.5 to 3.0 percent range for year one. That outcome is acceptable.

The ambulatory surgery center facility rate is the dispute that matters. The payer proposes a site-neutrality adjustment that would reduce ASC facility reimbursement by an estimated 11 percent. The payer's stated rationale is that its own analysis shows Brandywine's ASC rates at a premium to the regional market and that the practice's migration of volume — the same migration described in Section 4 — has increased the payer's total ASC spend by 34 percent over two years even as per-case hospital spend fell.

Brandywine's counter-position is that the payer's total orthopedic spend across sites of service has declined, not risen, because each case migrated from hospital to ASC saves the payer substantially more than the ASC rate premium costs it, and that a site-neutrality adjustment would reduce the practice's incentive to continue the migration.

### 8.3 Exposure

| Scenario | Description | Annual revenue impact |
|---|---|---|
| Base | 2.75% professional, ASC rates held | +$2.9M |
| Payer proposal accepted | 1.5% professional, ASC (11%) | ($3.4M) |
| Midpoint | 2.5% professional, ASC (5%) | ($0.2M) |
| Non-renewal, 90-day continuity | Out-of-network after Dec 31 | ($14M)–($19M) year one |

The non-renewal figure carries wide error bars and is presented for the committees' awareness rather than as a planning number. It assumes patient attrition of 45 to 60 percent of the affected book over twelve months, partially offset by out-of-network collections and by migration of some patients to other Brandywine-contracted plans during open enrollment.

### 8.4 Interaction With the Revenue Cycle

The committees should understand the timing interaction explicitly, because it is the reason this report treats the two subjects together rather than separately.

Under ordinary circumstances Brandywine would enter a difficult payer negotiation with roughly 90 days of cash on hand and the ability to sustain a period of contractual uncertainty. The $19 million held in excess accounts receivable reduces days cash on hand from an ordinary 88 to a current 71. That is not a distress figure and the practice's liquidity is not in question. But it does mean that a non-renewal scenario, which would introduce collection delay and payment uncertainty on 22 percent of revenue, would arrive while the practice is already carrying an abnormal receivable balance.

The payer is unlikely to be unaware of Brandywine's conversion. Conversions are visible to payers through claim submission patterns.

### 8.5 Recommended Posture

Practice operations, with the chief financial officer's concurrence, recommends the following posture for the committees' consideration.

Accept a professional fee outcome in the 2.5 to 3.0 percent range without further extended negotiation; the marginal value of additional rounds is small relative to the time cost.

Hold firm on ambulatory surgery center rates through at least one further round, and offer a structured alternative rather than a flat concession: a volume-tiered arrangement in which ASC rates step down modestly at defined volume thresholds. This gives the payer the spend-growth protection it says it wants while preserving Brandywine's migration incentive.

Trade term length for rate certainty. A three-year term is acceptable if year two and year three carry defined escalators rather than reopener language.

Secure the 180-day timely filing provision. Given the conversion experience, this term has material value to Brandywine and low cost to the payer.

Prepare, but do not initiate, non-renewal communications. A 30-day extension of the current agreement should be sought if terms are not settled by September 20, and the committees should authorize the chief executive to agree to such an extension without returning to the board.
