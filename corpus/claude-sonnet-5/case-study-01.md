# Coastal Harvest Alliance: The Cost of Speed

## A Case Study in Facility Investment, Crisis, and Correction at a Regional Food Bank

---

## Overview

In March 2023, the board of Coastal Harvest Alliance, a regional food bank serving twelve counties around Savannah, Georgia, chose speed over durability. Faced with a warehouse operating at 96 percent capacity and a state grant that would expire before a properly designed facility could ever open its doors, the board approved a $14.5 million retrofit of a leased building in Pooler, Georgia, over a $22 million purpose-built alternative. The retrofit was supposed to take fourteen months. It took twenty-three. Along the way, a refrigeration system sized for the wrong load failed in the middle of a Georgia summer, spoiling 140,000 pounds of food; partner agencies dropped from 214 to 189; on-time order fulfillment fell to 78 percent; and the organization spent $600,000 renting emergency reefer trailers just to keep food from rotting in a parking lot.

By June 2025, after a leadership-driven correction that began in October 2024, the organization was distributing more food, more cheaply, and more reliably than before the crisis: 31 million pounds a year at $0.33 per pound, versus $0.41 per pound before the project began, with on-time fulfillment at 94 percent and 221 partner agencies active — four more than at the pre-crisis peak. Total project spend landed at $17.9 million, still $4.1 million under the cost of the new-build alternative the board had rejected, but $3.4 million over the retrofit's original budget, and roughly nine months later than even the delayed schedule anyone had planned for.

This is the story of how that decision got made, what it cost the organization and the people who depend on it while things were going wrong, how leadership found its way out, and what the people who lived through it say they would do differently.

---

## The Organization and Its Situation

Coastal Harvest Alliance is not a small operation. By 2023 it employed 118 people and operated on a $41 million annual budget, distributing food across twelve counties in coastal Georgia — a service area that stretches from dense urban neighborhoods in Savannah to rural counties where the food bank's delivery trucks are sometimes the only regular link between a family and a meal. The organization's growth over the prior two years had been, by any measure, a success story: annual distribution rose from 17 million pounds in 2021 to 24 million pounds in 2023, a 41 percent increase driven by rising need coming out of the pandemic years, expanded partnerships with regional grocers and growers, and an increasingly sophisticated agency network.

That growth, however, had outrun the physical plant that supported it. The organization's warehouse, a 60,000-square-foot facility, was running at 96 percent capacity by early 2023 — a utilization rate that left almost no room for surge inventory, seasonal donation spikes, or the kind of buffer stock that lets a food bank absorb a bad month without turning away partner agencies. Worse, only 4,000 square feet of that warehouse was refrigerated or frozen space. For an organization increasingly receiving perishable donations from regional supermarket chains and produce growers — donations that make up a growing share of the food supply for food banks nationally as they shift away from a reliance on canned and dry goods — that ratio of cold storage to total space was badly out of step with where the food supply chain was heading.

Chief Executive Officer Marguerite Ostrowski and Chief Financial Officer Lucia Sandoval-Reyes had been raising the capacity problem with the board since late 2022. By the start of 2023, it was no longer a planning question but an operational one: the organization was turning away truckloads of donated product because it had nowhere to put them, and refrigerated donations in particular were being declined at a rate that both executives considered unacceptable given the organization's mission.

---

## The Decision: How It Was Made and On What Information

In March 2023, Ostrowski and Sandoval-Reyes brought the board two options.

**Option One** was a purpose-built facility — a ground-up warehouse designed specifically for food bank operations, with refrigeration and freezer capacity engineered to match projected volume, expandable dry storage, and modern loading and sortation infrastructure. The estimated cost was $22 million. The projected timeline, including site acquisition, permitting, design, and construction, was four years.

**Option Two** was a retrofit of an existing leased building in Pooler, a fast-growing suburb west of Savannah with good highway access. The estimated cost was $14.5 million, and the organization's contracted architecture and engineering firm projected the facility could be operational in fourteen months — a difference of roughly two and a half years against the new-build option.

The financial case for the retrofit was strengthened considerably by a piece of external information: the state of Georgia had awarded Coastal Harvest Alliance a $3.5 million grant explicitly earmarked for food bank infrastructure, and the grant carried a spending deadline of June 2024. If the funds were not obligated and substantially spent by that date, they would revert to the state. Under the four-year new-build timeline, the organization would not break ground until after the grant expired. Under the fourteen-month retrofit timeline, the grant could be fully deployed against the project.

Board Chair Cornelius Bledsoe became the retrofit option's most vocal advocate. In board discussion, he argued that walking away from $3.5 million in committed state funding — nearly a quarter of the retrofit's total cost — in order to pursue a facility that wouldn't open for four years was fiscally indefensible given the immediate capacity crisis the organization was already living with. His position, as recorded in board minutes from that period, was that the organization could not afford to tell partner agencies and donors to wait until 2027 for adequate cold storage when a solution existed that could be operational in a little over a year.

The information the board had in front of it at the time of the vote included:

- The architecture and engineering firm's fourteen-month construction estimate for the retrofit, based on a design and engineering review of the Pooler building's existing structure, electrical service, and loading infrastructure.
- A cost estimate for the retrofit that included new refrigeration and freezer installation, racking, dock upgrades, and code compliance work, totaling $14.5 million.
- The $3.5 million state grant commitment and its June 2024 spending deadline.
- Growth projections showing continued increases in both total distribution volume and the proportion of that volume requiring refrigerated storage.
- A capacity analysis showing the existing warehouse would reach an unworkable utilization level (defined internally as above 98 percent) within roughly a year if no additional space came online.

What the board did not have — and what would prove decisive in the two years that followed — was an independent, load-specific engineering assessment of the cold storage system design as proposed by the contractor. The mechanical specifications for the refrigeration compressors were developed by the general contractor's subcontracted mechanical engineer based on square footage and general food bank industry benchmarks, rather than on Coastal Harvest Alliance's specific and rapidly growing perishable volume. Sandoval-Reyes has since said that in retrospect, the organization treated the mechanical specification as a construction detail to be finalized during design development, rather than as a core assumption that needed independent verification before the board voted on the project's scope and budget. Nor did the board commission an independent owner's representative to monitor the contractor's schedule and specifications on the organization's behalf — a role that would later prove central to the project's recovery.

The board approved the retrofit option in March 2023 by a substantial majority, with the state grant deadline cited repeatedly in discussion as the deciding factor. Design work began that spring, and a construction contract was signed with a regional general contractor in the early summer of 2023, targeting an occupancy date in the late spring of 2024 — comfortably ahead of the June 2024 grant deadline, with a planned move-in and full operational transition to follow over the subsequent weeks.

## The First Eighteen Months: What the Retrofit Cost the Organization and the Agencies That Depend on It

Almost nothing about the project's execution matched the plan the board had approved.

### Schedule Slippage

The contractor's original completion target was spring 2024. The building was not substantially finished until nine months after that target — pushing actual completion into roughly the first quarter of 2025, nearly a full year past the point at which the organization had expected to be operating comfortably out of its new facility, and well past the June 2024 grant spending deadline that had been the central argument for choosing the retrofit in the first place. (The organization was able to obligate the grant funds against contracted work before the deadline, preserving the grant, even though the physical construction remained incomplete — but the timeline pressure that had shaped the entire decision evaporated almost as soon as the ink was dry on the contract.)

The delay was driven by a combination of factors that are familiar to anyone who has managed a commercial construction project on a compressed timeline: permitting delays with Pooler's building department, discovery of electrical service limitations in the existing structure that required a utility-side upgrade, subcontractor availability issues, and — most consequentially — a series of change orders related to the mechanical and refrigeration systems as the actual engineering requirements became clearer during construction.

### The Refrigeration Failure

The most damaging single event of the project came in July 2024. The cold storage compressors installed as part of the retrofit had been specified for a load significantly smaller than what the facility was actually being asked to handle — a mismatch between the mechanical engineering assumptions used during design and the real-world volume and thermal load of a food bank cold storage operation running at Coastal Harvest Alliance's scale and growth trajectory. In the middle of a Georgia summer, with ambient temperatures and humidity pushing the system past its design tolerance, the compressors failed.

The result was the spoilage of 140,000 pounds of food product — meat, dairy, produce, and other perishables that could not be moved or stored elsewhere quickly enough once the cooling failure was discovered. For an organization whose entire mission is measured in pounds of food delivered to people who need it, the loss of 140,000 pounds in a single incident was not just a financial cost; it represented food that had already been donated, transported, and warehoused, only to be destroyed by a mechanical failure baked into the facility's design from the outset.

### The Scramble for Cold Storage

In the aftermath of the July 2024 failure, and for months afterward while the compressor issue was diagnosed and a permanent fix engineered, the organization had no reliable way to store the volume of refrigerated and frozen donations it was continuing to receive. The solution — expensive, but necessary to avoid repeated spoilage — was to rent commercial reefer trailers on an emergency basis and use them as stopgap cold storage, parked on-site and running on diesel generators or shore power depending on availability.

Over the roughly year-long period this arrangement was needed, the organization spent $600,000 on emergency rented reefer trailers. That figure does not include the value of the spoiled product itself, the labor cost of managing a fleet of rented trailers as an ad hoc cold chain, or the operational inefficiency of running a warehouse operation split between a partially functional permanent building and a row of rented trailers in the parking lot.

### The Impact on Partner Agencies and Service

The organization distributes food to a network of partner agencies — food pantries, soup kitchens, shelters, and other community organizations across its twelve-county service area — who in turn are the direct point of contact with the families and individuals the food bank ultimately serves. It is through this network that the retrofit's operational disruption translated into real hardship for real people.

Between the onset of construction disruption and the depth of the crisis in late 2024, the number of active partner agencies fell from 214 to 189 — a loss of 25 agencies, or roughly 12 percent of the network. Some of these agencies left because they could no longer rely on consistent, predictable deliveries; others scaled back their partnership because order fulfillment had become too unreliable to plan around. On-time order fulfillment, a core reliability metric the organization uses to track whether partner agencies receive what they ordered when they were promised it, dropped to 78 percent — meaning that better than one in five orders placed by a partner agency did not arrive on time, a degree of unreliability that ripples directly into whether a food pantry has enough on its shelves on a given distribution day.

Internally, the cost-per-pound of food distributed — a standard efficiency metric for food bank operations that captures total operating cost divided by pounds distributed — rose to $0.41 per pound during this period, reflecting the combined weight of construction cost overruns, emergency rental expenses, spoilage losses, and the general inefficiency of operating across two inadequate facilities during the transition.

Staff morale suffered as well, though it is harder to reduce to a single number. Warehouse staff were working around a live construction site for months longer than planned; drivers and route planners were managing an increasingly unpredictable inventory picture; and the organization's frontline relationship staff — the people whose job it is to maintain trust with partner agencies — were absorbing complaints and, in a meaningful number of cases, watching long-standing partner relationships erode or end.

## The Correction: What Leadership Did

By the fall of 2024, it was clear to the organization's senior leadership that the retrofit project, as it was being managed, would not recover on its own. In October 2024, the board and CEO Ostrowski gave expanded authority to Chief Operating Officer Terrence Achebe and Warehouse Director Nadia Haddad to take direct control of the project's remaining scope and to make the operational decisions necessary to stabilize service to partner agencies while the physical facility issues were resolved.

Three decisions defined the correction.

### 1. Hiring an Owner's Representative

The first and, in the leadership team's own retrospective assessment, most consequential decision was to hire an independent owner's representative — a construction and engineering professional whose job was to represent Coastal Harvest Alliance's interests directly, rather than relying on the general contractor's own project management and the original architecture and engineering firm, both of whom had a financial and professional interest in the project as originally scoped. The owner's representative was tasked with auditing the remaining construction scope, independently verifying the corrected mechanical design for the refrigeration system, and managing the contractor's schedule and change orders going forward with a level of scrutiny the project had not had from its outset.

This single decision addressed directly the gap that had existed at the board's original March 2023 approval: the absence of independent technical verification of the facility's core engineering assumptions. The owner's representative's first substantive finding, delivered within weeks of being engaged, confirmed what the July 2024 failure had already made painfully clear — the refrigeration system had been designed against an inadequate load estimate — and produced a corrected specification and a scoped, priced plan to replace the compressors with units sized for the organization's actual and projected volume.

### 2. Phased Occupancy

Rather than waiting for the entire facility, including the corrected refrigeration system, to be complete before moving any operations into the Pooler building, Achebe and Haddad restructured the transition around phased occupancy. Dry goods storage and handling — the largest share of the organization's total volume, and the portion of the operation least affected by the cold storage problems — was moved into the completed sections of the new building first, freeing up capacity and reducing the strain on the old warehouse and the rented reefer fleet, even while the refrigerated and frozen sections remained under repair.

This phased approach did not solve the cold storage problem, but it stopped the bleeding on the dry goods side of the operation, allowed staff to begin operating in the new facility's more efficient layout, and reduced the total footprint of the crisis to the specific systems that were actually broken, rather than treating the entire facility as unusable until every system was finished.

### 3. Renegotiating the Remaining Scope

With the owner's representative's independent findings in hand, Achebe and Haddad renegotiated the remaining contract scope with the general contractor, using the documented mechanical engineering failure as leverage to secure corrected refrigeration equipment, revised warranty terms, and a firm completion schedule with penalty provisions for further delay — terms that had not been part of the original contract. This renegotiation is a meaningful part of why the project's final total cost, while over the original retrofit budget, did not spiral further out of control: the organization was able to hold the contractor accountable for a portion of the cost of fixing the mechanical failure, rather than absorbing the full cost of the correction as a wholly new expense.

Over the following months, the corrected compressors were installed and commissioned, the refrigerated and frozen sections of the building were brought online in stages, and the rented reefer fleet was wound down as permanent cold storage capacity came back online — reliably this time, sized for the load the organization was actually generating rather than the load the original design had assumed.

---

## The Results, By the Numbers

By June 2025, roughly twenty months after the crisis authority was granted to Achebe and Haddad, and just over two years after the original board decision, the numbers told a story of substantial recovery — and, on several dimensions, outright improvement over the organization's pre-crisis baseline.

**Distribution volume** reached 31 million pounds annually, up from 24 million pounds in 2023 and far above the 17 million pounds distributed in 2021. The corrected refrigeration capacity, combined with the larger overall footprint of the Pooler facility, allowed the organization to accept and store a volume and mix of donations — particularly perishable donations — that the old 60,000-square-foot warehouse, with its 4,000 square feet of cold storage, never could have handled.

**Cost per pound** fell to $0.33, down from the crisis-period high of $0.41 and below even the organization's pre-retrofit baseline efficiency. This reflects both the operating efficiencies of a properly functioning modern facility — better racking, better loading dock throughput, properly sized refrigeration running at appropriate load rather than being patched together with emergency rentals — and the simple arithmetic of distributing significantly more volume across a largely fixed cost base.

**On-time order fulfillment** climbed to 94 percent, up from the crisis low of 78 percent and representing a level of reliability that exceeds what the organization had before the retrofit began. Partner agencies planning their own distribution schedules around Coastal Harvest Alliance deliveries can now do so with a degree of confidence the organization had not achieved even at its 2023 growth peak.

**Partner agency count** recovered to 221 active agencies, surpassing the pre-crisis figure of 214 and representing a net gain over the low point of 189 agencies during the worst of the disruption. This is, in some ways, the most meaningful number in the case: it indicates that the trust damaged during the crisis period was not merely repaired but, over time, exceeded, as agencies that had left or scaled back returned, and new agencies joined a network operating with restored reliability and expanded capacity.

**Total project cost** landed at $17.9 million — $3.4 million over the original $14.5 million retrofit budget approved in March 2023, but $4.1 million below the $22 million estimated cost of the new-build alternative the board had declined to pursue. The organization also preserved the full $3.5 million state grant, having obligated the funds against contracted work before the June 2024 deadline even though physical completion of the building extended well beyond that date.

Taken together, these numbers describe an organization that, having endured a genuinely difficult eighteen-to-twenty-month period of construction delay, refrigeration failure, spoilage, rental costs, and eroded partner trust, emerged from the crisis operating at a scale and efficiency beyond what it had achieved before the project began — but at a real cost in time, money, spoiled food, and — for a period of many months — degraded service to the twelve-county community it exists to serve.

## What Leadership Says It Would Do Differently

In conversations reflecting on the project since its stabilization, the organization's leadership team has been candid about what they believe went wrong in the decision-making process, and what they would change if faced with a similar choice again.

**CFO Lucia Sandoval-Reyes** has focused her retrospective critique on the treatment of the mechanical engineering specification during the original decision process. In her view, the board's March 2023 vote treated the refrigeration system's design as a downstream construction detail rather than as a core assumption underlying the entire project's viability — a mistake given that cold storage capacity was the primary operational deficiency driving the project in the first place. She has said that any future capital decision of this scale should require an independent, load-specific mechanical engineering review — not merely a design provided by the contractor's own subcontracted engineer — before the board is asked to approve a budget and timeline, rather than during design development after the decision has already been made.

**Board Chair Cornelius Bledsoe**, who was the retrofit option's strongest advocate in 2023, has acknowledged that the grant deadline, while a legitimate and material financial consideration, was allowed to dominate the board's risk analysis in a way that crowded out adequate scrutiny of execution risk. He has said that in retrospect, the size of the grant relative to the project's total cost — $3.5 million against a $14.5 million budget, or roughly 24 percent — did not justify the degree to which its deadline shaped the board's tolerance for schedule and technical risk elsewhere in the project. He has also said the board should have required, as a condition of approving the retrofit option, that an owner's representative be engaged from the start of construction rather than added reactively more than a year later, once the project was already in crisis.

**CEO Marguerite Ostrowski** has framed the lesson in organizational terms: the absence of an owner's representative meant that for the first eighteen months of the project, the organization had no one whose sole job was to represent Coastal Harvest Alliance's interests against the contractor's and the original design firm's own incentives and assumptions. She has said that the cost of the owner's representative engaged in October 2024 — modest relative to the $600,000 spent on emergency reefer rentals alone — would have been easily justified from the outset, and that the organization's internal process going forward will require an independent owner's representative on any capital project above a defined cost threshold, engaged at the start of design rather than in response to a crisis.

**COO Terrence Achebe** has emphasized the value of the phased occupancy approach that he and Warehouse Director Nadia Haddad implemented in the recovery, and has said the organization should have built a phased transition plan into the project from the beginning rather than only adopting one under crisis pressure. A facility transition plan that assumes some systems may be delayed, he has argued, is more resilient by design than one that assumes the entire building will be finished on a single date — and building that assumption in from the start, rather than improvising it during a crisis, would have allowed the organization to begin realizing at least partial benefits of the new facility many months earlier, and would have reduced the operational strain that drove some partner agencies away during 2024.

**Warehouse Director Nadia Haddad** has pointed to the specific technical failure — a refrigeration system sized against generic industry benchmarks rather than the organization's own documented and rapidly growing volume — as evidence that capital projects for organizations experiencing fast growth need to be scoped against forward-looking volume projections, not current or historical averages. She has argued that had the mechanical specification been built around the organization's 2023 volume trajectory (24 million pounds and rising) rather than an industry-standard benchmark for a facility of its square footage, the compressor failure that spoiled 140,000 pounds of food in July 2024 would likely have been avoided entirely.

Across these individual reflections, a consistent theme emerges: the organization's leadership does not believe the retrofit decision itself was necessarily wrong, given the information and constraints in front of the board in March 2023. The capacity crisis was real, the grant deadline was real, and the four-year new-build timeline carried its own serious costs in continued turn-aways of donated food and continued strain on an over-capacity warehouse. What leadership uniformly identifies as the failure is not the choice between build and retrofit, but the absence of independent technical verification and independent project oversight at the point the decision was made — a gap that took eighteen months, 140,000 pounds of spoiled food, $600,000 in emergency rentals, and the loss of 25 partner agencies to fully expose and correct.

## Takeaways for Organizations Facing Similar Decisions

For leaders of mission-driven organizations — food banks and otherwise — facing a comparable choice between a faster, cheaper path and a slower, more thoroughly engineered one, the Coastal Harvest Alliance case suggests several specific lessons, drawn directly from what happened rather than from general principle.

**A grant deadline is a real constraint, but it is not a substitute for a technical risk assessment.** The $3.5 million grant's June 2024 deadline was a legitimate and material factor in the board's decision, but it should not have been allowed to substitute for independent verification of the retrofit's core engineering assumptions. Organizations facing similar time-limited funding should ask explicitly, before a vote: what have we independently verified about this project's technical design, separate from what the contractor pursuing the contract has told us?

**Mechanical and infrastructure specifications tied to an organization's core operational function deserve the same scrutiny as the budget and timeline.** For a food bank, cold storage capacity is not a construction detail — it is the operational core of the entire investment decision. When the thing driving the capital project (in this case, inadequate refrigeration) is also the thing whose design is least independently verified, that is a specific and identifiable risk that boards should require to be closed before a vote, not during construction.

**An owner's representative, engaged from the start, is inexpensive relative to the cost of not having one.** Coastal Harvest Alliance spent $600,000 on emergency reefer rentals alone during the period it operated without independent project oversight — to say nothing of the cost of the spoiled product, the lost partner agencies, and the schedule overrun. An owner's representative engaged at the outset of design, rather than fourteen months into a failing project, would very likely have cost a small fraction of that figure and could plausibly have caught the undersized refrigeration specification before it was ever built.

**Phased occupancy and phased transition planning are more resilient than single-date completion assumptions**, particularly for organizations undertaking capital projects while continuing to run essential, continuous operations. Building a phased transition into the original plan — rather than improvising one under crisis pressure eighteen months in — allows an organization to begin realizing partial benefits of a new facility as soon as any portion of it is safely usable, and limits the operational exposure created by any single system's delay or failure.

**Capital projects for organizations in a period of fast growth should be scoped against forward-looking volume, not historical or industry-standard benchmarks.** The refrigeration system that failed in July 2024 was sized using general food bank industry benchmarks for a facility of its square footage, not against Coastal Harvest Alliance's own documented and accelerating volume trend. An organization whose distribution grew 41 percent in two years, and whose leadership was explicitly planning for continued growth, needed a facility engineered against where it was headed, not where the industry average sits.

**Recovery is possible, and can exceed the pre-crisis baseline — but it requires clear authority, independent expertise, and honest renegotiation.** The turnaround achieved by October 2024 through June 2025 — from 78 percent to 94 percent on-time fulfillment, from 189 to 221 partner agencies, from $0.41 to $0.33 per pound, and from 24 million to 31 million pounds distributed — demonstrates that a badly executed capital project is not necessarily a permanent organizational wound. But the recovery required the board to grant real authority to operational leadership, the engagement of independent technical expertise the organization had not had from the start, and a willingness to renegotiate the remaining contract on the basis of documented failure rather than simply absorbing the cost of the contractor's original mistake.

For the twelve counties Coastal Harvest Alliance serves, the numbers by June 2025 represent genuine progress: more food, delivered more reliably, to more community partners, at a lower cost per pound than the organization had ever achieved. But that progress arrived only after a period — measured now in the 140,000 pounds of spoiled food, the 25 lost partner agencies, and the families who went without reliable pantry deliveries for the better part of a year — that leadership itself describes as avoidable, had a different set of questions been asked before the March 2023 vote was taken.
