# Driver Retention in Border-Region Trucking: A Review of the Compensation, Detention, Scheduling, and Safety Literature

**Border Commerce Institute, Laredo, Texas**
**Prepared by Ana Lucia Zepeda, PhD, Director of Research**
**Database construction: Fitzgerald Boakye, Research Analyst**
**July 2026**

---

## Introduction

The Border Commerce Institute exists because twenty-four member carriers operating roughly 6,900 tractors along the Texas–Mexico corridor cannot solve a shared problem alone. Over the past fiscal year, driver turnover among member fleets averaged 91 percent for over-the-road positions — a figure consistent with national averages for large truckload carriers but no less corrosive for being typical. Two members lost major shipper accounts after service failures traced directly to unstaffed lanes and inexperienced replacement drivers. The Institute holds a 400,000 dollar state workforce development grant that must be committed to a specific intervention before the fiscal year closes, and the member advisory board is not of one mind about what that intervention should be. Roseanne Kitcheyan, who chairs the advisory committee and runs a 210-truck fleet out of Deming, New Mexico, has argued in board meetings that pay is the only variable that matters and that the grant should fund a wage subsidy or bonus structure, full stop.

This review was commissioned to test that claim against the published research before the Institute commits scarce grant funds to a single lever. The underlying question is narrow and practical: for carriers of this size and this operating profile — regional and long-haul truckload fleets in the 100- to 400-truck range, operating in and around a border drayage and cross-dock environment — what does the peer-reviewed and federal research literature say actually reduces over-the-road driver turnover, and does it say so consistently enough to justify betting an entire grant on one mechanism?

The scope was set deliberately wide at the front end and narrowed only after full-text screening. We did not restrict the search to compensation studies, because turnover in trucking is rarely explained by a single cause in the literature, whatever any one operator's experience suggests. Four bodies of evidence turned out to dominate the eligible literature: compensation structure and its relationship to retention; detention and dwell time at shipper and receiver facilities; electronic logging device (ELD) mandates and hours-of-service regulation; and organizational safety climate. A fifth strand — the long-running economic argument over whether a "driver shortage" exists at all — cuts across the other four and is addressed separately because it shapes how carriers and policymakers interpret every other finding.

What follows is organized to set these bodies of evidence against one another rather than to summarize each study in isolation. The reason is that the studies disagree, sometimes sharply, and the disagreement itself is informative. A review that reported findings sequentially would flatten those contradictions into an illusion of consensus that the underlying research does not support and that would poorly serve a board about to spend down a finite grant.

## How the Literature Was Searched

Zepeda and Boakye searched a commercial transportation research database, Scopus, and federal motor carrier research repositories (including Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration contract reports and Department of Transportation-funded university transportation center output) for English-language items published between 1990 and 2026. Search terms combined driver-side outcomes (turnover, retention, attrition, driver shortage, driver satisfaction) with operational and regulatory constructs (detention, dwell time, hours of service, electronic logging device, safety climate, compensation, pay structure) and were run separately against trucking-specific and general logistics indexing terms to capture work published in transportation, labor economics, occupational safety, and management journals.

The search returned 2,105 records after deduplication. Boakye built a screening database to track title/abstract decisions, full-text retrieval, and inclusion criteria, coding each record for driver segment (long-haul, regional, short-haul, owner-operator, company driver), fleet size where reported, and study design. Titles and abstracts were screened against basic eligibility (empirical study, U.S. or Canadian trucking context or directly comparable regulatory environment, driver-level or fleet-level outcome data) to yield 264 records for full-text review. Of those, 71 met final inclusion criteria: sufficient methodological detail to assess effect direction and, where applicable, magnitude; a turnover, retention, crash, or safety-climate outcome; and a study population overlapping with truckload, less-than-truckload, or specialized over-the-road operations rather than private fleet, transit, or non-U.S. contexts with materially different labor regulation.

The 71 included studies break down as 24 addressing compensation structure, 15 addressing detention and dwell time, twelve addressing electronic logging and hours-of-service regulation, nine addressing safety climate, and eleven addressing the driver-shortage debate, methodological questions in turnover measurement, or driver segments (owner-operators, women drivers, short-haul work) that recur across the other four themes without constituting a literature of their own. Effect estimates, driver segments, fleet-size ranges, and study designs for all 71 studies sit in the extraction workbook Boakye maintains alongside this review; citations below are keyed to that workbook by author and year.

Two limitations in the search are worth stating up front, because they bear on how much weight the Institute should put on any single finding. First, the literature is heavily weighted toward large truckload carriers — the studies with fleet-size data skew toward operations well above 500 tractors, and studies of fleets in the 100–400 truck range comparable to most Institute members are a minority within every theme. Second, three driver populations relevant to a border operating environment — owner-operators, women drivers, and short-haul or drayage work — are thin throughout the included literature, a gap discussed at length below.

## Synthesis: Compensation Structure and Retention

Compensation is the most studied variable in this literature, and it is also the variable that produces the least consistent findings, a pairing that should itself give the board pause before treating pay as a settled question. Twenty-four included studies examine some form of compensation intervention or compensation structure against turnover or retention outcomes, and they converge on one point only: pay increases produce a measurable short-term retention effect. Beyond that point, they diverge.

Several studies find that flat per-mile rate increases produce retention gains that decay rapidly. Kowalczyk and Mensah (2017), using panel data across 40 mid-size truckload carriers, found turnover reductions following a rate increase that were statistically significant at three and six months but had fully reverted to baseline by month eleven. Grubbs and Okafor (2020) replicated this pattern at a single large carrier implementing a system-wide raise, reporting a nine-month decay window, and attributed the decay to a "recalibration" effect in which drivers' reservation wage adjusts upward to match the new rate rather than remaining satisfied by it. Fontaine (2003), working with earlier data from the 1990s deregulated rate environment, found an even shorter window of roughly six months. Taken together, this cluster — Kowalczyk and Mensah (2017), Grubbs and Okafor (2020), Fontaine (2003) — supports something close to Kitcheyan's instinct that pay matters, while directly undercutting her assumption that a pay intervention alone will produce durable retention gains from a single grant-funded bonus cycle.

A second cluster complicates this picture by finding durable retention effects from compensation changes, but only when pay is bundled with predictable home time. Sandoval, Kim, and Adeyemi (2019) studied a regional carrier that combined a rate increase with a guaranteed weekly home-time schedule and found retention gains that persisted through their eighteen-month observation window, well beyond the decay horizon reported elsewhere. Mbeki and Villalobos (2023) found a similar pattern using survey data across fourteen carriers, reporting that drivers who received both a pay increase and a scheduling guarantee were reported significantly more likely to remain at twelve months than drivers who received the pay increase alone, though drivers who received the scheduling guarantee without a pay change also showed elevated retention relative to a no-change control. Iyer, Patel, and Suarez (2022) found a comparable interaction effect but noted it was strongest among drivers with dependents at home, suggesting the mechanism runs through family stability rather than income satisfaction per se. This bundled-intervention literature — Sandoval, Kim, and Adeyemi (2019), Mbeki and Villalobos (2023), Iyer, Patel, and Suarez (2022) — is the strongest evidence in the entire 71-study corpus that a durable retention effect exists at all, and it points toward scheduling predictability as a necessary complement to, not a substitute for, pay.

A third cluster is more skeptical of compensation as a lever in the first place. Deshpande (2012) and Whitmore and Okonkwo (2018) both report null or near-null effects of pay increases on retention once controlling for baseline job satisfaction and dispatcher relationship quality, arguing that carriers with poor dispatch communication saw no retention benefit from raises at all, while carriers with strong dispatch relationships retained drivers at similar rates regardless of pay level. Reyes and Halloran (2013) reached a similar conclusion studying driver exit interviews, finding that "pay" was cited as the presenting reason for leaving in 68 percent of cases but that follow-up questioning revealed scheduling unpredictability, detention time, and dispatcher conflict as the underlying drivers in most of those same cases — a methodological finding that should worry anyone relying on exit-survey data (including, potentially, member fleets' own HR records) to conclude that pay is "the" variable. Ortega and Whitfield (2016) and Urrutia and Boschetti (2015) similarly found that above a regional market-rate threshold, additional pay produced diminishing and eventually negligible retention returns, suggesting a ceiling effect rather than a linear relationship.

A smaller set of studies examines pay structure rather than pay level — hourly versus mileage-based compensation, detention pay, and guaranteed minimums. Larrea (2009) and Quintero (2020) both find that shifting from pure mileage pay to a blended structure including guaranteed weekly minimums reduced turnover more than an equivalent-cost mileage rate increase, because it reduced income volatility rather than raising average income. This finding connects directly to the detention literature discussed below: several studies in that body treat detention pay specifically as a compensation-structure variable rather than a scheduling variable, and the line between "pay" and "predictability" turns out to be blurrier in the data than board discussions typically allow.

Overall, the compensation literature does not support treating pay as either irrelevant or sufficient. It supports treating pay as a necessary but time-limited lever whose durability depends heavily on what it is paired with — a conclusion that bears directly on how the Institute should structure any wage-linked use of the grant.

## Synthesis: Detention and Dwell Time

Fifteen included studies address detention — the time drivers spend waiting, unpaid or under-compensated, at shipper and receiver facilities — and its relationship to both turnover and crash risk. This is the most methodologically fractured literature in the review, and the fracturing matters because it is also the literature most directly relevant to a border corridor environment where cross-dock and drayage wait times are a known operational pain point.

The core substantive finding, replicated across most of the fifteen studies, is that detention time is associated with both elevated crash risk and elevated quit intention, but the studies cannot agree on how large either association is because they cannot agree on how to measure detention in the first place. Andrade and Pruitt (2017) define detention as any dwell exceeding the two-hour free period specified in typical carrier-shipper agreements and report a mean detention time of 1.2 hours per stop across their sample. Halvorsen and Ibe (2011), using GPS-derived dwell data without reference to contractual free time, report mean dwell of 6.5 hours per stop at the same class of facility type. Between these two anchors sit Bhatt (2013) at 2.8 hours, Gunnarsson (2016) at 3.4 hours, and Jaramillo (2019) at 4.1 hours, each using a distinct operational definition — contractual detention, total dwell, dwell beyond scheduled appointment time, or driver-reported wait time on end-of-trip surveys. This is not noise around a stable estimate; it reflects genuinely different constructs being measured under the same label, and it means the Institute cannot import any single hours-per-stop figure from this literature and apply it to member operations without first specifying which definition its own data uses.

Despite this measurement problem, the direction of effect on both outcomes is unusually consistent. Callahan and Mwangi (2020), Duarte (2009), Kessler and Ndiaye (2014), and Leclerc (2022) all find a positive association between detention time — however defined — and self-reported turnover intention, with effect sizes that, when standardized, cluster in a moderate range despite the underlying scale differences. Moreno and Patton (2008) and Nwosu (2015) link detention time to crash risk specifically through a fatigue-and-schedule-pressure mechanism: drivers who lose hours to detention often attempt to make up time later in their duty window, driving during higher-fatigue hours or exceeding safe following distance to meet delivery appointments, producing an indirect but measurable elevation in crash involvement. Ostrander (2018) and Pham and Ríos (2020) find the same crash relationship using different data (carrier safety scores versus insurance claims data respectively), which strengthens confidence in the direction of the relationship even as the disagreement over magnitude persists.

Espinoza and Farrow (2021) offer a finding especially relevant to the compensation debate: in their sample, drivers who received full detention pay (compensation for wait time beyond a threshold) showed retention rates statistically indistinguishable from drivers with low detention exposure in the first place, while drivers with high detention exposure and no detention pay showed the highest turnover in the sample. This suggests detention functions partly as an economic variable — uncompensated time is functionally a pay cut — and partly as an independent scheduling-stress variable, since Quiñones (2012) found that even fully compensated detention still elevated turnover intention relative to no detention at all, implying drivers weight schedule disruption and lost home time beyond its dollar cost.

The practical upshot for a review meant to inform an Institute serving border-corridor carriers is that detention is not a peripheral issue relative to pay; in several of these studies it is the more proximate cause of the exit decisions that carriers attribute to pay in exit interviews (compare Reyes and Halloran, 2013, above). But the measurement inconsistency across the fifteen studies means the Institute cannot adopt a specific detention benchmark from the literature without first getting Boakye's screening database to standardize a detention definition across member fleets' own dispatch data — a data problem the grant proposal should probably fund before or alongside any programmatic intervention.

## Synthesis: Electronic Logging, Hours of Service, and Scheduling

Twelve included studies examine the effects of the electronic logging device (ELD) mandate and associated hours-of-service enforcement on safety and, secondarily, on retention. This literature divides cleanly along a single axis — carrier size — and the division is sharp enough that pooling the twelve studies into a single "ELD effect" would misrepresent every one of them.

Studies examining large carriers report improved safety outcomes following ELD adoption. Chastain and Uzoma (2020), studying carriers above 500 tractors, found a measurable reduction in hours-of-service violations and a corresponding reduction in fatigue-coded crashes following mandatory ELD adoption, consistent with the devices' intended function of preventing falsified paper logs. Ferreira and Solis (2019) and Kowalski and Adegoke (2022) replicate this finding in similarly large fleets, and Monroe and Tavares (2015), studying an early voluntary-adoption cohort before the mandate took full effect, found the same direction of effect, suggesting the improvement is not an artifact of mandate-era reporting changes.

Studies examining small carriers report the opposite. Abrego and Hutton (2018), focused on carriers under 100 tractors, found increased crash rates following ELD adoption, which they attribute to reduced scheduling flexibility: without paper-log discretion, small-carrier drivers facing detention delays or weather disruption had no informal buffer to complete a run safely within a legal window, and instead drove more aggressively to beat the clock or parked in unsafe locations to avoid violation, with the latter producing its own crash and assault-exposure risk documented separately by Delacroix (2016). Gantry and Okwuosa (2011) and Iwu and Baptiste (2013), both examining carriers in the 50–150 tractor range, found similar increases in hard-braking events and near-miss reporting following ELD adoption, consistent with a compressed-schedule mechanism rather than a fatigue mechanism. Bello (2014) and Jelinek (2017) find mixed results in the 150–400 tractor range — the size band most relevant to Institute members — with Bello finding a modest safety improvement and Jelinek finding no significant change, which as a pair suggests this middle band sits near an inflection point where the large-carrier and small-carrier dynamics roughly offset.

Herrera (2021) offers the clearest account of the mechanism dividing these findings: large carriers typically have more scheduling slack built into dispatch (more available loads, more relief drivers, shorter average length of haul relative to duty-window limits) so that ELD-enforced hours simply formalize a schedule the carrier was already managing to, while small and mid-size carriers often operated closer to the legal margin under paper logs precisely because they lacked that slack, and the mandate removed a coping mechanism rather than closing a compliance gap. This is directly relevant to Institute members: at a median size well under the large-carrier threshold in this literature, member fleets sit in the band where ELD-era scheduling pressure is more likely to be a live retention and safety issue than a solved problem, which argues against assuming the regulatory environment is neutral background rather than an active contributor to the 91 percent turnover figure.

None of the twelve ELD/HOS studies examine retention as a primary outcome, which is itself a gap addressed below; the connection to turnover in this review is inferential, drawn through the scheduling-pressure mechanism these studies share with the detention literature. Read alongside Sandoval, Kim, and Adeyemi (2019) and the other bundled-intervention studies in the compensation cluster, the ELD findings reinforce that schedule predictability, not merely pay level, sits close to the causal center of retention outcomes for carriers of this size.

## Synthesis: Safety Climate

Nine included studies examine organizational safety climate — drivers' perception of whether their carrier genuinely prioritizes safety over schedule and cost pressure — as a predictor of both crash involvement and turnover. This is the smallest of the four core literatures and the most consistent internally, though it raises its own interpretive problem.

Aldrich and Puente (2016), Cisneros and Falk (2019), and Farley and Osei (2014) each find that driver-reported safety climate scores predict both self-reported crash-relevant incidents and turnover intention independent of pay level, using validated safety-climate survey instruments adapted from the broader occupational safety literature. Grissom (2018) extends this by showing that safety climate perception mediates a substantial portion of the relationship between detention exposure and turnover intention identified in the detention literature above — in other words, detention may drive turnover partly through the message it sends drivers about whether the carrier values their time and safety over the shipper relationship, rather than only through its direct schedule or income effects. Huerta and Nakamura (2022) find a similar mediating role for dispatcher responsiveness, echoing the Deshpande (2012) and Whitmore and Okonkwo (2018) finding from the compensation literature that dispatcher relationship quality conditions the effect of other interventions.

Barros (2012), Dominguez (2007), Ekwueme (2021), and Isaacs (2010) round out this literature with findings on measurement and intervention: safety-climate scores respond to management training and communication interventions independent of any change in pay or scheduling, and improvements in scores predict retention gains over horizons of twelve to twenty-four months — longer than the nine-to-fourteen-month decay window reported for standalone pay increases in the compensation literature, though these studies do not test pay and safety-climate interventions against each other directly, so the comparison should be read as suggestive rather than a head-to-head result.

The interpretive problem with this literature is that safety climate is measured through driver perception, which is itself likely to be shaped by pay, detention exposure, and scheduling predictability rather than sitting alongside them as an independent lever. Grissom's (2018) mediation finding supports this reading: safety climate may function less as a fourth, separate cause of turnover than as the channel through which the other three causes register with drivers. That does not make it a poor target for intervention — training and communication programs are inexpensive relative to wage increases and appear to have real, independently measured effects on both scores and outcomes — but the Institute should not treat "improve safety climate" as a fifth lever unrelated to the pay, detention, and scheduling findings above.

## The Driver Shortage Debate

Six included studies address whether a national driver shortage exists, and the disagreement here is not primarily empirical but definitional, which matters for how the Institute frames its grant application and its messaging to members. The American Trucking Economics Council (2021) and the Federal Motor Carrier Workforce Panel (2022) both estimate a persistent national gap in the range of 60,000 drivers, based on industry-reported unfilled positions and projected freight growth against projected new-entrant supply. Costello and Karickhoff (2019) support a similar estimate using carrier survey data on time-to-fill for driver positions.

Against this, Nassar (2016), Michaels and Ruan (2015), and Belman-adjacent labor-economics work summarized in this cluster argue that the "shortage" figure reflects a turnover and retention problem misclassified as a supply problem: the labor market clears at prevailing wages, but because turnover is so high, carriers are perpetually re-recruiting for the same seats, producing the appearance of an unfilled-position gap that a static headcount analysis reads as scarcity. Under this reading, the 91 percent turnover figure driving the Institute's grant application is not a symptom of a labor shortage that more recruitment spending would fix, but closer to its cause: high turnover creates constant vacancy, constant vacancy looks like shortage, and shortage-framed responses (recruitment bonuses, driver-school subsidies, expanded CDL pipelines) address the wrong end of the problem.

This review does not attempt to resolve the shortage debate, which sits partly outside its scope, but it flags the framing choice for the board: if the Institute's grant application or public messaging leans on the 60,000-driver shortage figure to justify a recruitment-focused intervention, it should do so with awareness that a substantial strand of the underlying economics literature treats that figure as a retention artifact rather than an independent labor-supply fact — which would argue for spending the grant on retention rather than recruitment regardless of which side of the debate is correct, since a shortage response that does not also address turnover will simply refill the same leaking positions.

## Gaps in the Literature

Several gaps recur across all four core themes and should temper how confidently the Institute generalizes any of the findings above to its own membership.

**Fleet size.** Across all 71 studies, fleet-size data is reported inconsistently, but where it is reported, the sample skews heavily toward carriers above 500 tractors or below 150. Studies squarely in the 150–400 tractor range — where most Institute members sit — are a minority in every theme, and in the ELD/HOS literature specifically, Bello (2014) and Jelinek (2017) are essentially the only studies centered on this band, with genuinely mixed results between them. The Institute is, in a real sense, extrapolating findings from carriers larger and smaller than its own membership to fill a gap the literature has not directly studied.

**Owner-operators.** Diallo, Osei, and Vance (2020) is the only included study to examine owner-operator retention and turnover as a distinct category rather than folding owner-operators into company-driver samples, and it finds that compensation-structure effects operate differently for owner-operators (who bear operating costs directly) than for company drivers, with detention time functioning as a more direct income variable rather than a scheduling-stress variable. A single study is not a literature; carriers in this membership that rely on owner-operator capacity should treat every other finding in this review as unverified for that population.

**Women drivers.** Femi and Castellano (2021) is similarly the lone study to disaggregate findings by driver gender, finding that safety-climate perception and dispatcher-relationship quality predict turnover more strongly for women drivers than for men, while raw pay level predicts turnover more strongly for men — a difference with real implications for recruitment and retention design that the rest of the literature simply cannot speak to, because most of the 71 studies do not report gender-disaggregated results at all.

**Short-haul and drayage work.** Guerrero (2011) is the only included study addressing short-haul or drayage-specific turnover, relevant given the Institute's border location and the likely presence of cross-dock and drayage work among member operations. Guerrero finds that detention dynamics dominate short-haul turnover far more than they do long-haul turnover, since a single delayed dock appointment can consume a much larger share of a short-haul driver's available duty hours — but again, this is one study, not a body of evidence, and the Institute's own drayage-adjacent members should treat the detention findings above as a starting hypothesis rather than a settled conclusion for that segment.

**Causal design.** Standridge and Auyeung (2013) reviewed methodological quality across an earlier subset of this literature and found that the large majority of turnover studies in trucking are observational or single-carrier pre-post designs rather than randomized or quasi-experimental studies with credible comparison groups. Nothing in the 71 studies included here overturns that assessment; even the strongest bundled-intervention findings (Sandoval, Kim, and Adeyemi, 2019; Mbeki and Villalobos, 2023) come from single-carrier or small-multi-carrier designs without random assignment. The consistent direction of findings across many independent studies is reassuring, but the Institute should not describe any single result in this review as causally proven in the way a wage-subsidy pilot funded by the grant itself could actually test.

**Interaction of the four themes.** No included study tests compensation, detention, scheduling/HOS, and safety climate together in a single design. Every synthesis above connecting these themes (e.g., Grissom's 2018 mediation finding, or the parallel between bundled pay-plus-scheduling interventions and ELD-era scheduling pressure) is a reasonable inference across separate literatures rather than a direct test, and a genuinely integrated study — arguably the most useful thing the Institute could fund with future research dollars — does not yet exist.

## Implications for Carriers This Size

Set against each other rather than read in isolation, these four literatures point toward a specific and somewhat uncomfortable conclusion for the board: the evidence does not support funding a pay-only intervention with the full 400,000 dollars, and it does not support ignoring pay either. Kitcheyan's position captures a real and replicated finding — pay increases do move retention, in both directions of the literature's disagreement, in the short run — but every study that measures durability past nine to fourteen months finds that pay alone decays, while every study that finds durable effects pairs pay with scheduling predictability, detention relief, or safety-climate investment. A grant spent entirely on a wage bonus is, on this evidence, likely to produce a visible retention bump the Institute can report to the state workforce agency by year-end and a return to the current 91 percent baseline within the following fiscal year — precisely the pattern that produced the two service failures motivating this review in the first place, if those failures followed an earlier round of ad hoc raises rather than resolved them.

The stronger case, on the weight of this evidence, is for a bundled intervention: a modest, sustained compensation adjustment paired with a detention-measurement and detention-pay standardization effort across member fleets, plus a scheduling-predictability commitment (guaranteed home time, appointment-window enforcement with shippers) for participating drivers. This design draws directly on the Sandoval, Kim, and Adeyemi (2019) and Mbeki and Villalobos (2023) findings, addresses the detention measurement problem this review identified as a genuine barrier to using the detention literature at all, and is consistent with the mid-size carrier vulnerability Herrera (2021) identifies in the ELD literature — member fleets in the 150–400 tractor range are close to the size band where scheduling slack is thinnest and where a detention-and-scheduling intervention is likely to matter most.

Two design cautions follow from the gaps section. First, because the underlying literature is dominated by carriers larger and smaller than most Institute members, whatever the Institute funds should be built and evaluated as a pilot with its own before-and-after measurement, not assumed to replicate published effect sizes. Boakye's screening database, extended to track member-fleet detention hours on a standardized definition, would let the Institute generate exactly the mid-size-carrier evidence this literature currently lacks — turning the grant into both an intervention and a contribution to a genuine gap. Second, if any member fleets carry meaningful owner-operator, women-driver, or drayage-heavy short-haul populations, the Institute should not assume the bundled intervention above transfers cleanly to those segments; Diallo, Osei, and Vance (2020), Femi and Castellano (2021), and Guerrero (2011) each suggest those populations respond to different mechanisms, and a single design applied uniformly risks underperforming for exactly the drivers member fleets may most need to retain.

Finally, the shortage-versus-retention framing matters for how the Institute justifies its use of the grant to the state workforce agency. Given that a substantial strand of the labor-economics literature reviewed here treats the widely cited 60,000-driver shortage figure as a retention artifact rather than an independent supply fact, the Institute is on firmer analytical ground presenting this intervention as addressing the turnover driving that apparent shortage, rather than presenting it as a recruitment response to scarcity. That framing is also simply more consistent with what this review found: the 71 studies here overwhelmingly examine why drivers leave, not why new drivers fail to enter, and the strongest, most durable findings in the entire corpus concern what makes drivers stay once hired.
