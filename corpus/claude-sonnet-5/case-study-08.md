# Saluda Textile Works: Automating Under Pressure

## A Case Study in Capital Investment, Commissioning Failure, and Workforce Recovery

---

### The Company and Its Situation

Saluda Textile Works occupies a single manufacturing site in Spartanburg, South Carolina, where it has spent three decades building a reputation as a specialist producer of technical fabrics for automotive interiors, industrial applications, and specialty upholstery. By the end of 2022 the company employed 480 people and generated $132 million in annual sales, a scale that made it a significant regional employer but a modest player against the backdrop of global textile competition. Saluda's business model depended on long-term supply relationships with a small number of large customers, a common structure in technical textiles where qualification cycles are long, tooling and process validation are expensive, and switching costs for the buyer are usually high enough to keep incumbent suppliers in place for years at a time.

That structural protection was also Saluda's greatest vulnerability. A single automotive interiors manufacturer accounted for 44 percent of the company's revenue. No other customer came close to that concentration, and no realistic diversification plan existed that could replace that volume on short notice. Saluda's leadership had long understood this concentration as a risk to be managed rather than solved, and for years the relationship had been stable: steady volumes, predictable specifications, and a supplier scorecard that Saluda consistently passed. That stability ended in the last months of 2022.

### The Commercial Threat

In late 2022, the automotive customer notified Saluda that it required a 12 percent price reduction phased over three years. The request was not framed as a negotiating opening; it was presented as a condition of continued business, with an explicit statement that the work would be moved to another supplier if Saluda could not commit to the reduction schedule. For a company with 44 percent customer concentration, this was not a routine pricing conversation. It was an existential threat to nearly half the company's revenue base, and by extension to the great majority of its fixed cost absorption, its employment base, and its standing with its lenders.

Saluda's leadership examined the numbers and concluded that the current cost structure could not absorb a 12 percent price reduction without destroying margin on the account. Three metrics stood out as both the cause of the cost gap and the most promising levers to close it. First, the defect rate stood at 3.8 percent, high enough to generate significant scrap, rework, and customer-facing quality complaints, all of which added cost without adding value. Second, direct labor represented 31 percent of cost of goods sold, a labor intensity typical of finishing operations that had not been meaningfully automated in over a decade. Third, overtime spending had reached $2.4 million annually, evidence of a production system that was chronically running hot to hit output targets rather than running efficiently, compounded by an absenteeism rate of 9 percent that forced overtime and temporary reassignment to cover gaps on a near-daily basis.

Put together, the picture was a plant that could produce good fabric, but not consistently enough, cheaply enough, or with enough labor efficiency to survive a double-digit price cut from its largest customer without a fundamental change in its cost structure. Do nothing, and the customer's business would very likely leave over the following three years, taking with it 44 percent of revenue and almost certainly forcing a headcount reduction far larger and more disruptive than anything a capital project could cause. Do something, and there was at least a chance of keeping the account, protecting the plant's base-load volume, and buying time to diversify the customer base afterward.

### The Investment Case

President Dagmar Ellingson and Vice President of Operations Malik Rasheed built the investment case around the theory that the defect rate and the labor intensity of finishing were the two levers most directly connected to the price gap the customer was demanding, and that both could be improved through automation of the finishing line combined with optical inspection to catch defects earlier and more consistently than the existing manual inspection process could manage. In February 2023 they secured board approval for $9.2 million in capital spending: new automated finishing equipment intended to reduce manual handling and improve process consistency, paired with an optical inspection system meant to catch defects at a rate and speed no manual inspection crew could match, reducing both scrap and the downstream cost of shipping defective material to a customer already primed to walk away over cost and quality.

The financing package combined a $6 million bank loan with a $1.5 million state economic development grant. The grant carried a condition common to this kind of regional incentive: a job-retention floor requiring Saluda to maintain at least 460 positions through December 2024. At the time this looked like an easy commitment. Current headcount was 480, and the entire rationale for the automation investment was to make the plant more competitive and therefore more likely to retain, not shed, jobs. Ellingson and Rasheed did not view the floor as a meaningful constraint on the project design. That assumption would be tested well before the eighteen-month window closed.

The project logic, as presented internally and to the lender, was straightforward. Reducing the defect rate from 3.8 percent toward best-in-class levels would cut scrap cost and reduce the customer-facing quality complaints that were part of the pricing pressure narrative in the first place. Automating finishing steps that were currently labor-intensive would reduce the 31 percent direct labor share of cost of goods sold, directly addressing the cost structure the customer was pushing against. And a more consistent, less manually dependent process would reduce the overtime and absenteeism-driven disruption that had been inflating the $2.4 million overtime line. If the investment performed as modeled, Saluda would be positioned to absorb the 12 percent price reduction without destroying account profitability, while also improving cost position on the rest of its book of business.

Saluda selected Brandywine Automation to design, supply, and commission the new finishing line and optical inspection system. The contract called for installation and commissioning to be substantially complete within a defined window in mid-2023, positioning Saluda to demonstrate improved cost and quality performance to the automotive customer well within the three-year price reduction horizon and, not incidentally, well within the eighteen-month job-retention window tied to the state grant.

### What Went Wrong in Commissioning

The commissioning process did not go according to plan. Brandywine Automation's installation and startup ran five months behind schedule, a delay that by itself consumed a meaningful fraction of the runway Saluda had built into its plan for demonstrating results to its automotive customer. The delay, however, turned out to be the smaller of the two problems.

Once installed, the optical inspection system did not perform to specification. It falsely rejected 14 percent of material that was, in fact, good — a false-positive rate high enough to erase most of the intended benefit of automated inspection and to create a new, self-inflicted scrap and rework problem layered on top of the defect issues the system was supposed to solve. Operators and supervisors could not simply trust the system's output, which meant that in practice the plant was running a dual system: the new optical inspection flagging enormous volumes of false rejects, and staff having to manually verify flagged material to determine whether it was actually defective or had been wrongly rejected by a miscalibrated system. This defeated much of the labor-saving rationale for the investment in the near term, since the plant needed people to check the machine's work in addition to running it.

The combined effect of late commissioning, false rejection rates, and the general disruption of integrating new equipment into an operating finishing line was a throughput decline of 18 percent that persisted for four months. For a plant already under pricing pressure from its largest customer, a sustained double-digit throughput drop during the very period when it needed to demonstrate improved cost competitiveness was close to the worst possible outcome the project could have produced. Instead of showing the customer early evidence of a lower cost base, Saluda was, for a third of a year, running less material through the plant than before the investment, at a moment when every day mattered against the three-year price reduction clock the customer had started.

### What Went Wrong With the Workforce

The throughput and false-rejection problems were compounded by a workforce transition that had not been adequately planned. The new finishing automation and optical inspection displaced 38 operators whose roles were built around the manual processes the new equipment was designed to replace. No structured retraining plan existed at the time these roles were eliminated or altered. Some of the displaced workers were reassigned informally, some were left in ambiguous status waiting for the system to stabilize, and the absence of a clear plan created exactly the kind of uncertainty that erodes trust on a shop floor, particularly one that had already lived through 3.8 percent defect rates, high overtime, and 9 percent absenteeism before the project even began.

The consequence was attrition: 21 of the 38 displaced operators quit before Saluda's leadership addressed the situation directly. Twenty-one departures out of 38 affected positions is not a modest leakage rate; it represents more than half of the affected group choosing to leave rather than wait out an unclear transition, and it occurred in a labor market where skilled textile operators are not simply replaced by posting a job listing. Every one of those departures represented lost institutional knowledge, additional strain on the remaining workforce covering the gap, and a direct threat to the 460-position floor tied to the state grant, since headcount was falling through attrition even as the company's public commitments assumed stability or growth in employment.

The plant's workforce did not absorb this quietly. Rosario Villanueva, leading the plant committee, brought the issue to a head at a January 2024 meeting, at which the committee threatened a walkout if management did not address the displacement and retraining failures directly. This was, in effect, the moment the commissioning crisis and the workforce crisis fully merged into a single problem that management could no longer treat as two separate operational issues to be managed in parallel. A walkout at a plant already running 18 percent below throughput baseline, already behind schedule with its largest customer watching, and already under a grant compliance floor it was at risk of breaching, would have compounded every other problem simultaneously. The January 2024 meeting was the point at which Saluda's leadership had to stop managing the automation project as an engineering and equipment problem and start managing it as an integrated engineering, workforce, and customer-relationship problem.

### The Recovery

Following the January 2024 meeting, Plant Engineer Josefina Huerta and Human Resources Director Clarence Boatwright took direct, sustained ownership of the recovery, working in close coordination rather than treating the technical and human sides of the problem as separate workstreams.

On the technical side, Huerta worked with a Brandywine Automation vendor engineer on site over a ten-week period to retune the optical inspection system's rejection thresholds and adjust the lighting conditions under which the system was making its accept/reject determinations. False rejection rates in optical inspection systems are frequently sensitive to lighting consistency and threshold calibration in ways that are not fully apparent until the system is running against real production variation rather than test samples, and the ten-week retuning effort reflected the iterative, trial-and-adjustment nature of that work. Alongside the threshold and lighting corrections, the plant added a manual second-look station: a deliberate, permanent human checkpoint positioned to catch and correct the optical system's remaining errors before they became scrap or shipped defects. This was not a reversal of the automation strategy but an acknowledgment that a hybrid human-machine inspection process, rather than a fully automated one, was what the plant's actual production environment required.

On the workforce side, Boatwright led the retraining of 44 operators into technician roles, a group larger than the 38 originally displaced, reflecting both the need to backfill for the 21 who had already quit and the broader shift in skill requirements created by the new equipment. These retrained technicians received a $3.10 per hour wage increase, a meaningful pay premium that both compensated workers for the higher-skill nature of technician roles on the new equipment and served as a visible, credible signal to the remaining workforce that the retraining path led somewhere better than the manual roles being phased out. This combination — a genuine retraining pathway, a real wage increase attached to it, and a management team led personally by the engineering and HR functions rather than delegated downward — addressed the trust deficit that had driven the January 2024 walkout threat and the earlier wave of attrition.

The ten-week technical retuning period and the retraining program ran roughly in parallel, meaning that by the time the optical inspection system was performing reliably, the plant also had a workforce that understood the new roles, had been compensated for the transition, and had a functioning second-look station providing a human check on the automated system's output. This combination of technical correction and workforce stabilization is what allowed the plant to move from crisis to recovery rather than lurching from one unresolved problem to the next.

### The Results

By mid-2025, roughly two and a half years after the original capital approval and about eighteen months after the low point marked by the January 2024 walkout threat, Saluda's plant performance had not merely recovered but had substantially exceeded the original baseline the investment was meant to improve.

The defect rate, which stood at 3.8 percent when the project was approved, fell to 0.9 percent, a reduction of roughly three-quarters and a level of quality performance that materially strengthens Saluda's standing with a customer that had been prepared to walk over cost and, implicitly, quality and reliability concerns. Throughput, which had fallen 18 percent below baseline during the worst of the commissioning crisis, ended up 23 percent above the original 2022 baseline once the retuned inspection system and the retrained workforce were both functioning as intended — meaning the plant not only recovered the lost ground but converted the automation investment into a genuine capacity and efficiency gain beyond what existed before the project began.

Overtime spending fell from $2.4 million to $700,000, a reduction of more than 70 percent, reflecting a production system that was no longer chronically compensating for absenteeism, rework, and inconsistent throughput with paid overtime hours. Cost per yard, the metric most directly tied to the customer's original demand for a 12 percent price reduction, came down 14 percent — a figure that not only met but modestly exceeded the reduction the customer had originally demanded, giving Saluda room to negotiate from a position of demonstrated cost improvement rather than promised future improvement.

The commercial outcome validated the underlying bet the company had made in February 2023: the automotive interiors customer signed a five-year renewal contract worth $58 million. This is the number against which the entire $9.2 million original investment, and the ultimate $11.4 million total spend, has to be measured. A five-year, $58 million renewal from the customer that represented 44 percent of Saluda's revenue and had explicitly threatened to leave is a decisively positive commercial outcome, and it is very unlikely to have been achievable without the combination of quality improvement, cost reduction, and throughput gains the automation project ultimately delivered.

Headcount, however, settled at 441, below the 460-position floor the state grant required Saluda to maintain through December 2024. This was a direct consequence of the 21 operator departures during the commissioning crisis, only partially offset by the fact that the 44 retrained technician roles represented a net addition relative to the 38 originally displaced positions. The shortfall against the grant floor required Saluda to renegotiate the terms of the state grant after the fact, converting what had been treated as an easy-to-meet condition at the outset of the project into an active compliance and negotiation matter by the time the project's other outcomes were otherwise strongly positive.

Total spend on the project reached $11.4 million against the original $9.2 million approval, a cost overrun of roughly 24 percent, driven by the extended commissioning timeline, the vendor engineering time required for the ten-week retuning effort, the addition of the manual second-look station that had not been part of the original scope, and the cost of retraining 44 workers at an elevated wage rate rather than the smaller number of roles originally anticipated to need retraining.

### What the Company Would Do Differently

Looking back across the full arc of the project — from the commercial threat in late 2022 through the $58 million renewal secured in 2025 — several points stand out as things Saluda's leadership would change if undertaking a similar investment again.

The first is workforce transition planning. The single largest self-inflicted cost of the project was the absence of a retraining and redeployment plan at the moment the finishing automation and optical inspection displaced 38 operators. That gap directly produced the 21 departures, the erosion of trust that culminated in the January 2024 walkout threat, and ultimately the headcount shortfall against the state grant's job-retention floor. Had a retraining pathway and wage structure been designed and communicated before commissioning began, rather than assembled reactively afterward under pressure from the plant committee, it is plausible that most of the 38 displaced workers could have been retained and transitioned into the same technician roles that were eventually created for 44 workers in 2024 — at lower cost, with less disruption, and without the loss of institutional knowledge that 21 departures represented.

The second is vendor performance validation before full-scale commissioning. A 14 percent false rejection rate on an optical inspection system is a substantial performance gap between specification and delivered reality, and the fact that it took a five-month schedule delay followed by a further ten-week on-site retuning effort to correct it suggests that Brandywine Automation's system was not adequately validated against Saluda's actual production conditions — its specific material, lighting environment, and line speed — before being installed as the plant's primary inspection method. A staged commissioning approach, with the manual second-look station built in from the start as a planned redundancy rather than a reactive fix, might have avoided both the throughput collapse and much of the trust damage with the workforce, since operators would have experienced the new system as reliable from the outset rather than as an unreliable system they were later told had been fixed.

The third is the treatment of the grant's job-retention floor as a live operational constraint rather than an administrative formality. At the time of approval, with headcount at 480 against a floor of 460, the condition looked comfortably achievable and was not built into the project's risk planning. In practice, the combination of workforce displacement and attrition brought headcount to 441, below the floor, and required a renegotiation that consumed management time and introduced compliance risk at exactly the point when the company most needed to focus on stabilizing production and its customer relationship. Treating retention floors, once accepted as financing conditions, as constraints that need explicit protection in the project's workforce plan — rather than as background facts expected to take care of themselves — would have avoided a late, avoidable complication in an otherwise successful outcome.

The fourth is the buffer built into both budget and schedule for a first-of-its-kind automation and inspection integration project. The 24 percent cost overrun and five-month schedule delay were not the result of a single identifiable error but of the ordinary difficulty of integrating new automated inspection technology into a live production environment with its own material variation and operating rhythms. A company undertaking a similarly ambitious automation investment, particularly one tied to a hard external deadline set by a customer's price reduction demand, should build meaningfully more schedule and cost contingency into the plan than Saluda did in February 2023, precisely because commissioning problems of this kind are common enough in industrial automation projects that they should be treated as a likely scenario to plan for, not a tail risk to be addressed only if it materializes.

### Conclusion

Saluda Textile Works' experience illustrates both the necessity and the difficulty of large capital investment undertaken under direct commercial threat. The company faced a credible possibility of losing 44 percent of its revenue within three years, and it responded with a capital plan that, in its final outcome, addressed the customer's cost demands, improved quality dramatically, and secured a five-year, $58 million contract renewal. That is a genuine, measurable success, and it validates the fundamental judgment Dagmar Ellingson and Malik Rasheed made in February 2023 that automation and optical inspection were the right levers to pull against the defect rate, direct labor share, and cost structure the customer was pushing against.

But the path to that success ran through a five-month commissioning delay, a malfunctioning inspection system rejecting 14 percent of good material, an 18 percent throughput collapse lasting four months, the loss of more than half of the displaced workforce before the problem was addressed, a workforce walkout threat that forced management's hand, and a final cost 24 percent above the original approval, along with a job-retention commitment the company ultimately failed to meet and had to renegotiate. The recovery led by Josefina Huerta and Clarence Boatwright — ten weeks of technical retuning paired with a genuine retraining and wage program for 44 workers — is what converted a crisis into the strong final results the company reported by mid-2025. For any organization contemplating a similar automation investment under similar commercial pressure, the lesson is not that the investment was wrong, but that the technical and workforce sides of such a project have to be planned and resourced together from the outset, with realistic contingency for both, rather than treated as an engineering project that happens to also involve people.

### Epilogue: The Grant Renegotiation

The renegotiation of the state grant's job-retention terms deserves more attention than a single line in the results summary, because it was, in its own right, a small case study in how a company manages a regulatory relationship when performance falls short of a commitment made in good faith under different assumptions. When Saluda accepted the $1.5 million grant in February 2023, the 460-position floor was not a negotiated compromise; it was a standard condition attached to the incentive program, and Ellingson and Rasheed had no reason at the time to treat it as anything other than a formality given headcount stood at 480. By the time the dust settled on the automation project in mid-2025, headcount was 441, nineteen positions below the floor, and the company found itself in technical breach of a condition tied directly to public financing.

Clarence Boatwright, who had spent the better part of 2024 managing the retraining program and the aftermath of the walkout threat, took the lead in engaging the state economic development office once it became clear that the 21 departures during the commissioning crisis would not be fully offset by net new hiring. Rather than waiting for the December 2024 deadline to pass and treating the shortfall as something to be disclosed after the fact, Boatwright initiated the conversation with the state agency in the third quarter of 2024, while the retraining program was still underway and before the final headcount numbers were locked in. This timing mattered. Presenting the shortfall as a known, actively managed issue, with a clear narrative — a five-month vendor delay, a malfunctioning inspection system, a workforce transition that had produced attrition the company was actively correcting through a $3.10 per hour retraining program — gave the state agency a materially different picture than a company simply reporting after the fact that it had fallen short of a job commitment attached to public money.

The renegotiation ultimately preserved the grant rather than triggering clawback, but it came with new terms: a revised floor calibrated to the post-automation headcount level, additional reporting obligations, and a requirement that Saluda demonstrate continued investment in the retrained technician roles as evidence that the missed floor reflected a productivity transition rather than a simple reduction in commitment to the Spartanburg workforce. The $58 million customer renewal, signed around the same period, played a meaningful role in that conversation, since it gave the state agency concrete evidence that the automation investment underwritten in part by the grant had produced the intended effect of securing the plant's long-term viability rather than simply cutting headcount to reduce cost. In this sense the customer contract and the grant renegotiation were not separate outcomes but connected ones: the commercial result gave Saluda the credibility it needed to renegotiate a public financing condition it had technically breached.

### The View From the Plant Floor

The numbers that define the project's success — 0.9 percent defects, 23 percent throughput gain, $58 million in renewed business — describe the outcome from management's vantage point. The experience looked different from the vantage point of the plant floor, and that difference is worth recording because it shaped how durable the recovery turned out to be.

Rosario Villanueva's committee did not go away after the January 2024 meeting simply because management responded. Villanueva continued to press, through the ten-week retuning period and the rollout of the retraining program, for regular reporting back to the workforce on where the optical inspection system stood and how many of the 38 originally displaced operators had been placed into new roles. This ongoing pressure, uncomfortable as it may have been for Huerta and Boatwright at points, arguably improved the quality of the recovery rather than merely constraining it. A retraining program designed and delivered without sustained workforce input has a tendency to look reasonable on paper while missing the specific concerns that drive attrition in practice — concerns about job security during the transition period, about whether the new technician role was a genuine promotion or a relabeling of a diminished job, and about whether the wage increase attached to retraining was structured to reward experienced operators fairly relative to newer hires moving into the same roles. Because Villanueva's committee remained engaged rather than stepping back once the walkout threat had achieved its immediate purpose, several of these design questions were surfaced and addressed during the ten-week period rather than after the retraining program had already been finalized and rolled out.

By the time the 44 retrained technicians were in place and drawing the $3.10 per hour premium, the plant committee's position had shifted from adversarial to cautiously supportive, a shift that mattered for the throughput and defect rate numbers that followed. A workforce that trusts the equipment it is operating, and trusts that management will address problems with the equipment rather than blame operators for the equipment's failures, behaves differently on the floor than a workforce operating under suspicion and uncertainty. Some portion of the gap between the four months of depressed throughput during the commissioning crisis and the eventual 23 percent throughput gain above baseline is attributable not only to the technical retuning of the optical system but to this shift in the working relationship between the plant committee and plant management — a factor that does not appear anywhere in Saluda's financial reporting but that Huerta and Boatwright both later described internally as central to how quickly the recovery took hold once the technical fixes were in place.

### Financial Reconciliation

It is worth setting the financial elements of the project side by side, since the story of cost, financing, and return is easy to lose amid the operational narrative.

The original capital approval in February 2023 was $9.2 million, financed through a $6 million bank loan and a $1.5 million state grant, leaving roughly $1.7 million funded from the company's own capital or existing credit facilities. By the time the project reached its stable operating state in mid-2025, total spend had grown to $11.4 million, a $2.2 million overrun consisting of the extended vendor engineering time during the five-month delay and subsequent ten-week retuning period, the capital and installation cost of the manual second-look station that was not part of the original scope, and the incremental cost of retraining 44 workers rather than a smaller number originally contemplated, at a $3.10 per hour premium sustained on an ongoing basis rather than a one-time cost.

Against that $11.4 million total investment, the recurring benefits are substantial and, unlike the one-time customer renewal, continue to accrue year over year. The overtime reduction from $2.4 million to $700,000 represents $1.7 million in annual savings, a figure that alone would repay the cost overrun in a little over a year if sustained. The 14 percent reduction in cost per yard, applied across $132 million in original sales volume, represents a materially larger recurring benefit, though the precise figure depends on the proportion of cost of goods sold affected by the finishing and inspection processes the project touched. The defect rate reduction from 3.8 percent to 0.9 percent reduces scrap and rework cost in a way that compounds with the throughput gain, since a plant running 23 percent more material through the same footprint at a quarter of the previous defect rate is generating substantially more good, sellable output per dollar of fixed cost than the 2022 baseline.

Layered on top of these recurring operational benefits is the $58 million, five-year customer renewal, which at minimum protects revenue that had been explicitly at risk of departure, and which came with quality and cost terms that Saluda could meet from a position of demonstrated performance rather than promised future improvement. Measured against this combination of recurring cost savings and a protected, renewed revenue base of $58 million over five years, the $11.4 million total investment — even 24 percent over its original budget — reads as a clearly favorable outcome in financial terms, notwithstanding the operational and workforce turmoil that occurred on the way to that result.

### Takeaways for Similar Organizations

A reader facing comparable circumstances — customer concentration risk, a demand for double-digit price concessions, and a cost structure that cannot absorb them without structural change — should draw several specific conclusions from Saluda's experience rather than a general endorsement of automation as a strategy.

First, the underlying diagnosis matters more than the specific technology chosen. Saluda's leadership correctly identified that its defect rate, labor intensity, and overtime-driven cost inflation were the actual drivers of the cost gap the customer was pushing against, and every one of the eventual results — the defect rate, the cost per yard, the overtime line — mapped directly back to that original diagnosis. A capital project aimed at the wrong cost driver would not have produced a renewal even if it had been commissioned flawlessly.

Second, commissioning risk on new automation and inspection technology should be assumed rather than hoped away, particularly when the technology must integrate with an existing production environment's specific material and conditions rather than operating in a controlled or standardized setting. A 14 percent false rejection rate and a five-month delay were not evidence that Saluda picked the wrong vendor or the wrong technology; they were close to the ordinary experience of this kind of integration, and a project plan that assumes smooth commissioning is a project plan that will be caught flat-footed, as Saluda was, when it is not.

Third, workforce transition planning is not a secondary consideration to be handled after the technical project is designed; it is a primary risk with direct financial consequences, in this case a compliance breach on a state grant and the loss of more than half of a 38-person displaced group before the problem was addressed. Organizations undertaking similar automation projects should build the retraining plan, and ideally the wage structure attached to it, before displacement occurs, not in reaction to attrition and a walkout threat.

Fourth, sustained engagement with workforce representatives during a recovery period, rather than treating a resolved walkout threat as a closed issue, appears to have measurably improved the durability and speed of Saluda's recovery. A plant committee that remains engaged is, on this evidence, more likely to produce a workforce that trusts the corrected system and works with rather than around it.

Fifth, external commitments attached to financing — in this case a state grant's job-retention floor — should be actively monitored against the operational plan throughout a project's execution, not treated as fixed at the point of approval. Saluda's early, proactive engagement with the state agency once a shortfall became likely preserved the grant relationship; a later or more passive disclosure might not have.

Finally, and most fundamentally, the case demonstrates that a genuinely difficult, costly, and disruptive execution process is not incompatible with a strongly positive final outcome, provided the organization is willing to treat problems as they emerge — a missed commissioning date, a malfunctioning inspection system, a workforce walkout threat, a breached grant condition — as issues to be solved directly and transparently rather than managed around or minimized. Saluda Textile Works spent 24 percent more than it planned, missed a regulatory commitment it had to renegotiate, and lost more than half of a displaced workforce segment before correcting course. It also cut its defect rate by three-quarters, increased throughput 23 percent above baseline, cut overtime by more than $1.7 million annually, reduced cost per yard by 14 percent, and secured a five-year, $58 million commitment from the customer whose demand had started the entire process. Both halves of that record are part of the same case, and both are necessary to understand what the investment actually required and what it actually returned.
