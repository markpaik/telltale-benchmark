# Did the Hourly Pay Conversion Reduce Regional Driver Turnover?

## A Research Brief Prepared for the Board of Directors, Sycamore Ridge Transport

**Prepared by:** Marisol Cabrera-Nunez, Workforce Analytics Lead
**Commissioned by:** Fatima Bensalem, Vice President of Driver Recruiting
**Operational context provided by:** Gene Prokop, Terminal Manager, Fort Wayne
**For review by:** Terrence Whitlock, Chief Financial Officer
**Date:** August 9, 2026

---

## 1. The Question and What Was Found

In March 2025, Sycamore Ridge Transport converted its 742 regional less-than-truckload drivers from mileage-based pay to an hourly rate of $28.50 with guaranteed detention pay, at an incremental annual cost of $3.4 million. The board is now considering whether to extend the same structure to the 310-driver over-the-road fleet at an additional annual cost of $6.2 million. Before that decision, the chief financial officer asked a direct question: did the pay conversion actually reduce regional driver turnover, or did turnover fall for other reasons that would have operated regardless?

This brief answers that question as fully as the available payroll and human resources records allow. The headline result is genuine and large: annualized regional driver turnover fell from 51 percent in the twelve months before the conversion (March 2024 through February 2025) to 33 percent in the twelve months after (March 2025 through February 2026), a decline of 18 percentage points. But the headline number cannot be attributed to the pay conversion alone, because two other forces moved at nearly the same moment. A $2,500 driver referral bonus launched in February 2025, one month before the pay change, and regional freight volumes fell roughly 9 percent industry-wide over the post-period, shrinking the pool of outside jobs that departing drivers could move into.

The over-the-road fleet, which received neither the pay conversion nor the referral bonus, provides the most useful benchmark for the market effect. Its turnover fell from 68 percent to 57 percent over the same window — an 11-point decline attributable almost entirely to the softening freight market and general labor-market conditions. Subtracting that background decline from the regional fleet's 18-point improvement suggests that the combined effect of the pay conversion and referral bonus was on the order of 7 percentage points, with the pay conversion accounting for most but not all of that residual. Analysis of the referral-hired cohort, presented in Section 5, indicates the referral bonus accounts for perhaps 1 to 2 of those 7 points, leaving an estimated 5 to 6 points of turnover reduction plausibly attributable to the pay conversion itself.

The effect was not uniform. Fort Wayne turnover fell from 46 to 24 percent and Louisville from 58 to 30 percent — dramatic improvements in the two terminals where drivers previously absorbed the most unpaid detention time under mileage pay. Columbus fell only from 44 to 41 percent, and Evansville was essentially flat, moving from 53 to 51 percent. The terminal pattern is itself informative: the conversion appears to have worked hardest where the old pay structure penalized drivers most, and barely at all where mileage pay already tracked hours reasonably well. Drivers with less than one year of tenure account for 71 percent of all separations in both periods, meaning the conversion's retention benefit operated overwhelmingly through improved first-year survival rather than through retention of veterans.

Translated into dollars, a 5-to-6-point net reduction in turnover across 742 regional drivers represents roughly 37 to 45 avoided separations per year. At a fully loaded replacement cost of $9,000 to $12,000 per driver — recruiting, orientation, training, and productivity ramp — the direct turnover savings are approximately $330,000 to $540,000 per year against a $3.4 million pay-cost increase. The conversion does not pay for itself on turnover savings alone, and this brief does not claim it does. Whether the conversion is worthwhile depends on operational benefits that lie outside this analysis: detention-time behavior, service reliability, safety outcomes, and recruiting yield. Section 8 sets out what the board would need to see before extending the structure to the over-the-road fleet, where the labor market, the competitive pay landscape, and the structure of the working day differ substantially from the regional operation.

## 2. Background

Sycamore Ridge Transport operates 640 tractors and employs approximately 1,100 people across four terminals in Fort Wayne, Columbus, Louisville, and Evansville. The regional fleet of 742 drivers runs day-cab and short-haul linehaul work, generally returning home daily or every other day; the 310-driver over-the-road fleet runs longer irregular-route freight with multi-day dispatches.

Through February 2025, regional drivers were paid by the mile, with a modest and inconsistently applied accessorial for detention beyond two hours. Driver feedback collected by recruiting through 2023 and 2024, and corroborated by exit interviews, identified unpaid waiting time as the leading stated reason for departure among regional drivers, particularly at customer docks served out of Fort Wayne and Louisville. Gene Prokop's route documentation for the Fort Wayne terminal shows that a substantial share of Fort Wayne regional routes serve manufacturing and food-distribution customers with historically long dock times; Louisville's book of business has a similar profile. Columbus and Evansville, by contrast, run a higher proportion of drop-and-hook and terminal-to-terminal linehaul work where drivers spend little unpaid time waiting.

In March 2025, the company converted all regional drivers to $28.50 per hour with guaranteed detention pay — meaning all on-duty time, including waiting time, is compensated at the full hourly rate. The change was designed to be roughly pay-neutral for the median driver on a typical week but materially favorable for drivers on detention-heavy routes. The realized incremental cost has been $3.4 million per year, somewhat above the original estimate, driven mainly by detention hours at Fort Wayne and Louisville.

One month earlier, in February 2025, the company launched a $2,500 employee referral bonus, paid half at the referred driver's hire and half at their six-month anniversary. The bonus applies company-wide but in practice has generated hires almost exclusively into the regional fleet, because regional openings dominated hiring during the period.

Finally, the freight environment shifted. Industry data and the company's own volume records show regional less-than-truckload volumes down approximately 9 percent year over year through the post-period. Softer freight means fewer competing carriers hiring, fewer recruiter calls to our drivers, and less confidence among drivers considering a jump. Every turnover analysis conducted during a freight downturn must reckon with the fact that turnover falls industry-wide in soft markets whether or not any individual carrier changes anything.

## 3. Data and Methods

### 3.1 Records used

The analysis draws on four record systems.

**Payroll records** (ADP, complete for both periods) supply pay type, hours, miles, detention pay, and gross earnings for every driver-week. These records are the authoritative source for identifying which drivers were on mileage versus hourly pay and when each driver converted.

**Human resources records** (the HRIS, complete for both periods) supply hire dates, termination dates, termination reason codes, terminal assignment, fleet assignment (regional versus over-the-road), and tenure. Voluntary and involuntary separations are distinguished by reason code; this brief's turnover figures include voluntary separations only unless otherwise noted, because the pay conversion could plausibly affect only voluntary quits. Involuntary terminations (safety, attendance, performance) ran at similar rates in both periods — approximately 6 percent annualized before and 7 percent after — and are excluded from the headline figures.

**Recruiting records** (the applicant tracking system) identify referral-sourced hires, referral bonus payments, application volumes, and time-to-fill by terminal.

**Telematics detention records** begin in April 2024, when the current telematics platform went live. They capture geofenced dwell time at customer locations and permit route-level measurement of detention exposure — but only for eleven of the twelve pre-period months and none of the period before April 2024. This truncation matters and is discussed in Section 7.

### 3.2 Definitions

Annualized turnover is calculated as voluntary separations during the twelve-month window divided by average monthly headcount, expressed as an annual rate. The pre-period runs March 2024 through February 2025; the post-period runs March 2025 through February 2026. February 2025 — the month the referral bonus launched but before the pay conversion — is retained in the pre-period; sensitivity checks excluding it change no result by more than half a percentage point.

Drivers are assigned to fleet and terminal by their status at the start of each period. Eighty-nine drivers changed terminals mid-period; their treatment is discussed below and in the limitations section.

### 3.3 Analytical approach

The core method is a difference-in-differences comparison. The regional fleet is the treated group; the over-the-road fleet is the comparison group. Both fleets faced the same freight downturn, the same regional labor markets, the same company management, and the same benefits structure. Only the regional fleet received the pay conversion and, in practice, the referral bonus effect. The over-the-road fleet's turnover change therefore estimates what would have happened to regional turnover absent the interventions, and the difference between the two fleets' changes estimates the interventions' combined effect.

This design rests on the assumption that the two fleets' turnover would have moved in parallel absent the intervention — the parallel-trends assumption. Section 4.2 examines the historical evidence for it. The design's known weakness is that regional and over-the-road driving are different jobs with different labor markets, and a 9 percent freight decline may not hit both markets equally. Section 7 treats this seriously.

To separate the referral bonus from the pay conversion, the analysis compares twelve-month survival rates of referral-sourced hires against non-referral hires from the same terminals and hire months, and bounds the bonus's contribution to the aggregate turnover change by its share of the post-period driver population.

Terminal-level results are computed the same way at each terminal, and detention exposure from the telematics data (April 2024 onward) is used to test whether the pay conversion's effect scales with the amount of unpaid time drivers previously absorbed — the mechanism the conversion was designed to address.

Tenure analysis uses monthly hire cohorts and tracks the share of each cohort still employed at 90 days, 180 days, and one year, comparing cohorts hired before and after the conversion.

### 3.4 Handling of terminal transfers

The 89 drivers who changed terminals mid-period are included in fleet-level results (a transfer is not a separation) but excluded from terminal-level turnover calculations, because assigning their exposure to a single terminal would distort both the origin and destination terminal's rates. Their exclusion reduces terminal-level sample sizes modestly; sensitivity runs assigning transfers to their majority-time terminal move no terminal's rate by more than 1.5 percentage points and change no qualitative conclusion.

## 4. Results: The Fleet-Level Picture

### 4.1 Before and after

Regional voluntary turnover fell from 51 percent annualized in the pre-period to 33 percent in the post-period. In raw counts, the regional fleet recorded 372 voluntary separations in the twelve months before the conversion and 243 in the twelve months after, against an average headcount that held near 742 in both periods. The decline was not concentrated in any single quarter; monthly separation counts fell within two months of the conversion and stayed lower through the end of the window.

A figure prepared for the board presentation (Figure 1, described here rather than drawn) plots monthly annualized turnover for both fleets from January 2023 through February 2026 as two lines, with vertical markers at the February 2025 referral-bonus launch and the March 2025 pay conversion. The regional line runs persistently below the over-the-road line, both lines drift downward from mid-2024 as the freight market softens, and the regional line breaks visibly away from the over-the-road line beginning in the second quarter of 2025 — the gap between the lines, roughly 17 points before the conversion, widens to roughly 24 points after it. That widening gap is the visual signature of the treatment effect.

### 4.2 The over-the-road benchmark and the difference-in-differences estimate

Over-the-road turnover fell from 68 percent to 57 percent over the same window — an 11-point decline with no pay change and effectively no referral hiring. This decline is the market speaking: a 9 percent freight contraction, fewer competing carriers hiring, and longer stays by drivers who in a hot market would have moved.

The difference-in-differences arithmetic is straightforward and worth stating plainly for the board:

| | Pre-period | Post-period | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regional fleet (treated) | 51% | 33% | −18 points |
| Over-the-road fleet (comparison) | 68% | 57% | −11 points |
| **Difference-in-differences** | | | **−7 points** |

The estimated combined effect of the pay conversion and referral bonus is a 7-percentage-point reduction in annualized regional turnover, over and above what market conditions alone would have produced.

The parallel-trends assumption receives reasonable support from history. Over the eight quarters preceding the conversion, quarterly changes in the two fleets' annualized turnover rates track each other closely: both rose through the tight labor market of 2023, and both began declining in the second half of 2024 as freight softened, with the gap between them holding between 15 and 19 points throughout. There is no pre-period quarter in which the two fleets' rates moved in opposite directions by more than three points. This is not proof — over-the-road and regional labor markets could have diverged in the post-period for reasons unrelated to our interventions — but it is the pattern one would want to see before trusting the design, and it is present.

Two considerations suggest the 7-point estimate may understate the true effect, and one suggests it may overstate it. On the understatement side: first, over-the-road turnover benefits from a compositional effect during downturns, because over-the-road fleets shrink hiring fastest and new hires — the highest-turnover group — become a smaller share of the fleet; some of the over-the-road fleet's 11-point decline reflects this composition shift rather than pure market softening, which would make the market benchmark too favorable and the treatment estimate too small. Second, awareness of the regional fleet's new pay structure may have induced a small number of over-the-road drivers to stay in hopes of transferring or of the structure being extended, which would again depress the comparison group's turnover and shrink the measured difference. On the overstatement side: regional freight may have softened somewhat less than over-the-road freight in our lanes, meaning regional drivers faced a somewhat weaker outside market than the over-the-road benchmark implies. On balance, the 7-point figure is presented as a central estimate with a plausible range of 5 to 9 points for the combined interventions.

## 5. Disentangling the Referral Bonus and the Freight Cycle

### 5.1 The referral bonus

The referral bonus and the pay conversion launched a month apart and cannot be separated by timing alone. Two other approaches narrow the bonus's contribution.

First, scale. From February 2025 through February 2026, the program generated 61 referral hires into the regional fleet, out of 204 total regional hires in that window. Referral hires are a retention story only to the extent that they survive better than the hires they replaced. They do: twelve-month survival for referral hires runs 71 percent, against 55 percent for non-referral hires made in the same months at the same terminals. That 16-point survival advantage is consistent with the research literature on referred employees, who arrive with realistic job expectations and a social tie to the workplace.

Second, arithmetic. Sixty-one referral hires with a 16-point survival advantage translate to roughly 10 avoided first-year separations relative to a counterfactual in which the same seats were filled through ordinary channels. Against a fleet of 742 and roughly 129 avoided separations implied by the 18-point gross decline, ten separations is about 1.3 percentage points of annualized turnover. Allowing for imprecision, the referral bonus plausibly accounts for 1 to 2 points of the 7-point difference-in-differences estimate.

One caution: referral hires may be better survivors partly because the drivers who refer them are themselves stayers, and because referrers vouch only for candidates they believe will last. The bonus may thus be capturing selection rather than creating retention. This cuts toward attributing even less of the aggregate effect to the bonus, since some of those 61 candidates might have been hired and survived anyway. The 1-to-2-point attribution is therefore treated as an upper bound on the bonus's contribution.

Subtracting the bonus's contribution from the combined estimate leaves 5 to 6 percentage points of turnover reduction attributable to the pay conversion itself — the central finding of this brief.

### 5.2 The freight cycle

The freight cycle's contribution is already netted out by the difference-in-differences design, but its magnitude deserves emphasis because it is the largest single component of the raw improvement. Of the regional fleet's 18-point gross decline, roughly 11 points — well over half — is market, not management. Any presentation of the conversion's results that leads with the 51-to-33 headline without this decomposition would materially overstate the program's effect, and this brief recommends against using the gross figure in board materials except alongside the decomposition.

The decomposition in summary:

| Component | Estimated contribution |
|---|---|
| Freight cycle and labor-market softening | ~11 points |
| Referral bonus | 1–2 points |
| **Hourly pay conversion** | **5–6 points** |
| **Total gross decline** | **18 points** |

A corollary worth stating now, before the market turns: when freight recovers, the market component reverses. If the pay conversion's true effect is 5 to 6 points, then in a tight 2027 freight market regional turnover should be expected to rise back toward the mid-40s — better than the counterfactual, but well above today's 33 percent. The board should not anchor on 33 percent as the new steady state.

## 6. Results by Terminal and by Tenure

### 6.1 Terminal-level results

The terminal pattern is the most analytically valuable evidence in this brief, because it lets us test the conversion's mechanism rather than merely its aggregate effect.

| Terminal | Pre-period turnover | Post-period turnover | Change | Avg. weekly detention hours per driver (post-Apr 2024) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fort Wayne | 46% | 24% | −22 points | 6.8 |
| Louisville | 58% | 30% | −28 points | 7.4 |
| Columbus | 44% | 41% | −3 points | 2.1 |
| Evansville | 53% | 51% | −2 points | 2.6 |

The conversion was designed on a specific theory: mileage pay drove drivers out because it forced them to absorb unpaid waiting time, and paying for all hours would remove that grievance. If the theory is right, the effect should be largest where detention was heaviest. It is. Fort Wayne and Louisville — the two terminals whose route books, per Gene Prokop's documentation, are heavy with live-load manufacturing and food-distribution customers — averaged roughly seven hours of geofenced detention per driver per week in the telematics data. Under mileage pay, that was seven hours of unpaid work; under the new structure it is roughly $200 of weekly pay. Those two terminals delivered 22- and 28-point turnover reductions. Columbus and Evansville, running drop-and-hook and terminal-to-terminal linehaul with two to three detention hours per week, saw the conversion change drivers' effective pay very little — and their turnover barely moved, declining by amounts consistent with the market effect alone or less.

A second figure prepared for the presentation (Figure 2, described) plots each terminal as a point, with average pre-conversion weekly detention hours on the horizontal axis and the terminal's turnover change on the vertical axis. The four points fall close to a downward-sloping line: more prior unpaid detention, larger turnover improvement. With only four terminals no statistical claim is made, but the pattern is exactly what the pay conversion's design theory predicts, and it is difficult to construct a confounding story that produces it. The freight cycle, for instance, hit all four terminals' markets broadly alike and cannot explain why Louisville improved 28 points while Evansville improved 2.

Two complications temper the terminal story. First, Columbus and Evansville's flat results raise the question of whether the company is now paying those terminals' drivers $28.50 hourly — with the attendant cost — for essentially no retention return. The answer at those terminals may be yes on retention narrowly, though pay-structure uniformity across terminals has administrative and fairness value, and drivers do transfer between terminals. Second, Evansville's flat result may partly reflect a local factor: a competing carrier opened a terminal in Evansville in mid-2025 and recruited actively there, which would suppress Evansville's improvement for reasons unrelated to our pay structure. The HRIS exit data show a cluster of Evansville departures coded as "left for competitor" in the third quarter of 2025 consistent with this account.

### 6.2 Tenure

Drivers with less than one year of tenure account for 71 percent of all voluntary separations, in both periods. The conversion's aggregate effect therefore had to run through the first-year population, and cohort survival curves confirm it did.

For hire cohorts entering in the pre-period, 90-day survival was 78 percent, 180-day survival 64 percent, and twelve-month survival 51 percent. For cohorts hired after the conversion, the corresponding figures are 85, 74, and 61 percent. Described as a figure (Figure 3), the two survival curves separate almost immediately — within the first 90 days — and the gap between them widens through six months before stabilizing. The early separation matters: it indicates the conversion changed the experience of the first weeks on the job, which is when new regional drivers at Fort Wayne and Louisville previously discovered that their paychecks did not reflect their hours. Under mileage pay, a new driver assigned detention-heavy routes could work a 55-hour week and be paid for the equivalent of 40; the exit interviews from 2024 are explicit that this discovery drove early quits. Under hourly pay the paycheck matches the hours from week one.

Veteran turnover — drivers with more than one year of tenure — fell from 15 percent to 12 percent annualized, a modest change consistent with the market effect alone. The conversion should be understood, and marketed internally, as a first-year retention intervention. Its economics accordingly depend heavily on hiring volume: in years when the company hires many drivers, the conversion prevents many early quits; in low-hiring years its retention return shrinks even if its cost does not.

The tenure finding also carries a caution for the difference-in-differences estimate noted in Section 4.2: because the over-the-road fleet hired very few drivers in the soft post-period market, its post-period population skewed toward veterans, mechanically lowering its turnover. Adjusting the over-the-road benchmark for this composition shift — reweighting the post-period over-the-road population to the pre-period tenure mix — puts the market-driven decline at roughly 8 to 9 points rather than 11, which would raise the pay conversion's estimated effect toward the upper end of the 5-to-6-point range or slightly above it.

## 7. Limitations

Six limitations bound the confidence the board should place in these estimates.

**The comparison group is imperfect.** Over-the-road and regional driving are different jobs drawing on overlapping but distinct labor pools. The 9 percent freight decline may have hit the two markets unequally, and over-the-road turnover is structurally higher and more volatile. The parallel pre-trends are reassuring but do not guarantee the post-period counterfactual. The 5-to-9-point range on the combined effect reflects this uncertainty; it cannot be narrowed with the data we have.

**The pay conversion and referral bonus launched a month apart.** No design can fully separate two interventions applied to the same population at nearly the same time. The referral-cohort analysis in Section 5.1 bounds the bonus's contribution, but the bound rests on comparing referral to non-referral hires, and those groups differ in ways beyond referral status. The attribution of 5 to 6 points to the pay conversion specifically is an estimate, not a measurement.

**Detention records begin in April 2024.** The telematics platform's April 2024 start date means we can characterize detention exposure for only eleven of twelve pre-period months and have no detention data for earlier years. The terminal-level mechanism analysis in Section 6.1 therefore assumes that the detention profile observed from April 2024 onward is representative of the routes drivers experienced before then. Prokop's route documentation supports this — the customer mix at each terminal was stable through the period — but it cannot be verified in the telematics record. Additionally, we cannot measure whether the conversion changed drivers' detention behavior (for example, drivers under hourly pay having less incentive to push customers to load quickly), which bears directly on the $3.4 million realized cost and is flagged for operations review.

**Eighty-nine drivers changed terminals mid-period.** Their exclusion from terminal-level rates is defensible but means terminal results describe stayers-in-place. If transfers were disproportionately drivers fleeing detention-heavy Fort Wayne and Louisville routes before the conversion, their exclusion could modestly overstate those terminals' improvement. Sensitivity runs suggest the distortion is under 1.5 points at any terminal, but the transfer population is too small to analyze separately with confidence.

**One year of post-period data.** Twelve months captures the conversion's effect on first-year retention — appropriately, given that first-year drivers dominate separations — but cannot reveal whether the conversion changes three- and five-year retention, nor how it performs in a tight freight market. The estimates here are one-downturn-year estimates.

**Turnover is not the only outcome.** This brief measures what it was asked to measure. The conversion may have affected safety incidents, service metrics, recruiting yield, application volumes, and driver productivity per hour — all of which belong in the full business case and none of which are quantified here. Recruiting records show regional application volume up 34 percent post-conversion, but disentangling that from the soft freight market (which increases applications everywhere) was outside this brief's scope.

## 8. Implications and What the Board Needs to Decide on the Over-the-Road Fleet

### 8.1 What the evidence supports

The evidence supports three conclusions with reasonable confidence. First, the hourly conversion caused a real reduction in regional turnover — the central estimate is 5 to 6 percentage points, distinct from the freight cycle and the referral bonus. Second, the mechanism was the elimination of unpaid detention time, demonstrated by the sharp concentration of the effect at the two detention-heavy terminals and in the first year of tenure. Third, the conversion does not pay for itself through turnover savings alone: 37 to 45 avoided separations per year at $9,000 to $12,000 each yields $330,000 to $540,000 against $3.4 million in incremental pay cost. The business case for the regional conversion, if it exists, rests on the retention benefit plus service, safety, recruiting, and detention-billing effects not measured here.

### 8.2 Why the over-the-road extension is not a simple extrapolation

The board should be cautious about reading the regional results across to the over-the-road fleet, for three structural reasons.

First, the mechanism may not transfer. The regional conversion worked where drivers absorbed heavy unpaid detention under mileage pay. Over-the-road drivers face detention too, but the over-the-road pay grievance in exit interviews is different — it centers on time away from home and on unpaid non-driving time of a different character (layovers, breakdowns, load cancellations). An hourly rate addresses some of this; guaranteed detention pay addresses some; neither addresses home time, which the same exit data identify as the leading over-the-road quit reason. The intervention that cut regional turnover was well matched to the regional grievance. It is not obviously well matched to the over-the-road grievance.

Second, the terminal results warn against uniform application. Columbus and Evansville received the identical intervention and showed essentially no retention response, because their drivers had little unpaid time to be compensated for. If over-the-road routes resemble Columbus more than Louisville in their unpaid-time profile, a $6.2 million extension could buy very little retention.

Third, the competitive context differs. Regional hourly pay at $28.50 is a distinctive offer in our markets; hourly pay for irregular-route over-the-road work is rarer industry-wide and its competitive value is untested in our lanes.

### 8.3 What the board needs before deciding

The board should require five things before approving the $6.2 million extension.

**One: an over-the-road unpaid-time audit.** Using the telematics data now available for the full over-the-road fleet, quantify how much on-duty, non-driving, currently-unpaid time over-the-road drivers actually absorb per week, by lane and customer. This is the single number that predicts whether the regional mechanism will transfer. If over-the-road unpaid time resembles Fort Wayne and Louisville (seven hours weekly), the extension has a plausible mechanism; if it resembles Columbus (two hours), it does not, and the $6.2 million would be better directed at home-time scheduling or a different pay design.

**Two: a piloted rollout rather than a fleet-wide conversion.** Convert a defined subset — one dispatch board of roughly 80 to 100 over-the-road drivers, selected to include both detention-heavy and detention-light lanes — for twelve months, with the remaining over-the-road drivers as the comparison group. The regional experience shows that terminal- and route-level variation dominates the average effect; a pilot structured across that variation would tell the board where the extension works before committing the full $6.2 million. Estimated pilot cost is $1.6 to $2.0 million for the year.

**Three: a full-cost business case, not a turnover-only case.** The regional analysis shows turnover savings covering roughly one-sixth of the pay-cost increase. Finance should quantify, for both the regional program retrospectively and the over-the-road proposal prospectively: detention revenue recovered from customers (hourly pay creates both the data and the incentive to bill detention), safety and claims trends, recruiting cost per hire, and service metrics. If those lines do not close most of the gap for the regional program, the case for extending it is weak regardless of the turnover result.

**Four: a freight-cycle stress test.** The post-period was a soft market. Finance should model the extension's cost and the expected turnover benefit under a tight-market scenario in which base turnover rises 10 to 15 points fleet-wide and competitor pay rises. The conversion's protective value may actually be greater in a tight market — an untested but plausible hypothesis the pilot could examine if the cycle turns during the pilot year.

**Five: continued monitoring of the regional program through the next freight upcycle.** The board should treat the regional program's 33 percent turnover figure as cyclical, not structural, and should see at least two more quarters of data — ideally spanning the beginning of a freight recovery — before using regional results as the template for a $6.2 million commitment. The monitoring package should also track whether detention hours themselves drift upward under hourly pay, which would signal an incentive problem requiring operational controls and would raise the program's realized cost.

### 8.4 Closing assessment

The pay conversion worked, where it had something to fix. That sentence carries both the finding and the caution. Regional turnover fell 18 points; roughly 11 of those points were the freight market, 1 to 2 were the referral bonus, and 5 to 6 were the conversion — concentrated almost entirely at the two terminals where mileage pay had forced drivers to work hours they were not paid for, and among the first-year drivers who quit when they discovered it. Where drivers had no such grievance, the conversion changed nothing measurable. The over-the-road decision should therefore not turn on whether the regional program succeeded — it did, modestly and for identifiable reasons — but on whether the over-the-road fleet has the same disease. The audit and pilot proposed above will answer that question for roughly one-third the cost of finding out the expensive way.
