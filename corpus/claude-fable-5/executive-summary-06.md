## Executive Summary 1 of 4

### Northside Trade Collective — Pathways to Trades Workforce Program

**Grant:** $1.8 million over three years, Opportunity Portfolio, January 2023 – December 2025
**Date of summary:** October 17, 2025

**Finding and recommendation.** The Pathways to Trades program is the strongest performer in the Opportunity Portfolio by every measure we track: enrollment, placement, wage gain, retention, and cost per outcome. Northside Trade Collective delivered what it proposed, on budget, and with results that compare favorably to national benchmarks for sector-based workforce training. I recommend that the council renew this grant at an increased level of $2.4 million over three years, contingent on a modest expansion plan and continued twelve-month retention tracking. This is the portfolio's clearest case for reinvestment, and the renewal decision carries the least risk of any before the council.

**What the money bought.** The foundation's $1.8 million funded a sector-based training program preparing unemployed and underemployed adults in Jacksonville's Northside for careers in the skilled trades — primarily HVAC, electrical, welding, and commercial construction. The grant covered instructor salaries, equipment and consumables for hands-on training, participant stipends during the training period, and a case management function that follows graduates for a full year after placement. Over the grant term, the Collective enrolled 480 adults, exceeding its target of 450. Of those enrolled, 71 percent were placed into jobs in their field of training. Median hourly wages for placed participants rose from $13.10 at intake to $21.40 at placement, a gain of 63 percent that moves a full-time worker from roughly $27,000 to roughly $44,500 in annual earnings. Most importantly, 64 percent of placed participants remained employed at twelve months — the measure that most workforce programs either fail to track or fail to achieve. The cost per twelve-month retained placement works out to approximately $8,250, well within the range the field considers strong value for training that produces wage gains of this magnitude.

**Evidence and analysis.** Three features of this program's performance deserve the council's attention, because they explain why the results are real rather than an artifact of favorable measurement.

First, the Collective's numbers are verified, not self-reported. Placement and wage data were confirmed through employer records and pay stubs for a sample of 96 participants drawn by my office, and the sample results matched the Collective's reported figures within two percentage points. The twelve-month retention figure comes from direct participant follow-up with an 88 percent response rate, which is exceptionally high for this population and reflects the case management investment the grant deliberately funded.

Second, the program did not achieve its results by screening out hard-to-serve applicants. Forty-two percent of enrollees had a prior justice-system involvement, 31 percent lacked stable housing at intake, and the median enrollee had been out of full-time work for more than a year. The Collective's outcomes were achieved with the population the Opportunity Portfolio was designed to reach, not around it.

Third, demand exceeds capacity. The Collective maintained a waiting list averaging 110 applicants throughout 2024 and 2025, and its employer partners — including three of the region's largest mechanical contractors — have formally requested larger training cohorts. Employer demand of this kind is the single best predictor that placement rates will hold as a program scales, because it means graduates are absorbed into real vacancies rather than pushed into marginal hires.

The program is not without weaknesses. Completion among women enrollees lagged the overall rate by nine percentage points, which the Collective attributes to childcare gaps during evening lab sessions; its expansion proposal includes a childcare stipend to address this, and I regard the proposed fix as credible but unproven. The twelve-month retention rate of 64 percent, while strong, also means roughly one in three placed participants left their jobs within a year, and the Collective's exit data on those departures is thin. A renewal grant should require better documentation of why retained and non-retained participants diverge.

**Recommendation to the council.** Renew at $2.4 million over three years, an increase of one third, to fund an expansion from 160 to 210 enrollees per year, the childcare stipend pilot, and continued twelve-month tracking. The increase is justified by demonstrated demand, verified outcomes, and a unit cost that improves modestly at the larger scale. I recommend the council attach two conditions: annual verification of placement and wage data by my office using the sampling method employed in this review, and a mid-grant report on the childcare stipend's effect on women's completion rates. Neither condition is burdensome, and the Collective's leadership has already indicated it would welcome both.

**What the foundation owes the grantee and its participants if funding changes.** Because I am recommending renewal at an increased level, the obligations question here is largely about continuity rather than wind-down, but the council should understand it in both directions. If the council renews, the foundation owes the Collective a decision communicated by November 14 so that the January 2026 cohort — recruitment for which is already underway on the strength of the existing grant — can enroll without interruption. A gap of even one quarter would strand roughly 40 applicants who have completed intake and would damage the employer relationships that make the placement rate possible. If the council declines to renew, contrary to my recommendation, the foundation's obligations are still substantial: the current grant funds twelve-month case management for participants placed as late as December 2025, which means the foundation has an outstanding commitment to follow-up services running through December 2026. I would recommend a $150,000 closeout supplement to fund that tail of case management, honest and early communication to the Collective's employer partners that the foundation, not the Collective, made the decision, and active assistance connecting the Collective to alternative funders — its results make it highly fundable, and a warm introduction from this foundation would materially shorten its search. Participants currently enrolled must in either scenario be permitted to complete their training; the foundation should never fund a cohort it is not prepared to graduate.

The bottom line for the council is straightforward. This is what the Opportunity Portfolio was created to find: a locally rooted organization producing verified, durable economic gains for the residents the foundation exists to serve, at a defensible cost, with room to grow. I recommend renewal without reservation.

## Executive Summary 2 of 4

### River City Legal Aid — Eviction Prevention Legal Services Program

**Grant:** $2.2 million over three years, Opportunity Portfolio, January 2023 – December 2025
**Date of summary:** October 17, 2025

**Finding and recommendation.** The eviction prevention program at River City Legal Aid has delivered high-quality legal representation to the households it reached, but it reached far fewer than promised: 610 households served against a target of 1,500, a shortfall of 59 percent, driven principally by the loss of two of its four staff attorneys within a nine-month period and a compensation structure that cannot retain the lawyers the program requires. The program now carries a backlog of 340 open matters — active cases involving real families facing displacement — that the foundation cannot ethically walk away from. I recommend a conditional one-year restructuring grant of $900,000, not a full three-year renewal, with continued funding beyond 2026 contingent on a credible staffing and retention plan approved by March 31, 2026. I further recommend that the council explicitly fund resolution of the existing backlog regardless of its renewal decision, for reasons of obligation set out below.

**What the money bought.** The $2.2 million grant funded four staff attorney positions, two paralegal positions, an intake coordinator, and associated litigation costs to provide free legal representation to low-income Duval County tenants facing eviction. The program was designed around the well-documented finding that represented tenants avoid displacement at dramatically higher rates than unrepresented ones; in Duval County eviction court, fewer than five percent of tenants historically appear with counsel while nearly all landlords do. Over the grant term, the program provided full representation to 610 households comprising approximately 1,580 individuals, including an estimated 690 children. Among closed cases, the program achieved outcomes it classifies as favorable — eviction avoided, judgment vacated, negotiated move-out with sealed record, or substantial monetary relief — in 74 percent of matters. That success rate, which my office spot-verified against court records for a sample of 60 cases, is genuinely strong and consistent with results from right-to-counsel programs in comparable jurisdictions.

**Evidence and analysis.** The council should hold two truths at once. Where this program showed up, it worked. It simply could not show up often enough.

The staffing collapse is the central fact of this evaluation. Two of four staff attorneys resigned between June 2024 and February 2025, both citing compensation: the program's attorney salaries, budgeted at $62,000 in the original proposal, sit roughly $25,000 below what the State Attorney's Office and comparable legal aid organizations in Central Florida now pay for equivalent experience. Both departures were to better-paying public-sector legal positions. Recruitment to backfill the roles took seven and nine months respectively, and one position turned over a second time. During the depleted period, the program throttled intake sharply — an appropriate professional decision, since accepting clients without capacity to represent them would be worse than turning them away — but the consequence was that in 2024, its planned peak year, the program served 143 households against a planned 550.

The 340-matter backlog requires the council's careful attention, because it is not an abstraction. These are open, active legal matters in which River City Legal Aid attorneys have filed appearances on behalf of tenant clients. Under the Rules Regulating The Florida Bar, an attorney who has appeared in a matter cannot simply abandon it; withdrawal requires court permission and, as a practical and ethical matter, an orderly transition that protects the client. There is no realistic alternative provider with capacity to absorb 340 eviction matters. If funding ends abruptly, the likely outcome is that hundreds of families the foundation's money brought into representation would face displacement proceedings mid-case with diminishing counsel. The foundation's name is on this program, and its exit obligations here are heavier than for any other grant in the portfolio.

It is fair to ask whether the shortfall reflects poor management rather than bad luck, and my assessment is that it reflects a design flaw the grantee and the foundation share. The original budget's salary assumptions were unrealistic when written and became untenable as public-sector legal salaries rose sharply in 2023 and 2024. River City's leadership flagged the retention risk in its first annual report; neither the grantee nor foundation staff acted on it. The organization's financial management is otherwise sound, its case outcomes are excellent, and its executive director has produced a restructuring proposal — three attorneys at market salaries rather than four below market, supplemented by a supervised pro bono panel drawn from three Jacksonville firms that have committed letters of intent — that I find credible though not yet proven.

**Recommendation to the council.** Approve a one-year restructuring grant of $900,000 for calendar 2026, allocated in two parts: approximately $350,000 dedicated to resolving the 340-matter backlog under the current team, and $550,000 to implement the restructured staffing model at market compensation with a reduced, honest target of 400 new households. Continued funding into 2027 and beyond should be contingent on the council's review, by early 2027, of attorney retention through the year, backlog reduction to under 75 open matters, and demonstrated pro bono panel activity. I recommend against both a full three-year renewal, which the program's track record does not yet support, and outright termination, which the foundation's obligations do not permit.

**What the foundation owes the grantee and its participants if funding changes.** If the council adopts my recommendation, the foundation owes the grantee clarity: written milestones, a named foundation contact, and a commitment that the 2027 decision will rest on the stated criteria and nothing else. If the council instead declines all further funding, the foundation still owes the 340 households in open matters a funded, orderly resolution — I estimate $300,000 to $350,000 and nine to twelve months to close or responsibly transfer every open case — and I would ask the council to treat that closeout as a non-discretionary obligation rather than a grant decision. The foundation also owes the grantee sixty days' written notice, assistance approaching the Florida Bar Foundation and other legal services funders, and candor with the courts and community partners about why the program is contracting. What the foundation owes participants above all is that no family brought into representation with the foundation's money loses its home because the foundation left mid-case. That principle should govern regardless of how the council votes on the future of the program itself.

## Executive Summary 3 of 4

### Compass Youth Collaborative — School-Based Youth Mental Health Initiative

**Grant:** $1.5 million over three years, Opportunity Portfolio, January 2023 – December 2025
**Date of summary:** October 17, 2025

**Finding and recommendation.** The school-based youth mental health initiative, operated by Compass Youth Collaborative in partnership with the Duval, Clay, and Nassau county school districts, has built real infrastructure — trained staff in schools, screening protocols, district relationships — and completed 1,140 mental health screenings of middle and high school students. But the initiative cannot yet answer the only question that ultimately matters: whether screened and referred students got better. Its data-sharing agreement with the districts, essential to tracking students from screening through treatment to outcomes, was not executed until March 2025, twenty-six months into a thirty-six-month grant. Compounding this, only 38 percent of students referred for services actually attended a first appointment, meaning most identified needs went unmet. I recommend an eighteen-month no-cost-logic extension funded at $650,000 — not a full renewal and not termination — with continuation beyond mid-2027 contingent on outcome data and a referral-conversion rate of at least 55 percent. The honest characterization of this initiative is that the foundation has paid for the capacity to do the work and is only now positioned to learn whether the work works.

**What the money bought.** The $1.5 million grant funded universal and targeted mental health screening in eleven schools across three districts, two licensed clinical social workers and four master's-level screening coordinators embedded in schools, training for 240 teachers and school staff in identifying warning signs, and a referral pathway connecting flagged students to treatment through Compass's own clinicians and community providers. Over the grant term, the initiative completed 1,140 screenings, identified 505 students as needing clinical follow-up, and made referrals for all of them. Of those 505 referrals, 192 students — 38 percent — attended a first clinical appointment. The initiative also negotiated, over two years of legal review by three separate district counsel offices, the data-sharing agreement under FERPA and HIPAA that now permits tracking of individual students from screening through treatment. That agreement, signed in March 2025, is genuinely valuable infrastructure; it is also two years late, and its lateness is why this evaluation contains no outcome data.

**Evidence and analysis.** The council needs a clear-eyed account of what went wrong and what is salvageable, because this initiative sits precisely at the boundary between a slow start and a failure.

The data-sharing delay was foreseeable and was foreseen. My office's file review shows that Compass's original proposal budgeted three months for agreement negotiation, an assumption foundation staff accepted without challenge despite ample sector experience that multi-district student-data agreements routinely take a year or more. The delay itself was driven substantially by district legal caution rather than Compass inaction — the correspondence record shows Compass pressing consistently — but the consequence is unavoidable: for twenty-six of thirty-six months, the initiative screened students without the legal ability to learn what happened to them afterward. Since March 2025, tracking has begun for newly screened students, and the first outcome cohort will be analyzable by roughly December 2026. Terminating now would mean the foundation paid for the pipeline and left before any water flowed through it.

The referral-conversion problem is more troubling because it is a service failure rather than a paperwork failure. A 38 percent conversion rate means that of 505 students identified as needing help, 313 did not receive even a first appointment. Compass's own analysis, which my office finds broadly credible, attributes the gap to three causes in descending order: family transportation and scheduling barriers, particularly in Nassau County where the nearest participating provider is over thirty minutes from two of the schools; parental consent forms that went home and never came back, with no funded follow-up capacity to pursue them; and community-provider waitlists of six to ten weeks that caused families to disengage. None of these causes is exotic, and all have known remedies — school-sited telehealth, active consent follow-up by the screening coordinators, warm handoffs rather than paper referrals — which Compass's extension proposal includes. But the council should be clear that identifying children's mental health needs and then failing to connect three in five of them to care is not a neutral outcome. Screening without follow-through can be worse than not screening, both for the child who is told help is coming and for the family's future willingness to engage.

The district partnerships, by contrast, are an unambiguous asset. All three districts have signed letters requesting the initiative's continuation, and Duval has offered in-kind space and a shared-cost position beginning in the 2026–27 school year. District co-investment of this kind typically takes years to secure and would be forfeited by termination.

**Recommendation to the council.** Approve an eighteen-month extension at $650,000, covering January 2026 through June 2027, scoped narrowly to three purposes: continue screening in the existing eleven schools at current volume, implement the referral-conversion fixes with an explicit target of 55 percent first-appointment attendance, and produce the initiative's first outcome report — symptom-change and school-engagement measures for the post-March-2025 cohort — by January 2027. Any decision about a full second-phase grant should wait for that report. I recommend the council reject both a full three-year renewal, which would reward promise over performance, and termination, which would abandon the foundation's investment at the exact moment it becomes measurable.

**What the foundation owes the grantee and its participants if funding changes.** The participants here are children, and that changes the character of the foundation's obligations. If the council funds the extension, the foundation owes Compass a decision by mid-November so that district staffing plans for the spring semester are not disrupted. If the council terminates, the foundation owes an ethically managed wind-down, not merely a fiscal one: the 313 students referred but never connected to care represent identified, documented, unmet clinical needs, and the foundation should fund a closeout period of no less than one semester, at roughly $180,000, in which the screening coordinators work exclusively on connecting those students to services before positions end. The foundation would also owe the three districts direct, senior-level communication — these are institutional partners who rearranged school operations on the strength of the foundation's commitment — and transfer of the screening protocols, training materials, and the hard-won data-sharing agreement to the districts so the infrastructure survives the funding. What the foundation must not do, under any scenario, is allow screening to continue past the point where follow-up is funded. Identifying a child's need and then closing the door is the one outcome this council should rule out explicitly, whichever way it votes.

## Executive Summary 4 of 4

### Timucua Housing Partners — Affordable Housing Predevelopment Fund

**Grant:** $4.0 million committed over three years, Opportunity Portfolio, January 2023 – December 2025; actual disbursements to date, $6.5 million
**Date of summary:** October 17, 2025

**Finding and recommendation.** The housing predevelopment fund administered by Timucua Housing Partners is the portfolio's most serious problem, and I will not soften that conclusion. Against a committed grant of $4.0 million, the foundation has disbursed $6.5 million — a $2.5 million overage absorbed from the portfolio's contingency reserve through a series of supplemental approvals whose documentation and authorization my office finds inadequate. Against a goal of advancing three affordable housing sites to construction readiness, one site is permitted, one is stalled in rezoning with an uncertain path, and one has not advanced past preliminary site work. An anchor donor has placed a $3 million pledge to the foundation on hold pending the outcome of this review, which means the fund's problems are now the foundation's problems in the most direct sense. I recommend that the council decline renewal, suspend further disbursements immediately, and direct an independent financial and governance review before any future housing investment is considered. Because the fund was championed internally by Chief Program Officer Omar Zaidi, who also approved the supplemental disbursements, I further recommend that the independent review report to the Board Treasurer rather than through program staff. This recommendation is about process integrity as much as program performance, and the council should treat it as both.

**What the money bought.** The fund was designed to solve a real and well-diagnosed problem: affordable housing developments in Jacksonville routinely die in predevelopment because nonprofit developers cannot finance the site acquisition, environmental assessment, design, engineering, and permitting costs that precede construction financing. The foundation's capital was meant to carry three sites — projected at a combined 240 affordable units — through predevelopment to the point where tax-credit and conventional financing could take over, with predevelopment costs recovered at each project's construction closing and recycled into future sites. The $6.5 million disbursed to date has paid for site acquisition and holding costs on all three parcels, full architectural and engineering work on two sites, environmental remediation at one site that proved substantially more contaminated than the Phase I assessment indicated, legal and entitlement costs across all three, and Timucua's administrative fee. The concrete results: one site, a planned 88-unit development on the Eastside, is fully permitted and has a plausible path to a 2026 construction closing; the second site remains in a contested rezoning with organized neighborhood opposition and no assured timeline; the third has consumed acquisition and carrying costs but little else. No predevelopment capital has yet been recovered, because no project has reached construction closing.

**Evidence and analysis.** Three distinct problems are entangled here, and the council should evaluate each on its own terms.

The first is cost performance. Predevelopment overruns are common in this field, and the environmental remediation surprise — roughly $1.1 million of the overage — is the kind of risk predevelopment funds exist to absorb. Had the overage been $1.1 million, transparently documented and formally approved, this summary would read very differently. It is not. The remaining $1.4 million of excess disbursement is spread across carrying costs extended by schedule slippage, redesign expenses on the rezoning-stalled site, and administrative fees that were charged against the enlarged disbursement base rather than the original commitment, a fee structure my office does not believe the original grant agreement authorizes.

The second problem is authorization. The supplemental disbursements were approved in four increments between August 2023 and February 2025. My office's file review found that two of the four increments, totaling $1.35 million, were approved by the Chief Program Officer under a delegation of authority capped at $250,000 per action, without the board-committee ratification the foundation's disbursement policy requires above that threshold. I make no finding of bad faith, and Mr. Zaidi has cooperated fully with this review; his stated rationale — that halting disbursements mid-predevelopment would have forfeited sunk costs on live projects — is a genuine dilemma familiar to anyone who has managed real estate. But a genuine dilemma is precisely what escalation procedures exist for. The pattern of a program's internal champion approving its overages outside policy is a governance failure regardless of motive, and it is the specific fact that, once known to the anchor donor, placed the $3 million pledge on hold. The foundation's credibility with that donor, and with the council itself, depends on how forthrightly this finding is handled.

The third problem is the fund's underlying model, which deserves a fairer hearing than the process failures will allow it in the short term. The permitted Eastside site is a legitimate achievement — 88 affordable units with a realistic 2026 closing that would return roughly $1.9 million of predevelopment capital to the foundation. The predevelopment gap the fund targets is real and remains unaddressed in this market. The lesson of this grant is not that the strategy is wrong; it is that a foundation of our size undertook real-estate-scale financial exposure with grant-scale controls.

**Recommendation to the council.** Four actions: decline renewal of the fund; suspend all further disbursements immediately, excepting only costs necessary to preserve the value of the permitted Eastside site through its construction closing, each such cost to require Treasurer approval; commission an independent financial and governance review, reporting to Treasurer Etheridge and the board's audit committee, covering all disbursements, the fee structure, and the delegation-of-authority breaches, with findings shared candidly with the anchor donor; and defer any successor housing strategy until that review concludes. I estimate the Eastside preservation costs at no more than $200,000 and regard them as clearly worthwhile: they protect an 88-unit project and the recovery of $1.9 million.

**What the foundation owes the grantee and its participants if funding changes.** The foundation's obligations here run in several directions, and none is discharged by simply closing the grant. To Timucua Housing Partners, the foundation owes due process — the independent review must give the organization full opportunity to respond before findings are finalized — and a negotiated, orderly disposition of the two stalled sites, which may mean sale, transfer to another developer, or a structured hold, but must not mean abandonment that strands the parcels and destroys their affordable-housing potential. To the future residents of the Eastside project — the fund's only true participants in prospect, 88 households who do not yet know this project exists — the foundation owes completion: having carried the site to permits, walking away now would be the one outcome that converts a troubled grant into a total loss for the community. To the foundation's own donors, and to the anchor donor specifically, the foundation owes the unvarnished findings of the independent review, delivered by the President and Treasurer personally. And to its own staff, the foundation owes a fair process for Mr. Zaidi that neither prejudges misconduct nor minimizes the policy breaches, together with revised delegation and escalation controls so that no future program depends on its advocate's self-restraint. The council should decline this renewal without hesitation — and should be equally unhesitating in meeting the obligations the foundation's own decisions created.

## Portfolio Synthesis and Recommended Council Actions

**Prepared by Hana Kobayashi, Director of Evaluation**
**Date:** October 17, 2025

**Purpose of this section.** The four summaries above stand on their own, and the council will vote on each grant separately. But the Opportunity Portfolio was conceived as a single $12 million commitment, and the council deserves an account of what the portfolio taught the foundation as a whole — including the financial position it leaves behind, the pattern of errors that recur across the four grants, and the sequence of decisions the November 6 meeting must produce.

**The portfolio's financial position.** The council committed $12 million over three years. Original commitments totaled $9.5 million across the four grants — $1.8 million to Northside Trade Collective, $2.2 million to River City Legal Aid, $1.5 million to Compass Youth Collaborative, and $4.0 million to Timucua Housing Partners — with the remaining $2.5 million held as a portfolio contingency reserve. That reserve is gone. It was consumed in its entirety by the Timucua supplemental disbursements described in the fourth summary, which is why the housing fund's $6.5 million in actual draws could occur without the portfolio breaching its overall $12 million ceiling. The council should understand the consequence plainly: the contingency that might have cushioned the eviction program's staffing crisis or funded the mental health initiative's referral fixes was silently absorbed by the one grant whose overages lacked proper authorization. Total portfolio disbursements through September 30, 2025 stand at $11.8 million. If the Eastside construction closing proceeds in 2026 as projected, approximately $1.9 million in predevelopment capital returns to the foundation and should, in my view, be treated by the council as restricted to the successor commitments recommended in these summaries rather than absorbed into general grantmaking.

**The cost of the recommendations, taken together.** If the council adopts all four recommendations, the new commitments are: $2.4 million over three years to Northside Trade Collective; $900,000 for one year to River City Legal Aid; $650,000 over eighteen months to Compass Youth Collaborative; and up to $200,000 in Treasurer-approved preservation costs for the Eastside site, plus the cost of the independent review, which I estimate at $75,000 to $100,000. That totals approximately $4.25 million in new money, of which roughly $1.9 million is recoverable from the Eastside closing. The net new cost to the foundation of following every recommendation in this evaluation is therefore on the order of $2.3 to $2.4 million — a defensible figure for a foundation granting $19 million annually, and one that funds the portfolio's proven performer at scale, resolves its ethical obligations in full, and buys the outcome data needed to make the 2027 decisions well.

**What the four grants have in common.** Three patterns recur across this portfolio, and each implicates the foundation's own practice as much as any grantee's.

First, every underperforming grant underbudgeted a known risk that foundation staff failed to challenge at approval. River City budgeted below-market attorney salaries; Compass budgeted three months for a data agreement that predictably took twenty-six; Timucua's contingency assumptions did not survive contact with a contaminated site. In each case the risk was identifiable from sector experience at the time of the grant, was flagged in at least one internal document, and was accepted without adjustment. The foundation's proposal review process tests grantees' aspirations far more rigorously than it tests their assumptions. That is backwards, and I will bring the council a revised due-diligence protocol in the first quarter of 2026.

Second, early warnings went unanswered. River City flagged its retention risk in its first annual report. Compass reported the data-agreement delay in mid-2023. Timucua's first supplemental request, in August 2023, was itself a warning. In no case did foundation staff convene a corrective conversation until the problem had matured into a crisis. Annual reports that no one acts on are a filing system, not an accountability system. The successor grants recommended above all carry quarterly milestone reviews with a named foundation officer accountable for response, and I ask the council to insist on that structure in each grant agreement it approves.

Third, exit obligations were never designed in. None of the four original grant agreements addressed what the foundation and grantee would owe participants if funding ended — which is why this evaluation has had to construct those obligations after the fact, case by case, for 340 open legal matters, 313 unconnected students, and 88 unbuilt units. Every future multi-year grant of this foundation should include a wind-down clause specifying participant protections, notice periods, and closeout funding formulas at signing, when the parties can negotiate them calmly, rather than at termination, when they cannot.

**Recommended sequence of motions for November 6.** To keep the meeting orderly, I recommend the council take up the grants in the following order, for the stated reasons:

1. **Timucua Housing Partners first** — decline renewal, suspend disbursements, authorize the independent review and the Eastside preservation envelope. Taking the hardest decision first establishes the review's independence before any other vote, and gives the President and Treasurer a settled position to carry to the anchor donor immediately.
2. **Northside Trade Collective second** — approve the $2.4 million renewal with the two stated conditions. The portfolio's clearest success deserves an unclouded, affirmative vote, and approval by November 14 protects the January cohort.
3. **River City Legal Aid third** — approve the $900,000 restructuring grant, and separately and explicitly, by its own motion, the backlog-resolution obligation, so that the closeout commitment survives even if a future council revisits the program grant.
4. **Compass Youth Collaborative fourth** — approve the $650,000 extension with the January 2027 outcome report and the 55 percent conversion target written into the agreement, together with the explicit prohibition on screening beyond funded follow-up capacity.

**Communication after the vote.** Whatever the council decides, four communications must follow within one week of the meeting: written decisions to all four grantees with reasons stated; a personal briefing of the anchor donor by the President and Treasurer covering the Timucua findings and the review's terms of reference; direct notice to the three school districts and to the Duval County court administration, whose operations depend on two of these programs; and a summary of decisions to the full board. My office will prepare draft communications for each scenario in advance of November 6 so that no decision waits on drafting.

**A closing note from the evaluator.** It is tempting to read this portfolio as one success, two struggles, and one failure, and to draw from that a lesson of caution. I would urge the council toward a different reading. The Opportunity Portfolio took four genuine risks on four hard problems. One paid off outright. Two produced real value that disciplined follow-through can still convert into results. One failed in ways that were preventable — but preventable by the foundation, through controls the council has the power to fix before a dollar of successor funding moves. The measure of a foundation is not whether its risks all succeed; it is whether it tells the truth about the ones that don't, honors its obligations to the people its money reached, and lets the evidence, rather than its attachments, decide what it funds next. These summaries are offered in that spirit, and I am available to the council, individually or in session, before the November 6 vote.

*— Hana Kobayashi, Director of Evaluation, First Coast Community Trust*
