# Sunflower Civic Code, Inc.
## Application to the Ardith Lammers Foundation Public Interest Technology Fund

**Applicant:** Sunflower Civic Code, Inc., Lawrence, Kansas
**Amount Requested:** $3,100,000 over three years
**Submitted by:** Priscilla Ojeda, Executive Director
**Date:** July 30, 2026

---

## Statement of Need

Sunflower Civic Code builds the software that stands between low-income households in Kansas, Missouri, Nebraska, and Oklahoma and the benefits and court systems that determine whether they keep housing, food, health coverage, and a driver's license. Last year our tools ran 218,000 benefits screenings for people whose median household income was $27,400 — well below the federal poverty line for a family of three in every state we serve. Sixty-one percent of those screenings happened on a mobile phone, often the only computing device a user owns, which is why our products have always been built mobile-first rather than adapted to mobile as an afterthought. Forty-six county agencies and seven legal aid organizations across four states now rely on our screening and court-reminder infrastructure as part of their daily casework.

The need this proposal addresses has two parts, and both are urgent.

The first is churn. Since the end of the federal continuous-coverage requirement, Medicaid renewal and SNAP recertification have become the two moments where eligible households most often fall out of benefits they still qualify for — not because their circumstances changed, but because a renewal notice was missed, a form was filled out incorrectly, or a deadline passed while someone was working two jobs. County caseworkers in our network report renewal-related case closures as their single largest driver of avoidable benefit loss. Our current screening tool was built for initial eligibility determination, not for the shorter, more time-sensitive, and more document-heavy renewal and recertification workflows. It does the wrong job for the problem our partner counties now face most often.

The second is language access and reach. Thirty additional county agencies across our four-state footprint have asked to join our network in the past eighteen months — a waiting list larger than our current footprint — and a meaningful share of them serve communities where English is not the primary language spoken at home, including Spanish-speaking populations across southwest Kansas and the Kansas City metro, and Vietnamese-speaking populations tied to meatpacking and food-processing employment corridors in Kansas and Nebraska. Our tools are currently English-only. For a household filling out a benefits renewal or trying to understand a court notice, a screening tool in a language they do not read is not meaningfully more useful than no tool at all.

The consequences of not meeting this need are measurable, because we have already measured a version of them. Across 14,000 hearings in three pilot counties, our text-based court-date reminder tool cut failure-to-appear rates from 22 percent to 9 percent — a reduction that translates directly into fewer bench warrants, fewer suspended licenses, and fewer people losing jobs over a missed court date they would have kept had they simply been reminded. That result exists in three counties. It does not yet exist in the other 43 counties in our network, and it does not exist at all in the 30 counties asking to join. The infrastructure that produced a 13-point drop in failure-to-appear is proven; it is not yet distributed to the places that need it.

This proposal asks the Foundation to fund the next phase of that distribution: a rebuilt screening engine tuned to renewal and recertification, language access in Spanish and Vietnamese, expansion to 30 additional counties, and a revenue model that lets the organization sustain this infrastructure without depending indefinitely on grant funding.

## Project Description

This is a three-year project with four components: a technical rebuild, a language expansion, a geographic expansion, and the launch of an earned-revenue support tier. We describe each in turn, followed by the partnership structure and a candid account of one setback in our expansion plan.

**Technical rebuild.** The current screening engine was architected around one-time eligibility determination: a user answers a set of questions once, and the tool returns a list of programs they likely qualify for. Medicaid renewal and SNAP recertification require a different logic: shorter question sets tied to what has changed since the last determination, document-upload handling for pay stubs and utility bills, deadline-driven notification logic tied to each household's actual renewal date rather than a generic reminder cadence, and status tracking so a caseworker can see where in the renewal process a household stands. Interim CTO Zsofia Balogh has led the technical design for this rebuild since January, working from a specification informed by structured interviews with caseworkers in twelve of our current partner counties. The rebuild will be modular, so that the renewal/recertification logic can sit alongside the existing initial-eligibility screener rather than replacing it, and so that future program modules — unemployment insurance recertification is the most frequently requested addition — can be added without another ground-up rebuild.

**Language access.** Spanish and Vietnamese will be built into the platform at the string and workflow level, not bolted on as a translation overlay. This distinction matters: a translated string that assumes a household reads left-to-right English legal terminology is not the same as a workflow designed with a Spanish- or Vietnamese-speaking user in mind from the first screen. We will contract bilingual eligibility specialists, not generalist translators, to write and test the Spanish and Vietnamese versions, and we will use our legal aid partner's client base for direct usability testing in both languages before either version goes live in a production county.

**Geographic expansion.** We will onboard 30 additional counties over the grant period — a pace of roughly ten per year — bringing our total footprint to 76 counties across four states. Onboarding a county involves a data-sharing agreement, integration of that county's specific benefit-program rules into the screener's logic, and training for caseworkers who will use the tool alongside applicants.

**Paid support tier.** We are launching a tier of paid technical support and onboarding services for county agencies and legal aid organizations, described fully in the Sustainability section below.

**Partners.** Three organizations are structurally embedded in this project, not advisory in name only. A regional legal aid organization will run direct usability testing with clients in both new languages before each goes into production, and will flag workflow failures from a client's-eye view that internal staff testing does not catch. A state judicial office is supplying the hearing-schedule data that makes the court-reminder tool possible in new counties; without a live feed of hearing dates, times, and locations, the reminder tool cannot function, and this partnership is the mechanism by which that data reaches us on a jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction basis. A university policy center, led by Dr. Ramona Chee, will conduct the independent evaluation described in the Evaluation Plan section, including the quasi-experimental analysis of failure-to-appear outcomes across pilot and comparison counties.

**A setback, addressed directly.** In February, the Nebraska Department of Health and Human Services withdrew from a pilot integration we had been developing with them, following an internal reorganization that shifted the department's technology priorities. This was a state-level agency relationship, not a county-level one, and it affected a planned data-sharing pathway for Medicaid eligibility verification in Nebraska specifically. It did not affect our county-level partnerships in Nebraska, none of which depend on that state agency integration, and it did not affect our screening or court-reminder operations in any other state. We have redesigned the renewal-verification workflow so that it does not require a state-level data feed to function — households can upload their own renewal notice, and caseworkers can manually confirm status — which means the Nebraska withdrawal changes our technical approach in that state but does not block Nebraska county onboarding under this grant. We are maintaining the relationship with the department and would welcome a future integration, but we are not building this project's success around it.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

**Goal 1: Expand the geographic reach of the screening and reminder tools.**
- Onboard 10 new counties in Year 1, 10 in Year 2, and 10 in Year 3, for a cumulative total of 76 partner counties by the end of the grant period.
- Maintain 99.5 percent platform uptime across the expanded network, measured monthly.

**Goal 2: Rebuild the screener for renewal and recertification workflows.**
- Launch the Medicaid renewal module in three pilot counties by month 6 and the SNAP recertification module by month 9.
- Achieve a 70 percent application-submission rate among users who complete a renewal or recertification screening, and a 55 percent approval rate among submitted applications, tracked from screening completion through agency-reported outcome.
- Roll the renewal and recertification modules out to all onboarded counties by the end of Year 2.

**Goal 3: Deliver language access in Spanish and Vietnamese.**
- Launch Spanish-language screening and reminders in three counties by month 9 and expand to all onboarded counties by month 18.
- Launch Vietnamese-language screening and reminders in two counties with significant Vietnamese-speaking populations by month 15, expanding to all relevant counties by month 24.
- Reach 15 percent of total screenings conducted in a non-English language by the end of Year 3.

**Goal 4: Reduce failure-to-appear rates through expanded reminder coverage.**
- Extend the court-reminder tool from 3 to at least 20 counties by the end of Year 2, contingent on judicial-office data-sharing agreements reaching those counties.
- Reduce average failure-to-appear rate across all counties using the reminder tool to below 12 percent by the end of Year 3, down from a network average currently estimated near 20 percent in counties without the tool.

**Goal 5: Build a diversified, sustainable revenue base.**
- Grow earned revenue from $190,000 currently to $1.1 million by the end of Year 3 through the paid support tier described below.
- Reduce cost per screening from its current level through platform efficiencies and scale, tracked quarterly and reported to the Foundation semi-annually.

These are the same categories — counties onboarded, screenings completed, applications submitted and approved, uptime, failure-to-appear rate, and cost per screening — that the Foundation requires in reporting, and we have built our internal dashboards around exactly these definitions so that what we report to the Foundation is what we track operationally, with no translation step in between.

## Evaluation Plan

Our evaluation approach has two layers: continuous internal monitoring, and an independent external evaluation conducted by a partner with no financial stake in the results.

**Internal monitoring.** Every screening, application submission, and reminder sent is logged in our platform database with a timestamp, county, language, and outcome status where available. Uptime is measured through an automated monitoring service that pings the platform every five minutes and logs any interruption longer than two minutes. Cost per screening is calculated quarterly as total program expense divided by total screenings completed in the quarter, a formula that will not change during the grant period, and any change to the formula in future years will be disclosed to the Foundation in advance rather than discovered in a report. Application-approval data is the hardest metric to track cleanly, because it requires a follow-up loop with county agencies weeks or months after a screening; we are building a structured follow-up protocol — a standardized data-sharing request sent to each partner agency on a 60-day cycle — specifically because our prior approach to this metric relied on informal, inconsistent check-ins with agency staff, which contributed to the data problems described in the Organizational Capacity section below.

**External evaluation.** Dr. Ramona Chee's policy center will conduct an independent evaluation of the failure-to-appear results as the reminder tool expands, using a quasi-experimental design that compares counties adopting the tool during the grant period against demographically similar counties in our network that have not yet adopted it, controlling for hearing type and prior appearance history where available from the judicial office data feed. This design lets us attribute outcome changes to the tool with more confidence than a simple before-and-after comparison within a single county, and it produces a published methodology that the Foundation and other funders can review independently of our own reporting. The policy center will also conduct an annual review of application-submission and approval data drawn from our internal dashboards, checking our internal figures against a sample of agency-reported records before those figures go into any external report.

**Reporting cadence.** We will submit reports to the Foundation on a semi-annual basis, each covering the metrics listed in Goals above, with the underlying data reviewed by Finance Director Hannah Steinmetz and the interim CTO before submission, and with any restatement of a previously reported figure flagged explicitly and explained in writing rather than presented as a routine update. Internally, the same metrics are reviewed monthly by staff and quarterly by the Board's newly formed Audit and Data Committee, described further below. We have deliberately built more frequent internal review than the Foundation requires, so that a reporting deadline is never the first moment our own team looks closely at the numbers.

## Organizational Capacity

Sunflower Civic Code operates with 24 staff and a $4.3 million annual budget, serving 46 county agencies and seven legal aid organizations across four states. Our track record — 218,000 screenings, a documented 13-point reduction in failure-to-appear rates, and a waiting list of 30 counties asking to join our network — reflects an organization that builds infrastructure people actually use and that measurably changes outcomes. We also want to be direct about two areas of recent strain, because a candid account of them, and of what we have changed as a result, is more useful to the Foundation than a proposal that omits them.

**Our history with this Foundation.** The Foundation declined to renew a prior $900,000 grant to our organization in 2024, after we missed two reporting deadlines and subsequently restated our outcome numbers. Both problems traced to the same root cause: our data pipeline at the time depended on a single staff member manually reconciling screening data from four separate county data formats before each report, with no independent check on that reconciliation. When that staff member was out for an extended medical leave during the second reporting period, the deadline passed before anyone else understood the reconciliation process well enough to complete it, and the figures eventually submitted contained an error in how we had counted screenings that crossed a fiscal-quarter boundary — an error that required a restatement once we caught it internally, weeks after the original report went out.

We have made three structural changes since then. First, screening and outcome data now flows through an automated pipeline built by our engineering team rather than a manual reconciliation process, which removes the single-point-of-failure that caused the 2024 problem. Second, we created a Board Audit and Data Committee, which reviews every dataset before it goes into an external report and which did not exist in 2024. Third, our reporting calendar is now set a full quarter in advance, with internal review deadlines built in ahead of each external deadline, rather than the external deadline being the only date on the calendar. We do not ask the Foundation to forget what happened in 2024. We ask the Foundation to evaluate this proposal against what we have changed since, and we would welcome a reporting structure in any resulting grant agreement that gives the Foundation direct visibility into our internal review process, not just the finished report.

**Leadership transition.** Our Chief Technology Officer left the organization in November after five years leading platform development, including the original build of the court-reminder tool. Zsofia Balogh, previously our senior platform engineer, has served as interim CTO since that departure and has led the technical design work for the renewal and recertification rebuild described in this proposal. She has been with the organization for four years and was the lead engineer on the reminder-tool deployment that produced the 22-to-9-percent failure-to-appear result, which means the person currently directing our most technically demanding project is the same person who built the system whose results anchor this proposal. We have retained a technical search firm and are conducting a national search for a permanent CTO, with a target hire date in the first half of Year 1 of this grant; Ms. Balogh has indicated she will apply for the permanent role. Regardless of the search outcome, we have documented our platform architecture and deployment processes more thoroughly in the past eight months than in the prior four years combined, specifically so that technical continuity does not depend on any single person's institutional memory — a lesson we take directly from how disruptive the CTO departure was in its first weeks.

**Governance and financial oversight.** Finance Director Hannah Steinmetz, with the organization for six years, oversees the budget underlying this proposal and the earned-revenue plan described below. Our Board includes members with backgrounds in state government technology, nonprofit finance, and civil legal aid, and the newly formed Audit and Data Committee reports to the full Board quarterly.

## Sustainability

The Foundation's award covers three years. Beyond that, this project needs to fund itself, and the mechanism for that is the paid support tier, which Finance Director Hannah Steinmetz has built into a three-year revenue plan taking earned revenue from $190,000 today to $1.1 million by the end of Year 3.

**What we are selling.** County agencies and legal aid organizations that use our platform today do so at no direct cost, funded by grants. The paid tier introduces three revenue lines layered on top of that free core service, targeted at agencies with the budget and the need for a higher level of support: a per-county annual licensing fee for agencies that want dedicated data-integration support connecting our platform to their internal case-management systems; a premium support subscription with guaranteed response times and a named account contact, aimed at larger county agencies managing higher screening volumes; and fee-for-service onboarding and training packages for organizations outside our core four-state network that want to adopt our open-source platform independently, which several out-of-region agencies have already inquired about after seeing our failure-to-appear results presented at conferences.

**The revenue path.** In Year 1, we project $450,000 in earned revenue, driven primarily by converting a subset of our current 46 partner counties — those with the largest screening volumes and the clearest integration needs — into paying licensing customers, alongside the first premium-support subscriptions. In Year 2, as the 30-county expansion adds new agencies who join the platform already aware of the paid tier as an option rather than as a later add-on, we project $780,000, with the fee-for-service onboarding line contributing meaningfully for the first time as out-of-region interest converts into contracts. In Year 3, we project the full $1.1 million, with all three revenue lines operating at scale and licensing renewals from earlier-adopting counties providing a recurring base rather than requiring new sales each year. Ms. Steinmetz has built these projections from actual inbound interest — eleven current partner counties have already expressed interest in the premium support tier in preliminary conversations, and four out-of-region agencies have inquired about fee-for-service onboarding — rather than from an unvalidated growth assumption.

**Why this model survives a setback like Nebraska.** The paid tier is built entirely around county-level and organization-level relationships, the same level at which our core platform partnerships already operate, and does not depend on any state agency's participation. The Nebraska DHHS withdrawal affected a state-level data integration, not a county relationship or a revenue relationship; no paying customer's service depends on that integration existing. This is a deliberate design choice: state-level data-sharing agreements are valuable when we can get them, but building the revenue model's foundation on county-level relationships means a single state agency's reorganization cannot threaten the organization's financial sustainability the way it could threaten a specific technical feature.

**Cost efficiency.** As we scale to 76 counties, our cost per screening declines because platform infrastructure, core engineering, and language-localization work are largely fixed costs spread across a growing user base, while marginal onboarding costs per county are lower than the platform's original build cost. We report this metric to the Foundation each period specifically so that our efficiency claims are checked against actual data rather than asserted.

**Beyond Year 3.** Our long-term goal is an earned-revenue base sufficient to cover core platform operations — hosting, security, and baseline engineering maintenance — independent of any single grant, with philanthropic and government funding directed toward expansion, new-language development, and new-program modules rather than keeping the lights on. We are not asking this grant to make us fully self-sustaining in three years; we are asking it to fund the specific technical and geographic expansion that gets the earned-revenue engine large enough to carry the organization's core costs on its own.

## Technical Appendix

**Current architecture.** The production screening platform runs as a modular web application, accessible through both a mobile-optimized browser interface and a lightweight SMS-based intake path for users without reliable data access, which together account for the 61 percent mobile share of last year's 218,000 screenings. The eligibility logic engine is separated from the presentation layer so that new program modules — such as the Medicaid renewal and SNAP recertification workflows proposed here — can be added without modifying the core intake and user-management systems. County-specific program rules are stored as configuration data rather than hard-coded logic, which is what currently allows a single platform to serve 46 counties across four different state benefit systems without 46 separate codebases.

**Renewal and recertification module design.** The new module diverges from the existing initial-eligibility screener in three technical respects. First, it is date-driven: rather than a stateless one-time questionnaire, each household record carries a renewal or recertification due date, and the system generates outreach and reminder logic keyed to that date rather than to a generic cadence. Second, it supports document upload — pay stubs, utility bills, and proof-of-residency documents — stored with encryption at rest and access-logged for compliance purposes, since renewal determinations more often turn on verifying a specific change in circumstance than on the broader intake questions used in initial screening. Third, it introduces a status-tracking object visible to caseworkers, showing where a household stands between screening completion, document submission, application submission, and agency determination — the same object that produces the application-submission and approval data reported under Goal 2.

**Language architecture.** Spanish and Vietnamese are implemented at the workflow level using a localization framework that separates all user-facing text, including conditional and program-specific language, from the underlying logic engine. This allows a bilingual eligibility specialist to review and edit workflow language directly without engineering involvement for routine updates, and it allows the same underlying eligibility logic to serve all three languages without duplicated codepaths that could drift out of sync with one another over time — a risk we specifically avoided given the maintenance burden a translation-overlay approach would have created as program rules change.

**Data pipeline and reporting infrastructure.** Screening, application, and reminder data now flows through an automated ETL pipeline built by our engineering team, replacing the manual reconciliation process responsible for the 2024 reporting failures. County-level data arrives in four different formats depending on each partner agency's own case-management system; the pipeline normalizes these formats on ingestion rather than at report-generation time, with validation checks that flag anomalies — such as a screening count that crosses a fiscal-quarter boundary, the specific error category that produced the 2024 restatement — before they reach any downstream report. The pipeline writes to a central data warehouse that powers both our internal operational dashboards and the datasets provided to Dr. Chee's policy center for independent evaluation, so that internal and external reporting draw from the same validated source rather than parallel extraction processes.

**Judicial data integration.** The court-reminder tool depends on a live hearing-schedule feed supplied by our state judicial office partner. This feed arrives as a structured data file updated on a rolling basis and is matched against household records using a probabilistic matching process that accounts for name variations, given the absence of a shared unique identifier between judicial and benefits systems. Expanding the reminder tool to new counties requires a jurisdiction-specific data-sharing agreement with the judicial office before that county's hearing data can be incorporated; this is the primary rate-limiting factor on how quickly the tool can expand under Goal 4, independent of engineering capacity.

**Security and compliance.** All data in transit is encrypted using industry-standard protocols, and the platform undergoes an annual third-party security review. Given the sensitive nature of benefits and court-appearance data, access controls are role-based, with caseworker access scoped to their own county's records and audit logging on all document and status views. This infrastructure was in place prior to this grant and will be extended, not rebuilt, to accommodate the renewal/recertification module and the 30-county expansion.

**Uptime monitoring.** Platform availability is tracked through an automated monitoring service pinging core application endpoints at five-minute intervals, with any interruption exceeding two minutes logged and attributed to a cause — scheduled maintenance, third-party outage, or platform failure — in a monthly uptime report reviewed internally before being rolled into the semi-annual Foundation report described in the Evaluation Plan.

**Interim technical leadership.** Ms. Balogh's design authority over this rebuild extends from her role as lead engineer on the original court-reminder deployment, giving her direct familiarity with the judicial-data matching logic described above and with the operational constraints that shaped the 22-to-9-percent failure-to-appear result this proposal seeks to replicate at scale. Architecture decisions for the renewal/recertification module have been documented in a technical design record maintained outside any single engineer's personal notes, consistent with the continuity practices described in Organizational Capacity, so that the permanent CTO hire — whether Ms. Balogh or an external candidate — inherits a documented system rather than an oral history.
