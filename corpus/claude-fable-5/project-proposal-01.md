## 1. Executive Summary

The City of Ridgemark operates under Cease and Desist Order No. R8-2025-0047, issued by the Santa Ana Regional Water Quality Control Board in December 2025 following four sanitary sewer overflows in eighteen months. Every one of those overflows was traced by the City and confirmed by the Regional Board's investigation to the same point of failure: the 1971 headworks, influent pump station, and primary clarifiers at the Bellota Road Water Reclamation Facility. The order requires the City to commence construction of a permanent remedy no later than July 1, 2027. If construction has not begun by that date, the order imposes penalties of $10,000 per day.

This proposal asks the Council to authorize the Bellota Road Headworks, Influent Pump Station, and Primary Clarifier Replacement Project: a full replacement of the plant's failed 55-year-old front end, at a total project cost of **$41.6 million**, delivered over **34 months** using a construction manager at risk (CMAR) and a four-person City owner's team. The plant will remain in continuous service throughout construction, operating on a managed bypass through two wet seasons under the direction of Wastewater Superintendent Gerardo Quintanilla.

The funding plan assembled by Finance Director Marta Kovalenko consists of **$33.0 million in State Revolving Fund (SRF) financing**, **$4.5 million from the sewer capital reserve**, and **$4.1 million in revenue from a sewer rate increase of 9 percent per year for four years**, subject to a Proposition 218 majority-protest process scheduled for October 2026. For the median Ridgemark household, the rate increase amounts to $4.34 per month in the first year and $19.84 per month by the fourth year.

Staff examined and rejected two principal alternatives — rehabilitation of the existing structures in place, and a regional connection to the Oro Vista Sanitation District — for reasons of cost, schedule, and regulatory acceptability, detailed in Section 8.

This report is written with full awareness that the Council's confidence in public works cost estimates was damaged by the 2021 Civic Center parking structure, authorized at $9.2 million and finished at $14.8 million. Section 7 describes, specifically and in detail, how this project's budget was built differently, how it will be controlled, and what authority the Council retains over every dollar of contingency.

**The action requested on March 10 is set out in full in Section 10.** In summary, staff asks the Council to approve the project and its budget, adopt the CEQA determination, authorize execution of the SRF financing agreement, authorize the CMAR procurement, appropriate design-phase funds, and direct initiation of the Proposition 218 rate proceeding.

## 2. Background: The Condition of the Bellota Road Plant

### 2.1 The facility

The Bellota Road Water Reclamation Facility treats all wastewater generated within Ridgemark's city limits — a service population of 68,400 — under NPDES Permit No. CA0105512. The plant has a permitted capacity of 8.2 million gallons per day (MGD), an average dry-weather flow of 6.1 MGD, and recorded peak wet-weather flows exceeding 14 MGD during the storms of early 2024 and late 2025.

The plant has been expanded and upgraded in stages since it opened. The secondary treatment process, disinfection system, and solids handling facilities were modernized between 1998 and 2013 and remain in serviceable condition. The problem is the front of the plant. The headworks, the influent pump station, and both primary clarifiers were built in 1971 and have never been replaced. They are the oldest operating structures in the City's utility system, and every drop of wastewater the City collects must pass through them before it can reach the newer processes downstream.

### 2.2 What has failed

**Headworks.** The headworks contains two mechanically cleaned bar screens and an aerated grit chamber. One screen has been out of service since March 2024; replacement drive components for a 1971-vintage mechanism are no longer manufactured, and the fabricated substitutes installed by plant staff have failed twice. When the single remaining screen blinds during high flow, unscreened debris passes to the influent pumps. The grit chamber's aeration piping is corroded through in multiple locations, and grit removal efficiency measured in 2025 was less than 40 percent of design, sending abrasive material into every downstream pump and pipe.

**Influent pump station.** The station houses four pumps. Two are the original 1971 units. The station's wet well has lost structural concrete cover over reinforcing steel across roughly 30 percent of its interior surface due to hydrogen sulfide attack; the City's 2024 condition assessment (Carollo Engineers, September 2024) rated the wet well at Condition 5 — "failure imminent or existing" — on the standard five-point scale. The electrical switchgear predates 1985, contains obsolete components, and cannot be integrated with the plant's SCADA system, meaning the station has no remote monitoring or automated failover.

**Primary clarifiers.** The two 85-foot-diameter primary clarifiers show exposed and section-loss rebar in the launders, walls, and center columns. The sludge collection mechanism in Clarifier No. 2 failed in November 2025 and was returned to service only through an emergency repair that the manufacturer's field representative described in writing as "not expected to survive a further mechanism jam." When either clarifier is out of service, the plant loses primary treatment redundancy entirely.

### 2.3 The overflows

Four sanitary sewer overflows occurred between June 2024 and November 2025, releasing a combined estimated 412,000 gallons of untreated or partially treated wastewater:

| Date | Estimated Volume | Cause | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| June 14, 2024 | 38,000 gal | Influent pump No. 3 failure; No. 1 failed to start | Contained on site |
| December 27, 2024 | 186,000 gal | Screen blinding during storm; wet well surcharge | Reached Bellota Channel |
| February 9, 2025 | 61,000 gal | Grit accumulation seized influent pump No. 2 | Contained on site |
| November 21, 2025 | 127,000 gal | Clarifier No. 2 mechanism failure; hydraulic overload | Reached Bellota Channel |

Two of the four overflows reached the Bellota Channel, a tributary to waters of the United States. Each was reported to the State Water Board's CIWQS system as required. The December 2024 and November 2025 events drew formal Notices of Violation, and the Regional Board issued the Cease and Desist Order on December 16, 2025.

The pattern matters as much as the volumes. These were not operator errors or collection-system blockages. Each event originated in the 1971 facilities, and each occurred despite an escalating program of preventive maintenance, emergency repairs, and staffing changes that has cost the sewer fund approximately $1.9 million since 2023. Superintendent Quintanilla's team has kept the plant in compliance with its effluent limits throughout — the failures have been hydraulic and mechanical, at the front of the plant, before treatment. The City is spending heavily to prop up equipment that can no longer be propped up.

## 3. What the Cease and Desist Order Requires

Order No. R8-2025-0047 requires the City to:

1. **Submit a corrective action plan** identifying the permanent capital remedy for the influent-works failures (submitted January 30, 2026; the project described in this report is the remedy identified in that plan);
2. **Commence construction of the permanent remedy no later than July 1, 2027**, with liquidated penalties of **$10,000 per day** for each day construction has not commenced after that date;
3. **Maintain interim reliability measures** — including rental bypass pumping capacity on standby, weekly condition inspections, and 24-hour Regional Board notification of any further overflow — until the permanent remedy is complete;
4. **Submit quarterly progress reports** to the Regional Board's Executive Officer; and
5. **Achieve full operational status of the permanent remedy** in accordance with the schedule in the approved corrective action plan.

Two points deserve the Council's attention. First, the July 1, 2027 construction-commencement deadline is the binding constraint on everything in this proposal. Working backward from that date, the Council must act in March 2026 to allow time for design, environmental review, SRF financing execution, CMAR procurement, and the Proposition 218 process. There is schedule float in the plan presented here — roughly four months — but a decision deferred past spring 2026 begins consuming that float immediately. Second, the order's penalty provision is not hypothetical. The Regional Board assessed a $278,000 administrative civil liability against the City for the December 2024 overflow, and its staff has stated in correspondence that further overflows or missed milestones will be pursued.

## 4. The Proposed Project

### 4.1 Scope

The project replaces, in their entirety:

- **The headworks:** a new structure with two mechanically cleaned multi-rake screens plus a manual bypass screen, two vortex grit chambers with grit washing and dewatering, odor control, and full SCADA integration; sized for a peak wet-weather flow of 16 MGD to restore hydraulic headroom the plant lost decades ago.
- **The influent pump station:** a new wet well and pump station with four submersible pumps in a firm-capacity configuration (three duty, one standby, any three meeting peak flow), new electrical switchgear, a permanent standby generator, and automated controls with remote monitoring.
- **Both primary clarifiers:** two new 90-foot clarifiers on a new foundation adjacent to the existing units, with modern collection mechanisms, launder covers, and scum handling; the existing clarifiers remain in service until each new unit is commissioned, then are demolished.
- **Associated yard piping, electrical, instrumentation, and site work**, including the temporary bypass pumping and conveyance system that keeps the plant treating flow throughout construction.

The project does not expand the plant's permitted treatment capacity and does not alter the secondary, disinfection, or solids processes.

### 4.2 Phased schedule

The 34-month program runs from Council authorization in March 2026 to final completion in January 2029:

| Phase | Duration | Dates | Key Activities |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phase 0 — Authorization & Procurement** | 4 months | Mar – Jun 2026 | Council approval; CEQA filing; SRF agreement execution; CMAR RFQ/RFP and selection; design kickoff |
| **Phase 1 — Design & Preconstruction** | 9 months | Apr – Dec 2026 | 60/90/100% design; independent cost estimates at each milestone; CMAR constructability review; Prop 218 hearing (October); guaranteed maximum price (GMP) negotiation |
| **Phase 2 — Early Works** | 4 months | Jan – Apr 2027 | GMP Amendment 1: bypass pumping system installation, temporary screening, site utilities, foundations. **Construction commences no later than March 2027 — four months ahead of the order's deadline** |
| **Phase 3 — Influent Pump Station & Headworks** | 12 months | Apr 2027 – Mar 2028 | New pump station and headworks constructed on adjacent footprint while existing units run; cutover of pumping and screening in spring 2028 shoulder season |
| **Phase 4 — Clarifiers** | 9 months | Mar – Nov 2028 | New Clarifier A constructed and commissioned; flow transferred; new Clarifier B constructed and commissioned; old units demolished |
| **Phase 5 — Commissioning & Closeout** | 2 months | Dec 2028 – Jan 2029 | Full-plant performance testing; Regional Board completion certification; punch list and demobilization |

### 4.3 Keeping the plant treating flow

The plant cannot stop. The construction sequence is built around that fact, and it is the single largest driver of the project's cost and duration.

The strategy is **build adjacent, cut over, then demolish**. The new pump station, headworks, and clarifiers are all constructed on open ground within the existing plant site, immediately adjacent to the units they replace. The old facilities continue operating until each new facility passes performance testing. No permanent facility is taken out of service before its replacement is proven.

The exception is the cutover periods themselves and certain tie-ins, which require a **temporary bypass pumping system**: engine-driven pumps and above-grade piping, sized at 16 MGD with 100 percent standby, routing flow around the work zone directly to primary or secondary treatment as each stage requires. The bypass system will be installed in early 2027 and will remain in place, maintained and exercised weekly, through **two wet seasons — winter 2027–28 and winter 2028–29 shoulder**. Superintendent Quintanilla will hold direct operational authority over the bypass at all times; the construction contract gives the Superintendent unilateral authority to stop work and restore normal flow paths on his sole judgment, with weather-triggered protocols requiring the contractor to secure the work zone and stand down tie-in activities whenever the National Weather Service forecasts more than a half-inch of rain in 24 hours. Major cutovers are scheduled only in the April–October dry window.

This is a demanding operating posture and the Council should understand it plainly: for roughly 22 months, the plant runs with less margin than anyone would like, dependent on temporary equipment through two winters. That risk is real, and Section 9 addresses it. But it is materially *less* risk than the status quo, because the temporary equipment is new, redundant, monitored, and maintained — which is more than can be said for the 1971 facilities it supplements.

## 6. Delivery Method and Staffing

### 6.1 Construction manager at risk

Staff recommends CMAR delivery under Government Code authority applicable to the City, rather than traditional design-bid-build, for four reasons specific to this project:

1. **The plant must stay in service.** The sequencing, bypass design, and cutover planning described in Section 4.3 are exactly the problems a CMAR solves during preconstruction, sitting at the table with the designer and Superintendent Quintanilla while the drawings are still changeable. In design-bid-build, the City would discover the low bidder's opinion of the sequencing plan after award, when it is too late.
2. **Price certainty before full commitment.** The CMAR delivers a guaranteed maximum price at the end of design. If the GMP cannot be negotiated within budget, the City retains the completed design and may bid the work competitively. The Council commits to the full construction amount only when it accepts the GMP — a decision that returns to this dais.
3. **Schedule.** Early-works packages let construction commence in the first quarter of 2027, protecting the July 1, 2027 regulatory deadline with margin. Design-bid-build's sequential process would put the deadline at risk.
4. **Open-book cost transparency.** All subcontracted work — more than 80 percent of the GMP — is competitively bid by the CMAR with City participation in bid openings, and the CMAR's fee, general conditions, and contingency are separately stated and auditable.

The CMAR will be selected through a qualifications-based RFQ/RFP process with fee competition, evaluated by a panel including the Public Works Director, the Wastewater Superintendent, the Finance Director, and an outside utility construction executive. Award of the preconstruction agreement (approximately $450,000, within the design-phase appropriation) will return to the Council in summer 2026.

### 6.2 The owner's team

The project will be run by a four-person owner's team reporting to Director Ontiveros:

| Position | Role | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Project Manager** | Overall delivery, budget, schedule, Regional Board reporting; single point of accountability | New hire, limited-term, funded in project budget |
| **Resident Engineer / Construction Manager** | Daily field oversight, quality assurance, pay application review, RFI/submittal management | Contract staff from CM services firm |
| **Operations Liaison** | Cutover planning, bypass operations, shutdown coordination; **reports jointly to Superintendent Quintanilla** | Existing senior plant operator, backfilled |
| **Project Controls Specialist** | Cost tracking, change management, earned-value reporting, contingency ledger | Contract staff, part-time, shared with Finance |

Total owner's-team cost of $1.8 million over the project is carried in the budget. The structure is deliberately lean; the CMAR carries the coordination burden a larger owner staff would otherwise bear. Superintendent Quintanilla and the plant's operating staff are not part of the construction organization — their job through 2028 is to run the plant, and the Operations Liaison position exists so that construction never pulls operators off the treatment process.

## 7. Cost Control and Accountability

Councilmember Shiraishi has stated that he will not vote for another public works number he cannot trust, and he has cited the 2021 Civic Center parking structure — authorized at $9.2 million, finished at $14.8 million. That project's post-completion review, accepted by the Council in 2022, found three root causes: the authorized number was a schematic-level estimate with no independent check; geotechnical conditions were investigated after award rather than before; and change orders were approved administratively without a contingency ledger visible to the Council. This project has been structured so that none of those three failures can recur, and so that the Council can verify that claim rather than take it on faith.

**How this estimate was built.** The $41.6 million figure rests on a completed 30-percent design, not a concept sketch; a geotechnical investigation of the Bellota Road site **completed in December 2025**, before this authorization request; and an independent estimate by a cost consultant with no design or construction role, reconciled line-by-line against the design engineer's estimate. Escalation to year-of-expenditure dollars is carried explicitly, not assumed away.

**How the price will be locked.** Independent estimates will be repeated at 60, 90, and 100 percent design and reconciled with the CMAR's estimates at each milestone; divergence gets resolved on paper before it becomes a claim in the field. The GMP is negotiated open-book, with every subcontract competitively bid, and **the GMP comes back to this Council for acceptance** in early 2027. If it exceeds the construction budget in Section 5.1, the Council will know before a single permanent facility is built, while the off-ramp to competitive bidding remains open.

**How changes will be controlled.** The City's $3.7 million contingency is governed by a written drawdown protocol: the Project Manager may approve individual changes up to $50,000; the City Manager up to $250,000; anything larger, and any cumulative drawdown past 50 percent of the contingency, requires Council action in open session. A contingency ledger — every draw, its cause, and the remaining balance — will be published in the quarterly report.

**How the Council will see it.** Staff will deliver a **monthly written report to the finance subcommittee** chaired by Councilmember Shiraishi, showing budget versus actuals, contingency status, schedule against baseline, and a look-ahead of pending changes; a **quarterly report to the full Council in open session**, aligned with the quarterly reports the order requires the City to file with the Regional Board; and a **public project dashboard** on the City website updated monthly. In addition, the Finance Director will commission an **independent audit of project costs at the GMP milestone and annually thereafter**, reporting directly to the finance subcommittee.

Staff does not ask the Council to trust a number. Staff asks the Council to approve a number and then watch it, with the tools to intervene at every point where the parking structure went wrong.

## 8. Alternatives Examined

Four alternatives were evaluated in the corrective action plan and the January 2026 alternatives analysis. Cost figures are planning-level, year-of-expenditure.

### 8.1 Rehabilitation in place — not recommended

**Estimated cost: $26–29 million. Estimated service life extension: 12–15 years.**

This alternative would recoat and structurally patch the existing wet well and clarifier concrete, replace screens and mechanisms within the existing structures, and replace pumps and electrical gear in place. It was examined seriously because its first cost is roughly $13 million lower. It fails on four grounds:

- **It rehabilitates 1971 concrete.** Coring performed during the 2024 assessment found chloride and sulfide penetration beyond the depth that coating systems reliably protect. The structural engineer's opinion is that rehabilitation buys 12–15 years, after which the City replaces the structures anyway — paying twice.
- **It does not fix the hydraulics.** The existing headworks and pump station are undersized for observed peak wet-weather flows. Rehabilitation preserves the bottleneck that caused two of the four overflows.
- **It is worse to construct.** Rebuilding inside operating structures requires longer and more frequent shutdowns of the very units that cannot be spared, with more bypass time, not less.
- **The Regional Board will not accept it.** In pre-submittal consultation on the corrective action plan, Regional Board staff stated that a rehabilitation of the existing works would not be approved as the permanent remedy under the order, given the documented condition ratings. Pursuing it would mean resubmitting the corrective action plan and consuming schedule the City does not have.

On a lifecycle basis — counting the second replacement that rehabilitation merely defers — this alternative is the more expensive path.

### 8.2 Regional connection to Oro Vista Sanitation District — not recommended

**Estimated cost: $58–72 million. Estimated timeline: 6–8 years.**

This alternative would decommission Bellota Road's liquid treatment and convey Ridgemark's flow approximately nine miles via new force main and lift stations to the Oro Vista Sanitation District's regional plant, purchasing capacity through a buy-in. Staff held two exploratory meetings with Oro Vista's General Manager. The alternative fails on:

- **Cost.** Oro Vista's preliminary capacity buy-in figure is $38–46 million for 8 MGD, before the estimated $20–26 million conveyance system.
- **Schedule.** A regional connection requires a negotiated agreement, LAFCO proceedings, full EIR-level CEQA review of a nine-mile alignment, right-of-way acquisition, and Oro Vista's own capital timeline. No credible schedule commences construction before 2030 — three years past the order's deadline, at $10,000 per day.
- **Interim exposure.** The City would still have to spend heavily on the 1971 facilities to keep them alive through 2030 or beyond, or build much of this project anyway as an "interim" measure.
- **Loss of control.** Ridgemark would become a perpetual contract customer for its most essential utility, with rates set substantially by another agency's board.

Regionalization may merit study for the 2040s. It is not a remedy for this order.

### 8.3 Reduced scope (headworks and pump station only, defer clarifiers) — not recommended

**Estimated cost: $24 million now; clarifiers later at higher cost.**

Deferring the clarifiers would reduce the immediate ask but leave in service two structures rated at or near Condition 5, one of which caused the November 2025 overflow. It would require the Regional Board to accept a partial remedy, which its staff has indicated it will not, and a later stand-alone clarifier project would repeat mobilization, bypass, and escalation costs — an estimated $3–4 million premium over building now.

### 8.4 No action — not viable

Continued emergency repairs at $1 million or more per year, a near-certainty of further overflows and their attendant liability, penalties of $10,000 per day beginning July 1, 2027 ($3.65 million per year), and escalating enforcement up to and including potential Regional Board or EPA action seeking receivership of the utility. No action is the most expensive alternative on the table.

## 9. Risks and Mitigations

No honest presentation of a $41.6 million project on an operating treatment plant would claim the risks are small. The material risks, and what the project does about each:

| Risk | Likelihood | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wet-weather event during a cutover or bypass period** | Moderate — two wet seasons on bypass | Overflow; regulatory exposure | Bypass sized at 16 MGD with 100% standby; cutovers restricted to Apr–Oct; weather-triggered stand-down protocol; Superintendent's unilateral stop-work authority; rental pump standby contract already required by the order remains in force |
| **Existing 1971 equipment fails before its replacement is commissioned** | Moderate to high | Overflow during construction | Adjacent-construction sequencing keeps old units untouched until cutover; critical spares (screen drives, pump rotating assemblies) purchased at project outset within early-works package; bypass system can substitute for failed influent pumping from mid-2027 onward |
| **GMP exceeds construction budget** | Low to moderate | Budget increase or delay | Independent estimates at 60/90/100% design; open-book subcontractor bidding; Council accepts or rejects GMP; off-ramp to competitive bid with completed design |
| **Differing site conditions (buried utilities, soils)** | Moderate on a 55-year-old plant site | Cost/schedule growth | Geotechnical program complete pre-authorization; potholing of all utility crossings in early works; contingency sized at 11.9% partly for this exposure |
| **Prop 218 majority protest defeats the rate increase** | Low, based on comparable proceedings, but not zero | $4.1M funding gap | Summer/fall public outreach; rate remains below regional average even after increases; fallback options (deeper reserve draw, supplemental SRF) identified in advance |
| **SRF terms or timing change** | Low — application complete, priority category | Higher financing cost | Execute financing agreement in Q2 2026; interim funding from reserve authorized so design does not wait on the State |
| **Supply-chain delay on long-lead equipment (switchgear, pumps, mechanisms)** | Moderate — current lead times 40–60 weeks | Schedule slip toward the July 2027 deadline | Long-lead equipment pre-purchased in GMP Amendment 1, January 2027; schedule carries four months of float ahead of the deadline |
| **Cost growth during construction** | The Council's central concern | Repeat of the parking structure | Full control regime in Section 7: GMP, tiered contingency authority, monthly subcommittee reporting, annual independent audit |
| **Key staff turnover (PM, Superintendent)** | Low to moderate over 34 months | Loss of continuity | Documented project controls independent of individuals; CM firm bench depth contractually required; succession planning in plant operations underway |

The risk the Council cannot mitigate by any action available to it is the risk of the 1971 equipment itself. That risk exists today, it grows every month, and this project is the only alternative examined that retires it on a schedule the order permits.

**A RESOLUTION OF THE CITY COUNCIL OF THE CITY OF RIDGEMARK, CALIFORNIA, APPROVING THE BELLOTA ROAD HEADWORKS, INFLUENT PUMP STATION, AND PRIMARY CLARIFIER REPLACEMENT PROJECT (PROJECT NO. WW-2026-01); ADOPTING A PROJECT BUDGET; ADOPTING A MITIGATED NEGATIVE DECLARATION; AUTHORIZING EXECUTION OF A STATE REVOLVING FUND FINANCING AGREEMENT; APPROPRIATING DESIGN-PHASE FUNDS; AUTHORIZING PROCUREMENT OF CONSTRUCTION MANAGER AT RISK SERVICES; DIRECTING INITIATION OF PROCEEDINGS UNDER ARTICLE XIII D OF THE CALIFORNIA CONSTITUTION; AND ESTABLISHING PROJECT REPORTING AND CHANGE-CONTROL REQUIREMENTS**

**WHEREAS,** the City of Ridgemark owns and operates the Bellota Road Water Reclamation Facility, which provides wastewater treatment to a service population of approximately 68,400 under NPDES Permit No. CA0105512; and

**WHEREAS,** the facility's headworks, influent pump station, and two primary clarifiers were constructed in 1971 and have been documented in the City's 2024 condition assessment as at or near the end of their structural and mechanical service lives; and

**WHEREAS,** four sanitary sewer overflows occurred between June 2024 and November 2025, each traced to the failure of the 1971 facilities, releasing a combined estimated 412,000 gallons of untreated or partially treated wastewater, two of which events reached the Bellota Channel; and

**WHEREAS,** on December 16, 2025, the Santa Ana Regional Water Quality Control Board issued Cease and Desist Order No. R8-2025-0047, requiring the City to commence construction of a permanent remedy no later than July 1, 2027, and imposing penalties of $10,000 per day thereafter for noncompliance; and

**WHEREAS,** the City submitted a corrective action plan on January 30, 2026, identifying replacement of the headworks, influent pump station, and both primary clarifiers as the permanent remedy; and

**WHEREAS,** City staff has evaluated alternatives including rehabilitation in place, a regional connection to the Oro Vista Sanitation District, a reduced project scope, and no action, and has determined that full replacement is the only alternative that satisfies the Order within its schedule at acceptable lifecycle cost; and

**WHEREAS,** an Initial Study/Mitigated Negative Declaration has been prepared for the project in accordance with the California Environmental Quality Act, circulated for public review from January 12, 2026 to February 11, 2026, and all comments received have been responded to in the record before the Council; and

**WHEREAS,** the Director of Finance has assembled a funding plan consisting of $33,000,000 in Clean Water State Revolving Fund financing, $4,500,000 from the sewer capital reserve, and $4,100,000 in revenue from proposed sewer rate adjustments subject to the procedures of Article XIII D, Section 6 of the California Constitution; and

**WHEREAS,** the City Council has received and considered the staff report for Project No. WW-2026-01 dated March 10, 2026, and all attachments thereto, and has heard public testimony on the item;

**NOW, THEREFORE, BE IT RESOLVED** by the City Council of the City of Ridgemark as follows:

**SECTION 1. Project Approval and Budget.** The Bellota Road Headworks, Influent Pump Station, and Primary Clarifier Replacement Project (Project No. WW-2026-01) is hereby approved with a total project budget of $41,600,000, as set forth in Section 5.1 of the staff report.

**SECTION 2. CEQA.** The City Council hereby adopts the Mitigated Negative Declaration and Mitigation Monitoring and Reporting Program for the project, finding on the basis of the whole record that there is no substantial evidence the project as mitigated will have a significant effect on the environment, and directs the City Clerk to file a Notice of Determination with the County Clerk of San Bernardino County and the State Clearinghouse within five working days.

**SECTION 3. SRF Financing.** The City Manager, or her designee, is authorized to execute a financing agreement with the State Water Resources Control Board under the Clean Water State Revolving Fund program in an amount not to exceed $33,000,000, together with such certifications, reimbursement resolutions, and ancillary documents as the program requires, on terms materially consistent with Attachment D, subject to approval as to form by the City Attorney.

**SECTION 4. Appropriation.** The sum of $4,900,000 is hereby appropriated to Project No. WW-2026-01 for design, preconstruction, permitting, and early procurement, funded from the sewer capital reserve and, upon execution of the financing agreement, reimbursed from SRF proceeds to the maximum extent eligible.

**SECTION 5. CMAR Procurement.** The Director of Public Works is authorized to issue a Request for Qualifications/Proposals for construction manager at risk services and to conduct the selection process described in Section 6.1 of the staff report. Award of the preconstruction services agreement, and any subsequent guaranteed maximum price amendment, shall each require separate approval of the City Council.

**SECTION 6. Rate Proceedings.** The Director of Finance is directed to initiate proceedings under Article XIII D, Section 6 of the California Constitution for the sewer rate adjustments described in Section 5.3 of the staff report, including mailed notice to all affected parcels not less than 45 days before the public hearing, which hearing shall be scheduled for the regular meeting of October 27, 2026.

**SECTION 7. Reporting.** Staff shall provide monthly written project reports to the Council finance subcommittee and quarterly reports to the full City Council in open session, in the form described in Section 7 of the staff report, for the duration of the project. The Director of Finance is authorized to engage an independent auditor to examine project costs at the GMP milestone and annually thereafter, reporting to the finance subcommittee.

**SECTION 8. Change Control.** The contingency drawdown protocol set forth in Section 7 of the staff report is hereby adopted. No single project change exceeding $250,000, and no contingency drawdown causing cumulative draws to exceed 50 percent of the owner's contingency, shall be approved except by action of the City Council in open session.

**SECTION 9. Effective Date.** This Resolution shall take effect immediately upon adoption.

**PASSED, APPROVED, AND ADOPTED** this 10th day of March, 2026.

## Attachment I: Anticipated Questions and Staff Responses

Staff has prepared responses to questions raised in subcommittee briefings and expected at the March 10 hearing.

**Q1. Why is the Council being asked to approve $41.6 million tonight if the GMP is not negotiated until 2027?**

The Council is being asked to approve a project budget — the ceiling within which staff must deliver — not to sign a construction contract. Tonight's binding financial commitment is the $4.9 million design-phase appropriation and the SRF financing agreement, which carries no obligation to draw funds beyond amounts actually expended. The construction commitment occurs only if and when the Council accepts a GMP within the budget in early 2027. Approving the full budget now is what the SRF program, the Regional Board's schedule, and the Proposition 218 notice all require: each depends on a Council-adopted total project cost.

**Q2. What happens if the GMP comes in above $31.2 million?**

Three options return to the Council, in staff's order of preference: (a) direct value-engineering revisions with the CMAR and designer and renegotiate; (b) reject the GMP and competitively bid the completed design; (c) amend the budget. Option (c) would require a further staff report identifying the cause and funding source. The Council is never obligated to accept a GMP, and the completed design has independent value under any delivery path.

**Q3. Could the City simply pay the penalties while pursuing a cheaper or slower alternative?**

The penalties are $3.65 million per year, they do not stop the overflows, and they do not cap the City's exposure. The Water Code authorizes escalating enforcement, including mandatory minimum penalties for effluent violations, referral to the Attorney General, and third-party citizen suits under the Clean Water Act, for which the December 2024 and November 2025 events already provide a factual basis. Treating the penalty as a cost of doing business would also prejudice the City's SRF priority ranking, which currently works in its favor. Staff considers this path both more expensive and more dangerous than the project.

**Q4. Why 9 percent for four years instead of a larger single-year increase?**

A single-year increase sufficient to produce the same revenue would exceed 30 percent, concentrating the burden on ratepayers in one year and increasing the likelihood of a majority protest. The phased structure matches revenue growth to the project's expenditure curve and debt-service start date, keeps the rate below the regional average throughout, and mirrors the structure used successfully in comparable Proposition 218 proceedings by three neighboring agencies since 2023.

**Q5. The parking structure also had monthly reports. Why will reporting work this time?**

The parking structure's reports tracked expenditures against an authorization that was never a real number — a schematic estimate with no independent check and no geotechnical basis. Reporting cannot fix a fictitious baseline. This project's baseline rests on 30-percent design, completed geotechnical work, and a reconciled independent estimate, and it will be re-verified three more times before the GMP. The reporting regime is the second line of defense, not the first. The additional differences — Council-held contingency authority, open-book subcontract bidding, and an independent auditor reporting to the subcommittee rather than to staff — did not exist on the parking structure.

**Q6. What does the plant's compliance record look like during construction? Are further overflows possible?**

Staff will not represent that overflow risk during construction is zero; Section 9 states the contrary. The representation staff can make is that risk declines at every stage: the bypass system installed in early 2027 provides redundant pumping capacity the plant has lacked for years, and each cutover permanently retires a failure mode. The Regional Board's order anticipates construction-period operations and requires the interim reliability measures already in place; the Board's staff reviewed the sequencing plan in the corrective action plan and raised no objection.

**Q7. Does the project create capacity for growth that existing ratepayers are subsidizing?**

No. The permitted treatment capacity remains 8.2 MGD. The 16 MGD hydraulic sizing of the headworks and pump station addresses peak wet-weather flow — storm-driven surges of the existing service area's wastewater — not new development. Capacity for future growth, if the Council ever pursues it, would be a separate project funded through connection fees.

**Q8. What is the impact on Bellota Road neighbors during construction?**

Construction traffic will use the plant's existing Bellota Road access, with haul routes and hours restricted under the Mitigated Negative Declaration (7:00 a.m. to 6:00 p.m., Monday through Saturday). The new headworks includes permanent odor control that the 1971 facility lacks; the project is expected to reduce the odor complaints the City currently receives from the adjacent neighborhood. A construction hotline and monthly neighborhood notice are included in the CMAR's general conditions.
