# PERFORMANCE EVALUATION 1 OF 3

**Employee:** Lourdes Manzanares
**Title:** Utility Billing Supervisor (Civil Service Classification U-31)
**Years of Service:** 12
**Review Period:** September 1, 2025 – August 31, 2026
**Reviewer:** Reginald Ashcroft, Deputy Director, Water and Wastewater Utility
**Date of Review:** August 9, 2026

## Section 1: Goals for the Review Period and Results Achieved

Ms. Manzanares entered this review period with four goals set at last year's evaluation: (1) complete the customer information and billing system conversion with no more than one billing cycle of disruption; (2) reduce call center abandonment to below 15 percent; (3) maintain billing accuracy at or above 99 percent after conversion stabilization; and (4) cross-train billing staff to cover a minimum of two functions each.

**Goal 1 — Billing system conversion.** The conversion went live in October 2025 and misbilled 4,100 of our 34,000 accounts in its first cycle, primarily through incorrect rate-tier assignments and dropped multi-unit master accounts. This was a serious operational failure, though its root causes sat largely with the vendor's data migration scripts and with decisions made above Ms. Manzanares's level during procurement. What matters for this evaluation is her response. Within 72 hours she had quantified the error population, segmented it by cause, and stood up a corrections workflow. All 4,100 accounts were corrected or credited within two billing cycles. She personally reviewed the highest-dollar corrections and directed staff to apply late-fee waivers proactively rather than waiting for customer complaints. The second cycle after go-live posted at 99.4 percent accuracy, and the system has been stable since January 2026. I rate this goal as substantially exceeded on the dimension that was within her control: recovery, accuracy restoration, and customer remediation.

**Goal 2 — Call abandonment.** Abandonment stood at 31 percent at the start of the period, spiked to 44 percent during the misbilling event, and closed the period at 9 percent — well below the 15 percent target. She achieved this without additional headcount by restructuring the call queue, building a callback option, publishing self-service correction instructions on the utility's web page during the misbilling event, and staggering shift starts to match call volume patterns she documented herself. Goal exceeded.

**Goal 3 — Billing accuracy.** Post-stabilization accuracy has held at 99.4–99.7 percent monthly since January. Goal met.

**Goal 4 — Cross-training.** This goal was overtaken by events. Two billing clerk positions vacated in November 2025 and February 2026 and could not be filled under the citywide hiring freeze, now in its fourteenth month. Rather than formal cross-training, Ms. Manzanares redistributed the vacant positions' duties across her remaining five staff and absorbed the supervisory-adjacent portions herself, including exception processing and the bankruptcy/lien queue. The practical effect is that her section is operating at 71 percent of authorized staffing while handling conversion cleanup and elevated call volume. I consider the goal met in substance if not in form; every remaining clerk can now cover at least two functions because they have had no choice but to do so, and Ms. Manzanares built desk procedures for each absorbed function so the knowledge is documented rather than personal.

## Section 2: Compliance History and Specific Incidents

Ms. Manzanares has no disciplinary history in twelve years of service. During this period there was one compliance-adjacent matter: the misbilling event generated 61 informal complaints to the City Secretary's office and two formal billing disputes that advanced to administrative hearing. Both hearings resolved in the city's favor on the merits, and the hearing officer in the second matter noted on the record that the utility's correction documentation was "unusually thorough." The misbilling event itself is addressed in Section 1; I do not treat it as a conduct or compliance failure on her part, and no discipline was issued or contemplated.

She timely filed all required cash-handling reconciliations and her section passed the FY2026 internal audit of billing adjustments with no findings, which is notable given the volume of manual corrections processed in October through December.

## Section 3: Feedback from Council, Coworkers, and the Public

**Council.** Two council members contacted my office during the misbilling event. Councilmember Okafor (District 3) initially expressed sharp concern about constituent calls; by the January utility committee meeting he stated publicly that "the billing office dug out of a hole that wasn't of their making faster than I expected." Councilmember Trentino asked that her thanks to "the billing supervisor and her team" be entered into the minutes of the March 17, 2026 committee meeting. Council did, however, table the classification study that would support Ms. Manzanares's requested reclassification to Billing Manager, deferring it to the 2027 budget process. That decision is addressed in Section 5.

**Coworkers.** All five remaining billing staff completed anonymous input forms. Comments were uniformly positive on competence and workload distribution; two comments noted that Ms. Manzanares "works too many hours herself instead of telling the city no," which I read as a caution rather than a criticism, and I share it. The utility's IT liaison rated her the most prepared business-side partner he worked with on the conversion.

**Public.** Complaint volume aside from the misbilling event fell 38 percent year over year. The utility received eleven unsolicited written commendations naming billing staff, four naming Ms. Manzanares specifically.

## Section 4: Employee Self-Assessment

The following are Ms. Manzanares's own words, submitted July 28, 2026, and are quoted verbatim; they are her statements, not mine:

> "The conversion year was the hardest of my career. I am proud that no customer was left with a wrong bill for more than two cycles and that we never let a shutoff go out on a disputed corrected account. I am concerned that we are running the section on five people doing seven jobs, and that is not sustainable past this fiscal year. I applied for reclassification because I have been performing manager-level duties — vendor management, budget input, cross-departmental project leadership — for over two years. I understand the study was tabled and I am not asking this review to fix that, but I am asking that the record reflect the duties I actually perform."

**Reviewer response:** I concur with her account of the duties performed, and this evaluation is intended to serve as part of that record. Her concern about sustainability of staffing is well founded and is reflected in my recommendations below.

Under this year's compensation plan, Ms. Manzanares receives the 2 percent across-the-board increase effective the first pay period of the new fiscal year. # PERFORMANCE EVALUATION 2 OF 3

**Employee:** Gustavo Delgado-Marin
**Title:** Plant Operations Lead (Civil Service Classification U-44)
**Years of Service:** 22
**Review Period:** September 1, 2025 – August 31, 2026
**Reviewer:** Reginald Ashcroft, Deputy Director, Water and Wastewater Utility
**Date of Review:** August 9, 2026

## Section 1: Goals for the Review Period and Results Achieved

Mr. Delgado-Marin's goals for the period were: (1) achieve full compliance with all discharge permit limits; (2) lead the apprentice operator rotation so that at least two operators complete the experience hours required for Class B and Class C license sign-off; (3) hold plant overtime to within 10 percent of budget; and (4) complete the aeration basin blower replacement project on schedule.

**Goal 1 — Permit compliance.** Fully achieved, and it deserves plain acknowledgment: the plant met every discharge permit limit for twelve consecutive months — BOD, TSS, ammonia, E. coli, and pH — through a summer that included two multi-day power quality events and an influent slug load in April that Mr. Delgado-Marin diagnosed and managed through the night shift personally. He is the only Class A licensed operator on staff, and the plant's compliance record this year is inseparable from his skill. This is the strongest sustained permit performance the plant has recorded in the nine years I have been with the utility. Goal exceeded.

**Goal 2 — Apprentice rotation.** Not achieved. Mr. Delgado-Marin declined to run the apprentice rotation when it came up in the fall cycle — the third consecutive time he has declined. Two operators, both of whom have passed their written license examinations, remain unable to obtain license sign-off because they cannot accumulate the supervised process-control experience hours that only a Class A operator can certify. Both have filed grievances (Grievance Nos. 26-011 and 26-014), discussed in Section 2. Mr. Delgado-Marin's stated reasons, which he has been consistent and forthcoming about, are that the rotation as structured pulls him off process control during high-risk periods, that the last rotation he ran left him personally responsible for a near-miss permit exceedance, and that with the hiring freeze leaving the plant two operators short, he cannot both run the plant and train on it. I address the merits of this position in Section 4. Whatever the merits, the goal was assigned, agreed to at last year's review, and not performed. Goal not met.

**Goal 3 — Overtime.** Not achieved. The plant logged 480 overtime hours in the second quarter alone, roughly triple the budgeted quarterly figure, and finished the year approximately 140 percent over the overtime budget. A substantial portion of this overtime was worked by Mr. Delgado-Marin himself. The causes are linked: the freeze holds two operator positions vacant, the two operators on staff who could relieve licensed coverage cannot get sign-off, and therefore the only Class A operator covers the gaps personally. The overtime problem and the training problem are the same problem, and I want this evaluation to state that connection clearly because the solution to one is the solution to the other. Goal not met, with significant mitigating context.

**Goal 4 — Blower replacement.** Completed in June 2026, one month behind schedule due to a vendor delivery delay outside city control, and $22,000 under budget. Commissioning was performed without any permit excursion. Goal met.

## Section 2: Compliance History and Specific Incidents

Mr. Delgado-Marin has no disciplinary record in 22 years. Two matters require documentation in this period.

**Grievances 26-011 and 26-014.** Two operators filed grievances in May and June 2026 alleging that the utility, through the operations lead, has denied them the training opportunities required for license advancement, with pay-progression consequences. The grievances name the utility's training structure and reference Mr. Delgado-Marin's refusals of the rotation. Both grievances remain pending at Step 2. This evaluation does not prejudge their outcome. I note for the record that the grievances allege an institutional failure as much as an individual one, and I accept that management — including my office — bears responsibility for building a rotation structure that depends entirely on one employee's uncompensated availability.

**State notice of violation, August 2026.** The utility received an August notice of violation from the state environmental agency for missed monthly disinfectant residual reports. That NOV concerns reporting administration, which sits with the compliance function, not plant operations, and I want this public document to be precise: the plant produced compliant water and compliant effluent; the failure was in report submission, and it is addressed in the third evaluation in this cycle and in the utility's corrective action plan. No portion of the NOV is attributed to Mr. Delgado-Marin.

## Section 3: Feedback from Council, Coworkers, and the Public

**Council.** At the June 9, 2026 utility committee meeting, Councilmember Braswell praised the plant's "perfect permit year" by name and asked that staff be recognized. At the same meeting, in response to the overtime budget report, Councilmember Okafor asked pointedly why the utility "has one man who can legally run the plant and no plan if he retires." That question is fair, and it frames this entire evaluation.

**Coworkers.** Operator feedback was polarized in a way I have not seen before. Senior operators describe Mr. Delgado-Marin as "the best process man in the Panhandle" and credit him with the compliance record. The two grievants and one other junior operator describe feeling that their careers are stalled and that asking for training help is "not welcome." One comment, which I include because it captures the tension honestly: "Gus will answer any question at 2 a.m. about the plant. He will not sit down and sign your hours. Those are different things and only one of them gets you licensed."

**Public.** No public complaints touch plant operations this year. The plant hosted two school tours, both led by Mr. Delgado-Marin, which drew written thanks from the district.

## Section 4: Employee Self-Assessment

The following is Mr. Delgado-Marin's self-assessment, submitted July 30, 2026, quoted verbatim:

> "I kept the permit clean for a whole year with two empty chairs on my shift board. I am not proud of the overtime number but I am proud of what it bought. On the rotation: I have said the same thing three times. Give me the two vacant positions filled, or give me a relief operator during rotation weeks, and I will train anybody you send me. I will not run the plant and teach the plant at the same time, because the last time I tried we came within one sample of a violation, and it would have been my license on the line, not the city's. The two operators who grieved are good operators. They deserve sign-off. They should be angry at the org chart, not at me. I have four years to retirement. If the city wants a Class A on staff after that, the clock started a long time ago."

**Reviewer response:** I accept the operational logic of his position and reject the conclusion he draws from it. He is right that the rotation as structured is unsafe with current staffing, right that management has not resourced it, and right about the succession clock. He is wrong that the answer is refusal. The lead operator position carries training and certification of subordinate hours as core duties in its classification description, not as a favor to be traded for staffing. Declining a core duty three times, however defensible the reasoning, is a performance deficiency, and this evaluation records it as one — alongside management's obligation, which I record with equal weight, to restructure the rotation so it can be performed safely.

## Section 6: Application of the Pay Structure and Hiring Freeze

Mr. Delgado-Marin receives the 2 percent across-the-board increase. Under the performance pool rules, employees rated 3 are eligible for a partial pool award at the reviewer's discretion; I am recommending no performance pool award this year, and I am putting the reason in writing: the pool must mean something, and an employee who declined an assigned core duty three times cannot receive performance pay in the same year, regardless of how well the other duties were performed. I am simultaneously recommending, by separate memorandum, (a) a freeze exception for both vacant operator positions on public-health-continuity grounds, (b) standby pay review for the periods he served as sole licensed coverage, and (c) a restructured rotation with relief coverage, which removes his stated objection. The freeze has materially harmed this employee and this plant, and the record should say so.

## Section 7: Goals for the Coming Review Period

1. Maintain 100 percent discharge permit compliance.
2. **Mandatory:** Conduct the restructured apprentice rotation beginning within 60 days of relief coverage being in place, with sign-off packages for both examination-passed operators submitted to the state by the end of the review period. This goal is a condition of the position; a fourth declination will be treated as a directed-duty matter, not a goal shortfall.
3. Reduce plant overtime to within 25 percent of budget by year-end, measured after the vacant positions are addressed.
4. Complete a written succession and knowledge-transfer plan for the Class A function, including documented standard operating procedures for the ten highest-risk process decisions, by February 2027.
5. Participate in resolution of Grievances 26-011 and 26-014 in good faith as directed by Human Resources.

# PERFORMANCE EVALUATION 3 OF 3

**Employee:** Simone Achterberg
**Title:** Environmental Compliance Analyst (Civil Service Classification U-27)
**Years of Service:** 6 months (hired February 2, 2026)
**Review Period:** February 2, 2026 – August 31, 2026 (partial year; probationary period completed July 31, 2026)
**Reviewer:** Reginald Ashcroft, Deputy Director, Water and Wastewater Utility
**Date of Review:** August 9, 2026

## Section 1: Goals for the Review Period and Results Achieved

Ms. Achterberg was hired into a newly funded position in February 2026, in direct response to the compliance reporting failures that produced the state environmental agency's notice of violation for missed monthly disinfectant residual reports. Because she joined mid-cycle, her goals were set at hire: (1) within 90 days, clear the backlog of unfiled and unorganized sampling records; (2) build a master regulatory reporting calendar covering all state and federal submission deadlines; (3) achieve on-time, accurate submission of all reports due after April 1, 2026; and (4) complete the utility's response documentation for the notice of violation.

**Goal 1 — Backlog clearance.** Ms. Achterberg inherited fourteen months of backlogged sampling records — some unfiled, some filed to the wrong monitoring points, some existing only as bench sheets in the laboratory. She cleared the entire backlog within her first 90 days, reconstructing chain-of-custody documentation where it existed and candidly flagging to me, in writing, the specific months where records could not be fully reconstructed rather than papering over gaps. That instinct — documenting the weakness instead of hiding it — is exactly what this position was created for, and it materially strengthened the utility's credibility with the state agency during NOV negotiations. Goal exceeded, both on timeline and on integrity of method.

**Goal 2 — Reporting calendar.** She built a master compliance calendar covering 61 recurring state and federal submissions, with owner assignments, data-source mapping, and a two-tier reminder structure. The calendar has been adopted utility-wide and is now referenced by both the plant and the laboratory. Goal met, and the product exceeds what was specified.

**Goal 3 — On-time, accurate submissions.** Substantially met, with one significant exception documented in Section 2. From April 1 through the end of the period, all 27 required submissions were filed on time. Twenty-six were accurate as filed. The March report — technically before this goal's April 1 start date, but I include it because it belongs in an honest record — was submitted to the state with the wrong laboratory identifier and required a corrected filing.

**Goal 4 — NOV response.** She assembled the corrective action documentation, the root-cause narrative, and the return-to-compliance evidence package for the notice of violation. The state agency's compliance officer told me by phone in June that the utility's submission was "one of the more complete packages we've received from a system this size." The NOV remains open pending the agency's review of six months of clean reporting, which the utility is on track to demonstrate in October. Goal met.

## Section 2: Compliance History and Specific Incidents

**March 2026 laboratory identifier error.** In March 2026, in her second month, Ms. Achterberg submitted a monthly report to the state bearing the identifier of the utility's former contract laboratory rather than the current one — a legacy value embedded in the report template she inherited. The underlying sampling data were correct. The error was caught by the state, and a corrected filing was required and submitted within five business days. Two things belong in the record. First, an identifier error on a compliance report submitted while under an NOV is not trivial; it invited exactly the scrutiny the utility was trying to retire, and I counseled her on the need for template verification, not just data verification. Second, her response was exemplary: she self-reported the error to me before the state's letter arrived, filed the correction promptly, and then audited every report template in the utility's library, finding and fixing four other templates carrying stale identifiers or outdated monitoring point codes that would have produced identical errors in future filings. She converted a mistake into a systemic fix. No discipline was issued; a counseling note is in the supervisory file.

There are no other compliance incidents. She completed probation on July 31, 2026 with a satisfactory probationary certification.

## Section 3: Feedback from Council, Coworkers, and the Public

**Council.** Ms. Achterberg presented the NOV corrective action status at the May 12, 2026 utility committee meeting — an unusual assignment for an analyst in her fourth month, given because she knew the material better than anyone. Councilmember Trentino commented afterward that it was "the first compliance briefing I've understood in six years on this committee." Councilmember Braswell asked whether the position would survive future budget cycles; I read that as an endorsement of the function.

**Coworkers.** Laboratory staff describe her as demanding about documentation but fair, and note that she asks for things in writing and returns the courtesy. One plant operator wrote: "She came out to the plant to see where the residual readings actually come from before she asked us to change how we log them. Nobody in that job ever did that." The plant operations lead noted that her calendar reminders have eliminated the end-of-month scramble on operational reports. One comment noted friction in her first weeks over the pace of her records requests during the backlog project; the same commenter noted it resolved once the purpose was explained.

**Public.** The position has limited direct public contact. The NOV was reported in the *Palo Duro Ledger* in September 2025, before her hire; the paper's April 2026 follow-up noted the utility "has hired a compliance analyst and cleared its records backlog," which is the public feedback that matters most for this role.

## Section 4: Employee Self-Assessment

The following is Ms. Achterberg's self-assessment, submitted July 27, 2026, quoted verbatim:

> "I took this job knowing the utility was under a notice of violation, and I saw that as the job description, not a problem with it. I am proud of the backlog work and prouder of the calendar, because the backlog was a one-time fix and the calendar is what keeps us from needing another one. The March identifier error is on me. I trusted an inherited template under deadline pressure, and I will not do that again — every template in the library has now been verified and version-controlled, and I check the header block on every filing before it goes out. My concern for next year is single-point-of-failure risk: right now the compliance function is one person, the same structural problem that caused the NOV in the first place. I would like a trained backup, even a partial one, before I take vacation with a monthly deadline pending."

**Reviewer response:** I concur with every element of this assessment, including her ownership of the March error, which she has characterized more severely than I would. Her single-point-of-failure concern is correct and echoes the identical structural risk documented elsewhere in this review cycle regarding licensed plant operations. It is addressed in her goals below and in my staffing memorandum to the Director.
