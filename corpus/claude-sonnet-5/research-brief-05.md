# Research Brief: Did the Regional Pay Conversion Reduce Driver Turnover?

**Prepared for:** Terrence Whitlock, Chief Financial Officer; Fatima Bensalem, Vice President of Driver Recruiting
**Prepared by:** Marisol Cabrera-Nunez, Workforce Analytics
**With route and terminal context from:** Gene Prokop, Fort Wayne Terminal Manager
**Date:** July 30, 2026

---

## The Question and What We Found

In March 2025, Sycamore Ridge Transport converted its 742 regional drivers from mileage-based pay to an hourly rate of $28.50 with guaranteed detention pay, at an annualized cost of roughly $3.4 million. The board now needs to decide whether to extend the same conversion to the 310-driver over-the-road (OTR) fleet, at an additional annualized cost of about $6.2 million. Before that decision is made, Mr. Whitlock asked a direct question: did the March 2025 conversion actually reduce driver turnover, and by how much?

The headline number looks strong. Annualized turnover among regional drivers fell from 51 percent in the twelve months before the conversion to 33 percent in the twelve months after — an 18-point drop across 742 drivers. Taken at face value, that would suggest the pay conversion was a clear success.

It is not that simple, and the board should not take the 18-point figure as the effect of the pay change alone. Two other things happened in the same window that plausibly reduced turnover on their own: a $2,500 referral bonus introduced in February 2025, one month before the pay conversion, and a 9 percent industry-wide decline in regional freight volumes that shrank the number of outside driving jobs available to anyone thinking about leaving. Critically, the OTR fleet — which received neither the pay change nor the bonus — also saw turnover fall sharply over the identical period, from 68 percent to 57 percent, an 11-point drop with no intervention at all. That single comparison is the most important fact in this brief: a substantial share of the improvement Sycamore Ridge is crediting to the pay conversion appears to have happened anyway, for drivers who got no pay change.

Once we treat the OTR fleet's unintervened decline as a rough floor for what the freight cycle alone can do, the portion of the regional improvement plausibly attributable to the pay conversion and referral bonus combined narrows to something in the range of 7 percentage points, not 18. That is still a real, useful effect — but at a different price. The naive calculation puts the cost of the program at about $25,500 per driver-year retained; the more conservative, freight-cycle-adjusted calculation puts it closer to $65,000 per driver-year retained. Both numbers matter, and the board should see both before it votes on $6.2 million more.

The results are also not uniform. Fort Wayne (46 percent to 24 percent) and Louisville (58 percent to 30 percent) improved sharply; Columbus (44 percent to 41 percent) barely moved; Evansville was essentially flat. Whatever is driving the regional improvement, it is not landing evenly across terminals, which argues against treating the pay conversion as a uniformly powerful lever and for taking terminal-level operating conditions seriously. Separately, drivers with less than one year of tenure account for 71 percent of all separations, which raises the question of whether an across-the-board hourly conversion is well aimed at the actual attrition problem, or whether it is a broad and expensive instrument applied to what is substantially an onboarding-period problem.

None of this means the conversion failed. It means the company does not yet have clean evidence of how large its true effect was, and the data available today have real gaps that limit how confidently that effect can be estimated. The remainder of this brief lays out the records used, where they fall short, the full set of results, and what we think the board needs before it commits to extending the program to the OTR fleet.

## Data and Methods

### Sources

This analysis draws on four record systems maintained by Sycamore Ridge's payroll and human resources functions:

**Payroll extracts.** Monthly payroll records for all regional and OTR drivers, covering pay type (mileage vs. hourly), base rate, detention pay amounts, and gross pay, from January 2024 through June 2026. These records let us confirm which drivers were paid under which structure and when the conversion took effect for each individual, since the March 2025 rollout was not instantaneous across all 742 regional drivers — a small number converted in the two weeks following the formal effective date as payroll processing caught up.

**HR separation records.** Termination records for all drivers company-wide, including separation date, terminal of record at separation, tenure at separation, and — where coded — a reason category (voluntary resignation, termination for cause, retirement, medical, other). These records are the basis for the turnover-rate calculations throughout this brief.

**Driver roster and tenure files.** Monthly headcount snapshots by terminal and fleet (regional vs. OTR), used to construct the denominators for annualized turnover rates and to identify the 89 drivers who changed terminal assignment during the analysis window.

**Telematics detention logs.** Automated recording of detention time (time spent waiting at shipper or receiver docks beyond the standard load/unload window) that feeds the guaranteed detention pay calculation. This system began recording in April 2024.

**Recruiting and referral records.** Payout records for the $2,500 referral bonus program, including referring driver, hire date, and terminal, maintained separately by the recruiting function and reconciled against payroll for verification.

### Analysis window and definitions

Turnover is calculated as an annualized rate: total voluntary and involuntary separations in a twelve-month window, divided by average headcount over that window, expressed as a percentage. The "before" window runs from March 2024 through February 2025, the twelve months immediately preceding the pay conversion. The "after" window runs from March 2025 through February 2026, the twelve months immediately following. Both windows are now complete as of this writing, which is why the analysis is being brought to the board at this time rather than earlier.

Turnover rates are calculated separately for the regional fleet, the OTR fleet, and each of the four terminals, and separately by tenure band (less than one year of service vs. one year or more) at the point of separation.

### Where the records run short

Four gaps in the underlying data limit how far this analysis can be pushed, and the board should understand each before weighing the results.

First, the telematics detention system did not begin recording until April 2024, one month into the "before" window. This means we have no automated detention data for March 2024, and — more importantly for cost purposes — no reliable pre-conversion baseline for how much detention time drivers were actually accumulating before the guarantee was in place. Detention pay claims before April 2024 rely on manually logged shipper/receiver paperwork, which is less complete and less consistent than the telematics feed. This does not affect the turnover-rate calculations, which come from payroll and HR systems, but it does mean we cannot fully verify how much of the guaranteed detention pay drivers are now receiving reflects a genuine change in dock wait times versus better capture of time that was always occurring but previously went uncompensated or unrecorded.

Second, separation reason coding in the HR system is inconsistent. Terminal managers enter a reason category at the time of separation, but the categories are not always applied the same way across terminals, and a meaningful share of records (we estimate on the order of 15 to 20 percent, though this was not the focus of a formal audit) are coded simply as "voluntary" or "other" without further detail. This means we can report overall turnover with confidence but cannot cleanly separate, say, resignations to a competing carrier from resignations for personal or family reasons, retirements, or terminations for cause. A driver leaving because a competitor now pays more is a very different signal than a driver leaving for health reasons, and the current data do not reliably distinguish them.

Third, 89 drivers — about 12 percent of the 742-driver regional population at the start of the window — changed terminal assignment at some point during the two-year analysis period. Our terminal-level turnover figures assign each separation to the terminal of record at the time of separation, which is standard practice, but it means a driver who transferred from Louisville to Columbus and then left six months later is counted as a Columbus separation even though most of their tenure, and possibly the working conditions that shaped their decision to leave, were at Louisville. We have not rebuilt the terminal-level rates using terminal-at-hire or terminal-at-midpoint as an alternative assignment rule; doing so would be a reasonable next step and could change the terminal comparisons discussed below.

Fourth, we do not have systematic exit interview data. Some terminals conduct exit interviews and log notes informally; others do not, or the notes are not retained in a form we could analyze. This means the reason coding gap described above cannot currently be filled in with qualitative context, and any explanation offered in this brief for why turnover moved as it did is necessarily inferential rather than directly evidenced by what departing drivers said.

Taken together, these gaps mean the turnover-rate numbers themselves are solid — they come from complete payroll and HR headcount records — but the attribution of why those numbers moved rests on comparison and inference rather than on direct evidence of driver motivation.

## Results

### Overall regional turnover, before and after

Across the full 742-driver regional population, annualized turnover fell from 51 percent in the twelve months before the conversion to 33 percent in the twelve months after — a decline of 18 percentage points. In raw terms, this is a drop from roughly 378 separations in the before-year to roughly 245 in the after-year, on a comparable average headcount. That is a large, operationally significant change on its face, and it is the number that has driven interest in extending the program.

The question this brief exists to answer is how much of that 18-point drop can reasonably be attributed to the pay conversion itself, as opposed to other things that happened at the same time.

### Two confounding events

**The referral bonus.** A $2,500 referral bonus for new regional hires began in February 2025, one month before the hourly pay conversion took effect in March 2025. The near-simultaneous timing of these two changes is the single biggest obstacle to isolating the pay conversion's effect. Referral bonuses are well documented in trucking and other high-turnover industries to improve early retention, on the theory that drivers referred by a current employee arrive with more accurate expectations about the job and a social tie to someone already at the company, both of which are associated with staying longer. Because the bonus and the pay conversion both apply to the same 742 regional drivers and began within a month of each other, the payroll and HR data available to us cannot cleanly separate "turnover improvement from higher, steadier pay" from "turnover improvement from a healthier mix of referred versus cold-recruited new hires." Recruiting records show referral-sourced hires as a share of new regional hires rose from roughly 14 percent in the twelve months before February 2025 to roughly 31 percent in the twelve months after, which is consistent with the bonus doing real work — but it also means a meaningful share of the driver population entering the "after" period looks different in composition, not just in pay structure, from the population in the "before" period.

**The freight cycle.** Regional freight volumes fell 9 percent industry-wide over roughly the same period, which reduced the number of open driving positions available at other regional carriers. When outside opportunities shrink, voluntary turnover tends to fall even without any change in a driver's own pay or working conditions, simply because there are fewer places to go. This is a well-established pattern in trucking labor markets and is not specific to Sycamore Ridge. We do not have terminal-level or lane-level freight volume data for our own four terminals, only the industry-wide figure, so we cannot say precisely how much this affected each location — but the direction of the effect should be a genuine reduction in voluntary turnover that has nothing to do with Sycamore Ridge's pay decision.

### The OTR fleet as an informal comparison group

The most useful evidence for separating the pay conversion's effect from these two confounds comes from the OTR fleet, which received neither the hourly conversion nor the referral bonus over this period, since the bonus program was structured around regional hiring needs. If freight-cycle softening and general labor-market conditions were the only forces at work, we would expect the OTR fleet's turnover to be roughly flat over the same window. Instead, OTR turnover fell from 68 percent to 57 percent — an 11-point decline, with no pay change and no bonus.

This is an imperfect comparison. OTR and regional driving are different jobs with different lifestyles, baseline turnover levels, and driver pools, so the OTR fleet is not a controlled experiment's control group — it is what we have, not what we would design if starting from scratch. OTR drivers were already paid differently before this period (their compensation was not affected by the March 2025 change and remains mileage-based), and their much higher baseline turnover (68 percent vs. 51 percent) suggests systematically different retention dynamics that may not respond to the same forces in the same way as the regional fleet.

With that caveat, the OTR decline is still the most direct evidence available that a large part of what happened to regional turnover would very plausibly have happened without the pay conversion. If we treat the OTR fleet's 11-point unintervened decline as an approximation of what freight-cycle and broader labor-market conditions contributed to the regional fleet as well, the residual improvement attributable to the pay conversion and referral bonus together narrows from 18 points to roughly 7 points. That residual still needs to be split between the pay change and the bonus, which the current data cannot do cleanly, since both started within a month of each other and apply to the identical population.

### Terminal-level differences

Regional turnover did not move uniformly across the four terminals, and the spread is wide enough that it should factor into the board's thinking about whether a single company-wide pay lever is the right tool.

**Table 2. Regional turnover by terminal**

| Terminal | Before | After | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fort Wayne | 46% | 24% | −22 pts |
| Louisville | 58% | 30% | −28 pts |
| Columbus | 44% | 41% | −3 pts |
| Evansville | — | — | Little change (roughly flat) |

Fort Wayne and Louisville account for essentially all of the regional-fleet improvement. Both saw turnover drop by 20 or more points, well above the fleet-wide average of 18. Columbus, by contrast, moved only 3 points, and Evansville moved little at all despite receiving the identical pay structure, the identical referral bonus program, and being subject to the same industry-wide freight softening.

We do not currently have a confirmed explanation for this spread, and this is an area where Gene Prokop's route-level context from Fort Wayne is useful but partial — it speaks to one terminal, not all four. Fort Wayne's route mix skews toward shorter regional lanes with more predictable dock scheduling, according to Mr. Prokop, which may mean the guaranteed detention pay is doing more real work there — drivers may have been more likely to encounter and be compensated for genuine wait time — than at terminals with different freight patterns. That is a plausible hypothesis, not a confirmed finding, since we lack comparable route-level detail from Columbus, Louisville, or Evansville. Local labor market conditions at each terminal city, which we have not measured directly, could also explain some of the spread: if Fort Wayne and Louisville happen to be in metro areas where competing trucking and warehouse jobs contracted more sharply during the 9 percent industry-wide freight decline, that alone could produce a bigger local turnover drop independent of Sycamore Ridge's own pay decision. Terminal management quality and local hiring practices are a third plausible factor we have not measured.

The terminal transfer issue described earlier also bears on how much confidence to place in these terminal-level numbers. With 89 drivers — about 12 percent of the regional population — having changed terminals during the window, and no way in the current data to know how those transfers are distributed across the four locations, it is possible that some of the terminal-level variation reflects reshuffling rather than genuine differences in local retention.

### Tenure differences

Separately from the terminal breakdown, tenure at separation shows a strongly skewed pattern: drivers with less than one year of service account for 71 percent of all separations across the two-year window (we have not yet split this figure into before-conversion and after-conversion sub-periods, which would be a natural next step). This is consistent with a common pattern in trucking, where the highest-risk period for voluntary turnover is the first several months on the job, as new hires discover whether the actual working conditions, home time, and pay match what they expected during recruiting.

This finding matters for how the pay conversion should be evaluated. An hourly rate with guaranteed detention pay is a structural, company-wide change that raises cost for every driver, including the roughly 29 percent of departures who come from drivers with a year or more of tenure and whose retention may respond to entirely different factors — route assignments, home-time predictability, equipment condition, relationships with dispatch. If the core problem is concentrated in the first year, the pay conversion is a broad and expensive instrument aimed partly, but not precisely, at the population driving most of the turnover. We have not yet been able to determine whether the 18-point regional improvement was itself concentrated among short-tenure drivers or spread evenly across tenure bands, because that cross-tabulation (turnover by tenure band, before vs. after) has not yet been built from the underlying separation records. This is a gap worth closing before the board acts.

## Limitations

We want to state plainly, in one place, the boundaries of what this analysis can and cannot support.

**We cannot isolate the pay conversion's effect from the referral bonus.** Both changes apply to the identical 742 regional drivers and began within a month of each other. Every number in this brief describing "the regional improvement" is really describing the combined effect of two simultaneous changes, not the pay conversion alone. Splitting these two effects would require either a natural experiment we do not have (for instance, a terminal or period where one change happened without the other) or a more detailed model using hire-date cohorts and referral status as covariates, which has not yet been built.

**We cannot fully net out the freight cycle.** The OTR fleet comparison is the best evidence available that a substantial share of the regional improvement would have happened regardless of company action, but OTR is not a true control group. It is a different job with different baseline turnover, different driver demographics, and possibly different sensitivity to freight-market softening. Treating its 11-point decline as a direct stand-in for what regional turnover "would have done anyway" is a reasonable approximation, not a precise estimate. The true freight-cycle contribution to regional turnover could be somewhat higher or lower than 11 points.

**We have not run formal statistical significance testing.** The figures reported here are simple before-and-after rate comparisons. We have not tested whether the terminal-level differences (for example, Fort Wayne's 22-point drop versus Columbus's 3-point drop) are statistically distinguishable from what could occur by chance given each terminal's driver count, which ranges from roughly 300 at the largest terminal down to a much smaller number at Evansville. Evansville's flat result in particular should be treated cautiously until we know how many drivers and separations that figure is built on; a small terminal can show little movement simply because the sample is too small to register a change reliably, not because nothing changed underneath.

**Separation reason coding is incomplete and inconsistent.** We can report that a driver left; we cannot always report reliably why, or distinguish a resignation to a competitor from a resignation for personal reasons. This limits how confidently we can say the pay conversion changed drivers' competitive position versus simply changing the mix of who was leaving for other reasons.

**Terminal transfers complicate the terminal-level results.** With 89 drivers reassigned across terminals during the window and separations counted at the terminal of record at exit, some portion of the terminal-by-terminal variation described above could reflect internal movement rather than genuine differences in local retention conditions.

**The detention pay baseline is incomplete.** Telematics detention data exist only from April 2024 forward, one month into the "before" window, and pre-conversion detention claims rely on less reliable manual logs. This limits our ability to verify how much of the guaranteed detention pay drivers now receive reflects newly compensated time versus time that was always occurring.

**We do not have terminal-level freight volume data.** The 9 percent freight decline is an industry-wide figure. We cannot currently attribute how much of it affected each of the four terminal markets individually, which limits our ability to explain the terminal-level spread in turnover improvement.

**We have not yet cross-tabulated tenure and time period.** We know that short-tenure drivers account for 71 percent of separations across the full two-year window, but we do not yet know whether that concentration increased, decreased, or stayed the same after the conversion, which is a more direct test of whether the pay change reached the population driving most of the turnover.

None of these limitations invalidate the headline before-and-after numbers, which are drawn from complete payroll and HR records. They do mean that any single number offered as "the effect of the pay conversion" should be treated as a range bounded by assumptions, not a precise, settled figure.

## Implications and What the Board Needs

### What we can say with reasonable confidence

Regional driver turnover fell substantially after the March 2025 pay conversion, and the size of the drop (18 points) is well outside normal year-to-year noise for a fleet this size. Some portion of that drop is very likely attributable to the combination of the hourly rate, the detention guarantee, and the referral bonus that accompanied it — drivers now have more predictable pay and a lower-variance income than they did under mileage pay, which is a commonly cited driver preference in trucking generally, independent of anything specific to Sycamore Ridge. At the same time, a comparably-timed 11-point drop in the untouched OTR fleet shows that a meaningful share of the regional improvement — plausibly more than half of it — would likely have occurred without spending a dollar, driven by the freight-volume contraction alone.

### What we cannot yet say

We cannot currently give the board a single, defensible number for "the return on the $3.4 million." The honest range runs from an optimistic reading, in which most of the 18-point drop is attributed to company action and the effective cost is roughly $25,500 per additional driver-year retained, to a conservative reading, in which only the roughly 7-point residual above the OTR fleet's unintervened decline is attributed to company action, putting the effective cost closer to $65,000 per additional driver-year retained. We also cannot currently separate how much of even that conservative 7-point residual is due to the pay conversion specifically versus the referral bonus, which is a materially cheaper program to run and extend on its own.

### What we recommend before the OTR decision

1. **Build the cohort-level model needed to separate the pay effect from the referral bonus.** This means analyzing turnover by hire date relative to the two program start dates (bonus in February, pay change in March), and by referral status, to see whether referred hires and directly-recruited hires show different retention patterns under the new pay structure. This is achievable with existing recruiting and payroll data and does not require new data collection.

2. **Get terminal-level freight and local labor-market context for all four terminals, not just Fort Wayne.** Mr. Prokop's route context has been valuable for understanding Fort Wayne's outsized improvement; comparable input from Columbus, Louisville, and Evansville management would help explain why the terminal results diverge so sharply, and would materially change how much confidence the board should place in a company-wide rollout to OTR.

3. **Run the tenure-by-period cross-tabulation.** Knowing whether the 18-point improvement was concentrated among short-tenure drivers, where 71 percent of all separations already sit, or spread across tenure bands, would directly test whether the pay conversion is reaching the population actually driving turnover, or whether a cheaper, more targeted first-year retention program might achieve similar results at lower cost.

4. **Clean up separation reason coding going forward**, even if this cannot be done retroactively, so that a future review of the OTR extension (if approved) can distinguish competitive departures from other causes.

5. **Consider a staged rather than fleet-wide extension to OTR.** Given that the regional results themselves varied by a factor of nearly ten between the strongest terminal (Louisville, −28 points) and the weakest (Columbus, −3 points), a full $6.2 million commitment across all 310 OTR drivers assumes a uniformity of effect that the regional data do not actually show. Piloting the conversion at one or two OTR terminals first, with the same before-and-after measurement discipline applied here, would let the board see whether the OTR fleet — which starts from a much higher 57 percent baseline turnover and a materially different job — responds the way the regional fleet did, before committing the full amount.

6. **Set an explicit decision threshold in advance.** Because the "true" effect of the regional conversion sits somewhere in a fairly wide range, the board should decide now what cost-per-retained-driver it considers acceptable for the OTR extension, rather than evaluating the outcome after the fact against whatever number happens to look most favorable. Given that OTR turnover is already declining independent of any pay action, the same confounding risk that makes this brief's regional numbers hard to interpret will apply just as much to any post-hoc reading of an OTR rollout, unless the measurement approach is fixed before the program starts.

### Bottom line for Mr. Whitlock

The pay conversion is very likely part of why regional turnover fell, and the qualitative case for it — steadier income, compensated detention time, a workforce that by informal account (via Mr. Prokop) reports feeling more fairly treated — has value that a turnover statistic alone does not fully capture. But the clean, single-number case that the board may be expecting is not yet supportable from the data on hand. Before committing $6.2 million to extend the program to the OTR fleet, we recommend the analytic work in items 1 through 4 above, which can be completed with existing records over the next quarter, and a staged rollout structure per item 5 that limits the company's exposure while that clearer picture is built.

### Proposed Timeline

To have a decision-ready package in front of the board before it takes up the OTR extension, we propose the following sequence, most of which can run in parallel:

**Weeks 1–3.** Workforce analytics builds the hire-cohort model separating drivers by hire date relative to the February 2025 bonus start and the March 2025 pay conversion, and by referral source. This uses existing recruiting and payroll extracts and requires no new data collection. Output: an estimate, with a confidence range rather than a single point figure, of how much of the regional improvement is attributable to the bonus alone versus the pay structure alone versus their interaction.

**Weeks 1–4, run in parallel.** Terminal managers at Columbus, Louisville, and Evansville are asked for the same kind of route and local-market context Mr. Prokop provided for Fort Wayne: freight mix, typical dock wait patterns, and any local competitive hiring activity they are aware of over the two analysis windows. This is qualitative input, not a data pull, and should take each manager no more than a few hours to assemble.

**Weeks 2–5.** Workforce analytics rebuilds the tenure-by-period cross-tabulation (turnover among sub-one-year drivers versus one-year-plus drivers, before and after the conversion, at both the fleet and terminal level) and reruns the terminal-level turnover figures using terminal-at-hire as an alternative assignment rule, to check how much the 89 mid-period transfers are affecting the terminal comparisons reported above.

**Weeks 4–6.** Recruiting and HR jointly review separation reason coding practices across the four terminals and standardize the category definitions going forward, so that any OTR pilot can be measured with better cause-of-departure data than was available for this review of the regional conversion.

**Weeks 6–8.** Workforce analytics and Ms. Bensalem's team jointly design the OTR pilot structure referenced in recommendation 5 — most likely one or two terminals, selected to give some contrast between a higher-detention-exposure route profile and a lower-exposure one, so that if the pattern seen in Fort Wayne and Louisville (large improvement) versus Columbus and Evansville (little improvement) is really about route and dock conditions rather than something else, the pilot will surface that before a full fleet-wide commitment is made.

**Week 8.** Consolidated findings and a revised cost-per-retained-driver range, incorporating the cohort model and terminal context, go to Mr. Whitlock and the board in advance of the meeting at which the OTR extension will be discussed.

This sequence is designed to fit inside a single quarter and does not require slowing down the board's timeline materially, since most of the underlying records already exist and the work is analytical rather than dependent on new data collection, with the partial exception of the terminal manager interviews and the reason-coding cleanup.

### Appendix: Supporting Calculations

The two cost-per-retained-driver figures cited in this brief are derived as follows, shown here so that Mr. Whitlock's office can stress-test the assumptions independently.

**Naive calculation (full 18-point drop attributed to company action).**
Regional headcount: 742. Turnover reduction: 51% − 33% = 18 percentage points. Approximate drivers retained who would otherwise have separated: 0.18 × 742 ≈ 134 driver-years. Annual program cost: $3.4 million. Cost per retained driver-year: $3.4M ÷ 134 ≈ $25,400.

**Freight-cycle-adjusted calculation (only the residual above the OTR fleet's unintervened decline attributed to company action).**
OTR decline over the same window: 68% − 57% = 11 percentage points, treated as an approximation of the freight-cycle and macro-labor-market contribution to the regional fleet as well. Residual attributable to the pay conversion and referral bonus combined: 18 − 11 = 7 percentage points. Approximate drivers retained attributable to company action: 0.07 × 742 ≈ 52 driver-years. Cost per retained driver-year: $3.4M ÷ 52 ≈ $65,400.

Both calculations use the full $3.4 million program cost, which includes both the hourly-rate increase and the guaranteed detention pay, but does not include the separate cost of the referral bonus program, which is tracked and paid through the recruiting budget rather than the payroll conversion budget Mr. Whitlock asked us to evaluate. If the referral bonus payouts over the twelve-month after-period are added to the $3.4 million to give a fuller picture of total retention-related spend associated with the two simultaneous changes, the cost-per-retained-driver figures above would rise further under either calculation. We recommend that figure be pulled from recruiting's budget records and incorporated into the week 1–3 cohort analysis described above, since it directly affects the number the board will use to evaluate the OTR extension.

Applying the same conservative logic to the proposed OTR extension: if the $6.2 million rollout produced a turnover reduction proportionally similar to the conservative 7-point regional residual, scaled to the OTR fleet's much higher 68 percent starting baseline and its 310 drivers, the board should not assume the dollar-for-dollar return will match either the regional program's naive or conservative figures, since the OTR fleet's baseline turnover, driver pool, and job characteristics differ enough from the regional fleet that neither figure should be treated as a reliable forecast. This is the central reason we recommend a staged pilot rather than a fleet-wide commitment based on the regional results alone.
