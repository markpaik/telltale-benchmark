## 1. Executive Summary

Alvarado Family Markets is losing the fight it is currently in. Same-store sales have declined for six consecutive quarters, most recently by 3.1 percent, while two discount chains have opened eleven stores inside our trade areas since early 2024. We cannot win a price war against operators whose entire business model is built on cost structures we do not have and should not want. We can win a different fight — one built on fresh and prepared foods, convenience, and the local identity this family built over two generations — but only if we move now and move with conviction.

This proposal requests board approval of a $62.3 million program with two integrated components:

1. **Conversion of eleven stores** to a smaller-footprint, prepared-foods-forward format ("Alvarado Fresh Market") over 30 months, and
2. **Consolidation of our distribution network** from three points into a single 240,000-square-foot facility in Goshen, closing the Porterville and Lindsay distribution points, with the Goshen build-out including a central commissary that makes the store format economically viable.

The program would be funded without new equity: $40.0 million from an expansion of our revolving credit facility, negotiated in principle by CFO Tobias Renner with our existing bank group, and $22.3 million from a sale-leaseback of four owned store sites. Net staffing impact is positive — 19 salaried kitchen manager positions and approximately 240 hourly food-service positions added, against 61 warehouse positions eliminated at Porterville — but we do not minimize the seriousness of those 61 losses, and Section 7 sets out in detail how we propose to treat the people affected.

We are candid in this document about the 2024 prepared-foods pilot, which lost $2.4 million over fourteen months before it was discontinued. Section 4 explains what the pilot got wrong and why this program is structurally different. The short version: the pilot asked three stores to run scratch kitchens without central production, without dedicated management, and without a supply chain built for fresh. This program builds the supply chain first.

Timing is not discretionary. The lease option on the Goshen facility expires August 1, 2026. If we do not exercise it, the next comparable facility opportunity in our operating triangle is at least 18 to 24 months out, and the price of waiting is measured in continued same-store decline. The board is asked to approve the program, the financing, and the exercise of the Goshen option at the May 21 meeting.

Projected returns: $11.4 million in annual run-rate EBITDA improvement by fiscal 2029 ($7.6 million from converted-store performance, $3.8 million from distribution consolidation), a blended after-tax internal rate of return of approximately 14.2 percent, and cash payback in approximately 5.6 years.

## 2. State of the Business: Why Now

### 2.1 Where we stand

Alvarado Family Markets operates 34 stores across Tulare, Fresno, and Kern counties, employs 4,100 people, and recorded $538 million in sales in the last fiscal year. Those are the numbers of a healthy mid-size regional grocer. The trend lines underneath them are not healthy.

- **Same-store sales have declined for six consecutive quarters.** The most recent quarter was down 3.1 percent — the steepest decline in the sequence, not the shallowest. The trend is accelerating, not stabilizing.
- **Eleven competitive discount openings** in our trade areas since the first quarter of 2024. Two discount chains have targeted the southern San Joaquin Valley deliberately. Nine of our 34 stores now have a discount competitor within three miles; six of those nine account for over 70 percent of our total same-store decline.
- **Basket erosion, not traffic collapse.** Transaction counts across the chain are down only 0.9 percent; average basket is down 4.7 percent in affected trade areas. Our customers have not left us. They are splitting their shop — buying center-store staples at the discounters and coming to us for perishables, service departments, and fill-in trips. That split-shop behavior is the single most important fact in this document, because it tells us what customers still want from us.
- **Center-store gross profit is deteriorating fastest.** Dry grocery, the category where discounters are strongest and where we have the least differentiation, has lost 210 basis points of gross margin over eight quarters as we have matched key-value-item pricing to defend traffic.

### 2.2 The structural problem

We cannot out-price the discounters. Their model — limited assortment, minimal service, opportunistic buying, low-cost labor — produces retail prices on packaged goods 12 to 18 percent below ours on a comparable basket. Matching those prices across the center store would eliminate our operating margin entirely. Nor can we out-scale them: their parent organizations buy for hundreds or thousands of stores.

What we can do — what the split-shop data says our customers are already inviting us to do — is become the destination for the part of the trip the discounters cannot serve: fresh produce, quality meat and seafood, and above all, prepared and ready-to-eat foods. Prepared foods carry gross margins of 45 to 60 percent versus roughly 26 percent for center store, are effectively immune to discount price comparison, and match the demographic reality of our markets: dual-income households, agricultural shift workers, and a growing population of customers who want dinner solved, not dinner assembled.

Industry data reinforces this. Regional grocers who have repositioned toward fresh-and-prepared formats — in markets demographically comparable to ours — have generated same-store sales growth of 4 to 8 percent in converted stores against flat-to-declining conventional comparables, with prepared foods penetration rising from a typical 3 to 4 percent of sales toward 10 to 14 percent.

### 2.3 The distribution problem underneath the store problem

Our three distribution points — Porterville, Lindsay, and the leased overflow space near Visalia — are a legacy of acquisitions made in the 1990s and 2000s. Combined, they give us roughly 205,000 square feet of usable space across three inefficient buildings, none with adequate refrigerated capacity for a fresh-forward business, none with room for food production. Our internal cost studies show:

- Combined outbound distribution cost of $19.7 million annually, roughly 3.7 percent of sales — 60 to 80 basis points above the benchmark for a single modern facility serving our store count and geography.
- Redundant inventory across three points totaling approximately $4.1 million in excess working capital.
- Cold-chain capacity that already constrains our perishables business and cannot support a prepared-foods program at any scale. This constraint was a direct contributor to the 2024 pilot failure, discussed in Section 4.

The Goshen facility — 240,000 square feet, modern clear height, dock capacity for our full fleet, expandable refrigerated envelope, and 35,000 square feet suitable for commissary build-out — sits at the geographic center of gravity of our 34 stores, adjacent to Highway 99, twelve minutes from headquarters in Visalia. We hold a lease option that expires August 1, 2026. Facilities of this quality do not come available in our operating area often; our broker's survey identified no comparable alternative available within 24 months.

### 2.4 Why the decision cannot wait for the August board meeting

Three clocks are running:

1. **The Goshen option expires August 1.** The next regular board meeting after May 21 falls after the option expires. Deferring the decision is functionally deciding against the facility.
2. **Competitive intrusion is compounding.** Each quarter of delay at the current trajectory costs approximately $4.0 to $4.5 million in annualized sales and roughly $1.0 million in gross profit, and every quarter a discount store operates unanswered in a trade area, shopping habits harden further.
3. **The UFCW contract reopens in 2027.** We are far better positioned to negotiate the labor provisions this program requires — new food-service classifications, transfer rights, and severance terms — from a posture of growth and net job creation announced well in advance, than in the middle of a contract reopener with the workforce learning of closures from a bargaining table.

## 3. The Proposal

### 3.1 Component One: Alvarado Fresh Market — eleven store conversions

We propose converting eleven of our 34 stores to a smaller, prepared-foods-forward format over 30 months. The conversions reduce active selling footprint by an average of 22 percent per store (subleasing, demising, or converting surplus space to back-of-house production support), reallocating space and inventory dollars from underperforming center-store categories into:

- **A prepared-foods department** anchoring the front of the store: hot bar, grab-and-go meals, made-to-order stations at the six highest-volume conversions, and an expanded ready-to-heat case. Target: prepared foods at 11 to 13 percent of converted-store sales by month 18 post-conversion, versus 3.2 percent chainwide today.
- **Expanded produce** with a Valley-grown local program that leverages relationships this company has held with area growers for forty years — an asset no discount chain can replicate.
- **Full-service meat and seafood** with value-added (marinated, oven-ready) items produced at the Goshen commissary.
- **A rationalized center store**: roughly 30 percent fewer dry-grocery SKUs, concentrated on top-velocity items and our private label, priced competitively on a defined key-value-item list rather than across the board.

**Store selection.** The eleven stores were selected against four criteria: (a) trade-area demographics supporting prepared-foods demand (household income, dual-income share, daytime worker population); (b) direct discount-competitor exposure, where the format shift matters most; (c) physical suitability for kitchen and service infrastructure; and (d) lease or ownership terms permitting reconfiguration. Six of the eleven are among the nine stores facing direct discount competition. The store-by-store list, with trade-area analysis, is provided in the confidential appendix distributed under separate cover.

**Average conversion cost** is $3.35 million per store ($2.7 to $4.1 million range depending on store condition and kitchen scope), covering demolition and reconfiguration, refrigeration and hot-holding equipment, service-department build-out, décor and wayfinding, and point-of-sale changes. Stores remain open during conversion, executed in phased zones over 10 to 14 weeks per store, with a modeled sales disruption of 6 to 8 percent during the conversion window (included in the financial projections).

### 3.2 Component Two: Distribution consolidation and the Goshen commissary

We propose to exercise the Goshen lease option, fit out the facility over approximately ten months, and consolidate all distribution from Porterville, Lindsay, and the Visalia overflow space into Goshen by the end of 2027. The Goshen build-out includes:

- Ambient, refrigerated, and frozen distribution capacity sized for all 34 stores with 20 percent growth headroom;
- A **35,000-square-foot USDA/CDPH-compliant commissary** producing prepared meals, salads, sauces, value-added meats, and bakery components for daily delivery to the converted stores — the structural correction to the failed in-store production model of the 2024 pilot;
- Consolidated fleet operations, reducing total weekly route miles by an estimated 19 percent given Goshen's central position relative to the store network.

**Why the commissary is the linchpin.** In-store scratch production, as the pilot demonstrated, cannot achieve viable labor productivity or waste control at our store volumes. Central production shifts roughly 60 percent of prepared-foods labor hours into one facility where they are scheduled efficiently, moves food-safety compliance into one controlled environment, and drops in-store shrink on prepared items from the pilot's 9.1 percent to a modeled 3.5 percent. Stores finish, merchandise, and serve; Goshen produces. The eleven converted stores provide commissary volume sufficient to reach production break-even at approximately month 14 of commissary operation; capacity exists to serve additional conversions in a potential second phase, which would come to this board as a separate future decision.

**Distribution economics.** Consolidation is projected to reduce annual distribution operating cost by $3.8 million at run rate (labor efficiency, elimination of inter-facility shuttles, route optimization, and exit of the Visalia overflow lease at $640,000 per year), and to release approximately $4.1 million of duplicated inventory as one-time working capital. These savings stand on their own: the consolidation clears its cost of capital even before any credit for enabling the store format.

### 3.3 What this program is not

This is not a bet-the-company transformation of all 34 stores, and it is not a permanent verdict on the other 23. The remaining stores continue in the current format with the benefit of lower distribution costs and selective assortment learnings. A decision on any second conversion wave would come to the board no earlier than the fourth quarter of 2027, with twelve-plus months of converted-store results in hand.

## 4. The 2024 Pilot: What Went Wrong and What Is Different

The board's memory of the 2024 prepared-foods pilot — three stores, $2.4 million lost over fourteen months — is the right memory to bring to this proposal. We asked for and received a candid post-mortem, conducted with an outside food-service consultancy in early 2025. Its findings shape every element of this program. We owe the board a direct accounting.

**What the pilot got wrong:**

1. **In-store scratch production with no central supply.** Each pilot store ran its own kitchen, its own recipes in practice if not on paper, and its own ordering. Labor cost ran 38 percent of prepared-foods sales against a viable target under 25 percent. There was no volume over which to spread skilled labor.
2. **No dedicated management.** Kitchen oversight was assigned to deli managers as an additional duty, with no culinary training and no accountability separate from their existing departments. Quality and consistency varied day to day and store to store — and customers noticed. Repeat purchase rates fell after the first eight weeks.
3. **Wrong stores.** The three pilot sites were chosen substantially for available back-room space rather than trade-area demand. Two of the three sat in trade areas with below-median daytime populations and weak lunch traffic.
4. **A sprawling menu.** Over 90 rotating items, which multiplied waste. Shrink on prepared items averaged 9.1 percent; several categories exceeded 15 percent.
5. **No demand forecasting.** Production quantities were set by manager judgment. We systematically overproduced early in the week and stocked out on weekends.
6. **Undercapitalized presentation.** The pilot spent an average of $410,000 per store — equipment dropped into unrenovated stores. The offer never looked like a reason to change shopping habits, and marketing support was minimal.

**How this program answers each failure:**

| Pilot failure | This program |
|---|---|
| In-store scratch production | Central commissary at Goshen produces ~60% of labor content; stores finish and serve |
| No dedicated management | 19 professional kitchen managers hired, dedicated role, culinary hiring profile, structured 6-week training |
| Wrong stores | Eleven stores selected on demand-side criteria: demographics, traffic, competitive exposure |
| Sprawling menu | Disciplined core menu of ~45 items plus limited seasonal rotation; every item margin- and waste-tested at commissary scale |
| No forecasting | Production planning and demand forecasting system included in the IT budget; daily store-level ordering against commissary production |
| Undercapitalized presentation | Full-store repositioning at $3.35M average, with a dedicated launch marketing budget per store |

One further difference deserves emphasis: **sequencing.** The pilot put stores in front of supply chain. This program builds Goshen and the commissary first; no store converts until central production is operational and validated. The first three conversions function as a controlled proof wave, with defined performance gates (Section 8) before the remaining eight proceed.

The pilot cost $2.4 million. Treated properly, it was tuition. This proposal is the application of what it taught.

The program runs 30 months from approval, May 2026 through November 2028.

- By June 15: Exercise Goshen lease option; execute credit facility amendment; sign sale-leaseback purchase agreements.

- April 2027: **Lindsay distribution point closes**; volume fully absorbed by Goshen.
- May 2027: Commissary commissioning and production validation.
- June 2027: Visalia overflow lease exited.

**Phase 2 — Proof wave and Porterville transition (April 2027–January 2028)**
- April–September 2027: **Wave 1 store conversions (3 stores)**, sequenced so the first store opens as commissary production comes online. First converted store reopens July 2027.
- October–December 2027: **Porterville distribution point winds down in stages**, closing fully by December 31, 2027 — a deliberate 19-month runway from approval, the longest we can operationally justify, chosen to maximize transfer, notice, and transition time for the 61 affected employees (Section 7).

**Phase 3 — Full rollout (January–November 2028)**
- January–June 2028: **Wave 2 conversions (4 stores)**.
- May–November 2028: **Wave 3 conversions (4 stores)**, final store reopening by November 2028 — month 30.

Two stores are under conversion at any given time at peak, deliberately limiting operational strain and construction-management load. The schedule carries approximately six weeks of float in each phase.

## 7. Staffing: Who Is Added, Who Is Affected, and How They Are Treated

### 7.1 Positions added

Under Mr. Baek's plan:

- **19 salaried kitchen managers** — a new professional classification, one per converted store plus eight commissary production supervisors and leads. Recruiting draws on culinary programs at College of the Sequoias and Fresno City College and on regional food-service management talent. Compensation is benchmarked to food-service management, not grocery department management — a pilot lesson.
- **Approximately 240 hourly food-service positions** across the eleven stores and the commissary: cooks, prep, service counter, and food-safety-certified leads. All store hourly positions are UFCW bargaining-unit roles; we will negotiate the new classifications with the union in advance of the 2027 reopener.
- **Approximately 25 to 30 net new Goshen warehouse and transportation positions** relative to the consolidated Porterville/Lindsay baseline after efficiency gains — Goshen's total staffing will be roughly 155 against a combined current 190 across three points.

Net program employment effect: approximately **+200 positions** company-wide.

### 7.2 Positions eliminated, and our obligations to those affected

**Sixty-one warehouse positions at Porterville are eliminated.** We state that plainly because the people affected deserve plain dealing, and because Chair Alvarado and her brother have raised principled objections to this closure that the whole board should weigh with the facts in front of it.

Our commitments, which we ask the board to adopt as conditions of approval:

1. **Right of first offer at Goshen.** Every Porterville warehouse employee receives a guaranteed offer of a Goshen position at equal or better classification and rate, seniority intact, before any external hiring. Goshen is 28 miles from Porterville. Based on confidential workforce surveys Mr. Achterberg's team conducted in April, we project 30 to 35 of the 61 will accept transfer.
2. **Store placement.** Employees declining Goshen receive priority placement into open store positions, including the new food-service roles, with paid retraining. The Porterville *store* — which is not closing and is not among the eleven conversions in this phase — will hold its next twelve months of hourly openings for Porterville warehouse employees first.
3. **Enhanced severance** for those not placed: two weeks per year of service (double the current contractual minimum), six months of continued health coverage, and funded outplacement — budgeted within the $2.4 million closure line.
4. **Retention pay** through the December 2027 closure date, so no one is penalized for staying to the end, and 19 months of notice — announced within days of board approval, not weeks before closure.
5. **UFCW engagement now.** We will open effects bargaining in June 2026, thirteen months before contract reopener discussions begin. Our labor counsel's judgment is that a package of 240 new bargaining-unit jobs, guaranteed transfer rights, and enhanced severance is a strong-faith opening position that protects the 2027 negotiation rather than poisoning it.

On the substance of the Porterville objection: we have examined every configuration that keeps Porterville open, including running it as a satellite fresh depot. Each alternative strands between $1.9 and $2.6 million of annual cost, splits the cold chain the commissary depends on, and — the post-mortem's hardest lesson — recreates the fragmented supply structure that helped kill the pilot. The Porterville facility is 61 years old, has 24-foot clear height against Goshen's 36, and would itself require an estimated $4 to 5 million in deferred capital within five years merely to remain code-compliant. Keeping it open does not preserve those 61 jobs indefinitely; it defers a harder version of this decision to a weaker moment. What we can honorably do — and what this plan does — is give every one of those 61 people a real path to continued employment with this company, on the longest runway we can provide.

The Porterville community keeps its Alvarado store, gains priority hiring consideration at Goshen 28 miles away, and we propose maintaining the company's charitable commitments in Porterville — including the scholarship program the founder established there — without reduction.

## 8. Alternatives Considered

The board should approve this program only against a fair reading of the alternatives. We evaluated four.

### 8.1 Stand pat (status quo, with tactical price investment)

Continue the current format, defend with promotional pricing and cost discipline. **Rejected.** The six-quarter trend, projected forward at even a moderating rate of decline, takes same-store sales down a cumulative 9 to 12 percent over three years and compresses EBITDA from approximately $21 million today to $12 to 14 million by fiscal 2029 — before the deferred capital that Porterville and Lindsay will demand regardless. Standing pat is not the low-risk option; it is a slow liquidation of competitive position that forecloses future options as the balance sheet weakens. It also surrenders the Goshen facility permanently.

### 8.2 Price-led counterattack

Reposition as a price competitor: cut retails 8 to 10 percent across center store, strip service departments, and cut labor to fund it. **Rejected.** It fights the discounters on their terrain with their weapons and our cost structure. Modeling shows the gross-profit sacrifice ($14 to 16 million annually) exceeds any plausible volume recapture, and it destroys the service identity that is our only durable differentiation. No regional grocer of our scale has won this fight.

### 8.3 Third-party prepared-foods fulfillment

Execute the format conversion but source prepared foods from a third-party commissary or co-packer rather than building our own, and either keep the current distribution network or consolidate distribution without the commissary. **Seriously considered; rejected.** We solicited proposals from three regional food manufacturers and one national fresh-foods co-packer. Findings:

- Landed cost of goods runs 11 to 14 points higher than self-produced at our projected volumes, cutting the prepared-foods contribution margin roughly in half and pushing store-level payback past nine years.
- Minimum order quantities and 48–72-hour lead times are incompatible with daily-fresh merchandising; the two viable suppliers are 140 and 190 miles away.
- We would surrender recipe control, local sourcing, and the "made here in the Valley" identity that is the marketing spine of the format.
- Contract terms (3-year minimums with volume commitments) create dependency without ownership of the capability.

Third-party supply remains a useful *supplement* — we will buy, not make, roughly 20 percent of the prepared assortment (breads, select desserts, specialty items) — but as the backbone it fails on cost, freshness, and strategy. It would, however, remain our fallback if the commissary underperformed (Section 9), which is itself an argument that the risk of self-production is bounded.

### 8.4 Distribution consolidation only, no store conversions

Take the Goshen facility and the $3.8 million of distribution savings; defer the format question. **Rejected as a standalone, but it is embedded in this program's structure.** Consolidation alone is a sound cost project that does nothing about the demand problem — the same-store decline continues, and we hold a modern facility serving a shrinking business. That said, the board should note that this program is *sequenced* so that the consolidation completes first and stands on its own economics; the performance gate after Wave 1 (Section 9.1) means the board effectively retains the option to halt at something close to this alternative if the store format disappoints, having lost only the Wave 1 conversion capital relative to it.

## 9. Risks and Mitigations

We present the six material risks as we see them, with mitigations, and we invite the board's challenge on each.

### 9.1 Risk: The converted stores underperform — the pilot repeats

*Likelihood: the board's central concern. Consequence if unmitigated: high.*

**Mitigations:** Every structural cause of the pilot failure is corrected (Section 4), but we do not ask the board to take that on faith. The program is built with a **hard performance gate**: Wave 2 and Wave 3 capital (approximately $26.8 million of the $36.9 million store budget) is released only after the January 2028 board review of Wave 1 results against pre-committed thresholds — prepared-foods penetration of at least 8 percent of store sales by month 5, prepared-foods shrink below 5 percent, converted-store same-store sales at least 400 basis points better than the non-converted control group, and store-level labor within 110 percent of model. If Wave 1 misses, the board can halt with roughly $10 to 11 million of store capital deployed, the distribution consolidation intact and paying its own way, and three stores that are improved even if the full format thesis fails. Maximum plausible loss in that scenario is a fraction of the program total, against a status-quo path that loses more with certainty.

### 9.2 Risk: Goshen transition disrupts store supply

*A botched distribution cutover would damage all 34 stores, not just eleven.*

**Mitigations:** Phased migration by temperature zone over four months rather than a single cutover; Porterville deliberately kept running through December 2027 as the safety net during and after Goshen ramp-up; warehouse management system installed and stress-tested in parallel operation before Lindsay closes; retention bonuses keeping experienced Porterville and Lindsay staff through transition; two weeks of elevated store safety stock during each migration stage. Mr. Achterberg has executed two facility consolidations in prior roles; the integration plan has been reviewed by an outside logistics engineering firm whose report is available to directors.

### 9.3 Risk: Labor relations deteriorate ahead of the 2027 reopener

**Mitigations:** Effects bargaining opened thirteen months early; a package that is net-positive for the bargaining unit (+240 unit jobs against −61, with guaranteed transfer rights and enhanced severance); new food-service classifications negotiated cooperatively rather than imposed; consistent, early, honest communication directly to affected employees before rumors do the work for us. The residual risk is real — the reopener will be harder than a normal one regardless — but announcing growth and protections in 2026 is a far better entry to 2027 than announcing closures in 2027.

### 9.4 Risk: Financing stress — covenant breach in a downside scenario

**Mitigations:** The downside case (Wave 1 at 60 percent of projected prepared-foods sales, distribution savings at 75 percent of plan, construction 10 percent over budget) keeps peak leverage at 3.62x against the 3.75x covenant — thin but intact — and the performance gate caps further capital outflow precisely in the scenarios where covenants would tighten. Additional dry powder: the uncounted $4.1 million working-capital release, the ability to defer Wave 3 by up to two quarters within the credit agreement, and unencumbered real estate under the majority of the chain. Mr. Renner will walk the board through the full sensitivity table in executive session.

### 9.5 Risk: Construction cost inflation and schedule slippage

**Mitigations:** Guaranteed-maximum-price contracts for the Goshen fit-out and each conversion wave; long-lead refrigeration equipment ordered at program start for all eleven stores at locked pricing; $2.2 million program contingency held centrally; six weeks of float per phase; the Wave structure itself, which lets later stores absorb earlier lessons and rebids.

### 9.6 Risk: Competitive response — the discounters escalate

**Mitigations:** Bluntly, the discounters' escalation is already underway and priced into our base case; eleven stores in two years is the escalation. The format shift moves the fight to categories where their model structurally cannot follow — they do not run kitchens, service counters, or local-grower programs, and their national formats do not flex to do so. If they respond with deeper center-store pricing, our rationalized center store and key-value-item strategy limit our exposure precisely because we will have stopped trying to match them item-for-item across 25,000 SKUs.

**A risk we accept openly:** execution capacity. This program will consume the attention of this management team for 30 months. We have structured it — a dedicated program office reporting to the President, outside construction management, no more than two simultaneous conversions, and the phase gates — so that the base business is never unattended. But the board should approve this knowing it is the company's principal undertaking through 2028, and hold us to that standard.

## 10. Governance, Oversight, and Reporting

If approved, we propose the following oversight structure, and ask the board to adopt it as part of the resolution:
