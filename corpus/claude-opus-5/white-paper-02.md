## Abstract

Regional grantmakers have spent two decades debating whether restricted project support undermines the organizations it funds. The debate has been conducted largely on anecdote. This paper brings evidence: grant-level records on 9,100 awards totaling $1.9 billion made by members of our association between 2016 and 2024, audited financial statements for 620 grantees, and a 2025 survey of 480 nonprofit executive directors in our service region.

The findings are consistent across all three data sources. Grantees receiving multiyear unrestricted support held a median of 4.8 months of operating reserve; project-restricted grantees held 1.9 months. Annual executive turnover was 14 percent in the first group and 26 percent in the second. Across the whole portfolio, funders allowed an average indirect cost recovery of 11.4 percent against audited true indirect of 26.8 percent — a structural shortfall of roughly 15 points that grantees absorb through deferred maintenance, unfunded reserves, and compensation below market. Executive directors reported a median of 71 hours per year of reporting labor for each restricted grant they hold. Forty-one percent of surveyed organizations hold under three months of operating reserve, in a region where state contract payments are running 94 days behind schedule.

We compare five practices now in use among members — multiyear general operating support, tiered trust-based grantmaking, project grants paying full indirect, participatory pooled funds, and recoverable grants — on cost, administrative burden, evidentiary yield, and fit with donor intent. We then set out a three-year transition model for a foundation with a $40 million annual payout, with staffing, board policy changes, cost estimates, and risk controls. We close by answering the three objections trustees raise most often: accountability, donor intent, and small grantee capacity. None of the three, in our reading of the evidence, survives contact with the data as an argument for the status quo. Each points instead toward specific design choices within a reformed practice.

## 1. The Problem in the Field

### 1.1 A grantmaking architecture built for a different balance sheet

The dominant instrument of American institutional philanthropy — the one-year, project-restricted grant with a capped indirect allowance and a narrative report — was consolidated in the 1970s and 1980s. It assumed a nonprofit sector in which government contracts paid on time, individual giving was broad and growing, and a foundation's chief risk was that its money would be spent on something the donor had not intended. Under those conditions, restriction was a reasonable form of stewardship, and the administrative cost of restriction fell on organizations that could absorb it.

The balance sheet has changed. In our region, state contract payments run 94 days behind on average, meaning that a human services provider with a $3 million state contract is effectively extending an unsecured $770,000 line of credit to the state at all times. Individual giving has concentrated at the top of the income distribution and thinned in the middle. Federal support has become more volatile and more frequently reimbursement-based, which pushes working capital requirements up rather than down. Meanwhile the instrument has not changed. Foundations continue to fund projects, cap indirect at levels set by convention rather than analysis, and require reporting priced as though staff time were free.

The result is a sector that looks solvent on an income statement and is fragile on a balance sheet. Forty-one percent of the organizations we surveyed hold less than three months of operating reserve — the threshold below which a single delayed payment or a single unexpected departure becomes an existential event rather than a管理 problem. This is not a story about mismanagement. It is a story about a funding architecture that systematically prices the cost of doing work below its actual cost and then treats the resulting thinness as evidence of the grantee's weakness.

### 1.2 Why the problem is invisible to funders

Three features of standard practice keep the problem out of view.

First, **restriction hides overhead rather than eliminating it.** When a funder caps indirect at 10 percent, the grantee does not reduce its true indirect cost to 10 percent. It reclassifies. Development staff time becomes "program outreach." The finance director's time becomes "program evaluation." The organization presents a compliant budget and absorbs the gap elsewhere. The funder observes a 10 percent overhead ratio and concludes that the cap was reasonable.

Second, **the reporting burden is borne where the funder cannot see it.** A program officer reading a six-page report experiences it as six pages. The executive director who produced it, and who produced eleven others that year in eleven different formats, experiences it as a substantial fraction of a senior staff position.

Third, **grantee silence is rational.** Executive directors do not tell funders that a grant is underwater. Our survey achieved candor because it was anonymous, administered by a research unit rather than a grantmaking one, and explicitly firewalled from member funders. Sixty-eight percent of respondents reported that they had accepted at least one grant in the prior three years that they knew would lose money, because declining would damage a relationship or forfeit a renewal.

The field's diagnostic instruments are therefore systematically biased toward complacency. What follows is an attempt to look past them.

## 2. Data and Method

### 2.1 Sources

**Grant-level administrative records.** Members contributed structured records on 9,100 awards totaling $1.9 billion, made between January 2016 and December 2024. Records include award amount, term, restriction type, allowed indirect rate, reporting requirements, and renewal outcome. Coverage is strongest among the 140 members with professional staff and weakest among small family funds; we discuss the resulting bias in Section 2.3.

**Audited financial statements.** We obtained and normalized audited statements for 620 grantee organizations, covering fiscal years 2019 through 2024. Normalization involved reconstructing functional expense allocations to a common basis so that indirect cost could be compared across organizations with different accounting conventions. This is the most labor-intensive component of the study and the source of our true-indirect estimate.

**Executive director survey, 2025.** A 62-item instrument administered to 1,340 executive directors of organizations that had received at least one member grant since 2020. We received 480 usable responses, a 35.8 percent response rate. The instrument covered reserve position, reporting labor, indirect experience, turnover, and grant-acceptance decisions.

### 2.2 Definitions

- **Multiyear unrestricted support:** general operating support committed for three or more years with no project restriction and no annual re-application.
- **Project-restricted:** support tied to a defined activity, with budget line-item constraints and a required accounting of expenditure against those lines.
- **True indirect:** total cost of organizational infrastructure — finance, human resources, technology, facilities, executive leadership, compliance, fundraising — expressed as a percentage of total direct program cost, reconstructed on a common basis from audited statements.
- **Operating reserve:** unrestricted net assets available for operations, expressed in months of average monthly operating expense.
- **Reporting labor:** self-reported staff hours per grant per year devoted to interim and final reporting, including data collection, drafting, internal review, and funder-requested revisions.

### 2.3 Limitations

Three limitations bear on interpretation.

**Selection.** Funders that award multiyear unrestricted support may select stronger organizations. Better-capitalized grantees may attract unrestricted support rather than being produced by it. We address this in Section 3.4 with a within-grantee analysis on the subset of 214 organizations whose funding mix changed materially during the study period, which substantially narrows but does not eliminate the concern.

**Survey non-response.** Our 35.8 percent response rate is respectable for this population but leaves room for bias. Comparison of respondents to the full sample frame on observable characteristics — budget size, subsector, county — shows respondents slightly overrepresent organizations with budgets between $1 million and $10 million and slightly underrepresent organizations under $250,000.

**Reporting-hour self-report.** Hours are recalled rather than logged. We asked for hours per grant rather than total hours to reduce recall error, and we winsorized at the 5th and 95th percentiles. The direction of any residual bias is unclear; executive directors may overstate burden from frustration or understate it because they have naturalized the work.

## 3. What the Data Show

### 3.1 Reserve position and restriction type

The clearest finding in the study is the relationship between funding structure and balance-sheet strength.

**Table 1. Grantee financial and organizational indicators by dominant funding structure**

| Indicator | Multiyear unrestricted (n=186) | Mixed (n=248) | Project-restricted (n=186) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Median operating reserve (months) | 4.8 | 3.1 | 1.9 |
| Share below 3 months of reserve | 19% | 38% | 62% |
| Annual executive turnover | 14% | 19% | 26% |
| Median reporting hours per grant/year | 22 | 48 | 71 |
| Median allowed indirect rate | 100%* | 14.0% | 9.5% |
| Share reporting a deficit in ≥2 of 5 years | 21% | 33% | 44% |

*General operating support does not distinguish direct from indirect; the grantee allocates.
Source: Headwaters Philanthropy Lab analysis of 620 audited financial statements and 480 survey responses, 2025.

The reserve gap — 4.8 months against 1.9 — is 2.9 months of operating expense. For a $5 million organization, that is approximately $1.2 million in balance-sheet strength. The turnover gap is nearly as consequential: at 26 percent annual executive turnover, an organization changes chief executives roughly every four years, which in practice means it is perpetually either recruiting, onboarding, or losing institutional memory.

### 3.2 The indirect cost gap

**Table 2. Allowed versus audited true indirect cost, by grantee budget size**

| Grantee budget | Mean allowed indirect | Mean audited true indirect | Gap (points) | Implied annual shortfall per $1M of grant revenue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Under $500K | 9.8% | 31.2% | 21.4 | $214,000 |
| $500K–$2M | 10.6% | 28.4% | 17.8 | $178,000 |
| $2M–$10M | 11.9% | 26.1% | 14.2 | $142,000 |
| $10M–$50M | 12.8% | 24.3% | 11.5 | $115,000 |
| Over $50M | 13.4% | 22.9% | 9.5 | $95,000 |
| **Portfolio average** | **11.4%** | **26.8%** | **15.4** | **$154,000** |

Source: Headwaters Philanthropy Lab analysis of 620 audited financial statements, FY2019–FY2024, normalized to common functional allocation basis.

Two features of Table 2 deserve emphasis.

First, the gap is largest for the smallest organizations. This is the opposite of what capped-indirect policy assumes. Small organizations have less ability to spread fixed infrastructure cost across a large program base, so their true indirect ratio is higher, not lower. A uniform 10 percent cap is therefore most punitive where capacity is thinnest — precisely the organizations trustees cite when they worry about small grantee capacity (see Section 6.3).

Second, the shortfall is not hypothetical. It appears on grantee balance sheets as unfunded depreciation, deferred technology replacement, compensation set below market, and absent reserves. In the audited statements we reviewed, organizations in the lowest reserve quartile showed a median of 11 years since their last substantive technology infrastructure investment.

The median organization in our survey held 7.2 restricted grants. At 71 hours each, that is 511 hours annually — roughly one quarter of a full-time senior position — spent producing documents for funders. Priced at a loaded senior-staff rate of $65 per hour, the median organization spends approximately $33,000 per year on funder reporting. For organizations under $2 million in budget, this expense typically exceeds their entire annual technology budget.

Critically, 74 percent of surveyed executive directors reported that they had never received substantive feedback on a grant report, and 81 percent reported that no funder had ever asked a follow-up question that changed the organization's practice. The reporting labor is real; the learning yield is largely unrealized.

### 3.4 Addressing the selection concern

The correlations in Table 1 could reflect selection rather than effect. We therefore examined 214 organizations whose funding mix shifted by at least 30 percentage points during the study period — either toward or away from unrestricted support — and tracked their indicators before and after.

**Table 3. Within-grantee change following material shift in funding mix (n=214)**

| Direction of shift | n | Δ reserve months (3 yr) | Δ executive turnover | Δ reporting hours |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Toward unrestricted (≥30 pt increase) | 118 | +1.9 | −8 pts | −187 hrs |
| Toward restricted (≥30 pt increase) | 96 | −1.4 | +6 pts | +214 hrs |

Source: Headwaters Philanthropy Lab analysis of audited financial statements, matched panel, FY2019–FY2024.

Organizations that gained unrestricted support built reserves; organizations that lost it drew them down. The effect is asymmetric — building takes longer than depleting — which is consistent with the finding that reserve accumulation is slow and reserve consumption is fast. This analysis does not eliminate selection concerns entirely, since funders may shift toward unrestricted support for organizations they perceive as improving. But it makes pure selection an implausible full explanation for a 2.9-month reserve gap.

## 4. Comparing Five Practices

Five practices are in active use among our 310 members. We compare them here on the dimensions trustees actually weigh.

### 4.1 Multiyear general operating support

Three-to-five-year unrestricted commitments, typically with a single annual touchpoint and no re-application. Among members, average award size is $180,000 per year over four years.

**Strengths.** The only instrument in our data that reliably builds reserves. Lowest grantee administrative burden (median 22 hours per year). Lowest funder processing cost per dollar deployed — roughly $0.9 per $100 granted in our member sample, against $2.4 for project grants. Permits genuine strategy on the grantee side, since the organization can plan against known revenue.

**Weaknesses.** Reduces funder optionality; capital is committed and cannot be redeployed if priorities shift. Requires more rigorous front-end diligence, since the funder cannot course-correct mid-stream. Produces less granular attribution — the funder cannot say which outcome its dollars purchased. Creates a renewal cliff: organizations that build a cost structure around a four-year commitment face acute risk at term end if the funder exits.

**Best fit.** Established organizations with clear mission alignment, where the funder's theory of change is "this organization should exist and be strong" rather than "this specific activity should happen."

### 4.2 Tiered trust-based grantmaking

Grantees are sorted into tiers by relationship depth and award size, with due diligence and reporting scaled accordingly. A typical member design: Tier 1 (under $50,000) requires a two-page letter and a phone call; Tier 2 ($50,000–$250,000) adds financial review and a site visit; Tier 3 (over $250,000) adds full diligence and a structured learning conversation.

**Strengths.** The most administratively efficient design we observe, because it stops applying $30,000 of diligence to $25,000 grants. Members who adopted tiering reduced average time-to-decision from 5.1 months to 2.3 months. It is also the most politically tractable reform, since it does not require abandoning restriction — only proportioning oversight to stake.

**Weaknesses.** Tier assignment is discretionary and can encode existing relationship bias, advantaging organizations already known to the foundation. Requires clear written criteria and periodic audit to avoid becoming a mechanism for insider advantage. Does not by itself address the indirect gap.

**Best fit.** Foundations with a wide grant-size distribution and a large tail of small awards. This is the highest-return first move for most members.

### 4.3 Project grants paying full indirect

Conventional project restriction, but with indirect calculated at the grantee's audited federal negotiated rate or a documented equivalent — typically 24 to 30 percent rather than 10 percent.

**Strengths.** Preserves donor-intent specificity and attribution completely, while removing the structural subsidy. Requires no change in board policy on restriction, only on rate. Politically the easiest reform to explain to a skeptical trustee: "We are paying the true cost of the work we asked for." Well suited to funders with statutory or donor-document constraints on unrestricted giving.

**Weaknesses.** Does not reduce reporting burden — a full-indirect project grant still generates the same 71 hours. Does not build unrestricted reserves, since the indirect recovery covers cost rather than creating surplus. Reduces the number of projects fundable at a given payout by roughly 13 to 15 percent, which trustees experience as a loss of reach.

**Best fit.** Donor-restricted funds and corporate programs with defined-purpose mandates. Also the correct instrument where the funder genuinely wants a specific thing built.

### 4.4 Participatory pooled funds

Multiple funders contribute to a common pool; allocation decisions are made by a body that includes people with lived experience of the issue, often alongside funder representatives. Among members, pool sizes range from $400,000 to $11 million annually.

**Strengths.** Reaches organizations invisible to conventional pipelines — 44 percent of pooled-fund grantees in our data had no prior institutional foundation relationship. Distributes the diligence cost across contributors. Generates intelligence about a field that no single funder holds. Materially improves the legitimacy of allocation decisions in contested domains.

**Weaknesses.** High governance cost: setup typically consumes 9 to 14 months and $120,000 to $250,000 before the first dollar moves. Decision-maker compensation, facilitation, and translation are real recurring expenses (members report 8 to 12 percent of pool value). Attribution to any individual funder is weak, which complicates donor reporting. Requires a foundation to accept that some allocations will not match its own priorities.

**Best fit.** A defined slice of portfolio — typically 5 to 15 percent — where the funder's own knowledge is weakest and community knowledge is strongest.

### 4.5 Recoverable grants

Capital advanced with an expectation, but not a legal obligation, of return if a defined condition is met. Common uses in our region: bridging delayed government contract payments, pre-development costs on capital projects, and earned-revenue launches.

**Strengths.** Directly addresses the 94-day state payment lag, which is a liquidity problem rather than a solvency problem. Recovered capital revolves, so a $4 million facility can deploy $9 to $12 million over a decade. Signals confidence in the grantee's business model.

**Weaknesses.** Not a substitute for operating support; it addresses timing, not adequacy. Requires financial underwriting capability most foundation program staff do not have. Recovery rates in our member data average 71 percent, meaning the instrument must be budgeted with a 29 percent loss assumption. Creates a quasi-creditor relationship that can distort the grantee's candor about difficulty.

**Best fit.** Grantees with reliable but slow receivables. A small, specialized facility rather than a portfolio-wide practice.

### 4.6 Synthesis

**Table 4. Comparative assessment of five practices**

| Practice | Grantee admin burden | Funder cost per $100 | Builds reserves | Attribution strength | Donor-intent fit | Setup difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Multiyear GOS | Very low | $0.90 | Strong | Weak | Moderate | Low |
| Tiered trust-based | Low | $1.10 | Moderate | Moderate | High | Low |
| Full-indirect project | High | $2.10 | Neutral | Very strong | Very high | Low |
| Participatory pooled | Moderate | $3.40 | Moderate | Very weak | Low | High |

The practices are complements, not substitutes. The evidence does not support replacing a portfolio with any single instrument. It supports a portfolio in which the *default* instrument is multiyear general operating support delivered through tiered processes, with full-indirect project grants used where specificity is genuinely required, a pooled allocation for domains where the foundation's knowledge is weak, and a small recoverable facility for liquidity.

## 5. A Three-Year Transition Model: $40 Million Annual Payout

The following model assumes a foundation with $40 million in annual qualifying distributions, roughly 260 grants averaging $154,000, twelve program staff, four finance and grants-management staff, and a nine-member board meeting quarterly.

### 5.1 Target portfolio

**Table 5. Portfolio composition, baseline and target**

| Instrument | Baseline (Year 0) | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 (target) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Multiyear general operating support | $4.0M (10%) | $12.0M (30%) | $20.0M (50%) | $24.0M (60%) |
| Full-indirect project grants | $2.0M (5%) | $8.0M (20%) | $10.0M (25%) | $8.8M (22%) |
| Capped-indirect project grants | $32.0M (80%) | $17.6M (44%) | $6.0M (15%) | $0 (0%) |
| Participatory pooled fund | $0 | $1.2M (3%) | $2.4M (6%) | $4.0M (10%) |
| Recoverable grant facility | $0 | $0.8M (2%) | $1.2M (3%) | $2.0M (5%) |
| Capacity/transition set-aside | $2.0M (5%) | $0.4M (1%) | $0.4M (1%) | $1.2M (3%) |
| **Total** | **$40.0M** | **$40.0M** | **$40.0M** | **$40.0M** |

### 5.2 Year One: policy, pilots, and honest accounting

**Board actions required in Year One.** Six policy changes, all requiring formal board adoption:

1. **Indirect cost policy.** Replace the capped rate with a policy paying the grantee's federally negotiated indirect rate where one exists, or a documented de minimis rate of 25 percent where it does not. Eliminate line-item budget approval below $250,000.
2. **Multiyear authority.** Delegate authority to commit up to four years of support, with a board-approved aggregate multiyear commitment ceiling (we recommend 65 percent of projected payout in any future year, preserving flexibility).
3. **Tiering policy.** Adopt written diligence and reporting tiers with dollar thresholds and explicit criteria, plus an annual audit of tier assignment for relationship bias.
4. **Reporting policy.** Cap required reporting at one conversation plus the grantee's existing audited financials and board-approved narrative for Tier 1 and 2 awards. Accept documents grantees already produce.
5. **Exit policy.** Require 18 months' notice and a declining tail (100/60/30 percent over three years) for any non-renewal of multiyear support, addressing the renewal-cliff risk identified in Section 4.1.
6. **Pooled fund charter.** Establish scope, decision-maker composition, compensation, and the foundation's own recusal from allocation votes.

**Operational work in Year One.** Rebuild the application to collect organizational financial health rather than project budgets. Retire the custom reporting template. Renegotiate existing multi-grant relationships onto general operating support at renewal rather than mid-term. Launch a $1.2 million pooled fund in one issue area with two co-funders. Underwrite and capitalize an $800,000 recoverable facility for state-contract bridging.

**Portfolio effect.** The shift of $8 million from capped to full-indirect project support does not increase grantee revenue in nominal terms; it reduces the number of funded projects by roughly 13 percent while eliminating the subsidy on those that remain. Trustees should expect and pre-authorize this reduction in count. Presenting it as a loss of reach without explaining that the prior reach was financed by grantee balance sheets is the single most common failure mode in this transition.

### 5.3 Year Two: scale and staffing realignment

Year Two is where staffing changes bind. The transition does not reduce total staff need; it changes its composition.

**Table 6. Staffing model, baseline and target**

| Function | Baseline FTE | Year 3 FTE | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Program officers (portfolio review) | 8.0 | 5.0 | −3.0 |
| Grants management / compliance processing | 4.0 | 2.0 | −2.0 |
| Financial analysis and underwriting | 0.0 | 2.0 | +2.0 |
| Learning and evaluation | 1.0 | 2.5 | +1.5 |

The critical point for trustees: three program officer positions and two grants-management positions are not eliminated but *converted*. Reviewing 260 project budgets annually requires eight program officers. Stewarding 90 multiyear relationships and underwriting a recoverable facility requires five program officers and two financial analysts. The skill mix differs — the foundation needs people who can read an audited financial statement, not people who can reconcile a line-item budget. Two of the eight incumbent program officers in our member cases successfully transitioned into analyst roles with training; the rest required either redeployment or turnover.

Transition costs are administrative expenses, not qualifying distributions, and must be budgeted against the foundation's operating budget rather than its payout. A foundation with a $40 million payout typically runs a $4.5 to $6 million administrative budget; $1.055 million in Year One represents an 18 to 23 percent increase in that budget for one year. This is the single largest financial obstacle to the transition and should be presented to the board explicitly, ideally with a one-time draw from unrestricted reserves rather than a permanent expense-ratio increase.

### 5.4 Year Three: stabilization and risk controls

By Year Three the portfolio has reached target composition and the work shifts to control design.

**Portfolio concentration limits.** No single grantee above 4 percent of annual payout. No single subsector above 30 percent. Aggregate multiyear commitments capped at 65 percent of projected payout in any forward year, preserving at least $14 million of annual flexibility for emerging needs.

**Grantee financial monitoring.** Annual review of audited statements against five indicators: months of reserve, current ratio, revenue concentration, debt service coverage, and unrestricted net asset trend. This review substitutes for project-level expenditure verification and is materially more predictive of organizational failure. In our data, no organization that failed during the study period did so without at least two of these five indicators having deteriorated for two consecutive years.

**Recoverable grant underwriting standards.** Maximum 15 percent of the facility to any single borrower. Documented receivable or defined revenue event as the recovery source. Loss reserve set at 30 percent, consistent with the 71 percent observed recovery rate.

**Pooled fund controls.** Written conflict-of-interest policy for decision-makers. Compensation at a documented professional rate. Foundation recusal from individual allocation votes, with authority retained over the pool's scope and total.

**Learning system.** Replace grant reports with three mechanisms: an annual portfolio-level outcome review conducted by the evaluation function; a rotating deep evaluation of one subsector per year at foundation expense; and structured, minuted learning conversations with each multiyear grantee. Total evaluation spend of $280,000 over three years buys more usable knowledge than 260 annual grant reports, which in our survey produced substantive funder feedback 26 percent of the time.

**Reversibility.** Every element of the model can be unwound. Multiyear commitments run off within four years. The pooled fund has an annual appropriation, not an endowment. The recoverable facility returns capital. The board should be told this plainly: the transition is a reversible experiment with a defined maximum exposure, not an irrevocable change in the foundation's character.

## 6. Answering the Trustee Objections

### 6.1 "Unrestricted support weakens accountability"

The objection assumes that project restriction produces accountability. Our data suggest it produces documentation.

Consider what a project-restricted grant actually verifies: that money designated for a defined activity was spent on that activity, as represented in a report prepared by the grantee. It verifies expenditure conformity. It does not verify that the activity worked, that it was the right activity, or that the organization delivering it is sound. In our sample, expenditure-conformity reporting failed to identify deterioration in any of the eleven grantee organizations that entered financial crisis during the study period. In nine of the eleven, audited financial statements available to the funder showed clear warning signs for two or more years before the crisis.

The reframing we propose to trustees is that accountability has three possible objects — compliance, performance, and institutional health — and that restriction addresses only the first, at the highest cost and with the lowest predictive value. A reformed practice substitutes:

- **For compliance:** the grantee's own audit, which is prepared by an independent professional and covers all funds, not just ours.
- **For performance:** portfolio-level outcome measurement designed and paid for by the foundation, plus periodic independent subsector evaluation.
- **For institutional health:** the five-indicator annual financial review described in Section 5.4.

This is more accountability, not less. It is also more expensive for the foundation and less expensive for the grantee, which is the correct allocation given who has the capacity to bear it. The honest version of the trustee objection is not "we will lose accountability" but "we will lose the *feeling* of control that line-item review provides." That feeling is worth examining, but it is not an outcome.

### 6.2 "This violates donor intent"

Donor intent deserves genuine deference, and this objection is the one we treat most carefully, because it is sometimes correct.

Three distinctions resolve most cases.

**First, distinguish purpose from mechanism.** A donor document specifying support for early childhood education in Hennepin County constrains purpose. It almost never specifies grant duration, indirect cost treatment, or reporting format. Paying full indirect on an early-childhood grant serves the donor's purpose more faithfully than paying partial indirect, because partial indirect means the donor's stated purpose is partly financed by the grantee's other donors. In reviewing donor documents for eleven member foundations, our senior fellow found that none specified an indirect cost cap and only two specified grant duration. The mechanisms trustees defend as donor intent are almost always institutional convention that accumulated after the donor's death.

**Second, where purpose is genuinely narrow, use the right instrument.** This is the case for full-indirect project grants. A fund restricted to a specific purpose should make specific grants — paying their full cost. The model in Section 5 retains 22 percent of payout in this form precisely for donor-restricted capital. Nothing in this paper argues that every foundation dollar should be unrestricted.

**Third, ask what the donor was trying to accomplish.** Most donors established foundations because they wanted particular problems solved or particular institutions sustained. A grantmaking practice that systematically depletes the balance sheets of the institutions the donor cared about is not a faithful reading of intent, however scrupulously it observes the letter of the gift instrument. Trustees who take donor intent seriously should find the 1.9-month reserve figure alarming on donor-intent grounds specifically: it means the donor's chosen instruments are being worn out.

We recommend that any foundation undertaking this transition commission a written legal review of its governing documents in Year One, and that the review be presented to the board. In our members' experience, this review almost always finds more latitude than trustees assumed, and it converts a values argument into a documented finding.

### 6.3 "Small grantees can't handle unrestricted money"

This objection inverts the evidence.

Table 2 shows that the indirect gap is *largest* for the smallest organizations — 21.4 points for grantees under $500,000, against 9.5 points for those over $50 million. Small organizations have the least ability to spread fixed infrastructure cost, so capped indirect harms them most. They also have the least administrative slack to absorb reporting burden: for a $400,000 organization, 71 hours per grant across four grants is 284 hours, or 14 percent of a single full-time position.

The empirical claim embedded in the objection — that small organizations mismanage unrestricted funds — is not supported in our data. Among grantees under $2 million receiving multiyear general operating support, we found no elevated rate of adverse audit findings, material weakness citations, or organizational failure relative to comparable restricted-funded peers. What we did find was a 2.2-month improvement in median reserve position.

There is, however, a legitimate concern inside the objection. Small organizations often lack the financial management infrastructure to *deploy* flexible capital well — not because their leaders are imprudent, but because they may have no chief financial officer, an outsourced bookkeeper, and no multi-year financial model. The correct response is to fund that infrastructure, not to withhold flexibility. We recommend three specific measures, budgeted in the Section 5 model at $1.2 million by Year Three:

1. **A shared services subsidy.** Underwrite grantee access to shared finance, human resources, and technology services. Among members offering this, participating grantees improved reserve position 40 percent faster than non-participants.
2. **A reserve-building match.** Match grantee additions to operating reserve at 1:1 up to a defined ceiling, with a three-year covenant restricting withdrawal except on board resolution. This converts a general operating grant into deliberate capitalization.
3. **Board financial capability support.** Fund treasurer and finance-committee training. In our survey, 52 percent of organizations under $2 million reported no board member with professional financial experience — a far better predictor of poor financial decisions than the presence of unrestricted funds.

The proper form of the objection, then, is not "small grantees cannot handle unrestricted money." It is "small grantees need capitalization support alongside unrestricted money." That is a design specification, and the model accommodates it.

---

## 7. Conclusion

The evidence assembled here describes a funding architecture whose costs have become larger than its benefits and whose costs fall almost entirely on parties who cannot decline to bear them.

The magnitudes are not marginal. A 2.9-month gap in operating reserve. A 12-point gap in executive turnover. A 15.4-point structural shortfall between what funders pay for infrastructure and what infrastructure costs. Seventy-one hours per grant of reporting labor that produces substantive funder engagement roughly a quarter of the time. Forty-one percent of organizations operating below three months of reserve in a region where the state pays 94 days late. These are not the symptoms of a sector that needs closer supervision. They are the symptoms of a sector being asked to absorb the administrative preferences of its funders out of capital it does not have.

The reforms available are neither novel nor untested. All five practices we compare are already in use among our members, with observable results. None is universally correct. The portfolio we model — 60 percent multiyear general operating support, 22 percent full-indirect project grants, 10 percent participatory allocation, 5 percent recoverable capital, 3 percent capacity investment, delivered through tiered processes proportioned to award size — is a defensible target for a $40 million foundation, reachable in three years at a transition cost of roughly $2.1 million in administrative expense.

The trustee objections are serious and deserve serious answers, which we have attempted to provide. But it is worth stating the asymmetry plainly. The objections concern risks that are hypothetical, reversible, and borne by the foundation. The status quo concerns harms that are documented, cumulative, and borne by grantees. A trustee body weighing these considerations should ask which of them it is better positioned to absorb, and which it is merely better positioned to ignore.

We offer one final observation. Every element of the transition described in Section 5 is reversible within four years. The reserve depletion documented in Section 3 is not reversible on that timescale — our matched-panel analysis found that organizations rebuild reserves at roughly two-thirds the rate at which they deplete them. The asymmetry of reversibility is, we think, the strongest single argument for acting on incomplete information. Foundations can undo a change in grant design. They cannot readily undo the loss of an institution that ran out of cash while filing a compliant report.
