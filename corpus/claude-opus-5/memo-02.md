**TO:** Plant Leadership Team; Manufacturing Engineering; Production Control
**FROM:** Hana Ishikawa-Brennan, Vice President of Manufacturing
**DATE:** December 15, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Opening the Roughing Insourcing Program — Scope, Sequence, and What I Need From Each of You

---

I want to start by describing the situation plainly, because I have heard three or four versions of it circulating on the floor this week and none of them is quite right.

On November 6, our largest customer completed a two-day supplier audit at Claremont and issued eleven nonconformances. Four are classified major. We have sixty days from the closing meeting to submit an accepted corrective action plan, which puts our deadline at January 5. Until that plan is accepted, the customer has placed a hold on releasing new part numbers to us. Existing part numbers continue to flow. Rasheed Mansour is issuing a separate memo to the quality and engineering staff this week that walks through all eleven findings in detail, and I would ask everyone on this distribution to read it carefully even if you do not think your area is implicated. Several of the findings are systemic rather than departmental, and the systemic ones are the expensive ones.

On November 26, Ridgeline Precision — the outside shop in Bellows Falls that has performed our five-axis roughing operations since 2019 — filed for Chapter 11 protection. We learned about it from a trade publication before we learned about it from Ridgeline. As of this morning we have 2,200 pieces of our work in process sitting on their floor, in various states of completion, subject to the automatic stay. Our counsel has filed the appropriate motions to recover the material as our property, and I will not speculate here about the timeline. Eli Castellanos will be reporting on the recovery effort at week twelve, and he will have better numbers than I do.

The immediate operational consequence is that thirty-eight percent of our open orders are now past their promise dates. That figure was eleven percent on November 1. We did not slip because we ran badly. We slipped because a single-source operation in the middle of our routing sequence stopped existing.

### What We Are Doing

The response, approved in concept by the board on October 22 and refined since, is to bring five-axis roughing in-house. The full program is $6.8 million and has four components.

First, two five-axis machining centers with a 34-week lead time from order placement. We have quotes from three builders and a preferred configuration. The lead time is the binding constraint on everything else in this program and I want that understood clearly: nothing we do on the financing side, the staffing side, or the quality side changes the fact that steel does not arrive until roughly week thirty-six after we sign.

Second, a rebuilt inspection cell. The current cell was laid out for a parts flow that assumed roughing happened forty miles away and arrived on a truck in batches. Insourced roughing changes the arrival pattern, the inspection frequency, and the fixturing requirements. It also happens that three of the eleven audit findings touch measurement system capability, so the inspection cell rebuild sits at the intersection of the customer response and the insourcing program. That is convenient in the sense that one capital project addresses two problems. It is inconvenient in the sense that the two problems have different deadlines.

Third, a second shift. Twenty-eight machinists. Ingrid Halvorsen will address this in her own memo and I will not get ahead of her, except to say that I have reviewed the labor market data with her and I consider staffing to be the highest-risk element of the program — higher than the equipment, higher than the money. Machines can be expedited. People in Sullivan County cannot.

Fourth, the process qualification work that has to happen between machine installation and first article approval on production parts. This is the part of the program that people forget to schedule. It is not trivial. We are talking about proving out roughing programs on titanium and nickel-alloy structures that have been run by someone else's process for seven years, on machines nobody here has operated, with fixtures we have not designed yet, to a customer who is currently auditing us.

### Sequence and Ownership

I am organizing the program in four workstreams with named owners.

**Capital and financing** — Gail Dombrowski. The board approved $4.5 million in October against a program that has since been scoped at $6.8 million. Gail is revising the capital request and will explain the gap and its consequences. I want to be direct with this group about something: the $2.3 million shortfall is not a rounding error and it is not going to be closed by value engineering the machine specification. Anyone who is asked to help find savings should help sincerely, but nobody should expect the gap to disappear through cleverness.

**Quality and corrective action** — Rasheed Mansour. Sixty days, eleven findings, four major. Rasheed has full authority to pull people from other work for this and I have told the department heads so individually. If Rasheed asks for one of your engineers for two weeks, the answer is yes, and if that creates a problem, you bring the problem to me rather than to him.

**Staffing and shift structure** — Ingrid Halvorsen. Twenty-eight machinists, a wage scale that is $3.40 below the Springfield market, and twenty-seven percent turnover last year. Ingrid and I have agreed that the wage adjustment is a program cost, not an HR discretionary item, and it will be presented and defended that way.

**Recovery and current production** — Eli Castellanos. Everything above is about the future state. Eli owns the present, which is 2,200 stranded pieces, thirty-eight percent past due, and a floor that is working overtime on the operations we can still perform. Eli's problem is the one the customer actually sees every week.

### The Sequencing Problem

I want to name the central difficulty of this program so that nobody has to discover it independently in February.

The corrective action plan is due in sixty days. The machines arrive in thirty-four weeks. The second shift has to be hired before the machines arrive, or the machines sit idle, but we cannot productively employ twenty-eight new machinists for eight months on a first shift that already has capacity. The financing gap has to close before we can place the machine order, and every week we delay the order pushes the thirty-four weeks out by a week. And the recovery of past-due orders — the thing the customer cares about most right now — depends on outside capacity we do not currently have contracted, because Ridgeline was our outside capacity.

These constraints do not resolve neatly. What I am asking for is that each of you understand your own workstream's dependency on the others, and that you flag conflicts early rather than absorbing them. The failure mode I am worried about is not that any one of these workstreams fails. It is that each one quietly protects its own schedule at the expense of a neighbor, and we discover in April that we financed machines we cannot staff to run parts we have not qualified for a customer who never lifted the hold.

### Immediate Actions

Between now and January 5:

Manufacturing engineering will complete the fixture concept study for the two new centers and deliver a preliminary tooling budget. I need this before Gail finalizes the revised capital request, not after.

Production control will requote the entire open order book against a no-Ridgeline baseline and give me a realistic past-due burndown under three scenarios: interim outside capacity secured in thirty days, sixty days, or not at all.

Every department head will identify the people they would assign to Rasheed's corrective action teams and give him the names by December 19. Not availability. Names.

I will be on the floor more than usual over the next several months. If you see me talking to machinists, I am not going around anybody. I have found that the second shift question in particular is one where the people who will actually work the shift know things that do not appear in the staffing model.

We have been a good supplier to this customer for a long time and we have been profitable doing work that most shops in New England cannot do. Neither of those facts is currently visible to the customer, and our job over the next nine months is to make them visible again.

**SUBJECT:** November 6 Customer Audit — All Eleven Findings, Response Structure, and the 60-Day Schedule

---

This memo covers all eleven nonconformances from the November 6 closing meeting, the response structure I am putting in place, and what each of you owes and when. It is long. Read the whole thing. I have had four separate conversations this week with people who believed their area was not affected, and in three of those four cases they were wrong.

### How the Findings Break Down

The four majors are Findings 2, 5, 7, and 9. The customer's classification logic is worth understanding: a finding is major when it represents a breakdown of a system rather than a lapse in an instance, or when it has escaped to product. All four of ours are system findings. None of them alleges that we shipped a bad part. I want that stated clearly because it matters both to how we respond and to how we feel about it.

The seven minors are 1, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 11. Minors are not free. The customer expects containment and correction on minors within the same sixty days, with effectiveness verification permitted to extend. But the majors are where the audit team spent its time and where our credibility will be judged.

### The Major Findings

**Finding 2 — Measurement system analysis is not current for characteristics classified as critical.** The auditors sampled fourteen critical characteristics across four part families and found that eleven had gage R&R studies older than the interval specified in our own procedure QP-7.6. Three had no study on file at all. On two of those three, the measurement method had changed since the last documented study — we moved from a CMM routine to a fixture-mounted comparator on one, and changed probe configuration on the other — and no revalidation was performed.

This finding is exactly as bad as it sounds. Our procedure sets a two-year interval; the customer's flow-down requirement sets it at annual for critical characteristics, and we have been running to our own interval on parts that carry their requirement. That is not an oversight in execution. That is a flow-down that never made it into our procedure. Priya's team will need to trace how that happened, because the corrective action has to address the flow-down review process, not just the eleven studies.

Correction: complete gage R&R on all fourteen sampled characteristics plus a full inventory of critical characteristics across the active part list. My preliminary count is 340 critical characteristics on active parts. We will not complete studies on all 340 by January 5 and the customer does not expect us to. What we must deliver by January 5 is the complete inventory, a risk-ranked schedule, studies completed on everything classified high-risk, and a revised QP-7.6 with the correct interval.

**Finding 5 — Nonconforming material control: segregation and disposition timeliness.** The auditors found nineteen items in the MRB area with disposition open longer than thirty days, seven of them longer than ninety. Two items had been moved from the MRB cage to a staging rack near the wash station without an MRB transfer record. Neither item shipped. But the auditors' point, which I accept, is that we cannot demonstrate that a part in that staging rack is under control, and if it had shipped we would not know.

The ninety-day items are largely a resource problem — we have engineering dispositions queued behind engineering capacity — and the corrective action has to be honest about that. Saying we will disposition faster is not a corrective action. Saying we have reallocated a named engineer's time and capped MRB queue depth with an escalation trigger is a corrective action.

Correction: clear all nineteen open items to disposition by January 2. Physical recontrol of the MRB cage with a badge-access log. Revised MR-4.2 with a fourteen-day disposition clock and automatic escalation to me at day fifteen.

**Finding 7 — Special process source control.** We use four outside sources for heat treat, three for penetrant inspection, and two for shot peen. The auditors checked our approved source list against the customer's current approved source list and found one heat treat vendor and one penetrant vendor that appear on ours and not on theirs. The heat treat vendor's approval lapsed in August. We processed material there in September and October.

This is the finding I expect to take the most work to close, because it requires us to demonstrate not just that we removed the vendor but that we have a mechanism to know when a customer's list changes. Right now that mechanism is a person checking a portal when they remember to.

Correction: immediate removal of both sources. Traceability run on all material processed at the lapsed heat treat source since the August lapse date — Devon, I need that lot list by December 22, and I expect it to be in the range of forty to sixty lots. We will need to determine whether any of that material requires customer notification, and I will make that call with Hana once I see the list. Systemic: a monthly source-list reconciliation with a named owner and a documented check record.

**Finding 9 — Corrective action effectiveness verification.** The auditors pulled twelve closed corrective actions from the past eighteen months and found that eight were closed without documented effectiveness verification, and that three of those eight addressed conditions that subsequently recurred.

I will not soften this one. This finding says that our corrective action system does not work, and the audit that produced the finding is now asking us to fix it using that same system. The customer's audit team said as much in the closing meeting, and they were right to.

Correction: reopen and properly verify all eight. Revise CA-3.1 to require effectiveness verification at a defined interval after implementation, with objective evidence, and with closure authority held at director level rather than at the originating department. That last change means more of these come to my desk. I am accepting that deliberately, because the alternative is a system where the person who caused the problem certifies that it is fixed.

### The Seven Minors

**Finding 1** — Training records: nine operators performing special processes without current certification on file. Six are expired renewals, three are missing documentation for training we believe occurred. Correction is straightforward; the systemic piece is a certification expiry report that does not currently exist.

**Finding 3** — Calibration: four instruments in service past calibration due date, all four low-risk hand tools. Correction is recall and recalibration. Systemic piece is that our calibration recall report runs monthly against a due date rather than weekly against a due window, so a tool can be up to thirty days overdue before it appears.

**Finding 4** — Document control: two work instructions on the floor at revision levels superseded in the system. Both were in a satellite tool crib that our controlled-copy distribution list does not cover. Correction is to eliminate uncontrolled printing entirely at that location.

**Finding 6** — Internal audit schedule: our FY26 internal audit plan called for eighteen audits and we completed eleven. The seven not completed were disproportionately in the areas where the customer subsequently found problems, which the audit team noted. I would rather they had not noticed that, but they did.

**Finding 8** — FOD control in the wash and deburr area. Observation-based, with photographs. Correction is housekeeping discipline and a revised FOD sweep frequency. This one is genuinely easy to fix and genuinely easy to let slide back, which is why it will carry a ninety-day and one-hundred-eighty-day effectiveness check.

**Finding 10** — First article inspection reports: three AS9102 forms with incomplete Form 3 characteristic accountability. All three parts were correct; the documentation was not.

**Finding 11** — Records retention: sampling found four job travelers from 2024 that could not be produced within the four-hour window. Three were subsequently located in an offsite storage box that was mislabeled.

That last element is the one teams skip and the one customers read first. **TO:** Board of Directors, Capital Committee

**FROM:** Gail Dombrowski, Chief Financial Officer
**DATE:** January 8, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Revised Capital Request — Roughing Insourcing Program; $2.3 Million Funding Gap and FY28 Forecast Impact

---

This memo revises the capital request approved by the committee on October 22, 2026, in the amount of $4.5 million. The program as now scoped requires $6.8 million. I am requesting authorization for the incremental $2.3 million, and I am setting out below what changed, what it costs us, and what happens if the committee declines.

### What Was Approved in October and Why It Was Wrong

The October request was prepared in eight days, at a point when the committee had before it the customer audit findings and had not yet learned of Ridgeline Precision's bankruptcy filing. The request was scoped as a capacity and capability addition: two five-axis machining centers, installation, and a contingency. It was not scoped as a supplier replacement, because in October we did not know we would need one.

I want to be precise about the sequence, because the committee is entitled to know whether it was given a bad number or an incomplete situation. It was given an incomplete situation. The $4.5 million was a defensible estimate of the program as it was understood on October 22. Ridgeline filed on November 26.

### The Revised Scope and the Gap

The revised program totals $6,795,000, which I will refer to as $6.8 million.

**Machining centers, two units, installed** — $4,120,000. This is firm against quotation from our preferred builder, valid through February 15, 2027. It includes the machines, standard tooling packages, foundation work, electrical service upgrade at the northeast bay, and rigging. The October estimate carried $3,950,000 for this line. The $170,000 increase is foundation and electrical, which the October estimate underweighted because the bay location had not been fixed.

**Fixturing and workholding** — $610,000. This line did not exist in the October request. When roughing was performed at Ridgeline, Ridgeline owned the roughing fixtures. Those fixtures are Ridgeline's assets, they are subject to the bankruptcy estate, and our counsel advises that recovering them is possible but slow and partial. Manufacturing engineering's fixture concept study, delivered December 30, identifies fourteen fixture families required to cover eighty-one percent of roughing volume. At an average of $43,500 per family this is $609,000. I have carried it at $610,000. This is the single largest driver of the gap and it is a direct consequence of the bankruptcy.

**Inspection cell rebuild** — $845,000. The October request carried $400,000 for inspection, scoped as a CMM upgrade. The revised figure reflects three changes. First, Findings 2 and 10 from the customer audit require measurement system capability we do not currently have, specifically a second CMM to relieve the throughput constraint that has been driving our gage R&R deferrals. Second, insourced roughing changes inspection from batch-arrival to continuous-flow, which requires a different cell layout and additional fixturing. Third, the customer's likely verification visit makes the inspection cell a demonstration item, and I would rather show them a finished cell than a plan.

**Process qualification and first article** — $475,000. Also absent from the October request. This covers the engineering hours, sacrificial material, and machine time required to prove out roughing programs and obtain first article approval on the affected part numbers. Manufacturing engineering's estimate is 3,400 engineering hours and approximately $180,000 in titanium and nickel-alloy material that will be consumed and scrapped. I have reviewed this with Hana and consider it conservative rather than padded. It is the line most likely to be underestimated in a program of this type.

**Second shift startup** — $340,000. Non-recurring costs only: recruiting, relocation assistance for a projected six hires, training hours before productive output, and the supervisory addition. The ongoing wage cost of the second shift, including the wage scale adjustment Ingrid Halvorsen is proposing, is an operating expense and appears in the FY28 forecast discussion below rather than in this capital request.

**Contingency at 6.5 percent** — $405,000. The October request carried ten percent on a smaller base. I have reduced the percentage because three of the five major lines are now firm-quoted rather than estimated. I am aware that reducing contingency on a program that has already grown thirty-eight percent invites skepticism. My reasoning is that the growth came from scope discovery, not from cost escalation on known scope, and the scope is now substantially discovered. I would rather present a lower contingency I believe in than a higher one that absorbs the committee's attention.

Total: $6,795,000. Approved: $4,500,000. Gap: $2,295,000.

### Funding the Gap

I have examined four sources and recommend a combination of two.

**Revolving credit facility.** We have $8.0 million committed with $2.1 million drawn as of December 31. Available capacity is $5.9 million. Drawing $2.3 million would take utilization to $4.4 million, or fifty-five percent. Our facility carries a fixed charge coverage covenant of 1.25x tested quarterly and a funded debt to EBITDA covenant of 3.0x. My projection under the full-draw scenario shows fixed charge coverage at 1.41x at the FY27 Q4 test and 1.28x at the FY28 Q2 test, which is the trough. That is a thirteen-basis-point cushion at the trough on a covenant that would trigger a default. I am not comfortable funding the entire gap this way.

**Equipment financing on the machining centers.** Our builder's captive finance arm has quoted a sixty-month structure at 7.4 percent covering eighty percent of the $4.12 million machine line, or $3.30 million. This converts a capital outlay into a lease obligation, frees cash, and does not consume revolver capacity. It does add to funded debt for covenant purposes, and the annual service is approximately $790,000.

**Deferral of the FY27 facility program.** We have $1.1 million approved for roof replacement on the west bay and HVAC in the grinding department. The roof has three to five years of remaining life per our 2025 engineering survey. The HVAC does not, and deferring it has a comfort and retention cost in a year when retention is already our binding constraint. I recommend deferring the roof only: $680,000.

**Reduction of program scope.** I have looked hard at this at Hana's request and I do not recommend it. The only line with meaningful discretionary content is the inspection cell, and reducing it means either not addressing Findings 2 and 10 with capital or deferring that spend into FY28. Both options trade a funded problem for an unfunded one in front of a customer who is currently auditing us.

**Recommendation:** Equipment financing on the machining centers at $3.30 million, plus deferral of the west bay roof at $680,000. This fully funds the $6.8 million program, leaves the revolver at $2.1 million drawn, and preserves $5.9 million of undrawn capacity — which I regard as essential given that we have thirty-eight percent of orders past due and no reliable forecast of when that normalizes.

### FY28 Forecast Impact

FY28 as presented in September projected revenue of $104.2 million, EBITDA of $12.8 million at 12.3 percent, and free cash flow of $4.1 million. The revised forecast reflecting this program is as follows.

**Revenue: $97.6 million, a reduction of $6.6 million.** Three drivers. The new part number hold costs us an estimated $3.9 million in FY28 revenue that was in the September forecast, and that number assumes the hold lifts by March 2027; every additional month of hold costs approximately $520,000 in FY28. Past-due recovery consumes capacity that would otherwise have gone to new work, costing an estimated $1.8 million. Process qualification consumes machine hours on the new centers during the ramp, costing approximately $900,000 in deferred output.

**EBITDA: $9.7 million at 9.9 percent, a reduction of $3.1 million.** Beyond the gross margin effect of lower revenue, we absorb the wage scale adjustment at approximately $1.16 million annualized against the current hourly population, the second shift supervisory and support structure at $380,000, and elevated quality system spend at $290,000. Partially offsetting: eliminating Ridgeline's outside processing markup saves an estimated $1.44 million annually once roughing is fully insourced, though only about seven months of that benefit falls in FY28.

**Free cash flow: negative $1.4 million, a reduction of $5.5 million.** Capital of $6.8 million, of which $3.3 million is financed and therefore appears as debt service rather than outlay. Working capital builds as we carry roughing inventory we previously did not own. Debt service of $790,000 on the equipment financing.

**FY29** returns to a normalized picture: revenue $109 million, EBITDA 13.1 percent, free cash flow $6.9 million, on the assumptions that the hold lifts, past-due clears, and the second shift is staffed and productive.

### If the Committee Declines

I owe the committee a clear statement of the alternative.

If the incremental $2.3 million is not authorized, we build the program to $4.5 million. That means one machining center rather than two, fixturing for eight families rather than fourteen, and the inspection cell deferred to FY28. One machine covers approximately fifty-five percent of roughing volume at full utilization on two shifts. The remaining forty-five percent requires an outside source. We do not currently have one. Our purchasing group has approached four shops with five-axis roughing capability in titanium; two declined on capacity, one quoted at a fifty-eight percent premium to Ridgeline's rates, and one is not on the customer's approved source list and would require a qualification cycle we estimate at five to seven months.

The premium quote, if we accepted it for forty-five percent of volume, costs approximately $1.9 million annually. That is a recurring operating cost, in perpetuity or until we fund the second machine, incurred to avoid a one-time capital outlay of $2.3 million. The payback on the second machine against that alternative is roughly fourteen months.

I recognize that this reads as though I am presenting a decision that has already been made. That is not my intention, and there is a genuine argument for the single-machine path: it preserves balance sheet flexibility in a year when our largest customer relationship is unsettled, and it defers commitment until we know whether the hold lifts. I do not find that argument persuasive, because the hold is more likely to lift if we can demonstrate recovered capability than if we cannot. But it is a real argument and the committee should weigh it.

I request authorization of the incremental $2,295,000, with funding as recommended above, at the January 21 meeting. The machine quotation expires February 15, and every week beyond that date moves first production a week further out on a thirty-four-week clock.

**TO:** All Claremont Hourly Employees; Supervisors; Department Leads
**FROM:** Ingrid Halvorsen, Director of Human Resources
**DATE:** January 26, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Second Shift Staffing and Wage Scale Adjustment

---

Two things are being announced together in this memo because they are connected, and because announcing them separately would have invited a set of reasonable questions that I would not have been able to answer honestly.

The first is that Kearsarge is establishing a second shift in machining, targeted at twenty-eight machinists plus supervision and support, beginning with a phased hire starting in March. The second is that effective with the pay period beginning February 15, the shop wage scale is being adjusted upward across all machining classifications.

### The Wage Adjustment

Our wage scale has run below the Springfield-area market for some time. Our most recent third-party survey, completed in December against a peer set of eleven precision machining employers within a seventy-mile radius, put the gap at $3.40 per hour at the midpoint of our machinist classifications. The gap was wider at the top of the scale than at the bottom, meaning our most experienced people were the furthest below market — which is exactly backwards from what a scale should do.

Effective February 15:

Machinist classifications receive an adjustment that closes the market gap in full at every step. The average increase is $3.40 per hour; individual increases range from $2.15 to $4.60 depending on classification and step, with the larger adjustments at the senior steps where our gap was widest.

Setup and CNC programming classifications receive an average adjustment of $2.90 to maintain proper differential above the machinist scale.

Inspection classifications receive an average adjustment of $2.55.

Deburr, wash, and material handling classifications receive $1.40, which reflects a smaller measured market gap in those roles.

Second shift will carry a differential of $2.25 per hour, up from the current $1.10. Third shift, should we establish one, would carry $3.00.

These are permanent scale changes, not bonuses. They fold into overtime calculation and into the base for future increases. The annual merit cycle in July proceeds normally and separately; this adjustment does not consume it.

### Why Now, and Why This Way

I want to answer the question that I would ask if I were reading this on the floor: if the gap was $3.40 in December, how long was it $2.00, and why did that not get fixed?

The honest answer is that the gap widened over about three years and we addressed it in increments that were smaller than the widening. Each individual year's decision was defensible and the cumulative result was not. Turnover ran twenty-seven percent last year. We lost thirty-eight people from the hourly population and we replaced them with people who, on average, needed nine months to reach full productivity in titanium and nickel-alloy work. The cost of that turnover — recruiting, training, scrap during the learning curve, overtime to cover gaps — exceeded the cost of the adjustment we are now making. That has probably been true for two years.

So this adjustment is not a reward and it is not a response to the second shift announcement in the sense of buying goodwill. It is a correction of something that should have been corrected earlier, and the second shift requirement is what forced us to actually measure it.

I also want to be direct that this adjustment is not sufficient by itself. Sullivan County unemployment is 2.1 percent. There is no reserve of unemployed five-axis machinists in this valley waiting for a wage signal. Closing the wage gap makes us competitive; it does not make us the only employer hiring. Everything else we do about retention — how supervisors treat people, whether the schedule is predictable, whether the tooling is available when the job hits the machine — matters more now than it did when we were cheap and people stayed anyway. I have said that to the supervisory group and I will keep saying it.

### The Second Shift

Twenty-eight machinists, four setup technicians, two inspectors, one shift supervisor, and one material handler. Target start is phased: eight in March, ten in June, and the balance by October, timed against the arrival and commissioning of the two new machining centers.

We will fill positions in this order. First, internal transfer. Any current first-shift employee who wants second shift may apply, and internal applicants will be considered before external candidates for every opening. I recognize that most people do not want second shift, and I am not going to pretend otherwise. ### Past-Due Position

Past due peaked at 41 percent of open orders in the week ending December 19. As of February 16 it stands at 29 percent.

That improvement is real but it is smaller than the raw numbers make it look, for two reasons.
