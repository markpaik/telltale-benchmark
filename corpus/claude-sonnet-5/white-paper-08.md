# Boarding Without Beds: A Five-Year Case for Redesigning Pediatric Behavioral Health Emergency Care in Southern Minnesota and Western Wisconsin

**A White Paper from Cedarbrook Health Institute**

Dr. Feliciano Arriaga-Mendez, System Chief of Emergency Medicine
Dr. Meena Venkataraman, Medical Director, Child and Adolescent Psychiatry
Kaleb Ironcloud, Director of Behavioral Health Crisis Services
Dr. Hollis Trembath, Health Economist

Cedarbrook Health Institute, Mankato, Minnesota

July 2026

---

## Abstract

Across the fourteen emergency departments of Cedarbrook Health Partners, children in behavioral health crisis wait longer for placement than for any other category of emergency care. Drawing on five years of data covering 11,900 pediatric behavioral health visits between 2021 and 2025, this paper documents a median boarding stay of 31 hours past disposition, a ninetieth-percentile stay of 106 hours, and a regional inpatient child psychiatric bed base of only 38 beds for a catchment population of 1.4 million. Restraint or seclusion occurred in 9.4 percent of boarded stays, and sitter labor alone cost the system $1.1 million last year. We compare five approaches under active consideration — a dedicated psychiatric emergency unit, telepsychiatry consultation into rural sites, mobile crisis teams with short-stay stabilization beds, a pediatric observation protocol embedded in the emergency department, and bridge clinics guaranteeing follow-up within 72 hours — on cost, reach, and plausible effect on boarding volume. We then build a financial model around the leading candidate, a twelve-bay psychiatric emergency unit requiring $6.8 million in capital and $4.2 million a year in operating cost, and test it against a state capital grant round closing in September 2026. Finally, we address the four objections raised most often by our own board and by external partners: rural travel distance, psychiatrist recruitment, Medicaid reimbursement adequacy, and whether upstream investment moves inpatient and boarding volume at all. Our conclusion is that no single intervention closes the gap; a bundled strategy — bridge clinics and telepsychiatry deployed first at low capital cost, layered with a right-sized psychiatric emergency unit funded through the 2026 grant cycle — offers the best combination of near-term relief and long-term capacity building for the region's children.

## 1. The Problem

Emergency departments were not built to hold children for days. They were built to stabilize and move patients along a continuum of care that, for physical illness and injury, mostly still functions. For pediatric behavioral health, that continuum has effectively collapsed in southern Minnesota and western Wisconsin, and Cedarbrook's fourteen emergency departments have become, by default, the region's largest psychiatric holding capacity.

The clinical problem is straightforward to state and difficult to solve: a child arrives in crisis — suicidal ideation, a suicide attempt, an acute psychotic episode, an aggression crisis tied to autism or a mood disorder — and is medically cleared within hours. The psychiatric disposition, however, requires either an inpatient bed, a partial hospitalization slot, or a structured outpatient plan that a rural or suburban family can actually reach. In our region, that disposition frequently does not exist on any timeline the emergency department can absorb. The child remains in a treatment bay, under one-to-one or intermittent sitter observation, while staff call bed lines, negotiate with receiving facilities, and manage a rotating set of behavioral escalations in a physical space — a nine-by-twelve room designed for suturing lacerations and reading X-rays — that was never designed to contain a psychiatric crisis for three or four days.

This is not a failure of any single hospital or any single clinician. It is a structural mismatch between the number of children the region generates in behavioral health crisis and the number of destinations available to receive them once emergency stabilization is complete. The mismatch has three compounding dimensions.

**First, bed supply has not moved while demand has.** The region operates 38 inpatient child and adolescent psychiatric beds for a catchment of 1.4 million people — roughly 2.7 beds per 100,000 population, well below even the modest benchmarks cited in state psychiatric bed planning documents. Those 38 beds sit in two facilities, both of which triage referrals against their own capacity and case mix, and both of which declined a substantial share of Cedarbrook's transfer requests in 2025.

**Second, the workforce that could staff alternatives is itself scarce.** Child and adolescent psychiatry is among the most acute shortage specialties in American medicine, and rural systems compete directly with urban academic centers and virtual-first national platforms for the same small pool of clinicians. Any solution premised on hiring our way out of the problem runs headlong into a labor market that will not clear at any price the system can sustain.

**Third, the financial architecture surrounding pediatric behavioral health does not reward the fix.** Boarding is expensive to the health system in staffing, opportunity cost, and risk, but it generates little identifiable revenue and is not separately reimbursed by Medicaid or commercial payers in ways that offset the cost of building alternative capacity. Meanwhile, the interventions most likely to reduce boarding — crisis stabilization, bridge follow-up, telepsychiatry consultation — sit in reimbursement categories that are either newly created, inconsistently covered across Minnesota Medicaid managed care organizations and Wisconsin Medicaid, or not billable at all in the emergency department context.

The consequence is a slow-motion crisis that shows up in three places: in the child, who spends days in a room without therapeutic programming, natural light schedules, or peer contact, at real risk of clinical deterioration and, in nearly one in ten boarded stays, restraint or seclusion; in the emergency department, which loses treatment bays to non-emergent holding and displaces care for other patients; and in the system's finances, where sitter labor, forgone bay throughput, and legal and reputational exposure accumulate without a corresponding funding stream to address them.

This paper exists because Cedarbrook's leadership believes the region is at a decision point. A state capital grant round closing in September 2026 offers a concrete, time-limited opportunity to fund at least one structural intervention. The purpose of this paper is to lay out the data, compare the live options honestly — including their limits — and give hospital executives, state Medicaid officials, county human services directors, payers, and legislators a shared factual basis for the choice ahead.

## 2. Five Years of System Data, 2021–2025

Cedarbrook Health Institute maintains a behavioral health emergency visit registry across all fourteen system emergency departments. The data below cover 11,900 pediatric (age 5–17) behavioral health emergency visits from January 2021 through December 2025. "Boarding" is defined, consistent with national quality measurement conventions, as any period exceeding twelve hours between the documented psychiatric disposition decision (admit, transfer, or discharge to a specific outpatient plan) and the child's actual departure from the emergency department.

### 2.1 Volume and boarding incidence

Pediatric behavioral health emergency visits rose across the study period, and the share of those visits that met the boarding definition rose with them, though the institute's data governance did not track a single clean year-over-year percentage change suitable for publication here; the more actionable finding is the duration distribution among children who did board, which has remained stubbornly severe across all five years rather than concentrating in any single year or facility.

### 2.2 Boarding duration

**Table 1. Distribution of pediatric behavioral health boarding duration, Cedarbrook system, 2021–2025 (n = boarded stays exceeding 12 hours past disposition)**

| Percentile / threshold | Duration |
|---|---|
| Median (50th percentile) | 31 hours |
| 90th percentile | 106 hours |
| Stays exceeding 72 hours (3 days) | 18 percent of boarded children |

A median of 31 hours means that a full half of children who board wait more than a full calendar day past the point their care team has already decided what should happen next. The tail is the more alarming figure: one in ten boarded children waits 106 hours or longer — well over four days — and nearly one in five waits more than three full days. These are not outlier facilities; the pattern recurs across urban, suburban, and rural sites in the system, with rural critical access sites generally showing longer tails because transfer logistics and specialist consultation both take longer to arrange at distance.

### 2.3 Restraint and seclusion

**Table 2. Restraint or seclusion incidence during boarded pediatric behavioral health stays, 2021–2025**

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Boarded stays involving restraint or seclusion | 9.4 percent |

Restraint and seclusion are last-resort interventions used when a child's behavior poses imminent risk to self or staff, and their use is not evenly distributed: it clusters heavily among the longest boarding stays, among younger children with autism spectrum diagnoses, and among stays that occur overnight when psychiatric consultation is hardest to reach. The 9.4 percent system-wide average therefore understates the risk faced by the subset of children who board longest — precisely the population a stabilization-focused intervention would be designed to reach first.

### 2.4 Resource consumption

**Table 3. Direct system cost and capacity impact of pediatric behavioral health boarding, most recent full year**

| Resource | Impact |
|---|---|
| Sitter labor cost (one-to-one and enhanced observation) | $1.1 million |
| Treatment bays occupied by boarding, daily average, system-wide | 4.2 bays |

Sitter labor is the most visible and most easily costed line item, but it understates true system cost. At 4.2 treatment bays occupied on an average day across fourteen emergency departments, boarding is functionally equivalent to operating three to four fewer emergency bays than the system has built and staffed for — capacity that cannot be used for chest pain, trauma, or any other acute presentation while a child waits for a bed that does not exist.

### 2.5 Regional bed supply and transfer refusal

**Table 4. Inpatient child and adolescent psychiatric bed capacity and transfer outcomes, region served by Cedarbrook, 2025**

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Inpatient child/adolescent psychiatric beds, region | 38 |
| Catchment population served | 1.4 million |
| Beds per 100,000 population | ~2.7 |
| Facilities holding regional beds | 2 |
| Transfer requests refused by receiving facilities, 2025 | 44 percent |

A 44 percent refusal rate on transfer requests is the clearest single number in this data set. It means that in nearly half of cases where a Cedarbrook emergency physician identifies a child who needs inpatient psychiatric care and formally requests transfer to one of the region's two receiving facilities, that request is declined — for reasons that include bed unavailability, case mix or acuity mismatch, insurance status, and behavioral history. Each refusal restarts the search, adds hours or days to boarding, and in practice means that regional bed scarcity, not clinical need, is the operative constraint on how quickly a child leaves the emergency department.

### 2.6 Reading the five years together

Two conclusions follow directly from this data. First, the problem is chronic and structural rather than episodic: it appears consistently across five years, fourteen sites, and both states in the service area, which rules out any explanation resting on a single bad year, a single facility's staffing problem, or a temporary spike in demand. Second, the constraint is downstream of the emergency department, not within it. Emergency clinicians are, in the large majority of cases, reaching a disposition decision in a clinically reasonable timeframe; what follows that decision — locating a bed, securing an accepting facility, arranging safe transport, or standing up an outpatient plan a family can use — is where the delay accumulates. Any intervention that only speeds up emergency department decision-making, without changing what happens after the decision is made, will not move these numbers.

## 3. Comparing the Approaches

Cedarbrook's leadership has five approaches under active discussion. None is mutually exclusive with the others, and the financial model in Section 4 assumes at least two are pursued together. Table 5 summarizes the comparison; the discussion below explains the reasoning behind each row.

**Table 5. Comparison of approaches under consideration**

| Approach | Approx. capital cost | Approx. annual operating cost | Geographic reach | Primary mechanism | Time to deploy |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dedicated psychiatric emergency unit (12 bays) | $6.8 million | $4.2 million | Single hub site; regional draw by transfer | Removes boarding children from general ED bays into a purpose-built, therapeutic space with dedicated staffing | 18–24 months |
| Telepsychiatry consultation into rural sites | $0.4–0.6 million (equipment, licensing, platform) | $0.9–1.3 million (contracted psychiatric coverage) | All fourteen sites, especially the ten rural/critical-access EDs | Speeds disposition decision-making and supports local management, reducing unnecessary transfer requests | 6–9 months |
| Mobile crisis teams with short-stay stabilization beds | $2.5–3.5 million (vehicles, 6–8 stabilization beds, staffing buildout) | $2.8–3.4 million | County-level, deployable across service area | Diverts children from the ED at the point of crisis and provides a 24–72 hour off-ramp before ED presentation | 12–18 months |
| Pediatric observation protocol in the ED | $0.2–0.3 million (space modification, training) | $0.6–0.9 million | All fourteen sites | Standardizes a structured, time-limited (up to 24–36 hour) observation pathway with defined clinical and disposition criteria, reducing variability and restraint use | 3–6 months |
| Bridge clinics with 72-hour follow-up | $0.5–0.8 million (clinic buildout, scheduling infrastructure) | $1.4–1.8 million | Regional hub clinics, 3–4 sites | Provides an immediate outpatient landing spot so children can be safely discharged rather than held awaiting inpatient placement | 6–12 months |

### 3.1 Dedicated psychiatric emergency unit

This is the largest and most capital-intensive option, and the one aligned with the September 2026 state grant round. A twelve-bay unit, physically separate from general emergency treatment bays and staffed with psychiatric nursing, milieu therapy, and on-site or closely integrated psychiatric coverage, directly addresses the boarding problem by giving boarded children an appropriate physical and clinical environment rather than a general-purpose treatment bay. It does not, by itself, add inpatient bed capacity or change the 44 percent transfer refusal rate; children may still wait days for a true inpatient placement, but they wait in a space designed for that wait, which the data in Section 2 suggest would materially reduce restraint and seclusion incidence and free general emergency bays for other patients. Its principal limitation is reach: as a single-hub facility, most plausibly sited in Mankato, it serves the system best for centrally located and transfer-in patients, and does comparatively little for a child boarding at a rural critical access site four hours away unless that child is transferred into the unit — itself a logistical and family-burden question.

### 3.2 Telepsychiatry consultation into rural sites

Telepsychiatry is the lowest-capital, fastest-to-deploy option with the broadest geographic reach, since it can, in principle, reach all fourteen sites simultaneously. Its mechanism is different from the psychiatric emergency unit: rather than improving the environment in which a child waits, it aims to reduce unnecessary waiting and unnecessary transfer requests in the first place, by giving rural emergency physicians real-time specialist input on de-escalation, medication management, and disposition planning. Evidence from comparable rural telepsychiatry programs elsewhere in the Midwest suggests this can meaningfully reduce both boarding duration and transfer-request volume for the subset of children who can be safely managed and discharged locally with a solid follow-up plan — which is precisely the population bridge clinics (below) are designed to catch. Its limitation is that it does not add stabilization or inpatient capacity; for the sickest children, telepsychiatry speeds the decision to transfer but does not solve the 44 percent refusal problem underneath that decision.

### 3.3 Mobile crisis teams with short-stay stabilization beds

Mobile crisis is the only approach designed to intervene before a child ever reaches the emergency department, and it is therefore the approach most directly aimed at reducing total ED volume rather than reducing the boarding burden of children who have already arrived. Short-stay stabilization beds (typically six to eight beds, with lengths of stay measured in hours to a few days) give crisis teams somewhere to bring a child other than the ED. This approach has the strongest theoretical case for "moving volume" system-wide, addressing directly the objection raised in Section 5.4, but it also carries the longest and most uncertain deployment timeline, since it requires new county and community partnerships, new physical stabilization capacity, and a workforce (crisis counselors, paramedicine-trained staff) that must be recruited largely from scratch.

### 3.4 Pediatric observation protocol in the ED

This is the cheapest and fastest option and the one most directly under the system's own control, since it requires no new facility, no new inter-agency agreement, and comparatively modest new staffing. A structured observation protocol — defined admission criteria, defined maximum observation windows, standardized de-escalation and sensory-support tools, scheduled psychiatric reassessment intervals — has been shown elsewhere to reduce variability in boarding management and reduce restraint and seclusion rates even without adding a single inpatient bed, essentially by making the existing wait safer and shorter through better process rather than more capacity. Its limitation is that it manages the symptom rather than the underlying capacity shortage; it will reduce harm during boarding but will not, by itself, reduce median boarding duration below the constraint imposed by the 38-bed regional inpatient supply.

### 3.5 Bridge clinics with 72-hour follow-up

Bridge clinics attack the discharge side of the equation: many children currently board not because they clinically require inpatient admission, but because the emergency team has no confidence that a safe outpatient follow-up will actually occur if the child is discharged, and no defensible alternative if it does not. A bridge clinic model — a guaranteed, scheduled psychiatric or therapy appointment within 72 hours of ED discharge, at a small number of regional hub locations — gives emergency physicians a legitimate third disposition option beyond "admit" or "board indefinitely," for the substantial share of children whose presentation does not require inpatient level of care. This is a moderate-cost, moderate-reach option; its principal constraint is that it only reaches children within reasonable travel distance of the three to four hub clinic sites, which raises the same rural-access concern discussed for the psychiatric emergency unit.

### 3.6 Reach and cost, taken together

No approach scores well on both cost and reach simultaneously. The psychiatric emergency unit has strong impact on the children it touches but limited geographic reach and the highest cost. Telepsychiatry and the observation protocol have the best reach-per-dollar but the weakest independent effect on the underlying bed shortage. Mobile crisis has the strongest theoretical claim to reducing total volume but the longest runway and greatest execution risk. Bridge clinics sit in the middle on every dimension. This is the basis for the bundled recommendation developed in Sections 4 and 5: the approaches are complementary rather than competing, and the region's capital constraints argue for sequencing rather than attempting all five simultaneously.

## 4. The Financial Model

This section builds the financial case for the highest-cost, highest-visibility option — the twelve-bay dedicated psychiatric emergency unit — because it is the one tied to a hard external deadline, the state capital grant round closing in September 2026, and because it is the option requiring board and legislative commitment now, not the smaller-scale options that individual hospitals within the system can advance administratively.

### 4.1 Capital and operating cost

**Table 6. Twelve-bay psychiatric emergency unit — cost summary**

| Category | Amount |
|---|---|
| Capital cost (construction, buildout, initial equipment) | $6.8 million |
| Annual operating cost (staffing, milieu programming, supplies, overhead) | $4.2 million |

We assume the capital cost is financed through a combination of the state grant and system capital reserves, and that the September 2026 round, if awarded, would fund the majority of the $6.8 million capital requirement, leaving the system to fund the balance and the full annual operating cost from operating revenue and cost offsets identified below.

### 4.2 Offsetting costs already being incurred

The operating cost of the new unit does not represent $4.2 million in entirely new spending; a portion is redirected from costs the system already absorbs to manage the same children in general emergency bays.

**Table 7. Estimated annual cost offsets available to apply against new operating cost**

| Offset source | Estimated annual value |
|---|---|
| Sitter labor currently spent on boarded pediatric behavioral health patients | $1.1 million |
| Recovered general ED bay capacity (4.2 bays/day) valued at average bay contribution margin | $1.0–1.4 million (facility-dependent) |
| Reduction in restraint/seclusion-related staffing, documentation, and incident review burden | $0.2–0.3 million |
| **Subtotal, plausible offsets** | **$2.3–2.8 million** |

Against a $4.2 million annual operating cost, this leaves a net new operating requirement in the range of $1.4 million to $1.9 million per year, before accounting for any new revenue the unit generates directly.

### 4.3 New revenue potential

A psychiatric emergency unit, properly licensed and staffed, can bill for psychiatric evaluation, crisis stabilization services, and, depending on Minnesota and Wisconsin Medicaid managed care contracting, a per-diem or bundled crisis stabilization rate that general emergency treatment bays cannot access under current fee schedules. Conservatively, at a system-wide pediatric behavioral health volume consistent with the 11,900 visits recorded over the study period (roughly 2,400 visits per year averaged across five years, with the boarded subset representing the population the unit primarily serves), even modest incremental reimbursement per stabilized child — on the order of a few hundred dollars in bundled crisis stabilization revenue above what a general ED bay currently generates — could close much or all of the remaining $1.4–1.9 million gap. We present this as a plausible range rather than a firm projection because it depends on rate-setting decisions that are, in part, the subject of Section 5.3 and squarely in the hands of the Medicaid officials and payers this paper is written for.

### 4.4 Net position, three scenarios

**Table 8. Net annual operating position under three reimbursement scenarios**

| Scenario | New operating cost | Offsets | Net cost before new revenue | Plausible new stabilization revenue | Net system cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative (no rate change; general FFS rates only) | $4.2M | $2.3M | $1.9M | $0.3M | ~$1.6M/year |
| Moderate (state approves a defined crisis stabilization add-on rate) | $4.2M | $2.5M | $1.7M | $1.0M | ~$0.7M/year |
| Favorable (add-on rate plus managed care case rate alignment) | $4.2M | $2.8M | $1.4M | $1.4M | ~$0M/year |

Under every scenario modeled, the unit does not pay for itself outright from clinical revenue alone in year one; it requires ongoing system subsidy in the conservative and moderate cases. The financial argument for the unit is therefore not that it is self-funding, but that the net annual system cost — between roughly zero and $1.6 million depending on reimbursement policy — is materially smaller than the true fully loaded cost the system already bears in sitter labor, lost bay throughput, incident management, and clinical and legal risk associated with restraint and seclusion, most of which are costs the system currently absorbs without any offsetting grant or capital support at all.

### 4.5 Capital timing

The September 2026 grant round is, in practical terms, the only near-term external capital source of this scale available to the system for this purpose. If the application is not made or is unsuccessful, the twelve-bay unit is unlikely to be financeable from system capital reserves alone within a comparable timeframe, given competing capital priorities across a nine-hospital system. This is the primary reason this paper treats the psychiatric emergency unit as time-sensitive, while treating telepsychiatry, the observation protocol, and bridge clinics — none of which depend on this specific grant cycle — as approaches the system can and should begin administratively in parallel, funded from operating budgets, over the next two to three quarters.

## 5. Answering the Objections

Four objections have been raised consistently in internal system discussions and in early conversations with county and state partners. We take each in turn.

### 5.1 Rural travel distance

The objection: a single-hub psychiatric emergency unit, most plausibly sited centrally, does little for a family in a rural county four hours away, and may simply relocate rather than solve the access problem for the majority of the geographic service area.

This objection is correct as far as it goes, and it is the central reason this paper does not recommend the psychiatric emergency unit as a stand-alone solution. The unit's design intent is to serve as a regional receiving and stabilization hub for children who are already being transferred or who present at or near the hub site, not as a rural access solution. Rural reach is instead the explicit design purpose of telepsychiatry consultation and, to a lesser extent, mobile crisis teams. The recommended sequencing in Section 6 pairs the hub unit with telepsychiatry precisely so that rural sites gain same-day specialist support and improved local disposition-making even before, or regardless of whether, a given child is ultimately transferred to the hub.

### 5.2 Psychiatrist recruitment

The objection: none of these approaches matter if the system cannot recruit the child and adolescent psychiatrists needed to staff them, given a national shortage that no single regional system can out-compete.

This objection is largely correct and is treated in this paper as a binding constraint rather than a solvable input. The financial and operational models above are deliberately built to minimize new psychiatrist full-time-equivalent requirements. Telepsychiatry uses contracted specialist time shared across all fourteen sites rather than requiring each site to recruit independently, and can draw on psychiatrists located outside the immediate labor market, including academic partners and national telepsychiatry networks. The psychiatric emergency unit is designed around psychiatric nursing and milieu staff as the primary on-site presence, with psychiatric medical coverage provided through a combination of limited on-site hours and telepsychiatry backup rather than requiring full-time in-unit psychiatrist coverage around the clock. Bridge clinics rely more heavily on psychologists, licensed clinical social workers, and psychiatric nurse practitioners than on psychiatrists specifically. None of this eliminates the shortage; it is intended to reduce the system's exposure to it.

### 5.3 Medicaid rates

The objection: Minnesota and Wisconsin Medicaid reimbursement for crisis stabilization and psychiatric emergency services is inconsistent, in some categories nonexistent, and in no case generous enough to make these investments self-sustaining.

This objection is also correct, and Section 4.4 quantifies its consequence directly: under current fee-for-service rates alone, the psychiatric emergency unit runs a net annual cost to the system of roughly $1.6 million. This paper does not treat that as an argument against the investment, but as the specific, quantified policy ask this paper is making of the state Medicaid officials and payers in its audience: a defined crisis stabilization add-on rate, and managed care case-rate alignment consistent with it, would move the system from a $1.6 million annual subsidy to a break-even position, without requiring any change in the clinical model itself. We consider this the single highest-leverage policy lever available to the state relative to its cost, since it does not require new state capital, only a rate-setting decision applied to a service the state already has an interest in seeing exist.

### 5.4 Whether upstream investment moves volume at all

The objection: none of this addresses whether investing "upstream" — in telepsychiatry, bridge clinics, mobile crisis — actually reduces the number of children who end up boarding in the emergency department, as opposed to simply adding cost on top of an unchanged boarding population.

This is the most serious objection, because it questions the core causal assumption behind three of the five approaches under discussion. We do not claim certainty here. What the five-year data in Section 2 does support is a more modest and, we think, more defensible claim: a meaningful share of boarded children — plausibly overlapping with the population whose stays fall in the shorter part of the distribution rather than the 90th-percentile tail — are boarding not because they require an inpatient bed that does not exist, but because no safe outpatient alternative was identified at the point of disposition. Bridge clinics and telepsychiatry are aimed specifically at that population: children who could be safely discharged with a guaranteed near-term follow-up, if that follow-up option existed and if the emergency physician had specialist input confirming it was appropriate. For the population at the long tail of the distribution — the 18 percent boarding more than three days, disproportionately represented in the restraint and seclusion figures — the constraint is more plausibly true bed scarcity and transfer refusal, which upstream outpatient investment will not resolve; that population is precisely who the psychiatric emergency unit and, over a longer horizon, mobile crisis stabilization beds are designed to serve. We therefore do not present upstream investment as a claim that volume disappears, but as a claim that it can be redistributed away from the highest-harm, highest-cost tail of the current distribution and toward shorter, safer, and less resource-intensive pathways — a claim that is testable, and which we recommend the system commit to measuring explicitly (see Conclusion) rather than assuming.

## Conclusion

Five years of consistent data across fourteen emergency departments show a regional system holding children in behavioral health crisis for a median of 31 hours and, for one in ten, well over four days, inside a bed of a general emergency department that was never designed for that purpose, at a measurable cost in restraint and seclusion incidents, sitter labor, and displaced emergency capacity. The underlying constraint — 38 inpatient child psychiatric beds for 1.4 million people, and a 44 percent transfer refusal rate against even that limited supply — is not a problem any single hospital, or any single intervention, can solve alone.

This paper does not recommend choosing one approach from the five under discussion. It recommends sequencing them according to the different problems each is built to solve. Telepsychiatry consultation and a pediatric observation protocol can be deployed within two to three quarters, at comparatively low cost, funded from operating budgets, and should begin now regardless of the outcome of any grant application. Bridge clinics should follow within the year, aimed specifically at the substantial population of children who board today for lack of a credible outpatient landing spot rather than lack of an inpatient bed. The twelve-bay psychiatric emergency unit is the correct target for the September 2026 state capital grant round, not because it solves rural access or reduces total system cost on its own, but because it is the only intervention under discussion that changes the physical and clinical environment for the highest-harm population — the children at the long tail of the boarding distribution — and because the capital opportunity to build it will not recur on a comparable timeline if this cycle is missed. Mobile crisis teams with stabilization beds represent the longest-horizon, highest-uncertainty investment and should proceed as a deliberate, county-partnered pilot rather than a system-wide commitment, with explicit measurement of whether they in fact reduce emergency department presentation rather than simply add a parallel point of contact.

None of this closes the gap between 38 beds and 1.4 million people. It is offered as the most defensible use of the capital and policy tools actually available to this system and this region in the next eighteen months, and as an explicit invitation to the state Medicaid officials and payers in this paper's audience: a defined crisis stabilization reimbursement rate is the single policy change that would do the most, at the least direct cost to the state, to make the rest of this plan sustainable.
