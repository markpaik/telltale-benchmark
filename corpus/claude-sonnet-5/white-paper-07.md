## Abstract

Between 2021 and 2025, 118 school districts across Iowa, Nebraska, and South Dakota operated tutoring programs under federal pandemic-relief funding, generating session-level records for 61,000 students that the Prairie States Education Research Collaborative has compiled into the region's largest tutoring dataset. Median attendance in scheduled sessions was 63 percent, and per-student annual cost ranged from $1,180 to $4,600 depending on staffing model. When relief funds expired, 74 of the 118 districts cut or eliminated tutoring for the 2025–26 school year, even as a new $18 million state grant program drew $71 million in requests — evidence that districts want to continue this work but cannot sustain it on their own budgets. This white paper defines the instructional and fiscal problem created by the funding cliff, presents four years of collaborative attendance and outcome data across five delivery models, and compares those models on cost, dosage, and achievement results. It then develops a concrete implementation and funding plan for a representative 3,400-student district, addressing master-schedule placement, tutor recruitment and retention, student selection, and fidelity monitoring. The paper closes by answering the four objections raised most often in the field: loss of instructional time, equity in who is selected, union questions about paraprofessional duties, and the reporting burden placed on principals. We conclude that a hybrid model — certified-teacher-led groups during the school day for the highest-need students, supplemented by a trained and well-retained paraprofessional or tutor-corps layer for moderate-need students — combined with a blended state-local-philanthropic funding stack, is the most defensible path for districts of this size going into 2026–27.

## 1. The Problem Districts Face

Three years of federal Elementary and Secondary School Emergency Relief (ESSER) funding allowed districts across Iowa, Nebraska, and South Dakota to build tutoring infrastructure at a scale the region had never before attempted. That funding is gone. Districts are now being asked to decide, with local operating dollars, whether and how to continue a set of interventions that were designed, staffed, and scheduled under emergency conditions.

The decision districts face is not simply "keep it or cut it." Our data show that tutoring, done well, produces measurable achievement gains at a cost that is a fraction of a certified teacher's annual salary per pupil served. Done poorly — with low attendance, high tutor turnover, and unclear selection criteria — it produces little benefit for a nontrivial cost and generates political friction with principals, unions, and families who question why some students are pulled and others are not.

The scale of the retrenchment already underway makes the stakes concrete. Of the 118 districts in our dataset, 74 — nearly two in three — cut or ended tutoring programs entering the 2025–26 school year once relief funding lapsed. At the same time, when the state introduced an $18 million competitive tutoring grant this spring, districts submitted $71 million in requests, a four-to-one oversubscription rate. Districts are not walking away from tutoring because they believe it does not work. They are walking away because they have not been given — or have not built for themselves — a model that is affordable, staffable, and defensible at the building level without emergency money behind it.

This paper is written to close that gap. It draws on the collaborative's four-year, 61,000-student dataset to answer four questions a superintendent or school board member must answer before the 2026–27 budget is finalized:

1. What does the evidence actually show about which tutoring models produce results, and at what cost?
2. What does a workable master schedule, staffing plan, and student-selection process look like for a mid-sized district — we use 3,400 students as our reference case — operating without relief dollars?
3. How can a district fund a program at this scale using a realistic blend of state, local, and philanthropic revenue?
4. How should a district answer the four objections — instructional time, equity, union duties, and reporting burden — that arise in nearly every board meeting where tutoring is discussed?

## 2. Four Years of Collaborative Data

### 2.1 Data Description

The Prairie States Education Research Collaborative has collected session-level tutoring attendance and outcome records from 118 partner districts in Iowa, Nebraska, and South Dakota continuously since the fall of 2021. The dataset covers 61,000 unique students and roughly 4.1 million individual tutoring sessions across five academic years (2021–22 through 2025–26, the last of which is partial as of this writing). Districts range from rural systems of under 400 students to two urban districts exceeding 12,000. Table 1 summarizes the composition of the sample.

**Table 1. Composition of the Collaborative Tutoring Dataset, 2021–2025**

| Characteristic | Value |
|---|---|
| Districts contributing data | 118 |
| Total states | 3 (Iowa, Nebraska, South Dakota) |
| Unique students served | 61,000 |
| Median district enrollment | 1,650 |
| Median annual tutoring caseload per district | 340 students |
| Grade bands represented | K–2, 3–5, 6–8, 9–12 |
| Subjects covered | Reading/ELA (58% of sessions), Mathematics (39%), Combined/other (3%) |
| Years of continuous data | 2021–22 through 2025–26 (partial) |

### 2.2 Attendance

Attendance in scheduled tutoring sessions is the single strongest predictor of achievement gain in our dataset, and it is also the metric on which programs most often underperform. Median attendance across all districts and all four full years was 63 percent of scheduled sessions — meaning that a student scheduled for three sessions a week over a 30-week block attended, on average, fewer than two. Attendance varied substantially by delivery model and by whether tutoring was scheduled inside or outside the regular school day (Table 2).

**Table 2. Median Session Attendance by Scheduling Placement, 2021–2025**

| Scheduling Placement | Median Attendance | Share of Total Sessions |
|---|---|---|
| Embedded in school day (replacing elective/intervention block) | 79% | 41% |
| Embedded in school day (replacing core instruction) | 71% | 12% |
| Before/after school, on campus | 52% | 31% |
| Before/after school, off campus or virtual | 44% | 16% |
| **All models, blended** | **63%** | **100%** |

The pattern is consistent across all four years: tutoring scheduled inside the school day, in a dedicated block rather than by pulling students from core instruction, attends at roughly double the rate of before- or after-school programming. This single finding drives much of the implementation guidance in Section 4.

### 2.3 Cost

Annual cost per student served ranged from $1,180 to $4,600 across the dataset, driven almost entirely by staffing model and group size rather than by subject, grade band, or geography. Hourly wage rates for the four staffing categories used across our partner districts were:

- Certified teachers: $52/hour
- Paraprofessionals: $22/hour
- Community college tutor corps members: $17/hour
- Vendor virtual tutors: $38/hour

Group size is the second major cost driver. A certified teacher working with a group of three students three times a week for 30 weeks costs roughly $2,340 per student annually; the same teacher working with a group of six costs roughly $1,170 per student. Districts that used small, high-intensity certified-teacher groups (2:1 or 3:1) for their highest-need students, paired with larger paraprofessional or tutor-corps groups (6:1 to 8:1) for moderate-need students, consistently landed in the $1,800–$2,600 blended per-student range — below the $4,600 ceiling of the most intensive single-model programs and well above the $1,180 floor of the leanest virtual-vendor programs.

### 2.4 Tutor Turnover

Average annual turnover among tutoring staff — across all four staffing categories — was 41 percent. Turnover was lowest among certified teachers tutoring within their own building (22 percent) and highest among community college tutor corps members (58 percent) and vendor virtual tutors, whose year-over-year assignment to the same district averaged only 51 percent. Turnover matters because our data show a clear, negative relationship between tutor turnover and both attendance and achievement gain: programs in the top quartile for tutor retention produced achievement effects roughly 60 percent larger than programs in the bottom quartile, holding dosage constant. This finding is discussed further in Section 3 and directly informs the retention strategy in Section 4.2.

### 2.5 The Funding Cliff in the Data

Seventy-four of the 118 districts in our dataset cut or eliminated tutoring for 2025–26 once ESSER funds expired. The 44 districts that continued tutoring share three characteristics disproportionately: they used a hybrid staffing model rather than an all-certified-teacher model; they had embedded tutoring in the master schedule rather than running it before or after school; and they had secured at least one non-federal funding source — state, local levy, or philanthropic — before relief funds ran out. These three characteristics form the backbone of the implementation and funding recommendations that follow.

## 3. Comparing Delivery Models on Cost and Results

Districts in our dataset used five delivery models, sometimes in combination: teacher-led small groups inside the school day, paraprofessional-led instruction using scripted materials, a community college tutor corps, a virtual vendor platform, and various hybrids of these four. Table 3 summarizes the four-year comparison on cost, attendance, dosage, and achievement effect size. Effect sizes are reported in standard deviation units on within-district, within-grade comparisons of tutored versus matched non-tutored students, pooled across reading and math and adjusted for prior achievement.

**Table 3. Delivery Model Comparison, 2021–2025 (Pooled Across 118 Districts)**

| Model | Median Cost/Student/Year | Median Attendance | Median Sessions Completed/Year | Achievement Effect Size (SD) | Annual Staff Turnover |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Certified teacher, small group, in-school-day | $2,890 | 79% | 71 | 0.18 | 22% |
| Paraprofessional, scripted curriculum, in-school-day | $1,540 | 74% | 66 | 0.11 | 34% |
| Community college tutor corps | $1,340 | 58% | 48 | 0.07 | 58% |
| Vendor virtual tutor | $1,180 | 47% | 37 | 0.05 | 49%* |
| Hybrid (certified teacher + paraprofessional/corps layer) | $2,140 | 70% | 61 | 0.15 | 31% |

*Vendor turnover reflects year-over-year continuity of the same tutor with the same district, not employment turnover within the vendor firm.

Three patterns are consistent across all four years of data and worth stating plainly for a governing board.

**Dosage, not model label, is the proximate driver of results.** When we control statistically for sessions completed, the achievement gap between the certified-teacher model and the paraprofessional model narrows by roughly half. Certified teachers produce better outcomes disproportionately because their students attend more often and stay in the program longer, not because instruction delivered by a credentialed teacher is inherently more effective session-for-session on scripted material. This matters because it means a well-run paraprofessional program with strong attendance can outperform a poorly attended certified-teacher program — a finding that should reassure districts that cannot staff an all-certified-teacher model.

**Group size interacts with staffing category.** Certified-teacher groups in our dataset averaged 3.4 students; paraprofessional groups averaged 5.8; tutor corps groups averaged 6.9; virtual vendor "groups" (often asynchronous or loosely synchronous) averaged 9.2. Effect sizes decline as group size rises within every staffing category, but the decline is steepest for paraprofessional and corps models, where effect size drops by roughly 0.03 SD for every additional student beyond a group of four. This is the empirical basis for the group-size ceilings recommended in Section 4.

**Cost-effectiveness favors the hybrid.** Dividing effect size by cost per student, the hybrid model delivers the second-best return of the five approaches (0.070 SD per $1,000 spent), trailing only the paraprofessional in-school-day model (0.071 SD per $1,000) and outperforming the pure certified-teacher model (0.062 SD per $1,000). The pure certified-teacher model produces the largest absolute gain but at a cost that few districts can sustain at scale without relief funding; the hybrid captures most of that gain at 74 percent of the cost.

Figure 1 illustrates the relationship between sessions attended and achievement gain across all five models, pooling students into deciles of attendance.

**Figure 1. Achievement Effect Size by Attendance Decile, All Models Pooled, 2021–2025**

*(Descriptive summary in place of graphic: Effect size rises roughly linearly from 0.02 SD among students in the lowest attendance decile — fewer than 20 sessions completed — to 0.31 SD among students in the top decile, who completed 90 or more sessions. The relationship is steepest between the 40th and 80th percentiles of attendance, suggesting a meaningful dosage threshold rather than a smooth linear return at every level of participation.)*

The dosage threshold visible in Figure 1 has direct implementation consequences. Programs that enroll students but cannot sustain attendance above roughly 60 sessions a year are unlikely to produce a detectable achievement effect regardless of which staffing model delivers them. This is the single most important reason 74 districts saw disappointing results and chose to cut programs rather than fund them locally: many were running before/after-school models with attendance below the 60-session threshold, and the achievement return did not justify the cost once free federal money was gone.

## 4. Implementation Model for a 3,400-Student District

Districts of roughly 3,400 students — large enough to have a central office and dedicated instructional staff, small enough that a single misstep in scheduling or staffing affects a visible share of the student population — represent the modal size among districts still weighing whether to continue tutoring for 2026–27. Corey Lindenbaum's district, which ran tutoring continuously for four years at 2,800 students, is close to this profile and its experience informs the recommendations below. We present a concrete plan covering four domains: master schedule, tutor recruitment and retention, student selection, and fidelity monitoring.

### 4.1 Master Schedule Constraints

The attendance data in Section 2.2 make the scheduling decision the most consequential one a district will make. Embedding tutoring inside the school day, in a dedicated block, produces attendance 27 to 35 percentage points higher than before/after-school placement. For a 3,400-student district, we recommend the following structure.

**Elementary (K–5).** Build a 30-minute "WIN" (What I Need) block into the daily schedule for every grade level, staffed on a rotating basis so that classroom teachers, interventionists, and tutors all have a defined role during the same protected window. Tutoring groups meet during this block three to four days a week. This avoids pulling students from core reading or math instruction — the single objection raised most consistently by principals and teachers (see Section 6.1) — because the block is built as protected intervention time for all students, not carved out of existing instruction.

**Secondary (6–12).** Use an elective or advisory period, or a common intervention period built into the bell schedule (a school-wide "flex" or "seminar" period of 40–45 minutes, three days a week), rather than pulling students from core courses. Where no such period exists, districts in our dataset that added one — even at the cost of shortening five other periods by three to four minutes each — saw attendance rise by a median of 22 percentage points the following year.

**Staffing coverage.** A 3,400-student district serving an estimated 12 percent of students in tutoring (roughly 410 students, consistent with median caseloads among comparably sized districts in our dataset) requires approximately the staffing shown in Table 4.

**Table 4. Recommended Staffing Plan, 3,400-Student District, 410 Students Served**

| Tier | Target Population | Group Size | Staff Type | FTE Required | Sessions/Week | Annual Cost/Student |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 3 (highest need, ~15% of served) | ~60 students | 3:1 | Certified teacher | 6.7 FTE (can be built from existing interventionists + 2–3 new hires) | 4 | $2,890 |
| Tier 2 (moderate need, ~55% of served) | ~225 students | 5:1 | Paraprofessional, scripted curriculum | 5.6 FTE | 3 | $1,540 |
| Tier 1 (targeted support, ~30% of served) | ~125 students | 8:1 | Community college tutor corps | 2.0 FTE-equivalent (part-time corps members) | 3 | $1,340 |
| **Blended program total** | **410 students** | — | — | **~14.3 FTE-equivalent** | — | **~$1,780 weighted average** |

This tiered structure follows the cost-effectiveness finding in Section 3: concentrate the most expensive, highest-return staffing (certified teachers) on the smallest number of students with the greatest need, and use lower-cost staffing with larger group sizes for students who need reinforcement rather than intensive remediation. At a weighted average of roughly $1,780 per student, a 410-student program costs the district approximately $730,000 annually before accounting for grant or philanthropic offsets — a figure we return to in Section 5.

### 4.2 Tutor Recruitment and Retention

Given that turnover is the second-strongest predictor of program quality after attendance, retention strategy deserves as much attention as recruitment.

**Recruitment.** For the certified-teacher tier, districts in our dataset had the most success recruiting from within — offering a stipend (median $3,400 per semester in our sample) to classroom teachers, interventionists, and instructional coaches already on staff, rather than hiring externally. This reduces onboarding time, keeps institutional knowledge of the curriculum and students in the building, and was associated with the lowest turnover rate of any staffing category (22 percent). For the paraprofessional tier, the most successful districts recruited from existing paraprofessional and educational associate staff and provided a stipend or hourly differential (median $3.50/hour above base pay) for tutoring duties, rather than hiring a separate tutoring-only paraprofessional workforce. For the community college tutor corps tier, partnership agreements with regional community colleges — several districts in our dataset partner with Kirkwood, Western Iowa Tech, and Mitchell Technical College — that build tutoring into education-pathway coursework or work-study produced the most stable pipelines.

**Retention.** Four practices distinguished the top quartile of districts on tutor retention:

1. **Predictable, protected schedules.** Tutors whose sessions were never bumped for assemblies, testing, or substitute coverage had 35 percent lower turnover than tutors whose schedules were frequently disrupted.
2. **A defined career or compensation ladder**, even a modest one — for example, a $1/hour increase after one full year of tutoring service — retained paraprofessional and corps tutors at meaningfully higher rates.
3. **Structured onboarding and ongoing coaching**, not just initial training. Districts that provided biweekly coaching check-ins (30 minutes, typically led by an instructional coach or the certified-teacher tier lead) saw paraprofessional turnover fall from a sample median of 34 percent to 24 percent.
4. **Explicit recognition and integration into the building's professional culculture** — including tutors in staff meetings, professional development days, and school communications — rather than treating them as a separate, temporary workforce.

We recommend a 3,400-student district budget for a 0.5 FTE tutoring program coordinator whose primary responsibilities are recruitment, onboarding, coaching, and schedule protection. Districts that created this role, even part-time, saw turnover fall by an average of 9 percentage points across all staffing tiers — a return that more than offsets the coordinator's salary in retained program quality and reduced re-hiring costs.

### 4.3 Student Selection

Selection is the domain most closely tied to the equity objection addressed in Section 6.2, and it deserves a transparent, data-driven process rather than teacher discretion alone, which our data show introduces substantial inconsistency.

We recommend a three-signal selection model:

1. **A universal screening or interim assessment score** (e.g., a fall benchmark reading or math score below the 25th percentile nationally, or more than one grade level below standard on a district-adopted interim assessment) as the primary, objective gateway.
2. **A secondary indicator of instructional need** — attendance rate, prior-year course failure, or English learner progress data — used to prioritize within the pool identified by the screening score, particularly when demand exceeds capacity.
3. **A capped teacher-referral allowance**, limited to no more than 15 percent of a program's total seats, to capture students whose need is evident to classroom teachers but not fully reflected in screening data (for example, a student with an IEP whose disability affects test performance but not underlying need).

Districts that used this combined model, rather than teacher referral alone, showed selected populations that were more representative of the district's low-income, English learner, and students-with-disabilities subgroups relative to those subgroups' share of students scoring below the screening threshold — a direct response to the equity concerns discussed in Section 6.2. We recommend that selection lists be reviewed twice a year (fall and after winter benchmark testing) so that students who catch up can exit and make room for students who have fallen behind, and that districts publish, at minimum to their school board, the demographic composition of the tutoring caseload relative to the district's overall enrollment each year.

### 4.4 Fidelity Monitoring

Fidelity monitoring is the domain most directly connected to the reporting-burden objection (Section 6.4), so the goal is a system that produces reliable data without adding substantial new work for building principals.

We recommend four elements, each designed to use data districts are already collecting where possible:

1. **Attendance, captured at the session level through the district's existing student information system** rather than a separate spreadsheet. Several districts in our sample built tutoring sessions into their SIS as a course or activity period, which allowed attendance to flow automatically into the same reporting the district already produces for other purposes.
2. **A quarterly, one-page dashboard per building**, produced by the analytics or assessment office rather than by the principal, showing enrollment, attendance rate, sessions completed, and interim benchmark movement for tutored students. This shifts the reporting burden from the building level to the central office, addressing the principal-burden objection directly.
3. **A brief (15-minute) coaching observation of each tutor twice per semester**, using a shared, simple rubric focused on group size adherence, use of the scripted curriculum or lesson plan, and student engagement — conducted by the program coordinator described in Section 4.2, not the principal.
4. **An annual outcomes review presented to the school board**, comparing tutored students' interim and summative assessment growth to a matched comparison group, following the methodology the collaborative uses in this dataset.

Districts that implemented all four elements had, on average, 11 percentage points higher attendance and were more likely to sustain their programs into a second and third year without relief funding — likely because building-level staff experienced the program as supported rather than as an unfunded reporting mandate.

## 5. Funding Model

The oversubscription of the state's new $18 million grant program — $71 million in requests against $18 million available — confirms that no single funding source will cover this work going forward. We recommend a blended funding stack, illustrated in Table 5 for the 3,400-student, 410-student-caseload program costed in Section 4.1 at approximately $730,000 annually.

**Table 5. Illustrative Funding Stack, 3,400-Student District, ~$730,000 Annual Program Cost**

| Source | Illustrative Share | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| State tutoring grant (competitive) | 25–30% (~$185,000–$220,000) | Given 4:1 oversubscription, districts should not build a budget assuming full funding; treat as a competitive supplement, not a base |
| Local general fund / levy | 35–40% (~$255,000–$290,000) | The stable core; districts that sustained programs past year one uniformly had committed local dollars, not solely grant dependence |
| Title I / federal formula funds (non-relief) | 15–20% (~$110,000–$145,000) | Legally usable for tutoring targeted at Title I-eligible students; already a recurring, non-expiring revenue source for most districts |
| Philanthropic / community college partnership in-kind | 10–15% (~$75,000–$110,000) | Tutor corps wage subsidy, coaching support, or local foundation grants; several districts in our sample secured multi-year commitments from community foundations specifically for tutoring |
| Family/community contribution (where applicable) | 0–5% | Reserved for enrichment or optional extension components only; core Tier 2/3 services should remain fully funded to avoid excluding high-need students |

Two principles should guide how a district builds this stack. First, the state grant should be treated as a supplement that funds the highest-cost tier (certified-teacher small groups) or a specific expansion, not as the base on which the whole program depends — the 4:1 oversubscription rate this year means many applicants will not be funded, and a program built entirely around a grant that a district does not receive is not a program at all. Second, the local general fund commitment should be set first, sized to cover the Tier 2 and Tier 1 layers described in Table 4 (paraprofessional and tutor-corps tiers, which are also the most cost-effective per Section 3), so that the program is not existentially at risk if state or philanthropic dollars do not materialize in a given year.

For governing boards weighing whether $730,000 is defensible against other budget priorities, it is useful to note that this figure represents approximately 0.9 percent of a typical 3,400-student district's total general fund budget, and produces an estimated aggregate achievement gain — using the hybrid model's 0.15 SD effect size applied to 410 students — roughly equivalent to what the collaborative's data show is otherwise associated with a one-percentage-point reduction in class size across the entire district, at a fraction of the cost such a reduction would require.

## 6. Answering the Field's Objections

Four objections surface in nearly every district conversation about continuing tutoring without relief funding. Our data speak directly to each.

### 6.1 "We Cannot Afford to Give Up Instructional Time"

This objection is best answered by the scheduling data in Section 2.2 and 4.1, not by argument. Programs that pull students from core instruction attend at meaningfully lower rates (71 percent) than programs built into a dedicated intervention or advisory block (79 percent), and pulling students from core instruction is also the configuration most likely to generate teacher and parent complaints. The recommended structure — a protected WIN or flex block that exists for all students, with tutoring as one use of that time — does not remove instructional time from any student; it reallocates a block of time that already exists in most master schedules for advisory, homeroom, or enrichment purposes. Districts without such a block should treat creating one as a scheduling investment that pays for itself in attendance, not as a concession made specifically for tutoring.

### 6.2 "Selection Raises Equity Concerns"

Left to informal teacher referral, selection in our dataset showed measurable inconsistency: two students with identical benchmark scores had, on average, a 34-percentage-point difference in likelihood of being referred to tutoring depending on which teacher taught them. The three-signal selection model in Section 4.3 — objective screening first, secondary indicators for prioritization, and a capped referral allowance — directly narrows that inconsistency and produces a caseload whose demographic composition districts can defend publicly. We recommend districts treat the annual demographic report described in Section 4.3 as a standing agenda item for the board's academic or equity committee, not a one-time exercise, so that drift back toward informal selection is caught early.

### 6.3 "What Does This Mean for Paraprofessional Duties, and Does It Raise Union Questions?"

Paraprofessional-led tutoring using scripted, structured curriculum was the second-most cost-effective model in our dataset (Section 3) and produced measurable results, but it does represent an expansion of instructional responsibility beyond many paraprofessionals' existing job descriptions and, in districts with paraprofessional bargaining units, beyond existing contract language. We recommend three steps, all of which appear in the districts in our sample that avoided grievances: first, tutoring duties and the associated stipend or wage differential should be defined in an explicit memorandum of understanding or contract addendum with the relevant bargaining unit before the program launches, not after; second, participation should be voluntary and compensated as extra-duty time distinct from base contractual duties, consistent with how most districts already treat coaching or extracurricular stipends; third, the scripted-curriculum design itself — which specifies content, pacing, and materials — should be explicitly framed in bargaining conversations as a support that keeps paraprofessional duties within a defined, trainable scope rather than an open-ended expansion into instructional planning, which is typically reserved for certified staff under most Midwest state statutes and local contracts. Districts should consult their own legal counsel and bargaining unit on specifics, but the pattern in our sample is that early, explicit MOUs prevented the disputes that arose in districts which began tutoring first and negotiated duties later.

### 6.4 "Principals Cannot Absorb Another Reporting Requirement"

The reporting burden objection is legitimate and is the objection most directly tied to whether a program survives past its first year in our dataset. The fidelity monitoring design in Section 4.4 is built specifically to address it: attendance flows through the existing student information system rather than a parallel spreadsheet; the quarterly dashboard is produced centrally by the analytics or assessment office, not compiled by the principal; and tutor observation and coaching are the responsibility of a program coordinator, not the building principal. Districts that shifted these functions away from the principal's desk had lower principal turnover complaints related to tutoring and were more likely to report satisfaction with the program in the collaborative's annual district survey. We recommend that any district considering tutoring for 2026–27 build the 0.5 FTE program coordinator role into its budget from the outset rather than treating central monitoring capacity as an optional add-on.

## 7. Conclusion

The choice facing districts in Iowa, Nebraska, and South Dakota this year is not whether tutoring works — four years and 61,000 students of collaborative data show that it does, with attendance and dosage, not staffing credential alone, as the primary driver of results. The choice is whether districts will build a version of tutoring that is affordable and sustainable without emergency federal money behind it. The 74 districts that cut programs this year did not, in most cases, cut because tutoring failed; they cut because the version of tutoring they built during the relief period — often before-or-after-school, single-staffing-model, loosely monitored — was not designed to survive the transition to ordinary district budgets.

The evidence in this paper points toward a specific, buildable alternative for a district of roughly 3,400 students: a tiered, hybrid staffing model concentrating certified-teacher time on the highest-need students and paraprofessional or tutor-corps time on moderate-need students; tutoring embedded in a protected daily or near-daily school-day block rather than before or after school; deliberate recruitment and retention practices built around internal staff, defined compensation ladders, and a dedicated part-time coordinator; a transparent, three-signal student selection process; fidelity monitoring that shifts reporting burden away from principals and into central analytics functions; and a blended funding stack that treats competitive state grants as a supplement rather than a foundation. At an estimated $1,780 per student and roughly 0.9 percent of a typical district's general fund, this model is within reach for districts of this size, and it directly answers the four objections that have stalled these conversations in board rooms across the region.

The collaborative will continue to track outcomes among the 44 districts that sustained tutoring into 2025–26 and will report updated cost, attendance, and achievement figures as the state's new grant program completes its first funding cycle. Districts designing programs for 2026–27 are encouraged to contact the collaborative directly for district-specific data extracts and technical assistance in applying the selection, scheduling, and funding frameworks presented here.
