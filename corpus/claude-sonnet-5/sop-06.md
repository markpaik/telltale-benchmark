## 1. Purpose

This procedure establishes the required sequence, timing, documentation, and competency standards for reprocessing flexible gastroscopes and colonoscopes at Klamath Valley Regional Medical Center (KVRMC) so that every scope used on a patient is verifiably clean, leak-free, disinfected, dried, and traceable from bedside use through storage and reuse. It exists to close the gaps identified during the spring cluster investigation, in which three patients acquired a common strain of *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* and borescope inspection found channel defects, retained debris, or damaged distal ends in 6 of 22 scopes in inventory. This procedure implements the corrective actions required by the Joint Commission requirements for improvement issued in May and the state health authority's request for a plan of correction.

## 2. Scope

This procedure applies to all flexible gastroscopes and colonoscopes used in the KVRMC GI Lab, all automated endoscope reprocessors (AERs), all personnel who preclean, transport, manually clean, verify, disinfect, dry, store, or handle these scopes, and all loaner or after-hours cases. It does not cover rigid endoscopes, bronchoscopes, or duodenoscopes with elevator mechanisms, which are addressed in separate procedures (SPD-END-015 and SPD-END-016).

## 3. Definitions

- **AER (Automated Endoscope Reprocessor):** Machine that performs high-level disinfection (HLD) via an automated cycle using a validated disinfectant.
- **ATP Test:** Adenosine triphosphate bioluminescence assay used to verify absence of organic residue on cleaned scope surfaces and channels; result reported in relative light units (RLU).
- **Borescope:** Fiberoptic inspection camera used to visually examine the interior of endoscope channels for damage, debris, or discoloration.
- **Hang Time:** The validated maximum interval a scope may remain in vertical storage in the drying cabinet before reprocessing is required again prior to patient use; at KVRMC this is 7 calendar days per manufacturer IFU and AAMI ST91.
- **HLD:** High-Level Disinfection, the minimum required processing level for semi-critical flexible endoscopes.
- **Leak Test:** A pressure test performed manually or via AER to detect breaches in the scope's internal channels or external sheath before manual cleaning.
- **Loaner Scope:** An endoscope not owned by KVRMC, brought in temporarily from a manufacturer or another facility.
- **Protein Residue Test:** A colorimetric swab test used in addition to or as an alternative to ATP testing to detect residual protein in channels.
- **Scope Tracking System (STS):** The barcode-based software system that logs every step of reprocessing to a specific scope serial number, patient, technician, and timestamp.
- **Technician:** Any staff member certified in flexible endoscope reprocessing under this procedure, regardless of title.

**Adaeze Nwosu, Manager, Sterile Processing and Endoscope Reprocessing** — owns this procedure; ensures staffing, competency records, and daily compliance with each step; approves exception-path decisions during business hours; escalates unresolved equipment or staffing gaps to the VP of Quality and Patient Safety.

**Reprocessing Technicians** — perform leak testing, manual cleaning, cleaning verification testing, AER loading/unloading, drying, and storage; complete STS documentation at each step; refuse to process any scope that fails a checkpoint until the exception path is followed.

**Procedure Room Nurses** — perform bedside precleaning immediately after scope withdrawal from the patient; initiate the STS record tying the scope to the patient and procedure; place the scope in the designated transport container.

**Transport Staff** — carry contained, precleaned scopes from the procedure room to SPD within the 60-minute delay window defined in Section 6.2; scan the STS barcode at pickup and drop-off.

**Infection Preventionist** — reviews STS and culture data weekly; leads root-cause investigation whenever a healthcare-associated infection is suspected to involve a scope; has authority to quarantine any scope pending investigation.

**Biomedical Engineering** — maintains and calibrates AERs, borescopes, and leak testers; performs quarterly water quality testing of the AER water supply; documents preventive maintenance and repairs in the equipment log.

**Department Educator** — conducts initial competency training for all new technicians and annual competency renewal for all reprocessing staff; maintains competency records; updates training content when manufacturer IFUs change.

## 5. Equipment and Materials

Automated endoscope reprocessors (2 units, GI Lab SPD room); manufacturer IFUs for each scope model (binder maintained at each workstation and in STS document library); scope tracking system with barcode scanners at bedside, transport, SPD intake, cleaning station, AER, drying cabinet, and storage; drying cabinets with HEPA-filtered forced air; borescope (Olympus-compatible, calibrated quarterly); ATP test kits (Hygiena or equivalent, lot-controlled); protein residue test swabs; leak testers (manual and AER-integrated); manufacturer-specified cleaning brushes in correct diameters for each channel type; enzymatic detergent; timers at each workstation.

## 6. Procedure

### 6.1 Bedside Precleaning (Procedure Room Nurse)

1. Immediately upon withdrawal of the scope from the patient, and before the scope is set down, wipe the exterior with an enzymatic detergent wipe from insertion tube to control head.
2. Suction enzymatic detergent solution through the air/water and biopsy channels until visibly clean fluid passes, minimum 90 seconds.
3. Flush air and water channels per manufacturer IFU using the manufacturer-specified adapter.
4. Detach removable components (valves, caps) per IFU and place in the transport container with the scope.
5. Scan the STS barcode on the scope and the patient wristband to open the reprocessing record; enter the performing physician and procedure type.
6. Place the scope in a closed, leak-proof transport container labeled with a biohazard label and the time precleaning was completed.
7. Notify transport staff; do not leave a precleaned scope unattended outside the transport container for more than 5 minutes.

### 6.2 Transport (Transport Staff)

8. Scan the STS barcode at pickup, confirming the precleaning timestamp from Step 6.
9. Deliver the scope to SPD within 60 minutes of precleaning completion. If the 60-minute window will be exceeded, follow the Delayed Reprocessing exception path (Section 7.3).
10. Scan the STS barcode at SPD intake to close the transport leg of the record and open the SPD leg.

### 6.3 Leak Testing (Reprocessing Technician)

11. Don personal protective equipment: fluid-resistant gown, gloves, eye protection, and mask.
12. Remove the scope from the transport container and inspect the exterior visually for any obvious cuts, kinks, or damage before testing.
13. Attach the leak tester per manufacturer IFU and pressurize the scope according to the specified pressure setting for that model.
14. Submerge the scope fully in water and observe for 30 continuous seconds, watching for a steady stream of bubbles from any point on the insertion tube, bending section, or distal end.
15. If no leak is observed, record "Pass" in the STS leak test field, initial, and proceed to manual cleaning (Section 6.4).
16. If a leak is observed, stop immediately and follow the Failed Leak Test exception path (Section 7.1). Do not proceed to manual cleaning.

### 6.4 Manual Cleaning (Reprocessing Technician)

17. Fully immerse the scope in a basin of fresh enzymatic detergent solution mixed at the manufacturer-specified dilution and temperature.
18. Brush all accessible channels (suction, biopsy, air/water) using the brush size specified in that scope model's IFU; pass the brush through each channel until it emerges visibly clean on both ends, minimum three passes per channel.
19. Brush and wipe the distal end, control head buttons, and valve ports.
20. Flush each channel with enzymatic solution using a syringe or channel-irrigation connector per IFU volume and pressure specification.
21. Rinse the entire scope and all channels with clean, treated water to remove all detergent residue.
22. Purge channels with air per IFU to remove residual rinse water before verification testing.
23. Discard the enzymatic detergent solution after each single scope; solution is never reused across scopes.

### 6.5 Cleaning Verification Testing

24. Perform an ATP test on the suction/biopsy channel after every manual clean, on every scope, before every disinfection cycle — 100% sampling, no exceptions.
25. Swab the channel per the ATP kit manufacturer instructions and read the result within the kit's specified window.
26. A result below 200 RLU is a pass. Record the numeric result in the STS field.
27. Perform a protein residue swab test on the same channel once per week per scope, on a rotating schedule maintained by the Manager, in addition to the daily ATP test, to cross-validate the ATP method.
28. If the ATP result is 200 RLU or greater, or the protein test is positive, follow the Failed Residue Test exception path (Section 7.2). Do not load the scope into the AER.
29. If both results pass, sign off cleaning verification in STS and proceed to the automated cycle.

### 6.6 Automated Endoscope Reprocessing Cycle

30. Attach the scope to the AER connectors per the AER manufacturer's channel-specific hookup diagram, posted at each machine.
31. Scan the scope barcode and the technician's badge into the AER's own tracking interface, which feeds the STS.
32. Select the correct cycle for the scope model; confirm the AER's automated leak test (a second, independent leak check) passes before the HLD cycle begins. If the AER leak test fails, remove the scope and follow Section 7.1.
33. Do not open the AER door or interrupt the cycle once HLD has started.
34. Upon cycle completion, confirm on the AER printout or digital log that disinfectant concentration, exposure time, and temperature all met the validated parameters. File or link this printout to the STS record.
35. If any parameter failed, the scope is not considered high-level disinfected; return to Step 30 and run a full new cycle. Do not transport or store the scope in the interim.

### 6.7 Drying and Storage

36. Remove the scope from the AER and immediately transfer it to the drying cabinet; elapsed time from AER completion to drying cabinet placement must not exceed 10 minutes.
37. Connect all channels to the drying cabinet's forced-air ports per the cabinet's channel map; confirm airflow indicator is active for each channel.
38. Dry for the manufacturer- and cabinet-validated minimum time (KVRMC standard: 60 minutes minimum for full channel drying) before removal to storage.
39. Hang the scope vertically in the designated storage cabinet, fully extended, with valves and caps detached and stored alongside per IFU.
40. Scan the scope into storage in STS; the system automatically starts the 7-day hang-time countdown.
41. If a scope's hang time reaches 7 days without use, STS generates an automatic alert; the scope must be fully reprocessed (Sections 6.3–6.7) before it can be used, even if never removed from storage.

### 6.8 Documentation (All Roles, at Each Step)

42. Every scan in STS must capture: scope serial number, patient identifier, procedure date, performing physician, technician identifier at each stage, and timestamp. No step in Sections 6.1–6.7 is complete until its corresponding STS scan is recorded.
43. Manual entries (leak test pass/fail, ATP numeric result, protein test result, AER cycle parameters) must be entered into STS by the technician who performed the test, not transcribed by a second person.
44. STS records are retained for a minimum of 7 years and must be retrievable by patient identifier or scope serial number within one business day for infection investigation purposes.

### 6.9 Quarterly Water Testing (Biomedical Engineering)

45. Every 90 days, Biomedical Engineering collects a water sample from each AER's final rinse water supply line.
46. Samples are sent to the contracted microbiology laboratory for heterotrophic plate count and are held to the AAMI ST91 action threshold.
47. Results are logged in the equipment file and copied to the Infection Preventionist. Any result exceeding the action threshold triggers an immediate AER water-line disinfection cycle and a repeat sample before the AER returns to service.

### 6.10 Initial and Annual Competency

48. New reprocessing technicians complete didactic training on this procedure, hands-on return demonstration of Sections 6.1–6.7 on a test scope, and a written exam with a minimum passing score of 90%, before independently reprocessing any patient scope.
49. Initial competency includes direct observation of five consecutive full reprocessing cycles by the Department Educator, each independently signed off.
50. All reprocessing technicians complete annual competency renewal consisting of a return demonstration and updated written exam reflecting any IFU or procedure revisions since the prior year.
51. Competency records are maintained by the Department Educator and are subject to audit by Infection Prevention and Biomedical Engineering at any time.
52. A technician whose annual competency lapses is removed from the reprocessing schedule until renewal is complete.

## 7. Exception Paths

### 7.1 Failed Leak Test

53. Remove the scope from service immediately; place it in a labeled quarantine bin marked "Failed Leak Test — Do Not Use."
54. Notify Biomedical Engineering the same day for further inspection and, if needed, manufacturer repair.
55. Document the failure, scope serial number, and technician identifier in STS and in the equipment repair log.
56. The scope may not return to the active inventory until Biomedical Engineering confirms repair and a passing leak test is independently re-verified and countersigned by a second technician.

### 7.2 Failed Cleaning Verification (ATP or Protein Residue) Test

57. Do not load the scope into the AER. Return the scope to the manual cleaning station.
58. Repeat the full manual cleaning cycle (Section 6.4) in its entirety, not merely the affected channel.
59. Repeat cleaning verification testing (Section 6.5). If the second attempt also fails, remove the scope from service, notify the Manager and Biomedical Engineering, and initiate a borescope inspection before any further reprocessing attempt.
60. Two consecutive verification failures on the same scope require the Manager's written approval before the scope may be used on a patient again.

### 7.3 Delayed Reprocessing (Transport or Workflow Delay Beyond 60 Minutes)

61. If precleaning-to-SPD-intake time will exceed 60 minutes, transport staff notify the SPD charge technician immediately.
62. Upon arrival, the technician performs an extended soak in enzymatic detergent per the IFU's delayed-reprocessing instructions (typically an additional soak cycle) before proceeding to manual cleaning.
63. Document the delay duration and the extended-soak step taken in STS.
64. Delays exceeding the IFU's maximum stated delay limit require the scope to be treated as contaminated beyond standard protocol; consult the manufacturer IFU addendum and notify the Manager before proceeding.

### 7.4 Loaner Scopes

65. Upon arrival, Biomedical Engineering inspects the loaner for external damage and confirms the accompanying reprocessing and maintenance history documentation.
66. The loaner is fully reprocessed per Sections 6.3–6.7 before its first patient use at KVRMC, regardless of documentation claiming it was reprocessed by the lending facility.
67. The loaner is entered into STS with a temporary asset number and flagged as "Loaner" for the duration of its stay; all standard documentation requirements in Section 6.8 apply.
68. Before return to the lending party, the loaner undergoes a final full reprocessing cycle, and the completed STS record is printed and sent with the scope.

### 7.5 After-Hours Emergency Case

69. During hours when SPD is not staffed, the on-call reprocessing technician is paged and must arrive within the facility's defined on-call response time (30 minutes).
70. All steps in Sections 6.1–6.9 apply without modification; no step may be skipped or abbreviated because the case is after-hours.
71. If no scope has completed a full reprocessing cycle and is available in storage, the case is delayed until one is ready, unless the ordering physician documents the delay poses greater risk than proceeding with the next available properly processed scope from within its 7-day hang time.
72. The on-call technician documents the after-hours designation in STS along with actual start and completion times for each step.

### 7.6 Scope Implicated in an Infection Investigation

73. Upon notification by the Infection Preventionist that a scope is suspected in a healthcare-associated infection, the Manager immediately quarantines that scope and removes it from the active rotation, tagging it in STS as "Under Investigation — Do Not Use."
74. The full STS history for that scope for the preceding 60 days is pulled and provided to the Infection Preventionist within 24 hours.
75. Biomedical Engineering performs a borescope inspection of the quarantined scope and documents findings with recorded images.
76. Culture samples are taken from the scope's channels per the Infection Preventionist's sampling protocol before any further cleaning is performed on it.
77. The scope remains quarantined until the Infection Preventionist and Dr. Escobar jointly approve either its return to service (following documented repair/reprocessing and clean culture results) or its permanent retirement from inventory.
78. All findings and the final disposition decision are documented in the infection investigation file and cross-referenced in STS.

## Appendix C: Cleaning Verification Failure Trend Review

The Manager reviews all ATP and protein residue results in aggregate every Monday, looking for:

1. Any single scope with two or more failures in a rolling 30-day period, regardless of whether each individual failure was resolved on retest.
2. Any single technician whose scopes show a failure rate exceeding 5% of their processed volume in a rolling 30-day period.
3. Any AER unit associated with failures across multiple scope models, suggesting a machine-level rather than scope-level or technician-level cause.

Findings from this weekly review are documented in the SPD quality log and reported monthly to the Infection Preventionist and to Dr. Escobar as part of the standing quality and patient safety review. Any trend meeting criteria 1–3 above triggers a targeted re-competency check for the technician involved, a targeted borescope inspection for the scope involved, or a Biomedical Engineering service call for the AER involved, as applicable, within 5 business days of identification.

## Appendix D: Borescope Inspection Schedule

Full-inventory borescope inspection of every scope's channels is performed on the following schedule:

- **Quarterly:** Random sample of 25% of active inventory, selected by the Manager without advance notice to reprocessing staff, so that every scope is inspected at least once per year through routine sampling.
- **Immediately upon:** any failed leak test that passes on retest (to confirm no residual internal damage), any two-time cleaning verification failure, any scope returning from external repair, any scope implicated in an infection investigation, and any scope reaching 10,000 cumulative reprocessing cycles.
- **Annually:** 100% of active inventory receives a full borescope inspection during the first quarter of the calendar year, coordinated by Biomedical Engineering and scheduled to avoid disruption to procedure volume, with results reported to the Manager and filed in the equipment record for each scope.

Any channel defect, retained debris, or distal end damage identified during a borescope inspection results in immediate removal of that scope from the active rotation and initiation of the manufacturer repair process; the scope may not return to service until a repeat borescope inspection confirms resolution and a full reprocessing cycle with passing leak test and cleaning verification is completed and countersigned by a second technician.

## Appendix G: Scope Retirement Criteria

A scope is permanently retired from inventory, rather than repaired and returned to service, when any of the following conditions is met:

1. Biomedical Engineering determines that channel damage cannot be repaired to manufacturer specification.
2. The scope has failed borescope inspection three or more times in a rolling 12-month period, regardless of intervening repairs.
3. The scope is confirmed by the Infection Preventionist and Dr. Escobar as the source of a healthcare-associated infection under Section 7.6 and manufacturer repair cannot provide documented assurance against recurrence.
4. The manufacturer discontinues support or replacement parts for that model.

Retirement decisions require joint sign-off from the Manager and Dr. Escobar, are documented in the equipment file, and are reported to the capital equipment committee for replacement scope procurement planning. A retired scope is removed from STS active inventory but its full historical record is retained per the 7-year retention requirement in Section 6.8.
