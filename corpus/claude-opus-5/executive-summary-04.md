# 1. Sepsis Early-Warning Bundle
**Program Review Window: January 1, 2024 – March 31, 2025**

## Finding and Recommendation

The sepsis early-warning bundle is the strongest-performing clinical initiative this office has evaluated in the last three review cycles. It moved three-hour bundle compliance from 61 percent to 88 percent and in-hospital sepsis mortality from 18.4 percent to 12.9 percent across all four campuses on total expenditure of $1.9 million. The effect is large, it is consistent across sites, and it survives the adjustments we can make for case mix and documentation.

The Office of Clinical Program Evaluation recommends that the Executive Council close the sepsis bundle as a capital project and move it into the system operating base at a recurring run rate of $624,000 per year, effective with the FY2026 budget. We further recommend that three components of the bundle — the nursing screen, the pharmacy antibiotic pre-stage, and the rapid-response escalation pathway — be extended to general clinical deterioration on the medical-surgical units during FY2026. We recommend against any further expansion of the alerting technology into new clinical domains until sepsis performance holds at or above 85 percent compliance for four consecutive quarters and until alert positive predictive value improves materially from its current level.

## Evidence

The review window covered 3,310 sepsis episodes across the four campuses, identified by the system's standard Sepsis-3 abstraction with retrospective chart validation on a 12 percent sample. Implementation was staged: Princeton campus in February 2024, East in April, West in July, and Shelby in October. That staging gave us something close to a stepped-wedge comparison, and it is the reason we have reasonable confidence that the mortality change is attributable to the intervention rather than to secular trend. Each campus showed its inflection within eight to eleven weeks of go-live, and no campus showed comparable improvement in the months before its own go-live date.

Compliance with the three-hour bundle — lactate, cultures before antibiotics, antibiotics within three hours, and fluid resuscitation for hypotension or lactate above 4 — rose from a 2023 baseline of 61 percent to 88 percent in the final two quarters of the window. The largest single gain came from antibiotic timing, where median time from recognition to first dose fell from 148 minutes to 71 minutes. Pharmacy's decision to pre-stage empiric regimens in the automated dispensing cabinets on all thirty-one adult inpatient units accounts for roughly half of that improvement by our estimate, and it cost $86,000.

Mortality among sepsis episodes fell from 18.4 percent at baseline to 12.9 percent in the final two quarters. Applied to the 1,290 episodes in those two quarters, the differential represents 71 deaths that did not occur, or approximately 142 on an annualized basis. We tested this against the possibility that improved documentation and coding had simply pulled less sick patients into the sepsis denominator. The observed-to-expected mortality ratio, which adjusts for severity, moved from 1.12 to 0.86 over the same period. That movement is directionally consistent with a real clinical effect and inconsistent with a pure denominator artifact, though we would not claim the adjustment is complete.

The financial case is favorable but secondary. Mean length of stay for surviving sepsis patients fell from 9.1 days to 8.2 days, and unplanned ICU transfers occurring more than six hours after floor arrival fell 22 percent. Together these produce an estimated $3.9 million in annualized variable cost avoidance, against $1.9 million of total program spend of which $1.24 million was one-time build, validation, and education. The recurring cost — 2.6 FTE sepsis coordinators, the analytics license, and ongoing chart audit — is $624,000 per year. Cost per death averted, using only the recurring figure, is approximately $4,400. Beyond direct cost, sepsis mortality carries 15 percent of the weighting in two commercial value-based contracts representing $2.4 million in at-risk quality dollars, and the improvement moved the system from partial to full achievement in both for the 2024 measurement year.

Three findings temper the result and shape the recommendation. First, alert burden is high and clinician tolerance is finite. The early-warning model generated approximately 41,000 alerts during the window at a positive predictive value of 18 percent, and nursing override without documented assessment ran at 62 percent. The program is currently working despite the alert, not because of it; the operational changes in pharmacy, nursing screening, and escalation are doing most of the work. Second, Shelby campus lags at 79 percent compliance, materially behind the other three, and its mortality improvement is correspondingly smaller. The evaluation team attributes this to nurse vacancy rates above 20 percent on its two medical units and to a rapid-response team that is staffed only sixteen hours per day. Third, the improvement is not yet self-sustaining. Compliance dipped four points at Princeton campus during the eleven weeks when its sepsis coordinator position sat vacant, and recovered when the position was filled.

## Commitment for FY2026

For the fiscal year beginning October 1, 2025, the system commits to the following. The sepsis bundle will be funded in the operating base at $624,000 with the coordinator positions designated as backfill-critical, meaning they may not be held vacant for cost management. Shelby campus will reach 85 percent three-hour compliance by the end of the second quarter, with the Chief Nursing Officer accountable and with rapid-response coverage extended to twenty-four hours as of October 1. The informatics team will retune the alerting model with a target positive predictive value of 30 percent and a documented override rate below 40 percent by the end of the third quarter; if that target is missed, this office will recommend that the alert be retired and the manual nursing screen retained. Compliance and risk-adjusted sepsis mortality will be reported to the Quality Committee quarterly rather than annually. No additional module of the early-warning platform will be purchased or deployed in FY2026.

# 2. Hospital-at-Home Program
**Program Review Window: January 1, 2024 – March 31, 2025**

## Finding and Recommendation

The hospital-at-home program did not achieve the scale required to test its own hypothesis. Against a plan of 400 patients, it enrolled 96 — 24 percent of target — while spending $4.2 million, and it recorded a 30-day readmission rate of 14.6 percent against 11.2 percent for a matched inpatient comparison group. The clinical care delivered was good by every measure available to us. The operating model was not viable at the volume achieved, and the payment authority on which the model depended expires on September 30, 2025.

The Office of Clinical Program Evaluation recommends that the Executive Council close the hospital-at-home program through an orderly wind-down concluding September 30, 2025, coincident with the expiration of the federal Acute Hospital Care at Home waiver. We recommend that the system not seek bridge funding, not pursue a state-level or commercial-payer substitute for the waiver in FY2026, and not carry the program in a suspended or minimally staffed state. We recommend that three assets be preserved and redeployed rather than dissolved, and that a defined re-entry trigger be recorded so that the system's institutional learning is not lost if federal policy changes.

## Evidence

The program was sponsored by Chief Medical Officer Julio Ferrante and launched in January 2024 with a design census of eighteen patients per day, a clinical model built around twice-daily paramedic visits and daily physician telemedicine rounds, and a 24/7 command center staffed by two nurses per shift. Average daily census across the fifteen-month window was 3.2. It peaked at 7 in August 2024 and never exceeded that.

The enrollment failure is well characterized and is not primarily a patient-acceptance problem. Of 3,180 admissions screened against the eligibility criteria, 1,140 met the diagnostic and clinical thresholds. Of those, 412 were offered to an attending physician for authorization. Of those 412, 168 received physician sign-off, and of the 168 patients then approached, 96 consented. The dominant attrition point is physician authorization, where 59 percent of eligible patients were declined, most commonly with the notation that the physician preferred direct observation for the first 24 hours. The secondary attrition point is geography: 31 percent of otherwise eligible patients lived outside the thirty-minute response radius that the model required, a constraint that was known at design but was assumed to affect roughly 15 percent of the population.

The economics follow directly from the census. Total spend of $4.2 million across 96 episodes is $43,750 per episode. Direct variable cost per episode — paramedic time, pharmaceuticals, durable equipment consumables, physician time — was $6,900. The remaining $36,850 per episode is fixed infrastructure amortized over a census that never approached design. The comparable inpatient episode for the matched diagnosis-related groups carried direct cost of $9,240. Under the waiver, the program billed at full inpatient DRG rates and collected $1.13 million, producing a net operating loss of $3.07 million over the window. Break-even census, holding the current fixed structure constant, is between fourteen and sixteen patients per day.

On September 30, 2025, the waiver expires. Absent it, the same care is reimbursable only at home health rates of approximately $155 per day, which converts an episode paying roughly $11,800 into one paying roughly $800. There is no plausible cost structure under which the program operates at that reimbursement. This is the decisive fact, and the Executive Council should treat it as such: even a program at full design census would not survive the payment change.

On the clinical question, we are obliged to report that we cannot answer it. The 30-day readmission rate of 14.6 percent represents 14 events in 96 patients, against 11.2 percent in a matched inpatient group of 384. The 3.4-point difference carries a 95 percent confidence interval running from roughly negative 5 to positive 12 points. That interval is consistent with the program being modestly safer, meaningfully more dangerous, or identical. We can neither raise a safety alarm nor certify non-inferiority, and the reason we cannot is that the enrollment shortfall left the evaluation underpowered by a factor of four. What we can report is that there were no deaths in the program, that seven patients (7.3 percent) required escalation back to a hospital bed during the acute episode, all of which occurred without adverse outcome, that there were no medication administration errors identified on audit, and that patient experience scores placed the program at the 91st percentile nationally. Families liked it. Patients liked it. The clinicians who staffed it did excellent work.

Two governance observations belong in this record. First, the program passed three quarterly checkpoints in 2024 at which enrollment stood at 18, 34, and 61 patients against a straight-line plan of 80, 160, and 240, and no checkpoint triggered a scope or funding decision. Second, the program's executive sponsor also chaired the committee reviewing its performance. Neither observation implies bad faith, and both point to the same structural remedy.

## Commitment for FY2026

The system commits to the following for the fiscal year beginning October 1, 2025. The program will cease new enrollment on August 15, 2025, and will close on September 30, 2025, with all active patients transitioned to inpatient care, skilled nursing, or home health under a documented plan reviewed by the Chief Medical Officer. No employee will be involuntarily separated: the 6.4 FTE of clinical staff will be redeployed to the transitional care and heart failure clinic teams, both of which carry open positions. The remote patient monitoring platform and 140 monitoring kits, carrying $480,000 of net book value, will be redeployed to post-surgical discharge monitoring and to the heart failure clinic, with a utilization report due to this office in March 2026. The system will write off $1.1 million of capitalized program build and will absorb $340,000 in lease and contract termination costs; final closure accounting will be presented to the Quality Committee in December 2025. The $2.9 million of FY2026 spend previously planned for this program is released and will not be reallocated to a successor initiative without Council action.

Finally, the system commits to a stage-gate standard for all new clinical service lines beginning in FY2026: any program failing to reach 40 percent of planned enrollment by the end of its second full quarter returns to the Executive Council for an explicit continue, restructure, or close decision, and that decision is presented by a party other than the program's executive sponsor. Should Congress reauthorize acute hospital care at home with a horizon of three years or longer, this office will support a new business case on the condition that it demonstrates a minimum viable census of twelve and a referral model that does not depend on discretionary attending authorization.

# 3. Community Paramedic Program for High-Utilizing Emergency Department Patients
**Program Review Window: January 1, 2024 – March 31, 2025**

## Finding and Recommendation

The community paramedic program produced a real and clinically meaningful reduction in emergency department use among the patients it enrolled — 34 percent, from 8.4 to 5.5 visits per patient-year across 212 patients — but reached only 40 percent of those referred to it and generated no cost offset the system can identify or book. The reduction is genuine, and a portion of it is attributable to the program rather than to regression to the mean. The problem is that the system currently holds no payment arrangement under which a reduction in emergency visits by predominantly Medicaid and uninsured patients converts into recovered dollars.

The Office of Clinical Program Evaluation recommends that the Executive Council renew the community paramedic program for twelve months at reduced scope and reduced cost, under four explicit performance conditions and a hard sunset date. We recommend that the program consolidate from four campuses to the two with the highest density of qualifying patients, that its annual cost fall from $1.08 million to approximately $940,000, and that it close on September 30, 2026 if two or more of the four conditions are unmet at review. We recommend against expansion, against the proposed fifth-campus extension, and against any incremental FTE in FY2026.

## Evidence

The program enrolled 212 of 530 patients referred. Referral criteria were four or more emergency visits in the preceding twelve months without a hospital admission, plus an identified social or behavioral driver. Enrolled patients received an initial home assessment within 72 hours, a paramedic-delivered care plan, medication reconciliation, and navigation to primary care and behavioral health, with a median engagement of 94 days.

Among enrolled patients, emergency visits fell from 8.4 to 5.5 per patient-year and inpatient admissions from 1.9 to 1.4. Taken alone, those are strong numbers. They are, however, substantially inflated by the mechanism that selects patients into the program: individuals are identified at the peak of their utilization, and peaks regress. This office constructed a comparison group of 196 patients who met criteria and were referred but never enrolled, and followed them over the same interval with no intervention. Their emergency visits fell 19 percent. The attributable effect of the program, on that comparison, is roughly 15 percentage points, or about 1.6 avoided visits per patient-year. That is still a real effect. It is less than half the headline number, and the Council should plan against the smaller figure.

The absence of a cost offset is not a measurement failure; it is a structural one, and it has four sources. The enrolled population is 63 percent Medicaid, 19 percent uninsured, 14 percent Medicare fee-for-service, and 4 percent commercial. Avoided emergency visits for the first two groups eliminate care the system was largely absorbing as uncompensated or below-cost, so avoiding them reduces charges more than it reduces cost. Second, at three of four campuses the emergency departments are not capacity-constrained at the hours these patients present, so freed capacity is not backfilled with revenue-generating volume; only the Princeton campus has meaningful boarding pressure. Third, the marginal cost of a low-acuity emergency visit is dominated by fixed cost, and 1.6 avoided visits per patient does not retire a nurse, a bay, or a shift. Fourth and most consequential, the system holds downside financial risk on only 26 of the 212 enrolled patients — those attributed to our Medicare ACO. For the other 186, a reduction in utilization is a reduction in our own revenue.

What the program did produce financially is modest and real: $210,000 in avoided bad debt and $96,000 in reduced charity care write-off, against annualized cost of $1.08 million and a cost per enrolled patient of $5,090. It also produced non-financial value the Council should weigh deliberately. The program's full cost qualifies as reportable community benefit, and emergency department overuse is a named priority in the system's current Community Health Needs Assessment. It has built working relationships with three municipal EMS agencies and two housing authorities that no other part of the system holds. And the clinical results are the kind of results the system says it exists to produce.

The recommendation to renew conditionally rather than close rests on the judgment that the value is realizable but is not realizable under the current design. The four conditions are directed at that gap:

1. **Enrollment.** The program must convert at least 65 percent of referrals by the end of the second quarter of FY2026, up from 40 percent. Root-cause work identifies the consent process as the barrier: consent is currently sought by telephone after discharge, and 44 percent of non-enrollments are unreachable contacts. Consent moves to a warm bedside handoff before the patient leaves the emergency department.
2. **Population alignment.** At least 50 percent of new enrollments must come from ACO-attributed or Medicare Advantage lives by the end of the third quarter, so that utilization reduction accrues to a contract that pays for it.
3. **Revenue capture.** The system must execute an Alabama Medicaid mobile integrated health or community health worker billing arrangement by the end of the third quarter. Preliminary analysis suggests recoverable revenue of approximately $1,400 per enrolled patient, which at target enrollment would offset roughly half the program's cost.
4. **Evidence quality.** The program must operate a prospective matched comparison with a pre-registered analysis plan, so that the FY2027 decision is made on an attributable effect rather than a raw pre-post difference.

## Commitment for FY2026

The system commits to funding the community paramedic program at $940,000 for the fiscal year beginning October 1, 2025, consolidated to the Princeton and East campuses, with staffing of 3.0 FTE paramedics, 1.0 FTE licensed social worker, and 0.3 FTE medical director. Accountability transfers from the emergency services line to the population health division, which holds the contracts under which the program's value can be captured. The four conditions above will be reported to the Quality Committee at six and twelve months. If two or more remain unmet at the twelve-month review, the program closes on September 30, 2026 without further Council action, and this office will bring a closure plan in July 2026. If the conditions are met, this office will bring an expansion business case that includes the fifth campus and a defined shared-savings arrangement. The system further commits to reporting the program's community benefit valuation in the FY2026 CHNA implementation report regardless of the renewal outcome.

# 4. Scheduling Center Consolidation
**Program Review Window: January 1, 2024 – March 31, 2025**

## Finding and Recommendation

The scheduling center consolidation is 59 percent over budget and materially under target on the only service measure that matters to patients. Budgeted at $6.1 million, it has spent $9.7 million, and this office projects $12.6 million to complete the work as currently scoped. The consolidated center handles 380,000 calls per quarter and abandons 17 percent of them against a 5 percent target. Four of nine planned practice-cluster migrations are complete. The strategy underlying the consolidation remains sound and is producing the cost-per-call improvement it promised. The execution is failing, and continuing to migrate volume into a center that cannot answer the volume it already has will compound the damage.

The Office of Clinical Program Evaluation recommends that the Executive Council suspend all remaining consolidation waves effective July 1, 2025, and direct a 120-day stabilization of the four clusters already migrated. We recommend that the program be re-baselined and brought back for reapproval in September 2025 with a full-cost estimate, a revised sequence, and a contingency reserve. We recommend that no further capital be released and no additional cluster be migrated until three gates are met. And we recommend that the Council designate a single accountable executive for the program, replacing the current dual sponsorship, which the evaluation identifies as a proximate cause of the overrun.

## Evidence

The consolidation was approved in late 2023 to move scheduling from twenty-two distributed practice-based teams into a single center under one telephony platform and one scheduling template library, with a business case built on labor efficiency and access improvement. It is led jointly by Nurse Executive Bridget Nkemelu and Chief Financial Officer Meredith Tanaka-Bell.

The $3.6 million overrun to date decomposes cleanly. The largest component, $1.9 million, is a telephony platform migration added to scope in the second quarter of 2024 after the incumbent system proved unable to support skills-based routing across more than two clusters. That addition was operationally necessary and was approved through the technology governance path, but it was never brought back to the Executive Council as a change to the program's approved budget. Agency and temporary labor accounts for $1.2 million, overtime for $410,000, and external consulting for $290,000. Approximately $200,000 of the balance is scope creep at the practice level.

Service performance is the more serious problem. At 380,000 calls per quarter and 17 percent abandonment, the center abandons approximately 64,600 calls per quarter, or 258,400 per year. At the 5 percent target the figure would be about 19,000 per quarter. The excess is roughly 45,600 abandoned calls per quarter and 182,400 per year. Average speed of answer is four minutes fifty-two seconds against a forty-five second target.

The staffing picture explains most of this. Against a validated model of 214 FTE, the center carries 38 vacancies. Annualized attrition is 44 percent, average tenure is seven months, and 31 percent of new hires do not complete ninety days. Agent occupancy runs at 91 percent, which is well above the sustainable band of 78 to 85 percent and is itself a driver of attrition — the center is burning out the staff it has in order to cover the staff it does not have. Average handle time is 7.4 minutes against a peer benchmark of 4.2, and this office regards handle time as the root operational failure rather than a symptom: template standardization, which was to have reduced 22 legacy scheduling templates to 6, was deferred behind the migrations rather than completed ahead of them. Agents are therefore navigating cluster-specific rules from memory, which lengthens calls, lengthens training, and drives error.

The revenue consequence is significant and, in our estimate, larger than the capital overrun. Call-back analysis on a sample of 4,100 abandoned calls found that 14 percent of abandoned callers never rebooked within sixty days. Applied to the excess abandonment, that is approximately 25,500 lost callers annually and, adjusting for repeat and non-appointment calls, approximately 11,900 net appointments never booked. At an average contribution margin of $268 per new outpatient appointment, that is $3.2 million in forgone annual contribution, with an additional estimated $2.1 million in downstream imaging and procedural leakage. Three referring practices have documented redirection of referral volume to competitors, and the access domain of the system's outpatient experience survey has fallen from the 62nd to the 29th percentile.

It would be wrong to characterize the program as a failure of concept. Cost per call has fallen from $6.90 under the distributed model to $5.40. Migrated clusters have eliminated 31 separate scheduling phone numbers. The single-platform architecture is in place and functioning. The program is achieving its cost thesis and failing its service thesis, and it is failing the service thesis because it sequenced migrations by practice-leader readiness and political availability rather than by scheduling complexity, and because it went live in three of four clusters with the center staffed below 80 percent of model.

The three gates we recommend before any further migration are as follows. Abandonment must hold at or below 8 percent for two consecutive months at current scope. Staffing must reach 92 percent of validated model with trailing attrition below 25 percent annualized. And template standardization must be complete for the next cluster before its go-live, not after.

## Commitment for FY2026

The system commits to the following. Waves five through nine are suspended effective July 1, 2025; no practice cluster will be migrated during the suspension regardless of local readiness. A single accountable executive will be named by the Executive Council no later than July 15, 2025, with the second sponsor moving to an advisory role. A re-baselined budget, revised sequence, and completion estimate will be presented to the Council in September 2025 and will include a contingency reserve of not less than 15 percent; any subsequent scope addition exceeding $250,000 returns to the Council. An independent operational assessment will be commissioned at a cost not to exceed $185,000, reporting to the Quality Committee rather than to the program, with findings due in October 2025. Abandonment rate, average speed of answer, handle time, vacancy, and attrition will be reported monthly to the Executive Council and quarterly to the Quality Committee throughout FY2026. Template standardization will be funded and completed for all nine clusters by March 31, 2026 as a prerequisite to resumption rather than a parallel workstream. And if the three gates are not met by March 31, 2026, the Executive Council will take up two alternatives that this office will prepare in the interim: outsourcing the center to a specialized vendor, or reversing the remaining waves and stabilizing a hybrid model in which high-complexity clusters retain practice-based scheduling permanently.

# Appendix A. Cross-Program Observations for the Executive Council

The four programs reviewed in this cycle were selected independently and share no common sponsor, funding source, or clinical domain. They nonetheless produced three findings that recur across all four, and this office believes those findings are more consequential than any single program decision before the Council today.

## A.1 The system approves programs well and governs them poorly

Each of the four programs was approved on a business case that this office, reviewing it retrospectively, judges to have been reasonable at the time. The sepsis bundle projected a mortality improvement of four to six points and delivered 5.5. The hospital-at-home program projected 400 patients on assumptions about eligibility prevalence that were, in fact, approximately correct — 1,140 patients met clinical criteria against a forecast of 1,050. The community paramedic program projected a 30 percent utilization reduction and produced 34 percent on its face. The scheduling consolidation projected a cost-per-call reduction from $6.85 to $5.60 and achieved $5.40.

What failed was not forecasting. What failed was the interval between approval and completion. The hospital-at-home program passed three quarterly checkpoints at 23, 21, and 25 percent of straight-line enrollment plan without a single decision being taken. The scheduling consolidation absorbed a $1.9 million scope addition through a technology governance path that had no obligation to inform the body that had approved the program's budget. The community paramedic program ran fifteen months before anyone constructed a comparison group, which meant that for fifteen months the system did not know whether it was measuring an intervention effect or a statistical artifact. Only the sepsis bundle was governed continuously, and it was governed continuously because it happened to fall under regulatory reporting requirements that forced monthly review.

The remedy is not more approval rigor. The system already spends substantial executive time on approval. The remedy is a standing obligation to decide again, at defined points, with the decision presented by someone other than the sponsor. This office recommends that the Executive Council adopt the following as standing policy for all programs above $1 million in total commitment: a mandatory continue-restructure-close decision at the point where cumulative spend reaches 40 percent of budget or twelve months elapse, whichever comes first; presentation of that decision by the Office of Clinical Program Evaluation rather than by the program sponsor; and a requirement that any scope addition exceeding 10 percent of approved budget return to the approving body regardless of which governance path authorized it.

## A.2 Clinical effect and financial return are separable, and the system has not been treating them as separable

Two of the four programs produced clear clinical benefit. One of them — sepsis — also produced clear financial benefit, and the coincidence has made it easy to describe the program as simply successful. The other — community paramedicine — produced clinical benefit with no financial return whatsoever, and the system has struggled to know what to do with that, cycling between defending the program on grounds of savings it cannot document and defending it on grounds of mission it has never priced.

This office's position is that these are different decisions requiring different evidence and different decision rights. A program that saves money is an operating decision and belongs to the Executive Council. A program that improves care at net cost is a mission decision and belongs to the board, which is the body that holds the system's charitable purpose. The community paramedic program should have been brought to the Quality Committee in 2024 as a request to spend $1.08 million per year on a population the system serves at a loss, with an explicit statement of what the system would receive in return. Instead it was carried as an operating initiative on the expectation of savings that its own payer mix made structurally impossible.

The practical consequence for FY2026 is that this office will classify every program it reviews into one of three categories at the outset of review — margin-positive, margin-neutral, or mission-funded — and will state the classification in the first paragraph of every summary. The Council should expect that some programs will move categories over their life, and that a move from margin-positive to mission-funded is a trigger for board-level review rather than a finding of failure.

## A.3 Fixed cost is the recurring failure mode

Three of the four programs failed or underperformed for the same underlying reason: they were built with a fixed cost structure sized to a volume they did not achieve. Hospital-at-home carried a 24/7 command center and a full clinical complement against an average daily census of 3.2, producing a per-episode cost of $43,750 of which $36,850 was fixed infrastructure. The scheduling center built to a 214 FTE model and ran at 176, but its telephony, real estate, and management layer were sized to the full model from go-live. The community paramedic program carries a medical director, a social worker, and a vehicle fleet against 212 patients.

Only the sepsis bundle was built variably — it added 2.6 coordinator FTE and a software license to existing clinical infrastructure, and its per-unit cost fell as it scaled because nearly all of its cost was already sunk in the nursing and pharmacy operations that executed it.

This office recommends that the Executive Council require, for any new clinical program above $1 million, an explicit statement of break-even volume, the ratio of fixed to variable cost at design volume, and a described operating posture at 50 percent of planned volume. The hospital-at-home program had no answer to the third question, and the absence of an answer is why it spent fifteen months at a quarter of its design census without anyone being able to say whether that was survivable.

# Appendix C. Note on Method and Limitations

The Office of Clinical Program Evaluation conducted these four reviews between February and May 2025 with a team of six, comprising two clinical outcomes analysts, two financial analysts, one health services researcher, and one nurse abstractor. Every finding in these summaries derives from source data held by the system: the electronic health record, the general ledger, the enterprise data warehouse, telephony platform logs, and payer remittance files. No program supplied its own performance data for use in these summaries without independent verification, and where program-reported figures diverged from verified figures, the verified figures appear here.

Three limitations bear on the Council's use of this material.

First, none of these evaluations is a randomized comparison, and none could have been. The sepsis bundle's staged rollout provides the strongest quasi-experimental design of the four, and its findings should be regarded as the most secure. The hospital-at-home comparison is a propensity-matched analysis that is badly underpowered. The community paramedic comparison is a matched observational design constructed retrospectively, which is why we have made prospective comparison a renewal condition. Estimates of forgone revenue in the scheduling analysis rest on a call-back sample and on an average contribution margin figure that varies substantially by service line; the $3.2 million estimate should be read as an order of magnitude, not a precise figure.

Second, the review window closed March 31, 2025, and the Council is deciding in June. Two of these programs — the sepsis bundle and the scheduling center — have continued to generate data in the interval.
