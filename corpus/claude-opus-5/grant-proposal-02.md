**Amount Requested:** $3,750,000 over five years (January 1, 2027 – December 31, 2031)
**Contact:** Elias Mwangi, Director of Grants and Contracts
**Submitted:** November 14, 2026

## Statement of Need

Platte Meridian Community Health operates six federally qualified health center sites across four counties in central Nebraska. In the most recent Uniform Data System reporting year we served 28,400 patients with 214 staff on a $41 million budget. Sixty-two percent of our patients identify as Hispanic or Latino. Eleven percent are Somali or Sudanese, most arrived through secondary migration to meatpacking and agricultural processing employment. Thirty-eight percent are best served in a language other than English; our interpreter services handle Spanish, Somali, Arabic, Karen, and Kizigua with meaningful volume. Forty-four percent of our patients live at or below 138 percent of the federal poverty level, and 27 percent are uninsured.

The problem this application addresses is specific and measurable. Our prenatal panel has grown from 610 patients in 2021 to 1,180 in the most recent twelve months, a 93 percent increase in five years. This growth reflects both population change in our service area and the withdrawal of private obstetric practice from the region. Over the same period, our first-trimester entry-to-care rate has been essentially flat at 51 percent. The Nebraska state average is 74 percent. Twenty-three percentage points separate our patients from the state, and behind that gap sit roughly 270 women each year who begin prenatal care in the second or third trimester, or who present in labor with no prenatal record at all.

The gap is not evenly distributed. Internal chart review of the 2025 cohort found first-trimester entry at 61 percent among English-preferred patients, 47 percent among Spanish-preferred patients, and 34 percent among Somali- and Arabic-preferred patients. Among patients who were uninsured at the time of their first visit, first-trimester entry was 39 percent. Among patients who had a prior delivery at Platte Meridian, it was 66 percent. The pattern points to a system that works reasonably well for patients already inside it and poorly for everyone else.

Three structural conditions drive the gap.

**First, distance to delivery.** Meadowlark Regional Medical Center closed its obstetrics unit in September 2023, citing an inability to maintain call coverage after two of its three obstetricians retired within eighteen months. The closure removed the only delivery room in the county. Our patients now travel a minimum of 38 miles to the nearest labor and delivery unit, and for patients at our two westernmost sites the distance is 61 miles. The Nebraska Department of Transportation reports that 34 percent of the road miles on the primary route are two-lane highway subject to seasonal closure. In the winter of 2024–2025 we documented eleven instances of patients missing scheduled prenatal appointments because of road conditions, and two precipitous deliveries en route.

Distance to delivery changes behavior earlier in pregnancy than one might expect. Patients who understand that they will have to arrange transportation, childcare, and time off work for a delivery 38 miles away begin discounting the value of prenatal care that is itself an additional series of trips. Our no-show rate for prenatal visits rose from 14 percent in 2022 to 22 percent in 2025. The rise tracks the obstetrics closure with a lag of roughly two quarters.

**Second, the entry point is wrong.** A woman who suspects she is pregnant in our service area has no obvious front door. Our scheduling system requires her to identify herself as a new obstetric patient, which requires her to know that she is pregnant, which requires a test, which for an uninsured patient means either a retail purchase or a visit she must pay for. We do not currently offer walk-in pregnancy testing at any site. Our first available new-obstetric appointment averaged 19 days at the time of our internal audit in March 2026, and 26 days at the two sites with the highest Spanish-preferred volume. A patient who tests positive at seven weeks and waits 26 days for an appointment enters care at eleven weeks — inside the first trimester, but only barely, and any friction at all pushes her past it.

**Third, the workforce is not sized to the panel.** We employ two family physicians with obstetric privileges and one nurse practitioner who provides prenatal care but does not deliver. Our prenatal panel of 1,180 divided across that capacity produces continuity that is nominal rather than real; the median patient in our 2025 cohort saw three different prenatal providers across her pregnancy, and 31 percent saw four or more. We have no certified nurse-midwives. We have no community health workers assigned to maternal health. We have no doula relationships.

The downstream consequences are visible in the outcomes the Trust scores. Our postpartum visit rate within twelve weeks is 48 percent against a Healthy People target of 90 percent. Our low birth weight rate is 9.4 percent against a state rate of 7.1 percent. We do not currently have a reliable severe maternal morbidity denominator because deliveries occur at three separate hospitals under three separate record systems, and this measurement gap is itself part of the need we are asking the Trust to help close.

The population most affected is also the population least visible to the existing system. In 2025 we identified 84 women who presented to our sites for a postpartum or infant visit with no prenatal record anywhere in our system or at any of the three delivery hospitals with which we exchange records. These are women who received no prenatal care at all, or received it in a form that generated no documentation. Seventy-one of the 84 were best served in a language other than English. Fifty-eight were uninsured at the time of delivery.

## Project Description

The Platte Meridian Maternity Care Home reorganizes prenatal and postpartum care around three propositions: that the first contact must be immediate and unconditional, that the care team must include people who share the patient's language and community, and that the delivery relationship must be secured contractually rather than assumed.

### Component One: Immediate Access and the Pregnancy Front Door

We will establish walk-in pregnancy testing with same-day prenatal intake at all six sites. A patient who walks in, at any hour the site is open, receives a test at no charge and, if positive, a nurse intake visit the same day. That visit establishes the pregnancy in the record, orders initial laboratory work, initiates presumptive Medicaid eligibility determination where applicable, assigns a community health worker, and schedules the first clinician visit within seven calendar days.

This requires no new physical space. It requires a protocol, a standing order, dedicated nursing time, and a scheduling rule that holds two intake slots per site per day. We have modeled the demand: at 1,180 pregnancies annually across six sites, the expected walk-in volume is between one and two per site per day. The held slots will be released at 2:00 p.m. daily if unused.

We will also place pregnancy testing and a direct-dial intake line at eleven community locations already identified through our community advisory board: four churches, two mosques, the Hall County WIC office, two meatpacking plant occupational health offices, the community college student health office, and the public library. Materials will be produced in Spanish, Somali, Arabic, and English, at a fifth-grade reading level, with pictographic instruction for low-literacy users.

### Component Two: Midwifery Capacity

We will recruit and employ two full-time certified nurse-midwives. Recruitment begins in month one with a target of both positions filled by month nine. The midwives will carry a combined prenatal panel of approximately 700, provide continuity across the pregnancy, and hold delivery privileges at the partner hospital described below.

We have budgeted for a recruitment reality rather than an ideal. Nebraska has 187 licensed certified nurse-midwives, of whom 22 practice outside the Omaha and Lincoln metropolitan areas. Our compensation model is set at the 75th percentile of the Medical Group Management Association rural Midwest benchmark, includes a $30,000 signing incentive with a three-year service commitment, and includes relocation support and spousal employment assistance through our relationship with the regional economic development corporation. If we have not filled both positions by month twelve, our contingency is a locum tenens midwifery contract at a budgeted premium, described in the budget narrative, which allows the clinical model to launch on schedule while recruitment continues.

The midwives will not practice independently of physician oversight. Chief Medical Officer Karin Lindholm will hold clinical supervision, and the collaborative practice agreement will follow Nebraska Revised Statute 38-611 requirements. Draft agreements are in appendix D.

### Component Three: Community Health Workers

We will hire four full-time community health workers, one each assigned to Spanish-language outreach at our two highest-volume sites, one assigned to Somali and Arabic outreach across all six sites, and one assigned to rural transportation and appointment navigation across the two westernmost sites.

All four will complete the Central Community College Community Health Worker certificate, a 120-hour program with a maternal and child health module that the college has agreed to deliver in a cohort format on our schedule. A letter from the college's dean of health sciences is in appendix B. Two of the four positions will be recruited from among our existing patient population and current medical assistant staff, consistent with our commitment that this workforce come from the communities it serves.

The community health worker role is defined narrowly and documented deliberately, for reasons that matter to the sustainability discussion below. Each community health worker will operate under a written care plan authorized by a supervising clinician, will document each contact in the electronic health record under a discrete encounter type, and will record time. Their scope includes pregnancy test outreach, initial engagement, appointment scheduling and reminders, transportation arrangement, benefits navigation and Medicaid application assistance, home visiting in the third trimester and at two and six weeks postpartum, and connection to WIC, housing, and food resources. Their scope excludes clinical assessment.

### Component Four: Doula Services

We will contract with Prairie Roots Birth Collective, a Grand Island–based nonprofit that trains and employs doulas from immigrant and refugee communities, to provide doula support to 240 patients per year by year three. The contract is structured per-birth at $1,150, covering two prenatal visits, continuous labor support, and two postpartum visits. Prairie Roots currently employs six doulas, four Spanish-speaking and one Somali-speaking, and will expand to eleven under this contract with recruitment support from our community health workers. A signed memorandum of understanding is in appendix C.

Doula assignment will be offered to all patients and prioritized by a risk screen weighting prior preterm birth, prior cesarean, language other than English, first pregnancy, and absence of a support person identified at intake.

### Component Five: Evening Hours

We will extend hours to 8:00 p.m. two evenings per week at our three highest-volume sites: Grand Island Central, Hastings, and Lexington. Evening sessions will be staffed by one clinician, one nurse, one medical assistant, one community health worker, and interpreter coverage. Evening capacity adds approximately 1,100 appointment slots annually.

The rationale is empirical. In a 2026 survey of 312 prenatal patients, 44 percent reported having missed or delayed a prenatal appointment because of work schedule conflict. Among patients employed in meatpacking, the figure was 67 percent. Plant shift schedules in our service area run 6:00 a.m. to 2:30 p.m. and 3:00 p.m. to 11:30 p.m., and neither is compatible with a clinic that closes at 5:00 p.m.

### Component Six: The Delivery Partnership

We must be direct with the Trust about a change since our letter of intent.

Prairie Women's Health, the eleven-physician obstetric group we named in our September 5 letter of intent as our intended delivery partner, withdrew its commitment on June 18, 2026, following its acquisition by a private equity–backed physician services platform. The withdrawal came in a two-paragraph letter citing "portfolio alignment" and did not reference our specific arrangement. We had a signed letter of intent from Prairie Women's Health dated April 2026; we did not have an executed contract.

We report this because the Trust will find it in diligence and because our response to it is a legitimate part of what we are asking the Trust to fund. Since June we have pursued three parallel paths.

**Path one, now primary:** We have negotiated with Kearney Valley Health System, whose Good Shepherd Hospital operates a level II obstetric unit 38 miles from our Grand Island Central site. On October 22, 2026, Kearney Valley's chief medical officer and board executive committee approved a term sheet providing delivery privileges for our two midwives under Good Shepherd's existing collaborative agreement structure, obstetrician backup coverage on a defined escalation protocol, participation in a shared perinatal quality committee meeting quarterly, and bidirectional record exchange through the Nebraska Health Information Initiative. The term sheet is in appendix A. A definitive agreement is scheduled for execution by January 31, 2027, ahead of any project start. Kearney Valley delivered 640 infants in 2025 and has capacity for the approximately 700 additional deliveries our panel would generate at full scale, phased over five years.

**Path two, contingent:** We have opened discussions with Sandhills Regional Medical Center, 54 miles from our westernmost site, as a secondary delivery site for patients in the two western counties. Discussions are preliminary. We have not budgeted for a Sandhills relationship in years one and two.

**Path three, structural:** We have applied to the Nebraska Department of Health and Human Services for designation of our Grand Island Central site as a Rural Emergency Obstetric Access site under the 2025 state pilot, which would fund stabilization and transfer capacity but not delivery. A decision is expected in the second quarter of 2027.

We do not ask the Trust to accept a partnership that does not exist. We ask the Trust to note that we lost a partner and secured a replacement within four months, that the replacement is better capitalized and less exposed to ownership change, and that we have added a contingency the original plan lacked. The Prairie Women's Health experience has produced a lesson we have written into project governance: no clinical partnership will be treated as secured until a definitive agreement with a termination-notice provision of no less than 180 days is executed, and the project director will report partnership status to the board quality committee quarterly.

### Implementation Sequence

**Year one:** Execute Kearney Valley agreement. Recruit midwives and community health workers. Launch walk-in testing at all six sites by month four. Community health worker certificate cohort completes by month eight. Evening hours launch at Grand Island Central month six, Hastings month nine, Lexington month twelve. Doula contract begins month six at 60 births. Build the maternity registry.

**Year two:** Both midwives in full practice. Doula volume to 140. Home visiting protocol at full implementation. First full-year outcome measurement.

**Year three:** Doula volume to 240. Sandhills secondary agreement if warranted. Begin billing infrastructure work for community health worker sustainability. Midpoint external evaluation.

**Year four:** Sustainability transition begins; two community health worker positions shift to blended funding. Negotiate value-based arrangement with Medicaid managed care plans.

**Year five:** Full sustainability model in operation. Final evaluation. Dissemination.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

Baselines are drawn from the twelve months ending June 30, 2026. Targets are cumulative to the end of the stated year.

### Goal 1: Increase early entry into prenatal care.

**Objective 1.1.** Increase the proportion of prenatal patients entering care in the first trimester from 51 percent to 62 percent by the end of year two, 70 percent by year four, and 74 percent — the current state average — by the end of year five.

**Objective 1.2.** Reduce the disparity in first-trimester entry between English-preferred and non-English-preferred patients from 21 percentage points (61 percent versus 40 percent combined) to no more than 8 percentage points by the end of year five.

**Objective 1.3.** Reduce median time from positive pregnancy test to first clinician visit from 19 days to 7 days or fewer by the end of year two, sustained through year five.

**Objective 1.4.** Reduce the number of women presenting for postpartum or infant care with no prenatal record from 84 annually to 30 or fewer by year five.

### Goal 2: Increase postpartum engagement.

**Objective 2.1.** Increase postpartum visits completed within twelve weeks of delivery from 48 percent to 65 percent by year two, 78 percent by year four, and 85 percent by year five.

**Objective 2.2.** Achieve at least one community health worker home or telephone contact within 14 days of delivery for 90 percent of project patients by year three.

**Objective 2.3.** Increase postpartum depression screening using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, in the patient's preferred language, from 54 percent to 92 percent of postpartum visits by year three, with documented referral for 100 percent of positive screens.

**Objective 2.4.** Increase postpartum contraceptive method provision or documented decline from 61 percent to 88 percent by year four.

### Goal 3: Reduce severe maternal morbidity.

**Objective 3.1.** Establish a validated severe maternal morbidity denominator through the Kearney Valley data exchange by the end of year one, reporting the CDC 21-indicator composite for 95 percent of project deliveries.

**Objective 3.2.** Reduce severe maternal morbidity among project patients by 25 percent from the year-one established baseline by the end of year five.

**Objective 3.3.** Achieve documented blood pressure assessment within 7 days of delivery for 90 percent of patients with a hypertensive disorder of pregnancy by year three. This is the single highest-yield severe maternal morbidity intervention available to a community health center, and our community health workers will carry validated cuffs.

### Goal 4: Reduce low birth weight.

**Objective 4.1.** Reduce the low birth weight rate among project patients from 9.4 percent to 8.2 percent by year three and 7.1 percent — the state rate — by year five.

**Objective 4.2.** Increase the proportion of eligible patients receiving 17-alpha hydroxyprogesterone caproate or an appropriate alternative for prior preterm birth from 43 percent to 85 percent by year three.

**Objective 4.3.** Increase documented tobacco cessation counseling among prenatal patients who use tobacco from 66 percent to 95 percent by year two.

**Objective 4.4.** Increase WIC enrollment among eligible prenatal patients from 58 percent to 85 percent by year three.

### Goal 5: Reduce cost per prenatal episode.

**Objective 5.1.** Establish a validated cost-per-prenatal-episode methodology by the end of year one, incorporating direct clinical cost, allocated overhead, community health worker and doula cost, and attributable facility cost from delivery partner claims data.

**Objective 5.2.** Reduce cost per prenatal episode by 12 percent from the year-one baseline by the end of year five, driven primarily by reduced emergency department utilization, reduced neonatal intensive care days from lower low birth weight rates, and improved visit efficiency.

**Objective 5.3.** Reduce prenatal-related emergency department visits from 0.71 per pregnancy to 0.45 by year four.

### Goal 6: Build durable capacity.

**Objective 6.1.** Fill both midwife positions with permanent employees by month twelve and retain at least one original hire through year five.

**Objective 6.2.** Certify all four community health workers by month ten and retain at least three through year three.

**Objective 6.3.** Execute a definitive delivery agreement by January 31, 2027, with no lapse in coverage through year five.

**Objective 6.4.** Achieve a state or Medicaid managed care reimbursement pathway covering at least 55 percent of community health worker cost by the end of year five.

## Evaluation Plan

### Design

We propose a mixed-methods evaluation with an interrupted time series as the primary quantitative design, supplemented by a contemporaneous comparison group and structured qualitative inquiry.

The interrupted time series uses 20 quarters of pre-intervention data (2022 through 2026) and 20 quarters of intervention data, modeling level and slope change in first-trimester entry, postpartum visit completion, and low birth weight. Segmented regression with Newey-West standard errors will address autocorrelation.

The comparison group comprises prenatal patients at three peer Nebraska federally qualified health centers of similar size and demographic composition who have agreed to share aggregate UDS-derived maternal measures quarterly. This is not randomization and we do not claim it is. It provides a check against secular trend — particularly relevant given ongoing changes in Nebraska Medicaid postpartum coverage that will affect all sites.

We will also conduct a within-cohort dose-response analysis, comparing outcomes across patients receiving no community health worker contact, community health worker contact only, and community health worker plus doula. Assignment is not random, and we will use propensity weighting on language, insurance status, parity, distance to site, and prior care history, reporting results as associational.

### Measures and Sources

Process measures come from our Epic-based electronic health record: walk-in tests administered, time from test to first visit, appointment slots offered and filled, no-show rate, evening utilization, community health worker encounters, doula assignments, home visits, interpreter encounters.

Clinical outcome measures come from the record and from the Kearney Valley data exchange: first-trimester entry, prenatal visit count against Kotelchuck adequacy, postpartum visit within twelve weeks, depression screening and referral, contraceptive provision, birth weight, gestational age, mode of delivery, severe maternal morbidity composite, blood pressure follow-up after hypertensive disorder.

Cost measures come from our general ledger, cost report, and claims data from the three Medicaid managed care plans covering 71 percent of our prenatal panel; data use agreements are in negotiation with all three, with one executed.

Patient experience will be measured by a modified Mothers on Respect index administered in Spanish, Somali, Arabic, and English at the postpartum visit, with a target of 300 completed instruments annually.

Qualitative data will come from semi-structured interviews with 40 patients annually, stratified by language and entry timing, including deliberate oversampling of patients who entered care after the first trimester; focus groups with community health workers and doulas semiannually; and interviews with Kearney Valley clinical leadership annually.

### Data Infrastructure

We will build a maternity registry in the first six months, using the existing Epic Healthy Planet population health module, structured to define a pregnancy episode from first documented positive test or first prenatal encounter through twelve weeks postpartum, with linkage to the infant record and to delivery hospital data. The absence of this registry is why we cannot currently report severe maternal morbidity, and building it is a year-one deliverable independent of clinical outcomes.

The registry will support monthly run charts reviewed by the project team, quarterly measure sets reviewed by the board quality committee, and annual reports to the Trust.

### Evaluator

We will contract with the University of Nebraska Medical Center College of Public Health Center for Health Policy at $58,000 annually, 7.7 percent of project cost. The evaluation team will be led by a doctorally prepared health services researcher with prior federally qualified health center evaluation experience. The evaluator will conduct the design, analysis, and reporting; will hold access to de-identified project data under a data use agreement; and will produce an independent midpoint report in month 30 and a final report in month 58. A letter of commitment is in appendix E.

We ask the Trust to note that we have structured the evaluator relationship so that the evaluator reports to our board quality committee, not to the project director. The evaluator's midpoint report will be transmitted to the Trust in the form received.

### Use of Findings

The midpoint evaluation will trigger explicit decision points. If first-trimester entry has not reached 58 percent by month 30, we will convene a redesign session with the community advisory board and reallocate up to 15 percent of remaining project funds. If community health worker retention falls below two of four original hires by month 30, we will revisit compensation and supervision structure. If the delivery partnership shows signs of instability, we will accelerate the Sandhills path.

We will disseminate through the National Association of Community Health Centers annual conference, the Nebraska Association of Federally Qualified Health Centers, and at least two peer-reviewed submissions.

### The 2025 HRSA Site Visit

We address this directly.

HRSA conducted an Operational Site Visit in April 2025 evaluating compliance with all nineteen Health Center Program requirements. The visit found us compliant on seventeen requirements and produced two conditions.

**Condition one, Credentialing and Privileging (Requirement 12).** Reviewers found that our credentialing files did not consistently document primary source verification of current licensure for six of thirty sampled clinicians, and that privileging decisions for four clinicians lacked documented evidence of the specific clinical procedures approved. This was a documentation failure, not a licensure failure — all six clinicians held current, unrestricted Nebraska licenses at all times, which we verified and demonstrated to HRSA.

Our corrective action, submitted within the 30-day window, replaced a paper-and-spreadsheet process with a credentialing module integrated to our human resources system, with automated primary source verification queries at 90, 60, and 30 days before expiration; created a 0.5 FTE credentialing coordinator position, filled in July 2025; re-credentialed all 47 clinical staff between July and October 2025 under the new process; and established quarterly board quality committee review of credentialing status.

**HRSA removed condition one on November 14, 2025.** The condition removal letter is in appendix F.

**Condition two, Sliding Fee Discount Program (Requirement 5).** Reviewers found that our sliding fee application did not consistently document the basis for family size determination, that we could not demonstrate consistent annual re-application for 22 percent of a sampled patient set, and that our policy did not adequately address patients who decline to provide income documentation.

**This condition remains open.** Our corrective action plan was approved by HRSA in July 2025 with a completion deadline of March 31, 2027. Progress as of this application:

Completed items include revision of the sliding fee discount policy, approved by the board in September 2025, adding an explicit self-attestation pathway for patients unable to produce income documentation and clarifying family size determination; retraining of all 31 patient access staff, completed December 2025; redesign of the sliding fee application in four languages with plain-language field-level guidance, deployed February 2026; and implementation of an automated 60-day re-application prompt in the practice management system, deployed April 2026.

In progress: a retrospective review and re-determination of the approximately 4,100 patient records identified as having incomplete documentation. As of October 31, 2026, we have completed 3,180 of 4,100 records, 78 percent, running ahead of the schedule filed with HRSA. Remaining records are those requiring patient contact for documentation, and our patient access team is completing approximately 190 per month. Projected completion is April 2027, one month past the deadline. We have filed a request for a 60-day extension with our HRSA project officer; the request is pending.

Interim monitoring shows improvement. Our audit of sliding fee files for October 2026 found 96 percent complete documentation against a target of 95 percent, sustained above target for five consecutive months.

We tell the Trust three things about this condition. First, it is a documentation and process condition affecting how we record eligibility determinations, not a finding that patients were denied discounts or charged incorrectly; the HRSA report states this explicitly. Second, it is on a defined path to closure with a specific date and demonstrable monthly progress. Third, and most relevant to this application, the remediation directly strengthens this project — 27 percent of our patients are uninsured, sliding fee determination is the first financial conversation a newly pregnant uninsured patient has with us, and a fast, well-documented, self-attestation-capable process removes a barrier that currently pushes women past the first trimester.

We will report the status of this condition in each annual report to the Trust until it is closed.

## Sustainability

The sustainability question the Trust asks is specific: how do the community health worker positions get billed after year five? We answer it directly, and we answer the broader question about the whole model.

### The Community Health Worker Revenue Question

Four community health workers at full cost — salary at $44,000, fringe at 26 percent, supervision, training, mileage, and equipment — cost approximately $268,000 annually by year five. Sustaining them requires four revenue streams, and we have modeled each with a probability and a fallback.

**Stream one: Medicaid managed care value-based arrangements.** *(Target: $110,000, 41 percent. Probability: high.)*

Nebraska Medicaid contracts with three managed care organizations, all three of which cover our prenatal panel and all three of which are measured on HEDIS Prenatal and Postpartum Care measures, including timeliness of prenatal care and postpartum visit. Our project targets are precisely the measures on which their state contract performance depends.

We have opened discussions with all three. One, Nebraska Total Care, has an existing value-based framework with performance payments tied to prenatal and postpartum measures, and has indicated willingness to negotiate a maternal health arrangement based on demonstrated results. Their existing framework pays $180 to $340 per member per year for measure achievement in target populations.

Our approach: use years one through three to generate the outcome data, negotiate arrangements in year four, and operate them in year five under grant support so that any shortfall is absorbed before the grant ends. At 850 Medicaid-covered prenatal patients and a conservative $130 per member performance payment, this stream produces $110,500.

Risk: managed care contracts are re-procured, and Nebraska's current contracts expire in 2029. Mitigation: we will seek arrangements that survive re-procurement and will engage all three plans rather than depending on one.

**Stream two: Nebraska Medicaid community health worker reimbursement.** *(Target: $72,000, 27 percent. Probability: moderate.)*

Twenty-eight states now reimburse community health worker services through Medicaid, most under the 2013 preventive services rule allowing licensed practitioner recommendation of services delivered by non-licensed personnel. Nebraska has not yet adopted this pathway. However, the Nebraska Legislature's Health and Human Services Committee held an interim study on community health worker reimbursement in 2025, and DHHS has convened a stakeholder workgroup on which our Director of Grants and Contracts serves.

If Nebraska adopts a pathway on the model of Minnesota or Indiana, reimbursement would run $22 to $28 per fifteen-minute unit. At a conservative 2,600 billable units annually across four community health workers, this produces approximately $72,000.

We are candid that this stream depends on a state policy decision we do not control. Our mitigation has three parts. First, we are building the documentation infrastructure now — discrete encounter types, time recording, clinician-authorized care plans — so that we can bill on day one if the pathway opens, rather than spending eighteen months retrofitting. Second, we are participating directly in the policy process. Third, our financial model treats this stream as contingent; the fallback allocation is described below.

**Stream three: Federally qualified health center prospective payment system encounter revenue.** *(Target: $54,000, 20 percent. Probability: high.)*

Our Medicaid prospective payment rate is $214 per billable encounter. Community health workers do not generate billable encounters. However, they generate them indirectly and reliably by converting no-shows into completed visits.

Our current prenatal no-show rate is 22 percent. Our diabetes community health worker program reduced no-shows in its panel from 24 percent to 13 percent. Applying a conservative 8-percentage-point reduction across 1,180 prenatal patients at an average 11 prenatal and postpartum visits produces approximately 1,040 additional completed encounters annually. At a 62 percent Medicaid mix and $214 per encounter, that is $138,000 in gross additional revenue.

We claim only $54,000 of this, roughly 39 percent, because additional encounters carry marginal cost and because we prefer to be conservative. This stream requires no policy change and no negotiation. It is the floor of the model.

**Stream four: Operating margin and philanthropy.** *(Target: $32,000, 12 percent. Probability: high.)*

The board has adopted a resolution, attached in appendix G, committing to include maternal health community health worker positions in the operating budget beginning in fiscal 2031 at no less than $32,000 annually, with a stated intent to increase this allocation if other streams underperform. We will additionally pursue local philanthropy — the Grand Island Community Foundation and two regional hospital community benefit programs — for a targeted $40,000 annually, which we treat as upside rather than base.

**The fallback.** If the Nebraska Medicaid pathway does not open by year five, the model produces $196,000 against $268,000 in cost, a gap of $72,000. Our response, in order: increase the encounter-revenue claim from 39 percent to 60 percent of demonstrated value, which is defensible on three years of data and produces an additional $55,000; increase the board operating allocation to $50,000; and, if both are insufficient, reduce from four community health workers to three, retaining the two highest-volume language-specific positions and the rural navigation position. We would rather tell the Trust now that we would sustain three positions with certainty than promise four we cannot fund.

### Sustaining the Rest of the Model

**Midwives** are self-sustaining and are not a sustainability question. Two midwives at $135,000 salary and 26 percent fringe cost $340,000. At a combined panel of 700 patients averaging 11 encounters, a 62 percent Medicaid mix at $214 and commercial and self-pay revenue at a blended $168, they generate approximately $1.52 million in gross encounter revenue against $340,000 in direct cost. Grant support for midwives is front-loaded: 100 percent in year one during recruitment and ramp-up, 65 percent in year two, 35 percent in year three, 15 percent in year four, and zero in year five.

**Doula services** are the hardest sustainability question because no current Nebraska payer covers them. Fifteen states now provide Medicaid doula coverage, and Nebraska's stakeholder workgroup is examining this alongside community health workers. Our plan: maintain full grant support through year three; in year four, reduce the contract to 180 births with the difference covered by a dedicated philanthropic fund we will begin raising in year two with a $200,000 five-year target; in year five, operate at 180 births with grant support at 40 percent. Post-grant, doula services continue at whatever volume the dedicated fund and any Medicaid pathway support. We will not represent to the Trust that 240 doula-supported births will continue at that volume without new revenue.

**Evening hours** sustain themselves through the encounters they generate. At 1,100 annual slots, a 78 percent fill rate, and a 62 percent Medicaid mix, evening sessions produce approximately $155,000 in encounter revenue against incremental staffing cost of approximately $128,000. Grant support phases out entirely by year four.

**The delivery partnership** carries no ongoing cost to Platte Meridian beyond staff time on the quality committee. Kearney Valley's interest is a steady referral stream to a unit operating below capacity. That alignment does not depend on grant funding.

**The registry and evaluation infrastructure** are built on modules we already license. Post-grant maintenance is approximately 0.2 FTE of existing analyst time, absorbed into operations.

### Summary

By the end of year five, of $978,000 in annualized project operating cost, approximately $712,000 is covered by earned revenue and existing operating capacity, $145,000 by new value-based and community health worker reimbursement, $72,000 by board-committed operating margin, and $49,000 by dedicated philanthropy. Total sustained cost, $978,000; total identified support, $978,000. The exposure is the $72,000 community health worker gap if Nebraska does not act, and we have described exactly how we would close it.
