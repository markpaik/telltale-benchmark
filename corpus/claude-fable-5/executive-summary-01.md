# Executive Summary 1 of 4

## Final-Mile Delivery Network — Atlanta and Charlotte Markets

**Review period:** July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025 | **Date:** August 9, 2025

### Finding and Recommendation

The Program Review Office finds that the final-mile network serving Atlanta and Charlotte is the strongest-performing program in the company's portfolio and has moved beyond pilot economics into a durable, scalable line of business. The program exceeded its volume plan by 26 percent, delivered a unit cost 12 percent below target, and sustained service quality at 97.2 percent on-time performance while doing so. The Program Review Office recommends that the Executive Committee designate the final-mile network as a core operating program in the FY2026 budget, fund it at the level required to sustain current volume plus contracted growth, and authorize a formal expansion assessment for one additional Southeastern market, with findings due back to the Committee by December 1, 2025.

### What Was Reviewed

The review covered the full fiscal year of final-mile operations across both metropolitan markets: route economics, parcel volume against plan, cost per stop against the target set at budget approval, on-time delivery performance, customer concentration, and the operational strain created by growth well beyond plan. The review team examined monthly operating statements, route-level cost data, customer scorecards, and dispatch and capacity records, and interviewed market managers, dispatch leads, and the three largest customers by volume in each market.

### What the Evidence Shows

The network moved 2.4 million parcels against a plan of 1.9 million, an overage of roughly half a million parcels absorbed without a corresponding overrun in cost. That is the central fact of this review, and it is worth stating plainly: the program did not merely hit its numbers, it absorbed 26 percent more work than it was built and budgeted for and became more efficient in the process. Cost per stop finished the year at $4.18 against a target of $4.75. The gap of 57 cents per stop, applied across the year's delivered volume, represents the single largest favorable cost variance in any program the office reviewed this cycle.

The efficiency gain is not an accounting artifact. Route density improved as volume grew, which is the expected dynamic in final-mile economics — each additional parcel in a dense urban route costs less to deliver than the one before it — but the program's management converted that density into realized savings rather than allowing it to be consumed by overtime, casual labor, or missed windows. Dispatch records show that the markets added routes ahead of demand in three of four quarters, which kept driver utilization in a sustainable band and prevented the service degradation that typically accompanies volume surges of this size.

On-time performance of 97.2 percent for the full year confirms that cost discipline did not come at the expense of service. The figure held within a narrow band across all twelve months, including the November-through-January peak, when volume ran furthest ahead of plan. Customer interviews were consistent: the two markets are regarded as reliable, and no customer interviewed indicated an intention to shift volume elsewhere. The office notes one caution on service: 97.2 percent is strong, but the program's largest customers are moving toward contractual service floors of 98 percent in renewal discussions. The gap is closable, but it should be closed deliberately, with investment, rather than assumed away.

The review identified two risks that temper an otherwise unambiguous result. First, capacity. The network absorbed 26 percent excess volume by running its cross-dock and fleet assets harder; the headroom that made that possible has largely been consumed. If FY2026 volume grows at even half the rate of FY2025's overage, the markets will need additional dock doors, sortation labor, and route capacity, and the favorable cost curve will flatten or reverse if that capacity arrives late. Second, concentration. Growth has come disproportionately from the largest customers, and the program's dependence on its top accounts has increased over the year. Neither risk argues against the program; both argue for funding it properly rather than starving a winner.

### What the Program Asks of the Executive Committee

The Program Review Office asks the Committee to take three actions before the August 14 budget lock:

1. Approve the final-mile network's FY2026 operating budget at the level submitted by market management, which funds current volume plus contracted growth and includes the capacity additions identified above.
2. Authorize a formal expansion assessment for one additional Southeastern market, staffed jointly by the final-mile leadership team and the Program Review Office, with a go/no-go recommendation to the Committee by December 1, 2025.
3. Direct the commercial team to address customer concentration in the FY2026 sales plan, with a target for volume from accounts outside the current top tier.

### Disposition of Committed Funds

All funds committed to this program during the review period were operating funds, fully deployed, and fully earned back through revenue-generating activity; there is no stranded or at-risk capital to address. The question before the Committee is not recovery but reinvestment. The program's FY2025 cost performance generated savings against budget; the office recommends that those savings be treated as the funding source for the capacity additions and the expansion assessment, so that the program's growth is financed by its own demonstrated efficiency rather than by new demands on the corporate capital plan. Should the December expansion assessment return a positive recommendation, capital for a new market would come to the Committee as a separate request with its own business case, not as an extension of this budget.

# Executive Summary 2 of 4

## Refrigerated Transport Pilot — Nashville

**Review period:** September 2024 launch – June 30, 2025 | **Date:** August 9, 2025

### Finding and Recommendation

The Program Review Office finds that the Nashville refrigerated transport pilot has failed on every measure that mattered to its approval: utilization, unit economics, and customer commitment. Ten months into operation, the pilot is running at 41 percent trailer utilization against a 70 percent breakeven threshold, has produced an operating loss of $2.3 million, and has lost two of the three anchor shippers whose committed volume was the foundation of the original business case. The Program Review Office recommends that the Executive Committee terminate the pilot, authorize an orderly wind-down to be completed by October 31, 2025, and direct that the pilot's capital assets be redeployed or divested under a recovery plan to be approved by the Committee in September. The office further recommends that no refrigerated transport funding be included in the FY2026 budget.

### What Was Reviewed

The pilot was approved in mid-2024 as Cumberland's entry into temperature-controlled freight, launched out of Nashville in September 2024 on $6.8 million of capital, principally refrigerated trailers, cold-chain handling equipment, and facility modifications at the Nashville cross-dock. The business case rested on committed volume from three anchor shippers, projected to carry the fleet past the 70 percent utilization breakeven within the first year, with spot and contract volume filling in behind. The review examined utilization by month, the full operating loss and its composition, the circumstances under which the two anchor shippers departed, the state of the remaining anchor relationship, and the realistic prospects for recovery under continued operation.

### What the Evidence Shows

The pilot's economics never approached viability, and — more importantly for the Committee's decision — the trend is not improving. Utilization for the ten months averaged 41 percent against the 70 percent breakeven. Refrigerated equipment carries high fixed costs regardless of whether it moves: financing, insurance, maintenance, and fuel burn for temperature units. At 41 percent utilization, the pilot pays those fixed costs on a full fleet while earning revenue on well under half of it. That arithmetic produced the $2.3 million operating loss, and it will keep producing losses at a comparable rate for as long as the pilot operates at anything near current volume. The loss is not the residue of launch-period inefficiency that will fade with maturity; it is the structural output of a fleet sized for volume that did not materialize.

The volume did not materialize because the anchor commitments did not hold. Of the three shippers whose committed freight justified the fleet size, two have withdrawn. The review examined both departures. In neither case was Cumberland's service performance the stated or apparent cause; both shippers consolidated their refrigerated freight with larger incumbent carriers that could offer network breadth and pricing Cumberland cannot match as a subscale entrant. That finding matters because it defines the recovery problem: this is not a service failure that better execution could fix, but a competitive-position problem. Replacing the lost volume would require winning refrigerated freight in an established market against carriers with deeper networks and lower unit costs — precisely the disadvantage that the anchor commitments were meant to neutralize, and did not.

The one remaining anchor shipper continues to tender freight and is satisfied with service. Its volume alone supports roughly a third of the fleet. The review team modeled the scenarios under which the pilot could be salvaged — a smaller fleet sized to the remaining anchor plus realistic spot volume, aggressive commercial investment to replace the lost anchors, or a strategic pause. None reaches breakeven within a defensible period, and each requires the company to keep funding losses while it waits. The office's conclusion is that every additional month of operation converts recoverable capital into unrecoverable operating loss.

### What the Program Asks of the Executive Committee

The Program Review Office asks the Committee to take four actions:

1. Terminate the refrigerated transport pilot effective with the FY2026 budget, with no refrigerated operating or capital funding carried into the new fiscal year.
2. Authorize a wind-down concluding by October 31, 2025, including a commercially responsible transition for the remaining anchor shipper — either a negotiated handoff to a qualified refrigerated carrier or fulfillment of existing commitments through the wind-down date.
3. Direct the preparation of an asset recovery plan for Committee approval in September, covering redeployment, sale, or lease-out of the trailer fleet and disposition of the Nashville facility modifications.
4. Instruct Finance to book the anticipated write-down in the appropriate FY2026 quarter once the recovery plan fixes its size.

### Disposition of Committed Funds

The $2.3 million operating loss is spent and unrecoverable; the Committee's decision governs only whether it grows. The $6.8 million of capital is a different matter, and most of it is recoverable in whole or in part. The trailer fleet, which represents the majority of the capital, is standard refrigerated equipment with an active secondary market; units are candidates for sale, lease-out, or trade against dry-van equipment needs elsewhere in the network, including the capacity requirements identified in the final-mile review. The cold-chain handling equipment at Nashville is partially redeployable and partially saleable. The facility modifications are largely sunk, and the recovery plan should assume minimal value there. A realistic outcome is recovery of a substantial majority of the fleet capital and a write-down concentrated in facility work and early-loss depreciation; the September recovery plan will put firm numbers on that estimate. The office's summary judgment is that terminating now preserves most of what can still be preserved, and that the pilot's remaining value lies in its assets and its lesson — that Cumberland should not enter asset-intensive adjacent markets on customer commitments without contractual volume protection — rather than in continued operation.

# Executive Summary 3 of 4

## Driver Academy

**Review period:** July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025 | **Date:** August 9, 2025

### Finding and Recommendation

The Program Review Office finds that the Driver Academy delivered its output commitments in full — 218 graduated drivers at a cost of $9,400 per graduate — and that the company's twelve-month fleet retention improved from 54 percent to 63 percent over the same period. The office also finds that it cannot yet attribute that retention improvement to the Academy, because a company-wide driver pay increase took effect in the same quarter the improvement appeared, and because Academy graduates in the Birmingham lane continue to turn over at 48 percent, a rate indistinguishable from the pre-Academy baseline. The Program Review Office recommends that the Executive Committee renew the Academy for FY2026 at its current scale and cost — approximately $2.05 million — but withhold any expansion, and condition FY2027 funding on the results of a formal attribution study and a corrective review of the Birmingham lane, both due to the Committee by March 31, 2026.

### What Was Reviewed

The Driver Academy, directed by Talent Director Yolanda Brissett, recruits candidates without commercial driving experience and carries them through licensing, training, and supervised onboarding into Cumberland's fleet. The program was funded on two promises: a pipeline of drivers at a cost below external recruiting and sign-on economics, and improved retention, on the theory that drivers trained inside the company stay with it. The review examined graduate volume and unit cost against plan, retention outcomes for Academy graduates against the fleet baseline, lane-level variation in graduate turnover, and — critically — the confounding effect of the company-wide pay increase on any retention claim the program might make.

### What the Evidence Shows

On its output measures, the Academy performed as promised. It graduated 218 drivers during the fiscal year at $9,400 per graduate, on plan for both volume and cost. For comparison, the office reviewed the company's cost of hiring experienced drivers externally over the same period; when sign-on incentives, recruiting fees, and early-tenure attrition are included, the external channel's effective cost per retained driver runs materially above the Academy's. As a supply pipeline, the program works, and in a driver market that remains tight across the Southeast, a reliable internal pipeline has real option value independent of any retention effect.

The retention evidence is where the picture becomes genuinely uncertain, and this summary will not pretend otherwise. Twelve-month fleet retention rose from 54 percent to 63 percent — a nine-point improvement, and a meaningful one. If the Academy caused it, the program is one of the best investments in the portfolio, because the fully loaded cost of a driver departure — recruiting, training, lost productivity, and service disruption — makes each point of retention worth a substantial multiple of the Academy's budget. But the company-wide pay increase took effect in the same quarter the improvement appeared, and pay is the single most reliable lever on driver retention in the industry. The improvement shows up among non-Academy drivers as well as graduates, which is exactly the pattern a pay effect would produce. The data as currently collected does not separate the two causes. The honest statement is that retention improved, two plausible causes arrived together, and the company does not yet know how much credit the Academy deserves.

The Birmingham lane sharpens the doubt. Academy graduates assigned to Birmingham turn over at 48 percent — essentially the old baseline, and dramatically worse than graduates elsewhere. Two readings are possible. The first is that the Academy's effect is real but can be overwhelmed by lane conditions: Birmingham's schedules, freight mix, or supervision may be difficult enough that no amount of training and onboarding holds drivers there. The second is that the Academy's apparent effect elsewhere is mostly the pay increase, and Birmingham — for whatever local reason — is simply where the pay effect is weakest, exposing the Academy's true contribution as modest. The current data cannot distinguish these readings. The office's judgment is that the first is more likely, because graduate retention outside Birmingham exceeds non-graduate retention in the same lanes, but that judgment rests on thin cohort sizes and should not be the basis for expanding a multi-million-dollar program.

### What the Program Asks of the Executive Committee

The Program Review Office asks the Committee to take three actions:

1. Renew the Driver Academy in the FY2026 budget at current scale — roughly 220 graduates and approximately $2.05 million — with no expansion.
2. Commission a formal attribution study, jointly designed by the Talent organization and the Program Review Office, comparing matched cohorts of Academy graduates and externally hired drivers under the new pay structure, with results to the Committee by March 31, 2026.
3. Direct an operational review of the Birmingham lane — scheduling, freight mix, and frontline supervision — reporting on the same timeline, since a 48 percent turnover rate is a business problem regardless of what it says about the Academy.

FY2027 Academy funding, at any scale, should be conditioned on the attribution study's findings.

### Disposition of Committed Funds

The Academy is an operating program, and the roughly $2.05 million spent in FY2025 is fully expended; it purchased 218 licensed, road-ready drivers who are in the fleet today, so the money bought a real asset even under the most skeptical reading of the retention data. Nothing is stranded and nothing requires recovery. The forward question is sizing. The office's recommendation holds FY2026 spending flat, which caps the company's exposure at a level justified by the pipeline value alone — the Academy pays its way as a hiring channel even if its retention effect proves small — while the attribution study determines whether the retention effect justifies growth. If the study confirms a genuine Academy effect, the Committee should expect an expansion proposal for FY2027 and should regard it favorably. If it does not, the program should be re-scoped as a recruiting pipeline and sized strictly against hiring need. Either way, the company will be deciding on evidence rather than on a coincidence of timing.

# Executive Summary 4 of 4

## Olive Branch Automation Retrofit

**Review period:** July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025 | **Date:** August 9, 2025

### Finding and Recommendation

The Program Review Office finds that the Olive Branch automation retrofit, sponsored by VP of Engineering Hasan Demirci, has failed as a managed capital project while succeeding, so far, as a technology bet. Approved at $14.2 million, the project has spent $19.6 million — a 38 percent overrun — and its completion date has slipped eight months, from March to November 2025. At the same time, the one zone that has gone live is producing a 31 percent improvement in picks per labor hour, which validates the core productivity thesis on which the project was approved. The Program Review Office recommends that the Executive Committee authorize completion of the retrofit under a re-baselined budget and hardened governance — not because the money already spent argues for finishing, which it does not, but because the forward economics do: the remaining cost to complete is small relative to the annual value the finished facility will generate. The office simultaneously recommends a project-controls finding against the original estimating and change-management process, with corrective requirements binding on all future capital projects above $5 million.

### What Was Reviewed

The retrofit converts the Olive Branch cross-dock from manual case-pick operations to a semi-automated goods-to-person configuration, zone by zone, while the facility remains in operation. The review examined the full spend history against the approved budget, the sources of the $5.4 million overrun, the causes of the eight-month schedule slip, the measured performance of the live zone, the independent estimate of cost to complete, and the governance failures that allowed the overrun to accumulate without earlier escalation to the Committee.

### What the Evidence Shows

The overrun decomposes into three parts, in descending order of size. First, the original estimate materially understated the difficulty of retrofitting an operating facility: structural, electrical, and fire-suppression work uncovered during construction was neither surveyed nor contingently budgeted, and change orders in this category account for roughly half the overrun. Second, scope grew after approval — additional conveyor tie-ins and software integration work requested during execution — through a change process that approved additions without returning to the Committee, because each individual change fell below the escalation threshold. Third, the schedule slip itself compounded cost, as extended vendor mobilization and the prolonged operation of parallel manual and automated processes added carrying expense month by month. The pattern is familiar and avoidable: a thin initial estimate, permissive change control, and delay costs feeding on themselves. The office's finding is that this was a controllable failure of project management and sponsorship discipline, not bad luck, and the corrective requirements below reflect that.

Against this stands the live zone. Since activation, picks per labor hour in the automated zone are up 31 percent over the facility's manual baseline, sustained across shifts and measured over a period long enough to exclude novelty effects. Quality indicators in the zone have held or improved. This is the number the entire investment was made to produce, and it is being produced. Extended across the full facility at completion, the labor productivity gain translates into annual operating savings that the office estimates will repay the remaining cost to complete in well under two years, and repay even the full overrun-inclusive project cost on a timeline that, while far worse than the original business case, still clears the company's capital return threshold.

The office commissioned an independent cost-to-complete estimate rather than relying on the project team's figure. That estimate puts remaining spend at approximately $3.1 million to reach the November 2025 go-live, bringing the total project cost to roughly $22.7 million against the original $14.2 million approval. The Committee should treat $22.7 million, not $19.6 million, as the decision-relevant number, and should treat the independent estimate — which includes contingency the original never carried — as the ceiling. The alternative courses were examined and rejected: abandoning now strands $19.6 million in a half-automated facility that is operationally worse than either a manual or a completed automated building, and pausing simply adds carrying cost while preserving every completion expense.

### What the Program Asks of the Executive Committee

The Program Review Office asks the Committee to take four actions:

1. Approve completion of the retrofit under a re-baselined budget of $22.7 million total project cost, with the $3.1 million remaining authorization treated as a hard ceiling; any request beyond it returns to the Committee before commitment.
2. Impose completion-phase governance: monthly written status to the Committee covering spend, schedule, and live-zone performance; a freeze on all scope additions through go-live; and change-control authority moved from the project sponsor to the COO's office for the remainder of the project.
3. Adopt a standing project-controls requirement for all future capital projects above $5 million: independent estimate validation before approval, a contingency line sized to survey risk, and cumulative — not per-item — change-order escalation thresholds.
4. Defer consideration of any further automation capital, including the anticipated proposals for other facilities, until Olive Branch reaches go-live and demonstrates ninety days of full-facility performance.

### Disposition of Committed Funds

The $19.6 million spent to date is sunk, and the Committee should be explicit with itself that sunk cost plays no part in the recommendation to complete; the case for finishing rests entirely on the forward comparison of $3.1 million in remaining spend against the annual operating value of the finished facility, a comparison the live zone's 31 percent productivity gain makes strongly favorable. What the sunk capital does determine is the shape of the downside: abandonment would convert most of the $19.6 million into a write-off attached to a compromised facility, while completion converts it into a productive asset that, on current evidence, will earn an acceptable return even burdened with the full overrun. The money already committed is best understood as recoverable through operation and only through operation. The office's closing judgment is that the Committee should fund the last mile of this project, hold it to the ceiling and the governance above, and ensure — through the standing controls in the third recommendation — that Cumberland never again learns about a $5.4 million overrun at the annual review rather than at the moment it began.

# Appendix B — Portfolio-Level Observations

**From the Director of Program Review to the Executive Committee**

The four summaries stand on their own, and nothing in this appendix changes any recommendation in them. But an annual review that examines four programs side by side sees patterns that no single review can, and the office would be failing in its duty if it filed the summaries without naming them. Three observations follow.

### 1. The portfolio's failures were failures of entry, not of operation.

The two programs in trouble this cycle — the refrigerated pilot and the Olive Branch retrofit — went wrong before their first day of operation. The pilot was sized against customer commitments that carried no contractual protection; when two of three anchors walked, there was no remedy and no cushion, and everything that followed was arithmetic. The retrofit was approved on an estimate that had never been independently validated and carried no contingency for the survey risk inherent in retrofitting a live building; the $5.4 million overrun was substantially present, unrecognized, in the approval document itself. By contrast, the two programs that performed — final-mile and the Academy — were launched against demand the company controlled and estimates that proved sound. The lesson is not that Cumberland executes badly; the live zone at Olive Branch and the pilot's service record show the opposite. The lesson is that the company's approval process is more permeable than its operations, and the project-controls requirement in the Olive Branch summary (Appendix A, Item 10) should be understood as the portfolio-level fix, not merely an engineering matter.

### 2. Escalation thresholds are being satisfied while their purpose is being defeated.

Olive Branch reached a 38 percent overrun without a single change order large enough to trigger Committee review, because the thresholds test items rather than accumulation. The refrigerated pilot's utilization ran below breakeven for ten consecutive months without an interim escalation, because the pilot's reporting obligations were annual. In both cases the letter of the governance was observed and the Committee learned of eight-figure problems at the annual review. The office recommends that the Committee ask for a revised escalation standard this fall — cumulative variance triggers for capital, and quarterly viability checkpoints for any pilot operating below its breakeven assumption — and the office will draft one for the Committee's consideration by October 31, 2025.

### 3. The company's next dollar has an unusually clear home.

It is rare for a review cycle to produce so unambiguous a ranking. The final-mile network is generating favorable variance, has validated its unit economics at scale, and is constrained only by capacity the Committee can choose to fund. The wind-down of the refrigerated pilot will release both capital equipment and management attention. The office draws the Committee's attention to the fit between these two facts: trailers, dock capacity at Nashville, and commercial focus freed by the pilot's closure map closely onto the needs identified in the final-mile review and the December expansion assessment. The recovery plan due in September should be built with that redeployment explicitly in view, so that the portfolio's clearest failure directly funds its clearest success.

# Appendix C — Methodology and Attestation

The FY2025 review cycle was conducted between May 12 and July 25, 2025, by the Program Review Office with data support from Finance and independent estimating support, in the case of Olive Branch, from an external engineering cost consultant retained for that purpose. Each program review followed the office's standard sequence: data collection against the program's original approval document, site work, structured interviews with program leadership and — where applicable — customers, draft findings shared with the program sponsor for factual correction, and final findings issued solely on the authority of this office. Program sponsors reviewed their respective summaries for factual accuracy prior to submission. Two factual disputes were raised and resolved; one interpretive dispute, concerning the attribution of the fleet retention improvement in the Driver Academy review, was not resolved and is disclosed here: Talent Director Brissett's position is that lane-level comparisons already support attributing the majority of the improvement to the Academy. The office's position, stated in the summary, is that the cohort sizes are too thin to carry that conclusion and that the attribution study should decide the question. The Committee has both positions before it.

The findings and recommendations in this package are those of the Program Review Office alone.
