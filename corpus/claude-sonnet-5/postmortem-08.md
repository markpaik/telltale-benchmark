# Quinsigamond Regional Water Pollution Control Authority
## Incident Postmortem: March 14–16, 2027 Wet Weather Bypass Event

**Document status:** Final
**Distribution:** Board of Directors, Member Town Managers, MassDEP Regional Office, QRWPCA Senior Staff
**Prepared by:** Office of the Executive Director, in coordination with Operations, Compliance, and Maintenance
**Date issued:** April 2, 2027

---

## 1. Summary

On the evening of March 14, 2027, a convective storm cell delivered 3.1 inches of rainfall across the Quinsigamond Regional Water Pollution Control Authority (QRWPCA) service area in 14 hours, driving influent flows well above dry-weather baseline. During the event, a cascading sequence of equipment failures, a disabled alarm, a misdiagnosed fault, and a mis-sequenced pump start combined to reduce the plant's firm hydraulic capacity from 34 million gallons per day (MGD) to 19 MGD at the moment flows were highest. The Authority was unable to maintain full treatment and declared a permitted-exceedance bypass to the Blackstone River. The bypass ran 41 hours and released an estimated 11.4 million gallons of partially treated wastewater.

No single failure caused this event. The plant's influent pump 2 tripped on a variable-frequency-drive (VFD) fault at 9:52 p.m., a fault whose permanent fix — VFD replacement — had been budgeted in FY25 and deferred in two subsequent capital cycles. The high wet well alarm that should have alerted staff to rising levels within minutes had been silenced six weeks earlier during a contractor's SCADA point test and was never restored; the report documenting the silenced point was routed to a supervisor who retired in November and was not reassigned. Rising wet well levels went undetected for roughly six hours until a chief plant operator found them on a physical walk-through at 3:40 a.m. The initial diagnosis attributed the high level to a power dip logged at 9:48 p.m., four minutes before the pump trip, rather than to the VFD fault itself — a misreading that shaped the initial response. When staff started standby pump 4 to restore capacity, a discharge valve left closed after a January exercise blocked flow, destroying the pump's impeller and permanently reducing available capacity for the remainder of the event. A separate, unrelated maintenance backlog item — a bar screen rake motor on a work order open 94 days — caused the second screen to blind under heavy debris loading 90 minutes later, forcing the bypass declaration.

This postmortem examines the plant's systems, procedures, and decision points rather than the individuals who operated within them. Each person named in this document acted on the information and tools available at the time. The findings below describe why that information and those tools were insufficient, and what the Authority is changing as a result.

## 3. Timeline

All times are local (EDT) and approximate, reconstructed from SCADA historian logs, radio traffic, work order records, and staff statements. March 14–15, 2027 was a Sunday into Monday.

**February 3, 2027 (six weeks prior)**
- A contractor conducting scheduled SCADA point testing silences the high wet well level alarm as part of a standard test-and-verify procedure.
- The point is not restored to active status following the test. A report listing all silenced/inhibited points generated by the testing software is emailed to the on-shift supervisor of record.
- The supervisor retires in November 2026 layoffs — *note: per authority records, this supervisor's retirement was effective in November 2026, prior to this report being generated; the point had been silenced under a different but related test in an earlier cycle and the restoration step was never completed or reassigned in the intervening period.* The alarm remains inactive with no owner tracking its restoration.

**January 2027 (exact date not recorded in the work log)**
- Staff conduct a routine standby pump exercise on pump 4 as part of scheduled preventive maintenance. Following the exercise, the discharge valve associated with pump 4 is left in the closed position. No closeout checklist item requires confirming valve position before ending the work order.

**March 14, 2027**
- **6:00 p.m.–9:00 p.m.:** Storm cell moves through the service area. Rainfall intensifies; influent flows begin climbing above dry-weather baseline across all five member towns' collection systems.
- **9:48 p.m.:** A brief voltage dip is logged on the plant's electrical monitoring system, consistent with a grid-side disturbance during the storm.
- **9:52 p.m.:** Influent pump 2 trips on a VFD fault. The VFD had a documented, recurring intermittent fault condition; its replacement was included in the FY25 capital budget, deferred to FY26, and deferred again to FY27, with the unit still in service pending funded replacement at the time of the event.
- **9:52 p.m.–3:40 a.m.:** With pump 2 offline and inflow rising, wet well levels climb steadily. The high wet well level alarm, silenced since February 3, does not annunciate in the control room. No audible, visual, or remote (SCADA dial-out) alert reaches on-shift staff during this period. Influent continues to arrive faster than the remaining online pumps can convey to treatment.
- **3:40 a.m. (March 15):** Chief Plant Operator, on a scheduled physical walk-through of the wet well and pump gallery, observes the level well above normal operating range and notifies the shift supervisor. This is the first human detection of the developing event, roughly 5 hours 48 minutes after pump 2 tripped.
- **3:40 a.m.–4:10 a.m.:** Staff review recent electrical logs to diagnose the cause of the high level. The 9:48 p.m. voltage dip — the most recent anomalous entry in the electrical log — is identified as the probable cause. The pump 2 VFD fault trip at 9:52 p.m., recorded in a separate maintenance/fault log rather than the electrical event log, is not cross-referenced during this initial review. The working diagnosis becomes "transient power dip," not "pump 2 down on VFD fault." This shapes the initial response as a monitoring situation rather than a capacity-restoration emergency.
- **4:10 a.m.:** To relieve rising wet well levels, staff start standby pump 4. The discharge valve left closed after the January exercise is not reopened before start, and the pump's start-permissive logic does not include valve-position confirmation. The pump runs against a closed discharge line, and the impeller is destroyed within seconds of full-speed operation. Standby capacity intended to restore the plant to near-full flow instead removes pump 4 from service entirely. Firm capacity, factoring pump 2 (already down) and pump 4 (now destroyed), falls to approximately 19 MGD against continued rising inflow.
- **4:10 a.m.–5:20 a.m.:** With reduced capacity, flow through the remaining treatment train increases, and debris loading on the bar screens rises with the storm's collection-system scouring. Bar screen 1's mechanical rake, out of service on a work order open since December 10 (94 days), cannot clear accumulating debris.
- **5:20 a.m.:** Bar screen 2, the only remaining active screen, blinds under debris load, cutting preliminary treatment capacity further and creating conditions under which incoming flow cannot be fully processed without risk to downstream equipment.
- **5:20 a.m.–6:05 a.m.:** Operations staff assess options, including manual raking, emergency screen bypass gates, and load-shedding to storage, and determine that available capacity cannot handle current and forecast inflow without a controlled bypass.
- **6:05 a.m.:** Director of Operations declares a permitted bypass, opening the emergency overflow to the Blackstone River in accordance with the plant's wet weather operating plan (issued 2016) to protect the treatment train and prevent an uncontrolled plant flood.
- **6:40 a.m.:** Compliance Manager notifies the Massachusetts Department of Environmental Protection (MassDEP) of the bypass. Measured from the onset of bypass-triggering conditions at approximately 4:10 a.m. (loss of pump 4 capacity), this notification falls roughly 30 minutes outside the permit's two-hour reporting window.
- **Morning, March 15:** Maintenance staff begin diagnosing the pump 4 valve arrangement and assessing impeller damage; parallel efforts begin on manual bar screen 1 debris removal and coordination with member town collection system operators to reduce inflow where possible (I/I reduction measures, storage activation).
- **2:00 p.m., March 15:** Maintenance Supervisor completes a rebuild of the pump 4 discharge valve arrangement, restoring the valve to correct working order (pump 4 itself remains out of service pending impeller replacement).
- **March 15–16:** Plant operates in bypass mode as storm flows recede and treatment capacity is progressively restored through manual screen operation and drying of the collection system. A downstream drinking water utility intake, located within the zone of potential influence, shifts to backup supply as a precaution.
- **Approximately 11:00 p.m., March 16:** Bypass ends after 41 hours as flows return within the plant's reduced-but-adequate treatment capacity and the collection system stabilizes.
- **Sunday, March 21:** Executive Director briefs the five member town managers on the event, initial findings, and immediate corrective steps taken.
- **March 15–24 (nine days):** River access downstream of the outfall remains posted closed under state and local public health guidance.
- **March 18–19:** MassDEP issues a formal notice of noncompliance citing the bypass volume, the notification delay, and prior permit conditions, with associated penalty exposure of $312,000.

## 4. Root Cause

**The Authority's change-management and alarm-restoration process lacked a mandatory, tracked verification step to confirm that safety-critical alarms disabled for testing or maintenance are restored to active status before the associated work order is closed.**

The high wet well level alarm — the single most consequential monitoring point for this event — was silenced as an incidental step in an unrelated SCADA point test on February 3 and never restored. This was not a hardware failure; the alarm logic, wiring, and annunciation path were all functional. The failure was procedural: no system existed to guarantee that a silenced point generated an open action item with a named owner, a due date, and a closeout requiring confirmation that the point had returned to active, alerting status. The report identifying the silenced point was generated and emailed once, to one recipient, with no escalation path, no expiration trigger, and no secondary review. When that recipient left the organization, the item did not transfer to anyone else, and the silenced alarm remained invisible to the organization for six weeks until it was needed and was not there.

Every other factor in this event — the deferred VFD, the misread electrical log, the closed valve, the aging work order — increased the plant's vulnerability to a capacity-loss event. But it was the six-hour detection gap caused by the disabled alarm that converted a manageable single-pump outage into an unmonitored, worsening condition that had already consumed most of the plant's operating margin by the time a human being became aware of it. Had the alarm functioned as designed, staff would very likely have identified and responded to the pump 2 trip within minutes of 9:52 p.m., long before the wet well approached critical levels, and several of the subsequent decisions — the rushed pump 4 start, the compressed timeline for screen management — would not have been made under the same time pressure.

## 5. Contributing Factors

No root cause operates in isolation, and this event surfaced several conditions across capital planning, maintenance management, staffing, and procedural design that increased the plant's exposure and shaped how the response unfolded.

**5.1 Deferred capital replacement of a known-faulty component.** The pump 2 VFD had a documented history of intermittent faults sufficient to justify a capital replacement request funded in the FY25 budget cycle. That replacement was deferred twice, to FY26 and then FY27, without a corresponding update to the plant's risk register or wet weather operating plan to reflect the ongoing exposure. Capital deferral decisions were made on budget grounds without a parallel operational risk sign-off documenting the accepted exposure and any interim compensating controls.

**5.2 Absence of a closed-loop point-restoration process.** As discussed under root cause, the organization's process for temporarily disabling SCADA points during testing or maintenance did not require positive confirmation of restoration, did not include an automatic expiration or escalation, and depended on a single named recipient rather than a role-based distribution or tracked work order.

**5.3 Knowledge and responsibility loss at staff transition.** The silenced-point report was tied to an individual rather than a position or shared queue. When that individual retired, no handoff process surfaced outstanding items tied to their inbox, and no exit-review step existed to catch open safety-relevant items before departure.

**5.4 Cross-system log fragmentation.** The 9:48 p.m. voltage dip and the 9:52 p.m. VFD fault trip were recorded in two separate logging systems — an electrical monitoring log and a maintenance/equipment fault log — that were not reviewed together during the initial diagnostic pass. This fragmentation made it easier to settle on the more recent, more visible, and ultimately incorrect explanation (the power dip) rather than the correct one (the pump trip), and it delayed recognition that the plant had one fewer influent pump in service than assumed.

**5.5 Maintenance closeout procedures that do not verify system state.** The January standby pump exercise left a discharge valve closed with no closeout requirement to confirm and document valve position, and no start-permissive interlock on pump 4 to prevent a run against a closed discharge line. A single missing verification step — valve position confirmation — converted a routine standby pump start into equipment-destroying event.

**5.6 Aging work order backlog on redundant equipment.** The bar screen 1 rake motor work order had been open 94 days, exceeding the Authority's internal target for corrective (non-emergency) maintenance closure. Because bar screen 1 was one of two screens and screen redundancy is a core design assumption for wet weather resilience, an extended-duration open work order on either unit represented an unmanaged single point of failure for preliminary treatment during high-flow events. The work order aging was consistent with a broader maintenance backlog driven in part by chronic staffing vacancy.

**5.7 Operator staffing vacancy.** Nine of 34 budgeted operator posts were vacant at the time of the event, a 26% vacancy rate. While the overnight response team on March 14–15 was staffed to minimum coverage requirements, the vacancy rate constrains the organization's capacity for proactive maintenance completion, redundant system checks, and the kind of margin that allows staff to catch anomalies (such as a silenced alarm point) through channels other than the primary alerting system.

**5.8 Ambiguity in the external notification trigger.** The two-hour state notification requirement is measured from the onset of an unpermitted or noncompliant discharge condition, but the plant's internal procedures do not clearly define which operational event starts that clock in a multi-stage cascading failure (loss of a pump, blinding of a screen, or formal bypass declaration). This ambiguity contributed to the compressed window between recognizing the need to notify and actually doing so.

**5.9 An outdated wet weather operating plan.** The plan governing bypass declaration, capacity management, and inter-agency coordination during high-flow events was last issued in 2016. It does not reflect the plant's current equipment inventory, current staffing model, current SCADA alarm architecture, or lessons from any operational changes made in the intervening eleven years, and it was not the source of the specific detection or valve-verification steps this event demonstrated were missing.

## 6. What Worked

- **Physical rounds caught what the automated system did not.** The Chief Plant Operator's scheduled walk-through, though it occurred hours after the initiating fault, was the actual detection mechanism for this event and performed exactly the backstop function physical rounds are meant to provide. In the absence of a functioning alarm, this manual practice prevented an even longer undetected period.
- **Bypass declaration protected the treatment train.** Once screen capacity was clearly insufficient, the decision to declare a controlled bypass rather than allow an uncontrolled plant flood or equipment damage was the correct operational call given the conditions at hand, and it followed the plant's existing bypass protocol.
- **Rapid valve arrangement rebuild.** The pump 4 discharge valve rework was completed within about eight hours of bypass declaration, reflecting effective emergency maintenance response once the mechanical problem was correctly identified.
- **Downstream coordination protected drinking water.** Communication with the downstream drinking water utility allowed that utility to shift to backup supply proactively, preventing any water quality impact to treated drinking water despite four days of reliance on backup sourcing.
- **Inter-agency and inter-town notification, once initiated, was substantive.** Although the state notification was delayed relative to the permit window, the content of that notification and the subsequent briefing to member town managers provided a clear account of scope and impact, supporting informed downstream decisions about intake operation and river access posting.
- **No injuries and no uncontrolled flooding.** Despite the compounding failures, the plant avoided any uncontrolled discharge event beyond the declared bypass, any equipment damage beyond the single destroyed impeller, and any safety incident to staff working in a fast-moving overnight response.

---

## 7. What Did Not Work

- **The primary automated detection system provided no signal for six weeks and specifically failed at the moment it was needed.** The high wet well alarm's silence was invisible to daily operations until a human being physically observed the condition it should have flagged automatically.
- **Diagnostic tools did not support fast, correct root-cause identification under pressure.** The separation between the electrical event log and the maintenance fault log meant that the more recent, more prominent electrical anomaly was diagnosed as causal before the actual equipment fault was identified, costing time and shaping an initial response that treated the situation as less urgent than it was.
- **Standby equipment start procedures did not verify system readiness.** No interlock, checklist, or verification step prevented a pump start against a closed discharge valve, despite that valve having been closed by a documented prior work activity weeks earlier.
- **Maintenance backlog management did not flag redundancy-relevant risk.** A 94-day-old work order on one of only two bar screens should have been visible as an elevated risk item, particularly heading into wet weather season, but no backlog review process weighted work orders by their impact on system redundancy.
- **Handoff processes did not catch open safety items at staff transitions.** A single person's departure was sufficient to permanently orphan an open item with plant-wide safety significance.
- **Internal notification-trigger definitions did not support timely external reporting.** Ambiguity about which point in a cascading event starts the regulatory notification clock contributed to a delayed report, compounding the compliance impact of the event itself.
- **The governing wet weather plan had not been updated to reflect current plant conditions.** An 11-year-old plan could not be expected to anticipate current equipment, current staffing, or current alarm architecture, and did not.

## Appendix C: Glossary of Terms

**Bypass:** A permitted or emergency diversion of flow around all or part of the treatment process, typically to a receiving water body, used to protect the treatment train or prevent uncontrolled flooding when hydraulic or treatment capacity is exceeded.

**Firm capacity:** The maximum flow a treatment plant can reliably process with its largest single unit out of service, used as the standard planning and permit-compliance benchmark rather than theoretical maximum capacity with all units running.

**Influent and Infiltration (I/I):** Extraneous groundwater and stormwater entering a sanitary sewer collection system through cracks, faulty connections, or direct stormwater cross-connections, a primary driver of wet weather flow surges independent of plant-level equipment performance.

**SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition):** The computerized control and monitoring system used to operate, alarm, and record plant equipment status and process conditions in real time.

**Silenced/inhibited point:** A SCADA alarm or data point that has been manually suppressed, typically for testing, calibration, or maintenance purposes, such that it will not annunciate or record even if the underlying condition it monitors occurs.

**Variable Frequency Drive (VFD):** An electronic controller that regulates motor speed by varying the frequency and voltage of power supplied to the motor, commonly used on pumps to allow variable-speed operation and reduce mechanical and electrical stress compared to fixed-speed operation.

**Wet weather operating plan:** The Authority's governing procedural document describing how the plant is to be operated, staffed, and managed during storm events, including criteria and procedures for bypass declaration, inter-agency notification, and coordination with member towns and downstream water users.

## Appendix D: Related Prior Findings

This event is not the first instance in which the Authority's asset and alarm management practices have been flagged as an area of risk. For completeness, the following prior findings are noted as relevant context, though none identified the specific failure mode that occurred on March 14–15, 2027:

- The Authority's FY24 capital plan narrative, submitted to the Board in support of the VFD replacement request, noted "recurring intermittent fault condition, risk of unplanned outage during high-flow events" as the stated justification for funding. This language was carried forward unchanged through the FY25 and FY26 deferral cycles without a corresponding updated risk assessment.
- An internal maintenance backlog report presented to the Director of Operations in October 2026 flagged that corrective work order aging had increased across the maintenance program relative to the prior year, attributing the trend in part to staffing vacancy, but did not break out backlog aging by redundancy criticality (i.e., distinguishing single-point-of-failure equipment from equipment with in-place redundancy).
- No prior internal or external audit specifically reviewed the SCADA point-suppression and restoration process; this gap had not previously been identified as an audit scope item.

The Authority's Compliance Manager will incorporate a review of SCADA change-management practices into the scope of the next scheduled internal operations audit, currently planned for the fourth quarter of 2027.
