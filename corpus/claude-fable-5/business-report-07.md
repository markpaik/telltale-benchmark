## 1. Executive Summary

Brandywine Medical Group closed the third quarter of fiscal 2026 with strong clinical volumes across most service lines, continued growth in orthopedics, and serious operational strain in three areas that will define the fourth quarter: the revenue cycle disruption following the April electronic health record conversion, the loss of three cardiologists effective August 1, and the unsigned renewal of our contract with the state's largest commercial payer, which expires September 30.

The practice generated net patient revenue consistent with the $488 million annual run rate, but cash performance deteriorated sharply. Initial claim denials rose from 6.9 percent before the EHR conversion to 12.8 percent at quarter end, and days in accounts receivable climbed from 41 to 58. Approximately $19 million in cash is currently trapped behind denied or delayed claims. This is a timing and process problem, not a demand problem — patient volumes remain healthy — but it is a cash problem that the finance committee should treat as the most urgent operational item on the agenda after the payer contract.

At the service line level, the quarter presents a mixed picture:

- **Primary care** delivered 412,000 visits across 98 providers and remains the referral engine of the group, but access is degrading (22 days to third next available appointment) and the division lost $4.2 million before referral contribution.
- **Orthopedics** grew surgical cases 9 percent to 6,100, migrated 61 percent of cases to the ambulatory surgery center, and contributed $31 million — the strongest financial performance in the group.
- **Cardiology** lost three physicians to resignation effective August 1, projects a 14 percent volume decline, and carries a 31-day echocardiogram backlog. The division chair attributes the departures to call coverage burden.
- **Behavioral health** operates with 41 clinicians against a budgeted complement of 50, delivers 68 percent of visits by video, and is running a 24 percent no-show rate that materially erodes effective capacity.
- **Imaging** performed 218,000 studies at 74 percent MR utilization, with one Middletown scanner out of service for six weeks due to a helium compressor failure.

The report that follows works through each service line, then the revenue cycle situation, then the payer negotiation, and closes with the fourth-quarter outlook and the decisions we are asking the committees to weigh.

## 3. Service Line Review: Primary Care

### 3.1 Volume and Access

Primary care remains the largest service line by visit volume and the front door for nearly every other division. The 98 primary care providers delivered 412,000 visits in the trailing twelve months through Q3, a 3.8 percent increase over the prior year. Panels average 2,180 patients per provider, which is at the upper edge of what most benchmarking sources consider sustainable for a mixed adult and family medicine practice without robust team-based support.

The access picture is where the strain shows. Days to third next available appointment — our standard access measure, chosen because it is less distorted by cancellations than first-available — now stands at 22 days across the division. That average conceals meaningful site-level variation: our three highest-demand sites in the Newark and Bear corridors exceed 30 days, while several Wilmington sites remain under 14. Twenty-two days is roughly double where the division stood two years ago and is well beyond the 10-day internal target set by the quality committee.

The consequences of degraded access are predictable and we are seeing all of them: rising same-day demand routed to urgent care and outside retail clinics, patient complaints trending upward in the access category, and — most consequentially for the enterprise — leakage risk on downstream referrals. When patients cannot get in to see their primary care provider, the referral pathway into our own orthopedics, cardiology, and imaging capacity weakens.

### 3.2 Staffing

The division's 98 providers break down as 71 physicians and 27 advanced practice providers. Turnover for the trailing twelve months was 8 percent, in line with regional norms, but recruiting replacement providers is taking longer: average time-to-fill for a primary care physician vacancy is now 11 months. Six searches are currently open. Medical assistant staffing, which is the binding constraint on rooming efficiency and throughput at several sites, is running a 12 percent vacancy rate, and we continue to compete with the two local health systems on medical assistant wages.

### 3.3 Financial Performance

Primary care lost $4.2 million before referral contribution over the trailing twelve months. This is worse than the prior year's $3.1 million loss, driven by three factors in roughly equal measure: provider compensation growth outpacing collections per visit, the medical assistant wage adjustments implemented in Q1, and — in the final quarter of the period — the denial spike hitting primary care's high volume of low-dollar claims especially hard, because the rework cost per denied claim is proportionally larger on a $140 office visit than on a $14,000 surgical case.

The phrase "before referral contribution" deserves emphasis for committee members newer to the group's economics. Primary care is not expected to be a standalone profit center. Internal attribution analysis indicates that primary care referrals originate the majority of orthopedic surgical volume, the substantial majority of imaging volume, and effectively all of the group's value-based contract attribution, which carried $6.8 million in shared savings and quality incentive revenue last year. Judged on a fully attributed basis, primary care is comfortably accretive. Judged on its own ledger, it is a $4.2 million loss, and the loss is growing. Both statements are true, and the strategic question — addressed in the Q4 outlook — is how much access investment the group is willing to fund against the attributed rather than the direct return.

## 4. Service Line Review: Orthopedics

### 4.1 Volume and Site-of-Service Migration

Orthopedics delivered the strongest quarter in the group. Surgical cases grew 9 percent year over year to 6,100, with growth concentrated in total joint replacement, sports medicine, and hand. More important than the raw growth is where the cases were performed: 61 percent went to the ambulatory surgery center, up from 52 percent last year. This site-of-service migration is the single most valuable operational trend in the group's portfolio, for two reasons. First, ASC cases carry materially better economics for the group under our current commercial contracts than hospital-based cases. Second, payers — including the payer whose contract is now under negotiation — are actively steering members toward ASC settings, so our migration positions us on the right side of network design rather than fighting it.

Clinic visit volume in orthopedics grew in step with surgical volume, and new-patient access remains acceptable at 9 days to third next available, though the two busiest surgeons are booking new patients past 3 weeks.

### 4.2 Staffing

The division fields 24 surgeons, 19 advanced practice providers, and a physical therapy staff that continues to run near capacity. One sports medicine surgeon search is open, driven by growth rather than turnover; the division has had no physician departures in eighteen months. This retention record is worth the committees' attention as a contrast case when we discuss cardiology: orthopedics restructured its call arrangement two years ago with a paid-call model and dedicated APP first-call coverage, and the retention results speak for themselves.

The near-term staffing constraint in orthopedics is not surgeons but operating room and ASC support staff. Surgical technologist vacancies at the ASC forced the cancellation of an estimated 40 case slots during the quarter. At current contribution margins per ASC case, that is a meaningful revenue opportunity left on the table, and the division has requested two additional surgical tech positions and a retention premium, which operations leadership supports.

### 4.3 Financial Performance

Orthopedics contributed $31 million over the trailing twelve months, up from $26 million the prior year. The improvement decomposes into volume growth, ASC migration, and favorable case mix as total joints continue to shift outpatient. The division also absorbed the denial spike better than any other service line, because surgical claims move through a dedicated authorization and coding team that adapted to the new EHR's workflows faster than the general billing operation.

### 4.4 Risks

Two risks temper the strong result. Second, the ASC is approaching physical capacity on peak days; if migration continues at the current pace, we will face a capital decision on a fourth operating room within the next fiscal year. A preliminary capacity analysis will come to the finance committee in Q1 FY2027.

## 5. Service Line Review: Cardiology

### 5.1 The Departures

Three cardiologists — two general cardiologists and one imaging-focused cardiologist — resigned effective August 1, 2026. The departures were announced within a three-week span in June and, while the physicians made independent decisions, division chair Dr. Stefan Marek is unambiguous about the common cause: call coverage. With the division's prior complement of 14 physicians covering a call schedule that includes STEMI-adjacent urgent coverage arrangements and a growing volume of after-hours imaging reads, call frequency had reached one-in-seven and was headed toward one-in-five as two senior physicians reduced their call participation ahead of retirement. All three departing physicians cited call burden in their exit interviews, and two of the three accepted positions with call frequencies of one-in-ten or better.

This is a retention failure with a clear proximate cause, and I want to state plainly for the record that Dr. Marek raised the call structure issue in writing to operations leadership in Q4 of last fiscal year. The compensation and call redesign proposal developed at that time was deferred pending the EHR conversion. That deferral now looks costly.

### 5.2 Volume and Access Impact

The division projects a 14 percent volume decline across office visits, diagnostics, and procedures for the period the vacancies remain open. The most visible symptom is the echocardiogram backlog, which stands at 31 days — meaning a patient referred today for a routine echo waits a month for the study, and then additional time for the interpretation and follow-up visit. Prior to the departures the backlog was 12 days. Urgent and inpatient-adjacent studies are being triaged appropriately and read within 48 hours, so the quality committee should understand this as an elective-access problem rather than an acute-safety problem at present; however, a 31-day backlog on a diagnostic study is the kind of delay that converts into safety events if it lengthens, and we are monitoring the triage queue weekly.

Secondary effects are already measurable. Primary care providers report growing difficulty placing routine cardiology referrals, and we have documented an uptick in referrals leaving the group for the two hospital-system cardiology practices in the county. Every leaked cardiology referral carries imaging and downstream procedural volume with it. The leakage cost of this staffing gap will exceed the direct compensation savings from the vacant positions within one to two quarters.

### 5.3 Stabilization Plan

The stabilization plan in motion has four components:

1. **Locum tenens coverage.** Two locum cardiologists begin in mid-September, contracted through December with an option to extend. Locum cost runs roughly 40 percent above employed-physician cost on a per-clinical-day basis; the finance committee has approved this through Q2 FY2027.
2. **Echo capacity.** Extended sonographer hours and weekend reading sessions began August 18, with a target of returning the echo backlog to under 14 days by October 31.
3. **Call redesign.** The deferred call restructuring proposal — paid call at market rates, an APP-supported first-call tier, and a cap on consecutive call assignments — goes to the physician board in September. Dr. Marek regards this as the precondition for any successful recruitment.
4. **Recruitment.** Three searches are open with a retained firm. Realistic time-to-start for cardiology hires is 9 to 14 months; the division will operate below complement for most of fiscal 2027 under any scenario.

### 5.4 Financial Performance

Cardiology contributed $8.9 million over the trailing twelve months, down from $10.4 million, with the decline concentrated in the final quarter as departing physicians wound down their schedules. The Q4 and FY2027 projection incorporating the 14 percent volume decline and locum costs shows contribution falling to an annualized $5.5 to $6.0 million range until staffing recovers.

## 6. Service Line Review: Behavioral Health

### 6.1 Staffing and Capacity

Behavioral health operates with 41 clinicians — psychiatrists, psychiatric nurse practitioners, psychologists, and licensed clinical social workers — against a budgeted complement of 50, a vacancy rate of 18 percent. The nine vacancies are concentrated among therapists rather than prescribers, which shapes the access problem: medication management access is adequate, while therapy access for new patients now extends past six weeks at most sites. Recruiting in this labor market remains the hardest in the group; candidates routinely weigh our offers against fully remote national telehealth employers, and our compensation structure has not kept pace with that comparison set.

### 6.2 The Virtual Care Model and the No-Show Problem

Sixty-eight percent of behavioral health visits are now delivered by video, a share that has held steady for two years and that patients broadly prefer. The virtual model has real advantages: it eliminated the division's physical space constraint, it broadens the recruiting pool geographically within licensure limits, and completion rates for established-patient medication follow-ups are strong.

The problem is the 24 percent no-show rate. On a base of scheduled visits large enough that completed visits reached 31,400 for the trailing twelve months, a 24 percent no-show rate means we scheduled roughly 41,000 visits to complete 31,400 — nearly 10,000 clinician-hours of capacity evaporated in a division that is nine clinicians short. The no-show rate is the single largest recoverable capacity source in the division, larger than any realistic hiring outcome this year. No-shows skew toward new-patient therapy intakes, Medicaid-covered patients, and appointments booked more than three weeks out — which, given the six-week therapy wait, describes most therapy appointments. The wait time and the no-show rate feed each other: long waits drive no-shows, and no-shows lengthen waits.

Countermeasures launching in Q4: a two-touch confirmation protocol with same-week rebooking of unconfirmed slots, a standing waitlist with automated same-day fill offers, and deliberate short-lead scheduling for the highest-risk appointment categories. The internal target is a no-show rate below 18 percent by end of Q2 FY2027, which would recover the equivalent of approximately 2,400 completed visits annually — comparable to adding two clinicians at zero recruiting cost.

### 6.3 Financial Performance

Behavioral health lost $1.6 million over the trailing twelve months, an improvement from a $2.1 million loss the prior year, driven by the lower facility footprint of the virtual model. As with primary care, the standalone ledger understates the division's value: behavioral health integration supports primary care panel management, contributes to value-based contract quality scores, and is an explicit network-adequacy requirement in the commercial contract now under negotiation. The payer's own network filings show behavioral health access as a documented deficiency in this market, which gives our 41-clinician division more negotiating relevance than its financial size suggests.

## 7. Service Line Review: Imaging

### 7.1 Volume and Utilization

Imaging performed 218,000 studies across 11 sites in the trailing twelve months, up 4.1 percent, with growth led by MR and CT. Magnetic resonance utilization across the fleet stands at 74 percent of available scan slots — a healthy figure that leaves ordinary headroom for growth at most sites but that conceals a wide spread: two Wilmington-area magnets are effectively at capacity during weekday hours, while the Middletown site — for reasons discussed immediately below — ran well under plan.

### 7.2 The Middletown Scanner Outage

One of the two MR scanners at the Middletown site was out of service for six weeks following a helium compressor failure. The failure occurred in late May; parts availability, not service response, drove the duration, as the compressor assembly required sourcing from the manufacturer's European supply chain. The scanner returned to service in mid-July.

Operational impact: approximately 1,100 MR studies were displaced during the outage. Roughly 60 percent were absorbed by extended hours on the site's second magnet and by rebooking to the Bear and Glasgow sites; the remaining 40 percent — around 440 studies — were either delayed beyond acceptable windows or lost to outside imaging centers. Direct financial impact, combining lost volume and premium staffing for extended hours, is estimated at $700,000 to $850,000.

Two follow-ups are underway. First, we are pursuing recovery under the service contract's uptime guarantee; the contract carries a 96 percent uptime commitment and the outage put the unit well below it, though the recoverable credit is capped at a fraction of the actual loss. Second, the failure has prompted a fleet reliability review: the Middletown unit is nine years old, two other magnets in the fleet are of similar vintage, and the finance committee will see a scanner replacement sequencing proposal alongside the FY2027 capital plan. The lesson of this quarter is that an aging magnet's failure mode is not gradual degradation but a six-week hole in capacity.

### 7.3 Staffing and Financial Performance

Technologist staffing is adequate in CT and radiography, tight in MR and ultrasound, with sonographer capacity now doubly stressed by the cardiology echo backlog response described in Section 5. Imaging contributed $18.2 million for the trailing twelve months, down modestly from $18.9 million, with the Middletown outage accounting for most of the decline.

## 8. Revenue Cycle: The EHR Conversion, Denials, and Receivables

### 8.1 What Happened

The group converted to its new electronic health record on April 6, 2026, replacing a platform in place for eleven years. The clinical conversion has gone acceptably: provider adoption is on track, no patient-safety events have been attributed to the transition, and clinical documentation quality is stable. The revenue cycle conversion has not gone acceptably, and the numbers are stark:

| Revenue Cycle Measure | Pre-Conversion (Mar 2026) | Q3 Close (Jun 2026) | August 2026 | Target |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Initial claim denial rate | 6.9% | 12.8% | 11.1% | <7.0% |
| Days in accounts receivable | 41 | 58 | 56 | <45 |
| Cash held behind denials/delays | — | $19M | ~$17M | <$5M |
| Clean claim rate | 91% | 79% | 83% | >90% |

### 8.2 Root Causes

Analysis of denied claims from April through July attributes the denial spike to four causes, in descending order of dollar impact:

1. **Charge capture and coding mapping errors (roughly 35 percent of excess denials).** The conversion's charge master and code-mapping crosswalk carried errors in several high-volume categories — most damagingly in imaging modifiers and preventive-versus-problem visit coding in primary care — that generated systematic denials until identified and corrected. The largest mapping fixes were deployed in June and July.
2. **Eligibility and registration workflow gaps (roughly 25 percent).** Front-desk registration workflows in the new system permitted claims to release without complete eligibility verification during the first two months. Hard-stop edits were implemented in June.
3. **Prior authorization linkage failures (roughly 22 percent).** Authorizations obtained in the legacy system did not consistently transfer to claims generated in the new system, producing avoidable authorization denials on procedures that were in fact properly authorized. These are recoverable on appeal but at significant rework cost.
4. **Payer-side enrollment and EDI transition issues (roughly 18 percent).** Several payers required re-enrollment of electronic claim and remittance connections; two mid-sized payers took over eight weeks to complete it, during which claims aged without adjudication.

The honest summary is that the conversion plan under-resourced revenue cycle testing and parallel validation relative to clinical readiness. Clinical go-live readiness was rehearsed extensively; claims-output validation was compressed in the final planning phase.

### 8.3 Recovery Program and Trajectory

A dedicated recovery program has been in place since mid-June: a denials task force meeting daily, 14 temporary billing staff engaged through a revenue cycle firm, weekly payer-by-payer aging escalation calls with our five largest payers, and a standing fix-verify-monitor cycle on system edits. August results show the corner has been turned — denials at 11.1 percent, AR days at 56, both improving month over month — but the recovery slope matters enormously to fiscal 2027 cash planning.

Current trajectory and targets:

| Milestone | Denial Rate | AR Days | Trapped Cash |
|---|---|---|---|
| October 31, 2026 | ≤9.5% | ≤52 | ≤$13M |
| December 31, 2026 | ≤8.0% | ≤48 | ≤$8M |
| March 31, 2027 | ≤7.0% | ≤45 | ≤$4M |

Two cautions accompany these targets. First, a portion of the aged denials — currently estimated at $1.5 to $2.5 million — will pass timely-filing or appeal deadlines and convert to permanent write-offs; a firm bad-debt estimate will accompany the year-end close. Second, the recovery program itself costs approximately $340,000 per month in temporary staffing and consulting, which is fully justified by the cash at stake but should be visible to the finance committee as a Q4 and Q1 expense.

Cash position remains adequate: the group drew $8 million on its working capital line in June and expects to repay it by Q2 FY2027 as the trapped receivables collect. Physician distribution timing was adjusted in Q3, which — as noted in Section 5 — is a morale and retention variable, not merely a treasury one.

## 9. The Commercial Payer Negotiation

### 9.1 Situation

The group's contract with the state's largest commercial payer expires September 30, 2026, and remains unsigned as of this report. This payer represents approximately 31 percent of net patient revenue — on the order of $150 million annually — and a larger share of orthopedics and imaging revenue specifically. It is, by a wide margin, the group's most consequential contract.

The gap between positions, in broad strokes: the group opened seeking rate increases that recover three years of below-inflation escalators, with the largest asks on evaluation-and-management codes supporting primary care and behavioral health. The payer's most recent counter offers increases well below our position on professional services, couples them with proposed site-of-service payment reductions on imaging, and seeks expanded prior authorization requirements. The payer has separately expressed interest in a broader value-based arrangement, which we regard as genuine and worth pursuing — but not as a substitute for adequate fee-for-service rates in year one.

### 9.2 Leverage Assessment

The committees should understand our leverage honestly, in both directions.

**Working for us:** Brandywine's 26-site footprint and 310 providers make us the largest independent multispecialty group in the county; network adequacy without us is genuinely difficult in primary care and behavioral health, where the payer has documented access deficiencies. Our 61 percent ASC migration in orthopedics saves this payer real money relative to hospital-based alternatives, and we can quantify it. Our value-based performance history is strong.

**Working against us:** The timing is poor. The payer's network team is aware of our EHR conversion difficulties — denial patterns are visible to them from their side of the adjudication — and a group with $19 million in constrained cash and adjusted physician distributions is less credible in an out-of-network standoff than one with a clean balance sheet. The cardiology departures modestly weaken the network-adequacy argument in that specialty. And an out-of-network period, even a short one, would compound the current cash strain unacceptably.

### 9.3 Status and Scenarios

Negotiating sessions are scheduled for the weeks of September 8 and September 22. Three scenarios frame the planning:

1. **Signed renewal before September 30** at rates between the parties' current positions, with a value-based rider negotiated for FY2027. This remains the most likely outcome and the one both sides' behavior signals.
2. **Short-term extension** (60 to 90 days at current rates) to continue negotiating. Acceptable, and preferable to scenario three, though it prolongs uncertainty and defers rate relief.
3. **Out-of-network effective October 1.** The contingency plan — patient communication templates, continuity-of-care protections for patients in active treatment, and a schedule-management approach — is drafted and held by the CFO's office. We assess this scenario as unlikely but not negligible, and the contingency cash modeling shows it would require additional working capital line access within 60 days.

Management's recommendation to the finance committee is to authorize a settlement corridor for the negotiating team — a defined floor on aggregate rate lift and defined limits on authorization-requirement and site-of-service concessions — rather than deal-by-deal board consultation, given the compressed timeline between the September 9 meeting and the September 30 expiration. The proposed corridor will be presented in executive session.

## 10. Quality and Patient Experience Notes

At the quality committee's request, three cross-cutting observations from the quarter:

- **Access is the dominant quality theme.** Primary care at 22 days, therapy intake past six weeks, echocardiograms at 31 days: the group's most significant quality exposure this quarter is not clinical performance within visits but time-to-visit. Patient experience scores confirm it — access-related items declined while care-experience items held steady.
- **The EHR conversion has not produced measurable clinical quality harm**, per the CQO's conversion surveillance dashboard: no attributed safety events, stable quality-measure documentation, and medication reconciliation rates recovered to baseline by June. Provider documentation-time burden remains elevated and is being addressed through personalization sessions in Q4.
- **The cardiology echo backlog carries the clearest safety-adjacent risk** in the current portfolio and is under weekly triage review. The quality committee will receive backlog and triage metrics monthly until the backlog returns below 14 days.

## 11. Fourth-Quarter Outlook and Requested Actions

### 11.1 Outlook

The fourth quarter's shape is largely set by three variables, in order of consequence:

1. **The payer contract.** A signed renewal at reasonable rates stabilizes 31 percent of revenue and lets the organization plan FY2027 normally. Anything else dominates every other item in this report.
2. **Revenue cycle recovery slope.** If the October and December milestones in Section 8 hold, trapped cash releases through Q4 and Q1, the working capital draw is repaid on schedule, and physician distributions normalize. If the slope flattens, the finance committee should expect a supplemental cash plan in November.
3. **Cardiology stabilization.** Locum coverage and echo capacity measures should contain the damage; the call redesign vote in September determines whether recruitment can succeed. Q4 will show whether referral leakage is contained or compounding.

Against those risks, the group enters Q4 with genuine strengths: demand is growing in every service line, orthopedics is executing the highest-value strategy in the portfolio, the Middletown scanner is back in service, and the behavioral health capacity-recovery program has a clear, quantified target.

### 11.3 Closing Assessment

Q3 FY2026 was a quarter in which the group's underlying franchise — growing volumes, a dominant local footprint, and a genuinely differentiated ASC strategy — performed well, while three self-inflicted or timing-driven wounds (the conversion's revenue cycle execution, the deferred cardiology call redesign, and the unsigned payer contract) absorbed most of management's attention and a material amount of cash. None of the three is structural. All three are resolvable within two to three quarters, and two of the three will be substantially decided within thirty days of the committees' September 9 meeting. The purpose of this report is to give both committees the information to decide them well.
