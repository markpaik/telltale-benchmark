# The Understudy

The stagehand who moves furniture between acts
knows the play better than anyone.
She has memorized not the words
but the weight of things—
which table legs stick, which chair
scrapes loud enough to wake
a sleeping metaphor.

She has never spoken a line
and never will. Her name
is not in the program. But watch her
in the dark between scenes,
how she sets the wine glass
exactly where the actress's hand
will find it without looking,
how she angles the lamp
so the shadow falls
like something the playwright intended.

There is a kind of love
that arranges the world
so someone else can be seen in it.

I think of my mother's hands
setting the table before guests arrived,
turning each fork so the tines
caught the light just so,
folding napkins into shapes
she learned from a book
she never told anyone she owned.

She was not in the photographs.
She was the reason
everyone was smiling in the photographs.

*

Somewhere a stagehand is dying
without ever taking a bow.
Somewhere a mother is folding
the last napkin of her life
and no one will know
it was the last one.

This is not a tragedy.
This is choreography
so exact it disappears—

the highest form of art
is the one that leaves no signature,
only the sense, in the audience,
that everything was always
exactly where it needed to be.

*

If I have a religion
it is this: that the unseen hand
is not absent but generous,
that somewhere behind the visible world
someone is moving the furniture
so the light falls correctly,
so the scene can happen,
so you can sit down
in a chair someone placed there
for you, and never once
wonder who.
