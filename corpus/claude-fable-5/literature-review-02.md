# Twelve-Hour Shifts and Worker Safety in Manufacturing: A Review of the Evidence, 1985–2025

**Southwest Advanced Manufacturing Institute — Research Report 2026-03**

Sofia Kuznetsova, PhD, Research Director
Bernard Achebe-Lomax, CIH, Industrial Hygiene Services

Prepared for the Board of Directors and the Operations Council
Chandler, Arizona — August 2026

---

## 1. Introduction

The question before the consortium is deceptively simple: are twelve-hour shifts more dangerous than eight-hour shifts in production environments, and if so, under what conditions and by how much? Three member plants are converting to twelve-hour rotating schedules. A fourth adopted a similar schedule and reversed it after its recordable injury rate climbed from 2.9 to 4.6 per 100 full-time workers within eighteen months. In April, a fatality occurred at a member foundry during the eleventh hour of a night shift. The board commissioned this review to establish what four decades of research can — and cannot — tell member plants about shift length, rotation, and safety.

The question resists a single answer because "twelve-hour shifts" is not one intervention. The literature covers fixed twelve-hour days, fixed twelve-hour nights, slow and fast rotations, forward and backward rotations, compressed workweeks with three or four consecutive shifts, and hybrid schedules with embedded overtime. Outcomes vary just as widely: laboratory vigilance measures, recordable injuries, lost-time injuries, near-misses, quality escapes, commuting crashes, absenteeism, and turnover. The evidence pulls in different directions depending on which schedule variant and which outcome a study measured, and a central task of this review is to explain those disagreements rather than average over them.

The scope of the review is production and process-industry work — manufacturing, chemical processing, mining, smelting, foundry, and utilities — from 1985 through 2025. Healthcare and transportation literatures are cited only where they supply mechanisms (sleep physiology, vigilance decrement) that generalize; their scheduling and task structures differ too much from plant-floor work to transfer outcome estimates directly. The review addresses six themes where the evidence conflicts most sharply: hour-by-hour fatigue and vigilance; injury outcomes in field settings; direction and speed of rotation; overtime stacking and cumulative exposure; environmental interactions, particularly heat; and quality escapes as an outcome distinct from injury. It then examines worker preference and retention, the design and measurement differences that drive contradictory findings, gaps in the evidence base, and implications for member plants choosing a schedule.

One organizational reality frames the review. Workers in surveyed plants overwhelmingly prefer twelve-hour schedules, primarily for the blocks of days off, and at least one member plant's workforce has signaled it would strike rather than return to eight-hour shifts. Any recommendation that ignores this preference evidence is not implementable. The review therefore treats preference and retention findings as evidence to be weighed, not as noise to be dismissed.

## 2. Search Strategy

We searched Scopus, PsycINFO, MEDLINE, and the federal occupational safety research database for records published from January 1985 through December 2025. Search strings combined shift-length terms (twelve-hour, 12-hour, extended shift, compressed workweek, shift length, shift duration) with rotation terms (rotating shift, shift rotation, forward rotation, backward rotation, fixed shift) and outcome terms (injury, accident, incident, vigilance, fatigue, alertness, error, quality, sleep, retention, turnover, absenteeism). Reference lists of ten identified meta-analyses were hand-searched, as were the proceedings of two international shiftwork symposia series.

The search returned 1,932 unique records after deduplication. Title and abstract screening excluded 1,622, principally for wrong population (healthcare, aviation, road transport as primary settings), wrong exposure (shift work generally, without shift-length comparison), or non-empirical content. We read 310 papers in full and included 94: 39 field studies with injury, incident, or error outcomes; 21 laboratory sleep and vigilance experiments; 18 surveys of worker preference, retention, and absenteeism; and ten meta-analyses. Both authors screened independently; disagreements (n = 41) were resolved by discussion. Extracted effect sizes, shift patterns, exposure definitions, and population characteristics are held in the consortium research database and cited here by author and year.

Two limitations of the search should be noted. Publication bias likely operates in both directions: plants that adopted twelve-hour schedules successfully have incentives to publish, while plants that reversed course rarely document why, so the field literature is dominated by volunteer sites. Second, the grey literature — internal safety analyses, insurer loss data, union studies — is largely inaccessible; the one insurer dataset we obtained (Duffield, Kwan, & Braithwaite, 2019) proved among the most informative sources in the review, suggesting the published literature undersamples the largest exposure databases.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Fatigue and Vigilance: The Laboratory Evidence

The laboratory literature is the most internally consistent body of evidence in this review, and it is uniformly unfavorable to hours ten through twelve. Halvorsen and Reed (1993) tracked psychomotor vigilance across simulated twelve-hour shifts and found reaction-time lapses stable through hour eight, rising modestly in hour nine, and increasing sharply thereafter; by hour twelve, lapse frequency was 2.3 times the hour-four baseline. Mirchandani, Beltrán, and Fossey (2004) replicated the pattern with a larger sample and added a simulated machine-tending task; error rates on the applied task tracked the vigilance decrement closely, with the steepest deterioration between hours ten and twelve. Brandt, Kimura, and Aslanov (2017) extended the paradigm to a simulated assembly line with lockout-tagout decision points and found that procedural shortcuts — skipped verification steps — increased fourfold in the final two hours relative to hours one through four.

The decrement interacts with circadian phase. Delacroix (1998) showed that on night shifts, the time-on-task effect compounds with the circadian trough: hour eleven of a night shift, falling near 04:00–05:00 for a typical start time, produced the worst vigilance scores of any condition tested. Okafor and Lindqvist (2011) formalized this as a multiplicative model in which time-on-task and circadian pressure each degrade performance and their product predicts the deep trough in the last hours of night twelves. Sandoval and Pryce (2015) documented EEG-verified micro-sleeps in 31 percent of participants during hours eleven and twelve of simulated night shifts, versus 4 percent during equivalent hours of day shifts. The April fatality at the member foundry, in the eleventh hour of a night shift, sits squarely in the window this literature identifies as highest-risk.

Cumulative effects across consecutive shifts are also consistent in the laboratory. Yee and Castellanos (2021) restricted sleep opportunity to six hours between simulated twelve-hour shifts — a realistic figure given commuting and domestic obligations — and found vigilance did not recover between shifts; performance on the fourth consecutive shift began at the level where the first shift ended. This finding matters because most field schedules cap consecutive twelves at three or four, and the laboratory suggests the fourth consecutive shift is qualitatively different from the first.

The limitation of this literature is external validity. Laboratory tasks are monotonous by design; plant-floor work varies in stimulation, autonomy, and physical engagement, all of which moderate vigilance decrement. Rosenkranz and Adeyemi (2008) showed that self-paced tasks with physical movement produced roughly half the decrement of externally paced monitoring tasks. The laboratory establishes the mechanism and the danger window; it cannot by itself establish the injury rate consequences on a real floor.

### 3.2 Injury Outcomes in the Field: A Divided Literature

The field literature does not speak with one voice, and the disagreement is genuine rather than an artifact of a few outlier studies.

On one side, several careful hour-by-hour analyses find escalating risk. Pellegrini and Moss (2001), analyzing incident logs from four chemical plants over seven years, estimated relative injury risk by shift hour and found risk in hour twelve approximately 1.9 times that in hour eight, with the increase concentrated in the final three hours. Duffield et al. (2019), using an insurer database covering 213 manufacturing sites, found an approximately exponential rise in claim risk from hour nine onward, reaching a relative risk of 2.2 by hour twelve, robust to adjustment for industry, task type, and time of day. Tran and Whitfield (2012), studying foundry workers specifically — the closest population match to the April fatality — found night twelves carried a lost-time injury rate 60 percent above day eights, with the excess concentrated in hot-work tasks. Osei and Lindstrom (2010) documented the reversal case most similar to the consortium's own: a fabrication plant whose recordable rate rose from 3.1 to 4.8 per 100 workers after converting to backward-rotating twelves with routine overtime, then fell to 3.3 within a year of reverting.

On the other side, a substantial group of field studies finds no difference or improvement. Northcutt (1994) compared matched chemical-processing units on eight- and twelve-hour schedules over four years and found no significant difference in recordable rates. Aldous (1989) reached the same conclusion in an earlier utility-sector comparison. More strikingly, Vasquez and Oyelaran (2007) found injuries *fell* 18 percent after a packaging plant moved from rotating eights to fixed twelves, and Grinberg, Tallis, and Munoz (2016) reported a 22 percent injury reduction after an automotive supplier moved to a compressed twelve-hour schedule — but their conversion bundled the shift change with a forward-rotation redesign, hard overtime caps, and a fatigue-reporting policy, making the shift length itself impossible to isolate. Hollenbeck and Diaz (2023), in the most recent large registry study, found fixed twelve-hour days indistinguishable from eight-hour days on injury rates, but twelve-hour nights and rotating twelves both elevated (relative risks of 1.4 and 1.5 respectively).

How can hour-by-hour risk rise while shift-level injury rates stay flat? Three reconciling mechanisms appear in the literature. First, exposure accounting: a twelve-hour crew works fewer shifts, fewer shift changeovers, and fewer commutes per unit of production. Changeover periods are independently hazardous — Ruiz-Camara (2008) attributed 12 to 15 percent of incidents in a continuous-process plant to handover-adjacent hours — and a twelve-hour schedule halves them. Second, task allocation: experienced supervisors informally shift high-hazard tasks toward the front of long shifts, a pattern Beaudry, Osterman, and Falk (2014) documented directly through task logs; the hour-twelve worker may be more impaired but less exposed. Third, selection and reporting: plants that adopt and retain twelve-hour schedules are disproportionately those with strong safety programs, and MacAllister (2003) found underreporting of minor injuries rose on compressed schedules because workers were reluctant to jeopardize their long weekends with an incident investigation.

The reconciliation matters for interpretation. The flat shift-level comparisons do not refute the hour-by-hour risk gradient; they suggest that well-run plants absorb the gradient through scheduling, task sequencing, and reduced changeover exposure. The gradient reasserts itself when those buffers fail — on nights, in heat, under stacked overtime, and in the reversal cases.

### 3.3 The Meta-Analyses: Why They Disagree

Ten meta-analyses fall within scope, and they split roughly by era and by inclusion logic. Trager and Willems (1997) and Bantjes, Corrigan, and Ilesanmi (1999), pooling the field literature available in the 1990s, both concluded that extended shifts carried elevated accident risk; Bantjes and colleagues fit the influential exponential curve showing risk roughly doubling by hour twelve. Chandrasekhar and Voss (2005) refined the analysis with relative risks by hour and by successive night shifts, and Emeka and Rothstein (2011) found the largest excess risks where shifts beyond twelve hours combined with night work.

The 2019 wave reached different conclusions. Larrabee, Osei-Bonsu, and Mikkelsen (2019), pooling 24 field studies with stricter design criteria — requiring within-plant comparisons and adjustment for task type — found no overall injury difference between fixed twelve-hour and eight-hour schedules, with substantial heterogeneity: rotating twelves and schedules permitting overtime beyond twelve hours drove nearly all the excess risk in subgroup analysis. Milius and Ferrante (2019), meta-analyzing error and quality outcomes rather than injuries, found consistent quality degradation on extended shifts even in studies reporting no injury difference.

The disagreement between the 1990s and 2019 analyses on the same schedule types is explained less by new data than by different pooling decisions. The earlier analyses pooled across rotation patterns, overtime regimes, and day/night status; the later ones stratified. When Larrabee and colleagues re-analyzed the Bantjes-era studies under their own criteria, roughly half were excluded for confounded comparisons (twelve-hour night crews compared against eight-hour day crews, for example). The most defensible reading is that the exponential hour-by-hour curve is real at the level of task performance, but its translation into injury rates depends on schedule architecture: fixed twelves with disciplined overtime caps can match eights, while rotating twelves, night-heavy twelves, and twelves with stacked overtime cannot.

### 3.4 Direction and Speed of Rotation

For the three converting member plants, all adopting rotating schedules, the rotation literature is directly relevant and moderately consistent. Whitcomb (1997) compared forward-rotating (days → nights) and backward-rotating (nights → days) twelve-hour patterns in two matched smelter crews and found the backward pattern produced shorter sleep between shift blocks and roughly 30 percent more incidents in the first night shift of each block. Ferreira and Nkemelu (2022) reached a compatible conclusion in a modern smelter study: forward rotation with at least 24 hours between the last day shift and first night shift produced sleep and alertness outcomes approaching those of fixed crews. Ondracek's (2015) meta-analysis of rotation direction pooled eleven studies and found forward rotation favored on sleep duration, subjective fatigue, and (in the five studies reporting it) incidents, though the injury effect did not reach significance in pooling.

The dissenting evidence concerns speed rather than direction. Hendricks and Paulsen (2010) found fast forward rotation (two days, two nights, four off) outperformed slow rotation (blocks of seven) on cumulative fatigue, since no worker accumulated more than two consecutive nights. Novak (2006), by contrast, found slow rotation superior for circadian adaptation in workers who fully committed to a nocturnal routine on night blocks — but only 20 to 30 percent of workers achieve such adaptation, a figure consistent across studies (Delacroix, 1998; Okafor & Lindqvist, 2011). The practical consensus that emerges: forward, fast rotation with a maximum of two to three consecutive nights and a genuine recovery gap at each transition is the least-bad rotating pattern; backward rotation and long night blocks are the configurations most often present in the studies reporting harm, including Osei and Lindstrom's (2010) reversal case and the consortium member's own reversed schedule, which rotated backward.

### 3.5 Overtime Stacking and Cumulative Exposure

If a single modifiable factor separates successful from unsuccessful twelve-hour implementations in this literature, it is overtime discipline. A twelve-hour base shift leaves no margin: hour thirteen and beyond enter territory where every meta-analysis, including the schedule-agnostic Larrabee et al. (2019), finds elevated risk. MacAllister (2003) tracked overtime patterns in three plants after twelve-hour conversion and found that "shift extension" overtime (holding workers past hour twelve to cover absences) was associated with an incident odds ratio of 2.8 relative to standard hours, far exceeding the risk of an additional scheduled shift on a rest day. Duffield et al. (2019) found the same asymmetry in insurer data: hours worked beyond twelve in a single shift predicted claims more strongly than total weekly hours.

Cumulative weekly exposure matters too, but the threshold appears higher than plants often assume. Kaplan and Meirovitch (2013) found no detectable injury excess up to 48 hours per week on twelve-hour schedules, with risk rising beyond 55 to 60 hours — a range easily reached when a 42-hour twelve-hour rotation absorbs two overtime shifts. Consecutive-shift counts show the sharpest field signal: Chandrasekhar and Voss (2005) estimated risk on the fourth consecutive twelve-hour shift at roughly 1.4 times the first, and on the fourth consecutive *night* twelve at nearly 1.8 times, converging with Yee and Castellanos's (2021) laboratory finding of non-recovery across a four-shift block.

The implication is that schedule policy on paper is not the exposure that matters. Grinberg et al. (2016), the strongest positive field result in the review, enforced a hard rule of no shift extensions beyond twelve hours and no more than three consecutive shifts; Osei and Lindstrom's (2010) failed implementation had a nominal twelve-hour schedule but median actual shift lengths of 13.1 hours during staffing shortfalls. Studies that measure scheduled hours and studies that measure worked hours are, in effect, studying different exposures, and this measurement difference accounts for a meaningful share of the field literature's disagreement.

### 3.6 Heat and Environmental Interaction

For a consortium headquartered in Chandler, Arizona, with a foundry fatality on record, the heat literature deserves specific weight despite its thinness. Kowalczyk and Serrano (2020) analyzed five years of incident data from three southwestern plants and found the shift-length injury gradient roughly doubled during months with wet-bulb globe temperatures above threshold: the hour-ten-to-twelve excess risk that was modest in winter became pronounced in summer. Tran and Whitfield (2012) found the foundry hot-work excess on night twelves noted above, and attributed part of it to cumulative dehydration and heat strain that recovery breaks did not reverse late in the shift. Laboratory support comes from Brandt et al. (2017), whose heat-condition arm showed vigilance decrement onset moving earlier — hour seven rather than hour nine — under thermal load.

The countervailing observation is that heat exposure is partly schedule-independent and partly schedule-*favorable*: twelve-hour schedules with 05:00 starts can front-load outdoor and hot-process tasks into cooler hours more effectively than three eight-hour shifts can, a scheduling advantage Kowalczyk and Serrano (2020) noted at their best-performing site. The interaction finding stands regardless: whatever a plant's baseline schedule risk, summer heat amplifies the late-shift portion of it, and fatigue countermeasures calibrated to temperate-climate studies will under-protect in the Southwest.

### 3.7 Naps and Fatigue Countermeasures

The nap literature is small but directionally consistent, with one important field-implementation caveat. Hafeez and Grantham's (2021) meta-analysis of workplace napping pooled nine studies (four in industrial settings) and found scheduled naps of 20 to 40 minutes during night shifts improved subsequent vigilance with effect sizes large enough to offset roughly two hours of the time-on-task decrement, provided sleep inertia was managed with a 15-to-20-minute recovery buffer before safety-critical tasks. Jamieson and Ohta (2005) ran the field trial most relevant here: a controlled nap policy on twelve-hour nights in a semiconductor plant reduced late-shift incidents by a third relative to the pre-policy baseline.

The caveat is implementation. Two survey studies (Prendergast, 2002; Liao & Bergstrom, 2016) found workers and supervisors alike resisted formal napping — workers from stigma, supervisors from coverage concerns — and informal, unmanaged napping produced the sleep-inertia incidents that formal policies are designed to prevent. Sandoval and Pryce (2015) documented that uncontrolled micro-sleeps occur on night twelves whether or not policy acknowledges them; the practical choice is between managed and unmanaged sleep, not between sleep and no sleep.

### 3.8 Quality Escapes: A Different Outcome, A Clearer Signal

Quality escapes — defects passing inspection, procedural deviations without injury — behave differently from injuries in this literature, and more consistently. Ibarra, Feldstein, and Nakagawa (2018) matched defect rates to shift hour across two electronics plants and found escape rates in hours ten through twelve running 35 to 50 percent above hours one through eight, in plants whose injury rates showed no shift-length difference. Milius and Ferrante's (2019) meta-analysis confirmed the pattern across settings: extended shifts degrade quality outcomes even where injury comparisons are null. Salazar-Wynne (1996) found the same dissociation two decades earlier in aerospace fabrication.

The dissociation is informative rather than paradoxical. Injuries are rare, lumpy, and buffered by the task-allocation and exposure mechanisms described in Section 3.2; quality escapes are frequent, continuously measured, and unbuffered — no supervisor reallocates inspection duties away from hour eleven the way they reallocate confined-space entries. Quality data therefore function as the sensitive leading indicator of the fatigue that injury statistics detect only after buffers fail. For member plants, the methodological implication is direct: a plant converting to twelves that monitors only its recordable rate is watching a lagging, insensitive gauge; hour-stamped quality and near-miss data will show fatigue effects one to two years before injury data can.

### 3.9 Preference, Retention, and the Labor Market

The survey literature is the most one-sided body of evidence in the review, and it points the opposite direction from the laboratory. Across eighteen surveys, support for twelve-hour schedules among workers currently on them ranges from 72 to 91 percent (Ashworth, 1992; Prendergast, 2002; Kingsley & Ojo, 2013; Liao & Bergstrom, 2016). The dominant stated reasons are consistent: consolidated days off, fewer commutes, and more usable recovery time. Kingsley and Ojo (2013) found preference strength scaled with commute distance — a finding with force in sprawling southwestern metros. Mutombo, Reyes, and Calloway (2020) documented the retention consequence directly: voluntary turnover fell 28 percent after a twelve-hour conversion at a plant competing for labor with twelve-hour employers, and Liao and Bergstrom (2016) found schedule reversal attempts triggered turnover spikes and, in two cases, organized resistance — consistent with the position of the consortium's own operations council chair that his crews would strike before returning to eights.

Two qualifications temper the consensus. First, preference is not uniform across the workforce. Cai and Ostrowski (2018) found women with primary childcare responsibility split roughly evenly on twelve-hour schedules, since childcare infrastructure is built around eight-to-ten-hour days; the aggregate preference figures reflect workforces that are 80-plus percent male. Farrow, Iqbal, and Demirci (2021), one of the few studies to sample workers over 55 deliberately, found preference for twelves declined with age while self-reported recovery difficulty rose — and found suggestive evidence of healthy-worker selection, in that older workers who disliked twelves had disproportionately already left twelve-hour plants. Second, preference and safety are distinct constructs that workers themselves conflate: Prendergast (2002) found workers on twelves rated their own late-shift alertness as adequate at the same time-points where their vigilance-test scores were degraded, a self-assessment gap the laboratory literature documents repeatedly (Halvorsen & Reed, 1993; Mirchandani et al., 2004). Worker preference is real evidence about retention, morale, and implementability; it is not evidence about fatigue.

Absenteeism findings are mixed and appear schedule-architecture-dependent: Devane, Klimova, and Ansah (2009) found lower absenteeism on twelves (each absence is costlier to the worker's block of days off), while MacAllister (2003) found absence *concentration* — absences clustering on the fourth consecutive shift — which converts an attendance statistic into a fatigue signal.

## 4. Why the Studies Disagree: Design and Measurement

The conflicts summarized above are largely traceable to five methodological fault lines, and reading the literature without them produces false confidence in whichever subset one samples.

**Exposure definition.** Studies measuring scheduled shift length and studies measuring actual worked hours reach different conclusions because shift extension is where much of the risk lives (MacAllister, 2003; Duffield et al., 2019). Several null comparisons rest on schedule-as-designed data from plants whose actual hours were longer.

**Comparison confounding.** Early studies routinely compared twelve-hour crews against eight-hour crews differing simultaneously in rotation, night exposure, task mix, and plant. The 1990s meta-analyses inherited these confounds; the 2019 re-analyses stratified them away and dissolved much of the apparent main effect of shift length into effects of rotation pattern and night work (Larrabee et al., 2019).

**Outcome sensitivity.** Injuries are rare and buffered by informal task reallocation; quality escapes and vigilance measures are frequent and unbuffered. Studies converge when the outcome is sensitive (quality, vigilance) and diverge when it is not (recordables), which is what one would expect if the fatigue effect is real but conditionally absorbed (Ibarra et al., 2018; Milius & Ferrante, 2019).

**Selection and survival.** Field studies observe plants that chose, and kept, their schedules. Plants that reversed twelve-hour schedules — the most informative failures — appear in this literature exactly twice (Osei & Lindstrom, 2010; and partially in Liao & Bergstrom, 2016). Similarly, workers who cannot tolerate twelves leave twelve-hour plants, biasing cross-sectional worker samples toward tolerators (Farrow et al., 2021).

**Co-intervention bundling.** The most positive field results (Grinberg et al., 2016; Vasquez & Oyelaran, 2007) implemented shift-length changes bundled with rotation redesign, overtime caps, and fatigue-management policy. These studies are best read as evidence about well-governed twelve-hour *systems*, not about twelve hours per se.

## 5. Gaps in the Evidence

Five gaps limit what this review can conclude, and two bear directly on the consortium's workforce.

**Older workers.** Workers over 55 barely appear in pooled samples, despite constituting a growing share of the consortium's production workforce. The physiology of recovery sleep worsens with age, and Farrow et al. (2021) provide the only targeted data — suggestive of reduced tolerance and healthy-worker attrition, but from a single cross-sectional survey. No study provides age-stratified injury risk on twelve-hour schedules.

**Women.** Women constitute under a fifth of pooled field samples and are essentially absent from the laboratory studies before 2010. Cai and Ostrowski (2018) show preference patterns differ; whether fatigue, recovery, or injury patterns differ is unstudied in industrial settings.

**Hot-climate field data.** One field study (Kowalczyk & Serrano, 2020) directly addresses the heat-by-shift-length interaction that describes the consortium's operating environment for five months of the year. The April fatality falls in an evidence gap, not an evidence base.

**Failed implementations.** The near-total absence of documented reversals means the literature cannot say what distinguishes plants that should not run twelves from plants that can. The consortium's own reversal case is, uncomfortably, among the better-documented failures available, and should be written up.

**Long-horizon outcomes.** Almost no field study follows a cohort beyond five years. Chronic health endpoints (cardiovascular, metabolic), cumulative injury careers, and late-career attrition on twelve-hour schedules are effectively unmeasured in manufacturing populations (Devane et al., 2009, is the partial exception, at six years).

## 6. Implications for Member Plants

The evidence does not support a categorical position for or against twelve-hour shifts, and the consortium should not adopt one. It supports conditional conclusions that are, on the whole, actionable.

**1. Shift length itself is a weaker risk factor than schedule architecture.** Fixed or forward-rotating twelve-hour schedules with hard overtime caps repeatedly match eight-hour schedules on injury outcomes (Northcutt, 1994; Vasquez & Oyelaran, 2007; Grinberg et al., 2016; Larrabee et al., 2019). Backward rotation, long night blocks, and routine shift extension are the configurations present in nearly every adverse field result, including the consortium's own reversal. The three converting plants should adopt forward, fast rotation (maximum two to three consecutive shifts, maximum two consecutive nights), a genuine recovery gap at the day-to-night transition, and a hard prohibition on extending shifts beyond twelve hours — covering absences with scheduled relief capacity rather than holdovers.

**2. The last three hours of night shifts are the identified danger window, and heat widens it.** The laboratory gradient, the hour-stamped field studies, the foundry data, and the April fatality all converge on hours ten through twelve of night work (Delacroix, 1998; Pellegrini & Moss, 2001; Tran & Whitfield, 2012; Duffield et al., 2019). Plants should formally restrict high-hazard tasks — confined-space entry, hot work, non-routine maintenance, lone work — after hour nine on nights, making explicit and auditable the informal task reallocation that well-run plants already practice. During heat-season months, the restriction should extend earlier into the shift (Kowalczyk & Serrano, 2020; Brandt et al., 2017).

**3. Managed napping on nights is supported and should be piloted, not debated.** The evidence favors 20-to-40-minute scheduled naps with sleep-inertia buffers (Jamieson & Ohta, 2005; Hafeez & Grantham, 2021), and shows that the alternative is unmanaged micro-sleep, not wakefulness (Sandoval & Pryce, 2015). Implementation resistance is the real obstacle and should be addressed through the operations council.

**4. Monitor quality escapes and hour-stamped near-misses, not recordable rates.** Injury statistics will not warn a plant that its schedule is failing until it has already failed. Hour-stamped quality and near-miss data detect fatigue effects earlier and more sensitively (Ibarra et al., 2018; Milius & Ferrante, 2019). The consortium should standardize hour-of-shift stamping on incident, near-miss, and defect records across member plants, which would also create the multi-site, hot-climate dataset the published literature lacks.

**5. Take the preference evidence seriously — as evidence about retention, not fatigue.** Worker preference for twelves is overwhelming, durable, and consequential for turnover in a competitive labor market (Mutombo et al., 2020; Liao & Bergstrom, 2016). Attempting to impose eight-hour schedules against it would carry its own safety costs through turnover, inexperience, and conflict. But self-rated alertness is demonstrably unreliable (Prendergast, 2002; Halvorsen & Reed, 1993), and schedule governance cannot be delegated to preference. The defensible position is the one the evidence describes: give workers the twelve-hour schedule they want, inside an architecture strict enough to make it as safe as the schedule they don't.

**6. Attend to the workers the literature ignores.** Older workers and women are underrepresented in the evidence and differently situated in fact. Member plants should offer voluntary accommodation pathways (eight-hour or hybrid roles) for workers over 55 reporting recovery difficulty, and should not read aggregate preference surveys as unanimity. The consortium's own workforce data can begin closing these gaps.

**7. Document the consortium's own experience.** The reversal at the fourth member plant, and the conversions now underway, are more relevant to member decisions than most of the published literature. A standing protocol — baseline data, hour-stamped outcomes, quarterly review, pre-registered reversal criteria — would convert three plant conversions into the controlled field evidence the question deserves.
