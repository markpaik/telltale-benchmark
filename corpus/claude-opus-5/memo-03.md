## Internal Memorandum Packet — Sycamore Street Integration
### April–May 2027

**TO:** All Riverbend Staff
**FROM:** Ayesha Nkemelu, Executive Director
**DATE:** April 20, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Board Action on Sycamore Street Family Center

I am writing to tell you directly what the board decided last Thursday evening, because you should hear it from me and not from a reporter, a partner agency, or a client who heard it somewhere else. On April 15 the Riverbend board of directors voted nine to four to absorb Sycamore Street Family Center of Manchester, effective July 1, 2027. Sycamore's programs, its approximately 620 active clients, its two facility leases, its outstanding obligations, and the majority of its staff will become part of Riverbend at the start of our new fiscal year.

I want to be plain about the circumstances, because a decision like this made quietly looks like ambition and made openly looks like what it actually is, which is a hard call under pressure. Sycamore came to us in late March. Their executive director had resigned in February. Their line of credit was exhausted. Their finance staff informed our CFO that without an infusion they would miss payroll on April 30 — two weeks from the date of the board vote. They serve families in Manchester and eastern Tolland County with case management, a food pantry, a small housing stabilization program, and a youth program that runs out of the Spruce Street site. Roughly 620 households are actively enrolled. Many of them are the same households we serve on the eviction prevention side. If Sycamore closed on April 30, those families would have lost their case managers with no notice, and the nearest comparable services would have been in Hartford, thirty minutes and two bus transfers away.

The board did not vote unanimously and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Four members voted no, and their concerns were serious and were argued at length. I will be addressing those concerns formally in a memo to the board later this month, and I will make the substance of that discussion available to staff, because you deserve to know that this was contested and why.

**What happens to Sycamore's employees**

Sycamore currently employs 40 people. Our intention, subject to the due diligence work that Hector Bonilla and Sunny Trinh are leading over the next eight weeks, is to extend offers of employment to the substantial majority of Sycamore staff effective July 1. I cannot yet tell you the exact number. What I can tell you is the process.

Sycamore's direct service staff — case managers, housing specialists, pantry coordinators, and youth program staff — are represented by a union. Riverbend's staff are not. This is a genuine and consequential difference between our two organizations and it will not be resolved by anyone pretending it does not exist. Sycamore's union has written to us regarding successorship obligations. We have retained labor counsel. My commitment, which I have made to the union in writing and am repeating here, is that we will meet our legal obligations and that we will not use this transaction as an occasion to strip represented employees of representation. What that means concretely — which bargaining unit, which contract terms carry forward, what happens at the first contract expiration — is a matter for negotiation and I will not preempt it here.

For Sycamore's administrative and management staff, the picture is different and harder. Riverbend already has a finance department, a development department, a human resources function, and an executive office. We do not need two of each. Some Sycamore administrative positions will not be continued. Every affected person will be told individually, by a person and not by email, no later than May 29, and every offer that is made will be made in writing with full compensation and benefit detail.

**What happens to Riverbend employees**

I will not soften this. Riverbend is eliminating 14 positions.

This is not because of Sycamore. It is because on March 6 we received notice that a federal reimbursement methodology change would reduce our refugee resettlement revenue by approximately $3.1 million beginning in FY28, with 90 days notice. That is a fourteen percent reduction in our total operating revenue against a $22 million budget. Hector will circulate the revised FY28 budget within the week and it will show you exactly where the reductions fall.

The 14 positions are being eliminated whether or not the Sycamore absorption happens. I want that understood clearly, because I know how these things get told and retold. It would be convenient for me if people believed the layoffs and the merger were the same event, because then the merger would look like the cause of a painful thing rather than a response to a different painful thing. They are not the same event. The federal change is the cause. Affected staff will be notified individually beginning May 4.

**The audit finding**

You will hear about this if you have not already. Our FY26 single audit, issued in February, included a material weakness in subrecipient monitoring. In plain language: we pass federal funds through to smaller partner organizations, and our documentation that we monitored how those partners spent the money was not adequate to the standard. This is not an allegation that funds were misspent. It is a finding that our controls were insufficient to demonstrate that they were not. That distinction matters legally and it does not matter much practically — the corrective action required is the same either way.

Sunny Trinh is issuing a memo to program managers with the new monitoring procedure. It will add work. I am aware that I am asking you to add compliance work in the same season that I am reducing headcount, and I do not have a way to make that not be true. The alternative is a repeat finding in FY27, which would move us toward high-risk auditee status and jeopardize the federal funding that pays roughly sixty percent of our salaries.

**What I am asking**

Between now and July 1 there will be a great deal that is unknown, and in the absence of information people will construct explanations. I would rather give you incomplete real information than let that happen. I will hold all-staff sessions on April 28 (Hartford, 10 a.m. and 3 p.m.) and April 30 (Manchester, 10 a.m.). Sycamore staff are invited to all three. I will answer what I can answer and I will say "I don't know yet" when that is the honest answer, which will be often.

I have been at Riverbend eleven years. This is the hardest thing I have been part of here. It is also, I believe, the right decision, and I would rather explain a hard right decision than a comfortable wrong one.

**TO:** Riverbend Board of Directors; Ayesha Nkemelu, Executive Director
**FROM:** Hector Bonilla, Chief Financial Officer
**DATE:** April 27, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Revised FY28 Operating Budget — Resettlement Revenue Loss, Position Eliminations, Assumed Sycamore Obligations

This memo transmits the revised FY28 operating budget reflecting three developments since the board reviewed the preliminary FY28 framework in January: the federal reimbursement methodology change announced March 6, the position eliminations required to close the resulting gap, and the obligations assumed under the April 15 Sycamore Street resolution. The full budget workbook is attached. This memo explains what changed and why.

**The resettlement revenue reduction**

The January framework assumed FY28 resettlement revenue of $8.4 million, essentially flat to FY27. On March 6 the Office of Refugee Resettlement issued a revision to the per-capita reimbursement methodology for the Reception and Placement and Matching Grant lines, moving from a case-based reimbursement to a modified formula weighted toward initial arrival volume rather than ongoing case duration. Because Riverbend's caseload is unusually weighted toward longer-duration cases — families with complex medical needs, single-parent households, and clients from arrival cohorts where secondary migration into Hartford has been high — the methodology change affects us disproportionately.

Applied to our projected FY28 caseload, the revised methodology yields $5.3 million against the $8.4 million we had budgeted. The reduction is $3.1 million. It is effective for the federal fiscal year beginning October 1, 2027, which falls three months into our FY28.

I want the board to understand two things about the notice period. First, 90 days is the notice we received, not the notice we needed. A $3.1 million revenue reduction in a $22 million organization requires roughly six to nine months to absorb through attrition, contract non-renewal, and program wind-down. We do not have that. Second, we cannot backfill this from reserves in any durable way. Unrestricted net assets stood at $2.6 million at June 30, 2026, representing about 43 days of operating expense. Board policy sets a 90-day floor, which we have been below since FY24. Drawing on reserves to cover a permanent revenue reduction converts a solvable problem into an insolvent one on a two-year delay.

**Closing the gap**

The revised budget closes the $3.1 million gap as follows.

Position eliminations account for $1.42 million. Fourteen positions are eliminated effective June 30, 2027. Nine are in the resettlement program directly: four case manager positions, two employment specialists, one housing coordinator, one intake supervisor, and the resettlement program's dedicated data analyst. Three are in shared administrative functions: one accounts payable specialist, one HR generalist, and one facilities coordinator. Two are in development: the grants writer position and the donor database administrator. The fully loaded cost of these positions, including benefits at our 28.4 percent load rate, is $1.42 million annually. Because the eliminations are effective at fiscal year end, FY28 realizes the full-year savings.

I recognize that eliminating two development positions during a campaign shortfall requires explanation. Charlene Whitcomb and I reviewed this jointly and it was her recommendation, not mine, that the grants writer position be eliminated rather than a program-facing position. Her reasoning was that the grants writer's output was concentrated in federal and state applications that are now either reduced or unavailable, and that the FY28 development strategy depends on major gift and foundation relationships that are held by her and by Ayesha personally and are not staffable at the level we can afford. I found the reasoning sound. Charlene's memo addresses the campaign position more fully.

Non-personnel reductions account for $610,000. These include: the Asylum Hill satellite office lease, which we will not renew at its September 2027 expiration ($148,000); reduction of the client direct assistance pool in resettlement from $520,000 to $310,000, tracking the reduced caseload ($210,000); elimination of the professional development and conference budget agency-wide ($94,000); consolidation of three separate case management software licenses onto a single platform ($71,000); and reductions in printing, translation vendor contracts, and vehicle fleet costs totaling $87,000.

Program scope reduction accounts for $780,000. The Matching Grant employment program will not be renewed for the FY28 cycle. This is the single largest programmatic change in the budget and it eliminates a service line we have operated for nine years. The program has been operating at a structural deficit of approximately $180,000 annually, cross-subsidized from the Reception and Placement line, and the reimbursement change makes that cross-subsidy impossible. The $780,000 figure is net: we lose $960,000 of revenue and $1.74 million of expense.

The remaining $290,000 is closed through a combination of vacancy hold savings, an assumed three percent reduction in health plan cost following the plan design change effective January 2027, and a $110,000 increase in the indirect cost recovery rate on our two state contracts, which we successfully renegotiated in February.

**Assumed Sycamore obligations**

The April 15 resolution directs the assumption of Sycamore Street's assets, liabilities, and contractual obligations effective July 1, 2027. I want the board to have a clear picture of what that means in dollars, because the discussion on April 15 was conducted with figures I was able to develop in eleven days and I have now had six weeks.

Deferred payables at Sycamore stand at $483,000 as of March 31, 2027. The composition matters more than the total. Approximately $211,000 is trade payables to vendors, of which $88,000 is more than 120 days aged. Approximately $147,000 is accrued but unremitted employee benefit obligations — specifically, health insurance premiums for January through March 2027 and unremitted 403(b) employee deferrals for February and March. The remaining $125,000 is accrued paid time off liability for staff who will transfer.

The unremitted 403(b) deferrals require particular attention. These are employee contributions withheld from paychecks and not forwarded to the plan custodian. This is a fiduciary matter under ERISA and it is not a payable in the ordinary sense; it is money that belongs to employees and was not delivered. The amount is approximately $34,000 across 31 participants. I have advised counsel that we should remit this immediately upon closing, with lost earnings calculated under the Department of Labor's Voluntary Fiduciary Correction Program, rather than treating it as an assumed payable to be worked down. The additional cost of correction, including lost earnings and the excise tax, is estimated at $6,000 to $9,000. I recommend the board authorize this separately and explicitly.

The two Manchester leases run through August 2030. The Spruce Street site is 8,400 square feet at $14.25 per square foot triple net, annual cost including operating expenses of approximately $164,000. The Elm Terrace site is 3,100 square feet at $16.00 per square foot gross, approximately $50,000 annually. Combined remaining obligation from July 1, 2027 through August 2030 is approximately $683,000. Neither lease contains an assignment or early termination provision favorable to the tenant. The Spruce Street landlord has indicated informal willingness to discuss a partial surrender of the second floor; I would not budget for it.

Sycamore's FY28 revenue, as we have been able to verify it, is approximately $2.9 million: $1.6 million in state DSS and DCF contracts, $640,000 in municipal and United Way funding, $410,000 in foundation grants, and $250,000 in individual giving and events. Of this, I regard $2.35 million as high-confidence and $550,000 as at-risk, principally the foundation grants, several of which were made to Sycamore as an organization and may not transfer without renewed application.

Sycamore's FY28 expense, restructured under Riverbend, projects at $2.71 million. This assumes 33 of the 40 positions continue, elimination of the executive director position, the finance director position, the development director position, and four administrative positions, offset by additions of 1.5 FTE at Riverbend for expanded finance and HR capacity. It assumes Riverbend's benefit structure applied to transferred staff, which is a net increase in cost per employee of approximately $1,900 annually, as our health plan is richer than Sycamore's.

On a full-year basis, the Sycamore operation as restructured contributes approximately $190,000 to overhead against an allocated indirect cost of approximately $340,000. The transaction is dilutive in FY28. It approaches break-even in FY29 only if the at-risk foundation revenue is retained and only if the Spruce Street lease is restructured.

**The audit finding and its cost**

The FY26 single audit material weakness in subrecipient monitoring carries costs that appear in this budget. We have engaged an outside compliance consultant at $48,000 to design and validate the corrective procedure. Our FY27 audit fee increases by $22,000 due to expanded testing. Sunny Trinh's department absorbs the ongoing monitoring workload without additional headcount, which I flag as a risk rather than a solution. I estimate the true annualized labor cost of the new procedure at 0.6 FTE, and we are asking existing staff to carry it.

**What I want the board to hold onto**

This budget is balanced. It is balanced in the way that a person who has lost their job is solvent because they cut their expenses — the arithmetic works and the underlying condition is not good. Days cash on hand at June 30, 2028 projects at 38, below where we started. We are assuming a $3.1 million revenue loss and simultaneously assuming $483,000 of someone else's liabilities and a $683,000 lease obligation. Any two of the three would be manageable. All three, together, means we have no capacity for a further adverse event in FY28.

I supported the Sycamore resolution and I would support it again. I want the record to show that I supported it with a clear view of what it costs.

**TO:** All Program Managers and Supervisors
**FROM:** Sunny Trinh, Director of Programs
**DATE:** May 4, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Sycamore Case Transfer Methodology and Revised Subrecipient Monitoring Procedure

This memo covers two things that are unrelated except that both land on your desks in the same eight weeks. Read both parts. The second part is the one that will get us in trouble if it is done poorly.

**Part One: Transfer of 620 Sycamore Cases**

As of the March 31 census, Sycamore Street Family Center has 620 active client households. "Active" is doing considerable work in that sentence, and the first thing we are doing is not accepting it.

Sycamore's case management system classifies a household as active if it has had any contact within 180 days. Riverbend's standard is 90 days with a documented service interaction. Applying our standard to Sycamore's census, we get 431 households. The 189-household difference is not a bookkeeping matter — it is 189 families who may believe they have an open case and may not, or who have moved, or who resolved their situation without closing out. We are going to find out which.

Beginning May 11, a team of six — four Riverbend case managers on temporary reassignment and two Sycamore staff who have volunteered — will conduct a full census verification. Every household on the 620 list gets a contact attempt: phone, then text, then a letter, then, for households with an open housing or benefits matter, a home visit. The verification runs through June 19. I expect it to be difficult. Sycamore's contact data is inconsistent, and a portion of these families have had three case managers in eight months.

Cases will be assigned in four tiers.

*Tier One — Immediate transfer with named continuity.* Approximately 145 households have an open, time-sensitive matter: an eviction proceeding with a court date, a pending benefits appeal, an active DCF involvement, or a housing voucher in lease-up. These families do not experience a transition. Their current Sycamore case manager, if that person is transferring to Riverbend, continues with them without interruption. Where the case manager is not transferring, the case moves to a named Riverbend case manager who makes contact before June 30 and who has read the file. I will be reviewing this tier personally, household by household, in the week of June 8.

*Tier Two — Scheduled transfer with warm handoff.* Approximately 210 households have an open matter that is not time-sensitive: ongoing case management, food pantry enrollment, youth program participation. These transfer on a schedule between June 15 and July 31. Each receives a three-way contact — outgoing worker, incoming worker, client — either in person or by phone. Not an email. Not a letter announcing a new name.

*Tier Three — Transfer to intake for re-assessment.* Approximately 76 households where the file indicates an open case but the service need is unclear or appears to have changed. These go to our Manchester intake function, which we are standing up at the Elm Terrace site, for a fresh assessment. They are not being closed. They are being asked what they need now.

*Tier Four — Administrative closure.* The remainder — approximately 189, matching the census discrepancy, though the final number will differ — are households we cannot reach, who have relocated out of service area, or who confirm they no longer need services. Every Tier Four closure requires a documented supervisor review. I am not accepting closures that are really failures to reach.

**Caseload implications for existing Riverbend staff**

Our current eviction prevention caseload standard is 1:35. Housing stabilization is 1:28. If we absorb 431 verified Sycamore households across a transferring Sycamore staff of approximately 19 direct service positions, the arithmetic is 1:23, which sounds comfortable and is not, because Sycamore's cases are heavier than the ratio suggests and because 19 assumes everyone we offer accepts.

Two Sycamore program directors have already resigned — the housing program director on April 24 and the youth program director on May 1. I do not read either resignation as a signal about Riverbend. Both had been through eight months of an organization in freefall and both had offers elsewhere. But it means we lose two people who knew where the bodies were buried in those programs, and it means their direct reports are being asked to transfer into an unfamiliar organization without their supervisor. I am asking Riverbend program managers to be conscious of that. The Sycamore staff coming to us have watched their leadership leave.

I am reassigning Priya Venkataraman to serve as interim manager of the Manchester housing program effective June 1 and posting the youth program manager position internally with a June 15 close.

**Part Two: Revised Subrecipient Monitoring Procedure**

The FY26 single audit identified a material weakness in our monitoring of subrecipients. I want to describe what actually happened, because "material weakness in subrecipient monitoring" is a phrase that means nothing until you translate it.

We passed through approximately $2.4 million in federal funds to eleven subrecipient organizations in FY26 — smaller agencies that deliver services under our federal awards. Uniform Guidance requires that we, as pass-through entity, evaluate each subrecipient's risk of noncompliance, monitor their activities to ensure the award is used for authorized purposes, verify that they are audited when required, and issue a management decision on any findings in their audits.

What we did was collect their annual audits, file them, and pay their invoices. What we did not do, in a documented way, was assess risk, conduct any monitoring beyond invoice review, verify allowability of costs at the subrecipient level, or follow up on two subrecipient audit findings that we received and filed without response. The auditors selected four subrecipients for testing and found no documented monitoring for any of them. That is a material weakness. It is a fair finding and I am not going to argue with it.

Effective immediately, the following procedure applies to every subrecipient agreement. This is not optional and it is not a guideline.

*Risk assessment at award.* Before any subaward is issued or renewed, the responsible program manager completes a written risk assessment using the standard form, which will be in the shared drive by May 10. The assessment covers: prior experience with the same or similar awards, results of prior audits, whether the subrecipient has new personnel or new systems, and the extent of federal monitoring the subrecipient receives from other sources. The assessment produces a rating of low, moderate, or high risk. It is signed by the program manager and by me.

*Monitoring calibrated to risk.* Low-risk subrecipients receive quarterly desk review of financial reports and an annual documented conversation. Moderate-risk receive quarterly desk review plus one on-site or virtual program review annually, with a written report. High-risk receive monthly financial review, two on-site reviews annually, and testing of a sample of transactions against source documentation.

*Invoice review with substance.* This is the change that will affect your daily work most. We are no longer approving subrecipient invoices on the basis that the total is within budget. Every invoice requires: verification that costs are within the approved budget categories, review of supporting detail for at least one cost category on a rotating basis, confirmation that the period of performance is correct, and a signed approval that specifically attests to allowability. The approval language is on the new form. Do not sign it if you have not done the review. I would genuinely rather have a late invoice than a false attestation.

*Single audit tracking.* Our finance office maintains the schedule of which subrecipients meet the $750,000 threshold requiring a single audit. Program managers are responsible for obtaining the audit within nine months of the subrecipient's fiscal year end and for flagging any finding to me within five business days of receipt. I will issue a written management decision within six months of receipt, as required. We will not file a finding again.

*Documentation standard.* If it is not written down, it did not happen. This is the entire lesson of the FY26 finding. Several of you told the auditors, correctly, that you had regular contact with subrecipients and had substantive conversations about their performance. The auditors did not doubt you. They could not test it. A note in the file after a call — date, participants, topics, any issues, any follow-up — converts a real relationship into evidence of monitoring.

*Sycamore's subrecipients.* Sycamore has three subaward relationships that transfer to us, totaling approximately $310,000. All three are being rated high-risk on transfer regardless of what the assessment produces, for the simple reason that we know nothing about them and Sycamore's own documentation is thin. Marguerite Osei will conduct initial reviews of all three during July.

**What I know I am asking**

I am asking you to absorb a case transfer, cover for two departed program directors, take on new compliance work, and do it in the same season that fourteen of your colleagues are being told their positions are eliminated. I do not have a version of this that is easier. What I can tell you is that the monitoring work is the part that protects everything else. Sixty percent of our salaries are paid by federal funds. A repeat finding in FY27 moves us toward high-risk auditee designation, which means more testing, more cost, and a materially worse position when awards are recompeted. The compliance work is not separate from the mission work. It is what allows the mission work to continue existing.

Office hours on the new procedure: Tuesdays 3:00–4:30 in the Hartford conference room and by video, starting May 11. Bring the invoice you are stuck on.

**TO:** Development Committee of the Board
**FROM:** Charlene Whitcomb, Director of Development
**DATE:** May 12, 2027
**SUBJECT:** FY27 Campaign Position and Proposed Bridge Request to Three Family Foundations

**Where we are**

With four months remaining in FY27, we have raised $3.3 million against a $4.2 million annual goal. We are $900,000 behind. I want to give the committee an honest account of how we got here and what I propose to do, and I want to do it without the softening language that development reports usually carry.

The shortfall is not evenly distributed. Individual giving is at $1.42 million against a $1.5 million goal — behind, but within normal variance, and our spring appeal has not yet closed. Events are at $310,000 against $340,000, essentially on plan. Corporate giving is at $290,000 against $400,000, which is a real miss driven principally by the loss of two Hartford-based corporate donors following acquisitions. The foundation line is where the problem lives: $1.28 million against $1.96 million, a gap of $680,000, and that gap accounts for three-quarters of our total shortfall.

Three things caused it. First, the Hallowell Foundation, which had given $250,000 annually for six years, notified us in November that it is sunsetting its Connecticut human services portfolio to concentrate on early childhood. This was not about us and was not preventable. Second, we submitted a $300,000 request to a national funder in October for resettlement capacity that was declined in February; the decline letter cited portfolio saturation in refugee services, and in retrospect the request was optimistic. Third — and this is the one I own — we did not build enough new foundation pipeline in FY25 and FY26 because I was carrying an unfilled major gifts position for eleven months and I chose to protect existing relationships rather than prospect. That was a defensible triage decision at the time and it produced exactly the outcome you would predict two years later.

**The complication**

I need the committee to understand something that makes this conversation harder than a normal shortfall conversation. We are asking foundations for money in the same season that we announced a merger, eliminated fourteen positions, and are carrying a material weakness in our single audit.

Program officers read audits. Several of our funders require the single audit as a condition of reporting, and they will have it. A material weakness in subrecipient monitoring is not a finding of misuse, but it is a finding that our controls were inadequate, and a program officer who is deciding between us and another applicant does not have to weight it heavily for it to matter. I have already had one conversation, with a program officer at a foundation I will name in committee, in which the audit came up before I raised it.

My approach is to raise it first, in every conversation, without being asked. I would rather be the organization that says "we had a finding, here is the corrective action, here is who is accountable, here is the timeline" than the organization that waited to be asked. Sunny Trinh's monitoring procedure, issued May 4, is genuinely a strong document and I intend to attach it. An organization that produces a detailed corrective procedure within ninety days of a finding is a different proposition than one that produces a paragraph of assurance.

The merger is a harder narrative problem, but it is also an opportunity, and I want to be careful about the difference. The opportunity is real: we prevented the disorderly closure of an agency serving 620 households, and we did it in three weeks. That is a story about institutional capacity and community responsibility, and it is true. The risk is that it reads as an organization in financial distress acquiring another organization in worse distress, which is also, in a narrow sense, true. The distinction I am drawing in every conversation is that our revenue loss is exogenous — a federal methodology change we did not cause and could not have prevented — and our response to it was to make hard structural decisions quickly rather than to defer them. Hector's budget supports that narrative because it is what the budget actually shows.

**The bridge request**

I am proposing a coordinated bridge request of $750,000 to three family foundations, structured as a two-year commitment with the majority in FY28. I want to describe each and be specific about probability, because a proposal that treats all three as equally likely is not useful to you.

*The Delaney Family Foundation.* Request: $350,000 over two years ($200,000 FY28, $150,000 FY29), unrestricted operating support. Delaney has given us $75,000 to $100,000 annually for nine years, always restricted to eviction prevention. Their trustee committee includes Robert Delaney, who has known Ayesha since her time at Hartford Legal Aid, and who has said to her twice in the last three years that he wished the foundation could do more general operating. This is the request where the relationship is strongest and the ask is furthest above precedent. I put probability at 55 percent for a commitment at or above $250,000, and near-certain at their historical level. Board contact: Miriam Rothstein knows Ellen Delaney through the Hartford Foundation's nominating committee. I would like to discuss whether that contact is best used now or held.

*The Wentworth Trust.* Request: $250,000 over two years, restricted to the Manchester operation — specifically the transition costs of the Sycamore integration and the first year of the Manchester housing program. Wentworth's giving is geographically concentrated in Tolland County, which is precisely where Sycamore sits, and they have never funded Riverbend because we have never had a meaningful Tolland presence. This is the request where the case is strongest and the relationship is weakest. Their program officer, Anne Kirkpatrick, has taken a call and was genuinely interested; she also told me plainly that the trustees are conservative about new grantees and that a first grant above $75,000 would be unusual. Probability at $250,000: 25 percent. Probability of a first grant in the $75,000 to $100,000 range: 70 percent. I would take the smaller grant and treat it as the beginning of a relationship worth ten times that over a decade.

*The Osgood-Lin Family Fund.* Request: $150,000 single-year, restricted to the census verification and case transfer work. Osgood-Lin is a newer donor-advised structure with two principals who have given us $40,000 and $60,000 in the last two years, both times quickly and after a single conversation. They have explicitly said they are interested in "the unglamorous operational things." The case transfer of 620 households — six staff, ten weeks, home visits for families in eviction proceedings — is precisely an unglamorous operational thing, and it is the kind of specific, bounded, high-consequence work that this donor responds to. Probability at $150,000: 60 percent. This is the request I am most confident in and the one that requires the least time.

Expected value across the three, weighted, is approximately $430,000. That is not $750,000 and I am not going to present it as though the ask and the outcome are the same number.

**What the bridge does and does not do**

It does not close the $900,000 FY27 gap. Two of the three requests are FY28 money and would not be recognized in the current year. The FY27 gap will close, to the extent it closes, through the spring appeal, a year-end push on major gifts, and — I will be candid — probably not entirely. My working projection is that we finish FY27 at $3.75 million to $3.9 million against $4.2 million, a shortfall of $300,000 to $450,000. Hector and I have discussed this and the revised FY28 budget does not assume we make it up.

What the bridge does is give the FY28 budget a margin it currently does not have. Hector's budget projects 38 days cash on hand at June 30, 2028. A $430,000 bridge is roughly seven additional days. That is not comfort. It is the difference between a bad quarter being a bad quarter and a bad quarter being an emergency.

**What I need from the committee**

Three things. First, a decision on the Delaney board contact by May 22, because the Delaney trustees meet in September and their program officer wants a draft proposal in July. Second, two committee members willing to join site visits in Manchester — Wentworth will want to see Spruce Street and Elm Terrace, and a board member in the room changes that visit. Third, and this is the one I care about most: I need the committee to be comfortable with me talking about the audit finding and the merger plainly, in writing, in proposals. There is an instinct in fundraising to minimize. I think minimizing is the more dangerous course here, because everything I would minimize is discoverable, and being caught minimizing costs more than the thing being minimized.

I also want to note, for the record and without complaint, that the FY28 budget eliminates the grants writer and the donor database administrator from my department. I recommended the grants writer elimination and I stand by it. The database administrator position I did not recommend and I understand why it was chosen. Both eliminations mean that the pipeline-building work I described as the root cause of the foundation shortfall becomes harder, not easier, in FY28. I am not asking the committee to reverse anything. I am asking that when we look at FY29 results, we remember what we decided in FY28.

**TO:** Riverbend Board of Directors
**FROM:** Ayesha Nkemelu, Executive Director
**DATE:** May 26, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Response to Dissenting Views on the Sycamore Resolution and to the Union's Successorship Letter

At the April 15 meeting I committed to responding in writing to the four members who voted against the Sycamore resolution. I am doing that here, along with responding to the letter received April 29 from Local 1199 New England regarding successorship. I have not attempted to be diplomatic in this memo. Diplomacy in response to a serious dissent is a form of condescension.

**To the four dissenting concerns**

*The concern that we cannot afford it.* This was raised most directly by two members and it is the strongest objection. The argument is that an organization absorbing a $3.1 million revenue loss with 43 days of cash on hand has no business assuming $483,000 of payables and a $683,000 lease obligation.

I accept the premise and reach a different conclusion. Hector's April 27 memo shows the transaction is dilutive in FY28 by roughly $150,000 on an overhead-allocated basis and approaches break-even in FY29 under two conditions. That is a real cost and I do not want it minimized. But the alternative was not zero cost. If Sycamore had closed on April 30, a significant portion of those 620 households would have arrived at Riverbend's door within ninety days, because we are the only other provider of several of those services in the region, and we would have absorbed them with no revenue, no staff, and no facilities. The state DSS contract Sycamore held would have been re-procured, and we would have been the likely bidder, at which point we would be acquiring the obligation without the staff who know the clients. The counterfactual is not that we save $150,000. The counterfactual is that we spend an unknown amount, later, in worse conditions, with less control.

I could be wrong about that. The dissenters may be right that I am rationalizing an ambitious decision with a defensive story. What I can offer is a commitment to a measurable test: if by December 31, 2027 the Manchester operation is not tracking to Hector's revenue projections within ten percent, I will bring the board a wind-down plan for the Elm Terrace site and a proposal to consolidate to Spruce Street. I am not asking the board to trust the projection. I am proposing a date on which the projection gets checked.

*The concern about process.* One member said, correctly, that the board voted on a $2.9 million transaction with eleven days of financial due diligence and a term sheet drafted in a weekend. That is what happened. It is not how we should make decisions and I would not defend it as a model.

What I will say is that the timeline was not ours. Sycamore came to us on March 24 with a payroll date of April 30. We could have said no on the grounds that we could not diligence it properly, which would have been a defensible governance position and would have resulted in the agency closing. We could have asked for sixty days, which Sycamore did not have. The board's actual choice was between an inadequately diligenced yes and a well-governed no. I recommended the first. Reasonable people voted the second way.

I have asked Miriam to put a standing item on the governance committee agenda for the fall: what our process should be for emergency transactions, so that the next time this happens — and in this sector it will happen again — we are not inventing the procedure while the clock runs.

*The concern about the audit finding.* One member argued we should not take on new compliance obligations while carrying a material weakness. This is the concern I have thought about most, because I think it is right in a way the others are not.

Sycamore brings three subaward relationships we know nothing about into a monitoring function that just failed an audit. Sunny is rating all three high-risk on arrival and Marguerite Osei will review them in July. But the honest answer is that we are adding compliance surface area at the moment our compliance capacity is weakest, and we are doing it while eliminating an accounts payable position. If we take a repeat finding in FY27, the dissenting members will have been right and I will say so.

The mitigation is the $48,000 compliance consultant, Sunny's procedure, and my personal review of the FY27 monitoring file in October, before the auditors arrive rather than after. I will report to the board in November on what I find.

*The concern about mission drift.* One member asked whether Riverbend is becoming a holding company for distressed agencies rather than a service provider. I take this seriously and I want to answer it directly: no, and I do not intend for this to be the first of several.

Sycamore was a specific case. Overlapping client population, adjacent geography, service lines we already operate, and a closure that would have landed on us regardless. I would not have recommended absorbing an agency that met fewer of those conditions. If the board wants a policy statement on future acquisitions, I would support one, and I would support it being restrictive.

**On the union's letter**

Local 1199 wrote on April 29 asserting that Riverbend is a successor employer under *NLRB v. Burns* and *Fall River Dyeing*, that we are obligated to recognize and bargain with the union as representative of the transferred bargaining unit, and that we may not unilaterally set initial terms if we have communicated an intent to retain the predecessor's workforce. They have requested recognition, a bargaining schedule, and a commitment that we will not condition employment offers on any waiver of representation.

Our labor counsel's assessment is that the successorship analysis likely favors the union on the substantial continuity factors — same work, same clients, same facilities, substantially the same supervisors — and that the majority-of-workforce test will likely be met within the Manchester unit if we extend offers to 19 of Sycamore's 24 represented positions as planned. Counsel's view is that we should anticipate a bargaining obligation and that litigating it would be expensive, slow, and probably unsuccessful.

I want to be clear that my position is not driven solely by counsel's risk assessment. I told the union in our May 6 meeting, and I am telling the board now, that I do not intend to use this transaction to eliminate representation for people who have it. That is a decision about what kind of employer we want to be, and I want the board to actually make it rather than have it arrive as a legal inevitability.

Here is what I propose. First, recognition of Local 1199 as representative of the Manchester bargaining unit, contingent on verification that a majority of positions in the unit are filled by former Sycamore represented employees at the July 1 close. Second, an announcement that we intend to retain the substantial majority of represented staff, with the understanding that this forecloses our ability to set initial terms unilaterally. I recommend this anyway, because the alternative — staying silent to preserve a legal option — would mean leaving 24 people in uncertainty for eight weeks in order to gain a bargaining advantage, and I am not willing to do that. Third, that we begin bargaining in August, with the Sycamore contract terms continuing in effect as a practical matter until a successor agreement is reached, notwithstanding that we are not legally bound by them.

I want to flag one issue honestly. Riverbend's Hartford staff are not represented and perform substantially similar work at somewhat different compensation. Our health plan is richer than Sycamore's; our paid time off accrual is less generous at longer tenure. There will be represented Manchester employees and unrepresented Hartford employees doing similar jobs under different terms, and Hartford staff will notice. Some may conclude they should organize. That is their right and I will not interfere with it, and I will say so publicly if asked, because the alternative is to be an employer who accepts a union only where the law compels it.

This is the part of the transaction the board has discussed least and it may be the part with the longest consequences.

**Closing**

The four members who voted no did the board's work. A nine-to-four vote after three hours of argument is a healthier outcome than a thirteen-to-zero vote after forty minutes, and I would rather run an organization where the hard question gets asked in the room.

We will know by roughly March 2028 whether this was right. The measures are: whether the 431 verified households are receiving services from a named worker at twelve months; whether the FY27 single audit is clean on subrecipient monitoring; whether Manchester revenue tracks within ten percent of projection; and whether we end FY28 above 35 days cash. I will report against all four.

If we fail on two or more, the dissent was correct, and I will say that in those words.
