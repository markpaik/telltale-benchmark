# Blue Ridge Hardware and Supply: What It Costs to Skip the Foundation

## A Case Study in Retail Inventory Transformation and Omnichannel Fulfillment

---

## I. The Company and the Situation

Blue Ridge Hardware and Supply is a regional hardware and building-supply chain headquartered in Knoxville, Tennessee, operating 38 stores across eastern Tennessee and southwest Virginia. The company employs approximately 1,900 people and generated $310 million in revenue in fiscal 2022. Its stores range from 18,000 square feet in small county-seat towns to 52,000 square feet in the Knoxville and Tri-Cities markets. Roughly 60 percent of revenue comes from retail customers — homeowners, weekend renovators, hobbyist woodworkers — and 40 percent from a professional trade base of contractors, plumbers, electricians, and small builders who open accounts and buy on terms.

The company has been family-adjacent for most of its history. Founded in 1954 as a single lumber and feed store, it grew through acquisition of independent hardware operators across the 1980s and 1990s, a strategy that left it with a store base of considerable physical variety and an operating culture that prized local autonomy. A store manager in Greeneville ran his store differently from a store manager in Bristol, and for a long time that was understood as a feature rather than a defect. The chain competed on depth of assortment in trade categories, on staff who knew what a customer was actually trying to accomplish, and on the willingness to special-order almost anything.

By late 2022 that model was under visible strain. Comparable store sales had declined for six consecutive quarters, averaging minus 4.2 percent. The decline was not dramatic in any single quarter — it ranged from minus 2.1 percent to minus 6.4 percent — but its persistence was what alarmed the board. The company had absorbed the arrival of two big-box competitors in its larger markets over the prior decade and had held its ground reasonably well, largely on the strength of the trade business. What had changed was not the big boxes. It was the emergence of same-day and next-day fulfillment expectations, driven partly by the national chains' investment in curbside pickup during the pandemic years and partly by the broader normalization of online ordering across every category the company sold.

Two operating metrics told the more specific story. Inventory record accuracy — the percentage of SKUs where the system quantity matched a physical count within a tolerance of one unit — stood at 71 percent. This figure came from a sampling audit conducted in the third quarter of 2022 across nine stores and 4,000 SKUs. Management had suspected the number was poor. It had not expected it to be that poor. The second metric was equally damaging: the company's top 200 items by unit velocity, the fast-moving consumables that pulled traffic and anchored trade relationships, were out of stock 18 percent of the time. Nearly one visit in five for a core item resulted in a customer being told the product was not available.

The two numbers were related. When the system's record of what was on hand was wrong 29 percent of the time, replenishment triggered late, or not at all, or triggered for items that were already sitting in the back room. Store managers had adapted in the way experienced operators always adapt: they built private systems. Several kept notebooks. One in Morristown maintained a spreadsheet on a personal laptop covering roughly 600 SKUs he considered critical. Buyers at headquarters had learned to discount system data and call stores directly before making decisions. The organization ran on relationships and workarounds, and it had run that way long enough that most people had stopped noticing.

Chief Executive Loretta Ashby-Ng had joined in 2019 from a larger specialty retailer in the Midwest. Chief Information Officer Ellis Trumbo had been with Blue Ridge for eleven years, having risen from a systems analyst role. Between them they held a shared diagnosis: the company could not defend its trade business against national competitors offering reliable online ordering and fast pickup, and it could not fix the customer-facing problem without first fixing the inventory record. What they underestimated — and this is the center of the case — was how much organizational work sat between those two statements.

## II. The Bet

The January 2023 board presentation ran to 40 slides. Its logic was straightforward and, in its essentials, correct.

The argument began with the trade customer. A contractor working a residential remodel makes three to five supply runs per week, typically under time pressure, typically for a mixed basket of consumables and one or two job-specific items. That customer's decision about where to buy is driven less by price than by confidence — whether the trip will be productive. An 18 percent stockout rate on core items is a direct attack on that confidence, and the company's account data showed the effect: among trade accounts active in 2019, 22 percent had reduced annual spend by more than 30 percent by 2022, with the steepest declines concentrated in accounts located within six miles of a big-box competitor.

The second argument concerned fulfillment. Ashby-Ng's team had commissioned a survey of 400 trade customers in October 2022. Seventy-three percent said they would use an online ordering and store-pickup service if available. Fifty-eight percent said they had used such a service at a competitor in the prior 90 days. The company had no such capability. Its website functioned as a store locator and a weekly-ad viewer; it did not display inventory and could not accept orders.

The third argument was defensive. The existing inventory system was a heavily customized package installed in 2008, running on hardware the CIO described in the presentation as "well maintained and increasingly lonely." The vendor had sunset support for the version in use. Two of the three people who understood the customizations had retired.

The proposal was a $6.8 million program with two components. First, replacement of the core inventory management system — perpetual inventory, automated replenishment, purchase order management, receiving, and a store-level mobile application for counting and adjustments. Second, launch of buy-online-pickup-in-store, or BOPIS, integrated to the new inventory record and to a new customer-facing web front end.

The vendor selected was Cardinal Retail Systems, a mid-market provider with roughly 200 retail clients, of which about 30 were in hardware and building supply. Cardinal came through a competitive process against two larger vendors and one smaller one. It won on three grounds: category-specific functionality, particularly around variable units of measure — lumber sold by the linear foot, fasteners by the pound and the each, rope by the foot from a spool; a demonstrated implementation at a 24-store hardware chain in Ohio; and price, at roughly 30 percent below the nearest competitor.

The centerpiece commitment, and the one that would later attract the most scrutiny, was a nine-minute pickup promise. Cardinal's reference client in Ohio was averaging eleven minutes from customer arrival to vehicle departure. Cardinal's implementation lead argued Blue Ridge could beat that figure because its stores were smaller on average and its planned pickup locations were nearer the front. Ashby-Ng liked the number. It was specific, it was memorable, and it was better than anything the national competitors were advertising in the region. "Nine minutes" went into the board deck, into the internal launch materials, and eventually onto signage.

The budget broke down as follows: $2.9 million in software licensing and subscription over the initial term; $2.1 million in implementation services from Cardinal and a systems integrator; $900,000 in hardware, principally mobile scanning devices, in-store pickup lockers at eight higher-volume locations, and network upgrades; $500,000 in internal program staffing; and $400,000 in contingency. The board approved the program in January 2023 with a target of piloting at twelve stores in the fourth quarter and completing the chain by mid-2024.

Two things about the approval are worth noting because they shaped everything after.

First, the business case was built on system capability rather than on operating discipline. It answered the question "what will the software let us do?" thoroughly and the question "what will our stores have to do differently, and are they currently capable of doing it?" hardly at all. The word "cycle counting" appeared twice in the 40 slides, both times in a list of features.

Second, the timeline placed the pilot in November. This was a deliberate choice and it was defended at the time: fourth quarter is a high-traffic period, the pilot would be stress-tested under real conditions, and a successful November would give the full rollout momentum going into spring, the hardware category's true peak. The counter-argument — that a pilot's purpose is to learn, and that the fourth quarter is the worst possible time for a store team to absorb a new way of working — was raised by Store Operations Director Bo Nakashima in a planning meeting in March 2023. It was noted and overruled. The rollout schedule was already tight.

## III. Build and Launch

The implementation ran from February through October 2023 and was, by the standards of mid-market retail systems work, reasonably well executed on its own terms.

The data conversion was the largest single workstream. Blue Ridge carried approximately 47,000 active SKUs across the chain, with significant variation by store and a long tail of items stocked at one or two locations only. The item master had accumulated fifteen years of duplication, inconsistent unit-of-measure coding, and orphaned records. A cleansing effort ran from March through July and reduced the active file to 41,200 SKUs, standardized units of measure across 94 percent of the file, and rebuilt the category hierarchy. This work was genuinely good and would prove to be one of the program's durable assets.

Integration to the point-of-sale system, a separate package the company had installed in 2016, required more custom development than scoped, adding approximately $340,000 and six weeks. The customer-facing web front end was delivered on schedule. Store hardware — 340 mobile scanning devices, lockers at eight stores, wireless network upgrades at all twelve pilot locations — was installed in September and October.

Training consisted of a two-day session for each pilot store's management team, held regionally in September, and a four-hour session for store associates delivered in-store during the last two weeks of October. The associate training covered the mobile application, the pick-and-stage workflow, and the pickup handoff. It did not cover cycle counting, because cycle counting was not, at that point, part of anyone's plan for the pilot period. The assumption embedded in the program — never stated explicitly, which is precisely the problem — was that the new system would produce accurate inventory records as a consequence of its own design. Better receiving, better transaction capture, automated replenishment logic: these would drive accuracy up.

This assumption was wrong, and it was wrong in a way that is common enough to be worth stating plainly. An inventory management system does not create inventory accuracy. It maintains a record. The record's fidelity to physical reality depends on the completeness and correctness of the transactions flowing into it — every receipt, every sale, every return, every transfer, every unit of damage and shrink and misplacement — and no transaction capture is ever complete. The gap between recorded and actual is closed by physical verification, which in a retail environment means counting. Systematic, scheduled, disciplined counting of a rotating subset of the item file: cycle counting. The new system had excellent cycle counting functionality. Nobody had built the operating discipline to use it.

Blue Ridge's existing practice was an annual wall-to-wall physical inventory conducted by a third-party service, plus ad-hoc counts when a discrepancy became painful enough to notice. Between annual counts, record accuracy decayed. The 71 percent figure measured in Q3 2022 was taken nine months after the prior count. Nobody had asked what that number would be at the moment BOPIS went live, because nobody had framed inventory accuracy as a precondition rather than an outcome.

The pilot went live at twelve stores on November 6, 2023. The twelve were selected for geographic spread and volume mix: four in the Knoxville metro, three in the Tri-Cities, three in smaller Tennessee markets, and two in southwest Virginia. Marketing supported launch with radio in the Knoxville and Tri-Cities markets, in-store signage, and a direct email to 14,000 trade accounts. The nine-minute promise was in all of it.

## IV. What the Pilot Exposed

The problems appeared within the first week and did not improve.

**Pick accuracy ran 62 percent.** This is the figure that defined the pilot. Of every hundred orders picked, 38 could not be completed as ordered from the shelf stock the system said existed. A picker would go to the location, find nothing or find four units where the system claimed eleven, and the order would go into exception handling — a substitution call to the customer, a partial fulfillment, or a cancellation.

The mechanism was straightforward. BOPIS does something no prior process at Blue Ridge had done: it converts the inventory record into a promise to a named customer at a specific time. A walk-in customer facing an empty shelf experiences disappointment and adapts, often buying something else. A BOPIS customer has already committed. They have received a confirmation. They have often driven to the store. The 29 percent record inaccuracy that had been a chronic background irritant became, under BOPIS, an acute and highly visible failure delivered directly to the customer with the company's name on it.

The inaccuracy was also not randomly distributed. It concentrated in exactly the categories BOPIS customers ordered most: fast-moving consumables, fasteners, electrical and plumbing fittings, adhesives, and abrasives. High-velocity items accumulate transaction volume, and transaction volume accumulates error. Slow-moving specialty items, where the record was often perfectly accurate, generated almost no online orders.

**Actual pickup time averaged 41 minutes against a nine-minute promise.** The gap decomposed into three parts. First, picking took longer than modeled — an average of 19 minutes per order against a planned 7, because pickers were store associates picking between customer interactions, because the mobile application required more taps than the workflow assumed, and because 38 percent of orders hit an exception requiring investigation, a phone call, or a manager decision. Second, staging and handoff added time nobody had modeled: locating a staged order in a back room that had no formal slotting scheme, verifying identity, processing payment adjustments for substitutions. Third, and most damaging, orders were frequently not picked at all when the customer arrived. The promise was nine minutes from arrival, but the operating assumption was that picking happened in advance. At a store with three associates on the floor on a Saturday in December, orders sat in the queue until someone had a gap. Customers arrived to find their order untouched and waited while it was picked in front of them.

**2,300 orders were cancelled in six weeks.** Against approximately 11,400 orders placed across the twelve pilot stores in that period, this is a cancellation rate of 20 percent. Cancellations came from three sources: items unavailable and no acceptable substitution (roughly 1,500), customers abandoning after excessive wait (roughly 500), and system or process errors including duplicate orders and orders staged at the wrong store (roughly 300). The cancellation figure understates the damage, because it excludes orders fulfilled late, fulfilled partially, or fulfilled with substitutions the customer accepted without enthusiasm.

**Store managers began keeping their own paper counts.** This was the signal that mattered most, and Nakashima flagged it in a memo dated December 18, 2023. Within five weeks of go-live, managers at nine of the twelve pilot stores had established manual tracking for high-velocity items — handwritten counts on clipboards, updated once or twice daily, used to decide whether to accept or reject incoming online orders. Several had begun manually suppressing items from online availability regardless of system quantity.

This behavior was rational and it was correct. Faced with a system whose promises they could not keep, managers built a defense that protected the customer at the cost of the channel. But it was also, from a program perspective, a categorical failure. The entire purpose of the investment was to establish a single trusted inventory record. Five weeks in, the people closest to the inventory had concluded the record could not be trusted and had reverted to the paper systems the program was meant to eliminate. Once that reversion happens it is very difficult to undo, because the manual system, however crude, is now the one people believe.

VP of Merchandising Sofia Karagiannis raised a related concern in the same period. Automated replenishment, driving from the same corrupted record, was generating purchase orders that did not match need — over-ordering items the system undercounted, under-ordering items it overcounted. Buyers, who had spent years learning to distrust system data, had been told to trust the new system. Some did, for a few weeks, with poor results. Most reverted to calling stores. The replenishment component of the program was, in practice, not operating.

By early January 2024, the program was in visible trouble. Weekly order volume across the pilot stores had peaked in the first week of December and declined for five consecutive weeks. Customer service contacts related to pickup orders were running at 340 per week. Two large trade accounts had written to complain. An internal survey of pilot store associates found 71 percent disagreed with the statement "the new system helps me serve customers better."

## V. The Diagnostic

Ashby-Ng made a decision in the second week of January 2024 that, in retrospect, is the pivot of the case. She did not accelerate. She did not replace the vendor. She did not fire the program manager. She stopped the rollout schedule and commissioned a two-month diagnostic led by Karagiannis and Nakashima, with explicit authority to recommend cancellation.

The choice of leads was deliberate. Both were operators rather than technologists, both had been skeptics, and neither owned the original business case. Trumbo, the CIO, supported the decision and made his team available but did not lead the review — a piece of organizational judgment that gave the diagnostic credibility with store teams who had come to see the program as an IT initiative done to them.

The diagnostic ran from mid-January to mid-March 2024. It included a full physical count at four pilot stores, structured interviews with all twelve pilot store managers and 60 associates, transaction-level analysis of all 11,400 pilot orders, time-and-motion observation of picking at six stores, and a review of Cardinal's implementation against contract.

The findings, delivered to the executive team on March 14, 2024, were direct.

**Inventory record accuracy at the pilot stores was 68 percent** — lower than the 71 percent baseline measured eighteen months earlier. The new system had not improved accuracy. In some respects it had degraded it, because the conversion had introduced errors and because the increased transaction volume from picking, staging, substitution, and cancellation created new opportunities for error that the store teams had not been trained to handle. The finding was uncomfortable and Karagiannis presented it first.

**The system's design assumed a cycle counting discipline that had never existed at Blue Ridge.** This was the diagnostic's central conclusion. Cardinal's replenishment algorithms, safety stock calculations, and availability logic all assumed a maintained record. Cardinal's own implementation methodology included a cycle counting workstream. That workstream had been descoped in April 2023 during a schedule compression exercise, on the reasoning that it was an operational practice rather than a system implementation task and could be addressed after go-live. The descoping was documented in a change log. It was approved by the program steering committee, which included Trumbo and a Cardinal engagement director. Nobody at the time understood they were removing the load-bearing element.

The diagnostic was careful about blame here, and the framing it chose proved useful. The failure was not that someone made a bad decision in April 2023. The failure was that the organization had no mechanism for recognizing that an operational-discipline item removed from a technology project's scope does not thereby disappear — it simply becomes nobody's job. Cardinal had proposed the work. Blue Ridge had removed it. Neither party had subsequently owned the question of whether the system's core assumption would hold.

**Picking could not be a shared duty.** The time-and-motion work found that picking performed by floor associates between customer interactions took 2.7 times longer than picking performed by a dedicated picker in a continuous block, and had a substantially higher error rate. Interleaving picking with customer service imposed a switching cost the operating model had ignored. The finding pointed to a labor model the business case had not funded: BOPIS at meaningful volume requires dedicated labor.

**The nine-minute promise was not achievable and should not have been made.** The diagnostic reconstructed the promise's origin and found it had been adopted from a vendor reference client without validation against Blue Ridge's store layouts, staffing, or order profile. The reference client's eleven-minute average covered orders picked in advance during a two-hour pre-staging window with a dedicated staffer — an operating model Blue Ridge had never adopted. The promise had been treated as a marketing input rather than an operational commitment, and nobody had run the arithmetic.

The diagnostic recommended against cancellation. Its reasoning: the underlying strategic logic was sound, customer demand was real (11,400 orders in six weeks from twelve stores, despite terrible service, was evidence of appetite), the data cleansing and system foundation were genuinely valuable, and the failure was in operational preconditions that could be built. Cancelling would forfeit the investment and leave the company with the same 2008 system and the same 71 percent accuracy.

## VI. The Reset

The executive team approved the reset plan in late March 2024. It had four components.

**Pause the rollout.** The 26 unconverted stores would remain on the legacy system indefinitely. The twelve pilot stores would keep the new system but with BOPIS suspended at eight of them; four would continue at reduced volume as a learning environment. This was announced internally on April 2, 2024, and Ashby-Ng did the announcement herself on a video call to all store managers. She used the word "we" throughout and did not name individuals. Several managers later cited that call as the moment they began to believe the company was serious.

**Fund a dedicated picker at each store.** The single largest ongoing cost of the reset. Each store converting to the new model would receive funded headcount for a dedicated picker — approximately 30 hours per week at smaller stores, 55 at larger ones, with a defined role covering picking, staging, handoff, and, critically, cycle counting during low-order periods. Combining picking and counting in one role was Nakashima's design and it proved to be one of the better decisions in the program: the person who experiences the pain of a wrong record is the person who fixes it, and the feedback loop is same-day.

**Run a six-month cycle count program.** The core of the reset. Every store, converted or not, would execute a structured cycle count program beginning May 2024. The design was ABC-stratified: the top 1,200 SKUs by velocity counted every two weeks, the next 6,000 monthly, the remainder quarterly. Counts were performed on mobile devices against blind quantities — the counter could not see the system figure — with variances above threshold triggering a recount and a root-cause note. Root-cause notes fed a weekly review at each store and a monthly review at headquarters.

Two design choices mattered. First, counts were scheduled and non-negotiable, with completion reported weekly to district managers and included in store manager performance reviews. Blue Ridge had a long history of good operational intentions dissolving under the pressure of a busy Saturday, and the program was designed to be resistant to that. Second, root-cause analysis was mandatory rather than optional. Variance without diagnosis produces a corrected record and an uncorrected process. The monthly headquarters review identified systemic drivers across the chain — a receiving practice that was double-counting bundled items, a returns process that put product back on the shelf without a system transaction, a transfer procedure that decremented the sending store on shipment and incremented the receiving store on receipt with no visibility into the gap — and fixed them centrally.

**Change the customer promise to two hours.** The nine-minute promise was withdrawn and replaced with a commitment that orders placed would be ready within two hours during store hours, with a notification when ready. This was a substantial retreat and it was debated. Marketing argued the promise was now unremarkable. Nakashima's counter, which prevailed, was that a two-hour promise reliably kept beats a nine-minute promise kept 20 percent of the time, and that the operating model — pick in batches, notify on completion, hand off on arrival — eliminated the interleaving problem the diagnostic had identified. Handoff at arrival, measured separately, would target under five minutes, and that number could be honestly advertised because it described only the part of the process the store controlled.

**Restart store by store, gated.** No store would launch BOPIS until it met three criteria sustained over four consecutive weeks: inventory record accuracy above 90 percent on the top 1,200 SKUs, cycle count completion above 95 percent of scheduled counts, and a dedicated picker hired and trained. Gate assessment was performed by district managers using an audit count they conducted personally.

The gated restart began in September 2024 with three stores. It expanded to nine by November, eighteen by January 2025, and all 38 by early March 2025. Notably, the first stores through the gate were not the largest or the most sophisticated; they were the ones whose managers had committed hardest to the cycle count discipline. Two of the original twelve pilot stores were among the last to re-launch. Nakashima made a point of publicizing this, and the message — that this program rewarded counting discipline rather than tenure or volume — landed.

## VII. Results

By March 2025, twenty-six months after program approval and twelve months after the reset:

**Inventory record accuracy: 94 percent**, up from 71 percent at baseline and 68 percent at the pilot low point. Measured monthly by district-manager audit count across a stratified sample. The top 1,200 SKUs measured 96 percent; the long tail measured 89 percent.

**Out-of-stock rate on top 200 items: 6 percent**, down from 18 percent. This improvement came from two sources: automated replenishment finally operating against a trustworthy record, and buyers who had begun to trust system data. Karagiannis noted that the second was harder to achieve than the first and took roughly four months longer.

**Pickup channel: 11 percent of sales, $34 million annualized.** From effectively zero. Order volume in March 2025 ran at approximately 4,100 per week chainwide with an average order value of $159 — meaningfully higher than the in-store average of $61, reflecting the trade-account skew of online ordering.

**Comparable store sales: plus 3.8 percent**, against minus 4.2 percent average over the six quarters preceding approval — a swing of eight points. Management's internal attribution assigned roughly half to reduced stockouts driving recovered in-store trade volume, roughly a third to incremental pickup volume, and the remainder to general market conditions and merchandising changes made in the same period.

**Operating metrics on the channel itself:** pick accuracy 97 percent, up from 62. Cancellation rate 2.4 percent, down from 20. Median time from order to ready-notification 71 minutes against the two-hour promise, with 96 percent of orders ready within promise. Median handoff time at arrival 4 minutes.

**Trade account behavior:** among the 22 percent of accounts that had reduced spend by more than 30 percent between 2019 and 2022, 31 percent had increased spend year over year by March 2025. Trade accounts using the pickup channel showed 19 percent higher annual spend than matched accounts not using it — a correlation management was careful not to over-read, since early adopters were likely more engaged to begin with.

## VIII. The Full Cost

The program was approved at $6.8 million. Final cost was **$9.1 million**, plus **$1.4 million in added store labor**, for a total of **$10.5 million** — 54 percent above approval.

The $2.3 million overrun on program cost:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Extended implementation timeline (14 months beyond plan) | $780,000 |
| Cycle count program design, tooling, and training | $410,000 |
| POS integration overrun | $340,000 |
| Diagnostic and reset planning | $260,000 |
| Additional training and change management | $240,000 |
| Web front-end rework | $170,000 |
| Contingency consumed and exceeded | $100,000 |

The $1.4 million in store labor represents the annualized run rate of dedicated picker headcount across 38 stores — roughly 1,600 hours per store per year at fully loaded cost. This is not a one-time cost. It is a permanent addition to the store operating model, and Ashby-Ng was explicit with the board that it should be understood as the true cost of operating an omnichannel fulfillment capability rather than as a program overrun. Against $34 million of channel revenue at the company's gross margin, the labor is comfortably covered; against the original business case, which assumed picking absorbed into existing labor, it is a material omission.

Worth noting on the other side: the pilot's damage was not only financial. The 2,300 cancelled orders and several thousand poorly served ones represented real relationship cost with the customers most likely to be early adopters — the engaged, tech-forward trade accounts the strategy most needed. Karagiannis estimated that roughly 800 customers who used the pilot never returned to the channel after re-launch. That cost does not appear in any budget line.

## IX. What the Leadership Team Would Do Differently

In a session with the board in June 2025, Ashby-Ng, Trumbo, Karagiannis, and Nakashima presented a retrospective. Their conclusions:

**Treat inventory accuracy as a precondition, not an outcome.** Trumbo was blunt: "We bought a system that maintains a record and assumed it would create one." The cycle count program should have run for six months before the first line of integration code was written, at a cost of perhaps $400,000, and the pilot should have been gated on 90 percent accuracy. Doing the work first would have been cheaper, faster, and would have avoided the pilot failure entirely. It would also have been unglamorous, unfundable as a standalone initiative, and invisible to the board — which is precisely why it did not happen.

**Never adopt a customer promise from a vendor reference.** The nine-minute figure was the program's most consequential single error, because it converted an operational shortfall into a public broken commitment. Karagiannis's rule: no externally communicated service commitment without a documented operating model showing how it is met at peak volume with actual staffing, validated by observation. "Nine minutes was a number we liked. Two hours is a number we can keep. The second one is worth more."

**Do not pilot into peak season.** Nakashima had raised this in March 2023 and been overruled. The retrospective vindicated him without ceremony. A pilot's purpose is learning, and learning requires slack. November provided none.

**Watch for reversion to manual systems as the leading indicator.** The paper counts appearing in week five were the clearest early signal available, and the program had no mechanism to surface them. Nakashima's recommendation: instrument for workaround behavior explicitly. Ask store teams monthly what manual records they are keeping and why. "People closest to the work tell you the truth with their behavior before they tell you in a survey."

**Understand that descoping operational discipline does not remove the requirement.** The April 2023 change log entry removing the cycle counting workstream was, in retrospect, the decision that cost $2.3 million and fifteen months. Trumbo's proposed control: any descoping decision must document what organizational capability the removed item was providing and who now owns providing it. If the answer is "nobody," the scope cannot be removed.

**Fund the labor model honestly.** The business case assumed picking would be absorbed. It could not be. Any fulfillment capability that makes a time commitment to a named customer requires dedicated capacity, because the alternative — interleaving with customer service — imposes a switching cost that destroys both activities. Ashby-Ng's framing: "We budgeted for a technology project and bought an operating model change. The operating model change had a labor cost and we should have put it in the case."

**Give the diagnostic to skeptics who do not own the original decision.** The choice to have Karagiannis and Nakashima lead the review, with cancellation authority, was the reset's enabling condition. A review led by the program's owners would have produced remediation rather than diagnosis, and remediation would have addressed pick times and training rather than the record accuracy underneath them.

## X. What a Reader Should Take From This

If you are considering a similar investment, the transferable lessons are these.

**A new system cannot fix a data-quality problem that is generated by operating practice.** Blue Ridge's inventory record was inaccurate because transactions were incomplete and nothing systematically verified physical reality. Replacing the system that held the record changed nothing about either condition. Ask, before you buy: what operating practices does this system's core logic assume, do we perform them today, and to what standard? If the honest answer is that you do not, the practice is your project and the software is a subsequent step.

**Digital channels convert chronic problems into acute ones.** A 71 percent accurate record is survivable in a walk-in store, because the customer absorbs the failure through adaptation. It is not survivable when you have made a specific promise to a named individual. Any channel that converts inventory data into a customer commitment raises the required accuracy threshold by an order of magnitude. Estimate the new threshold before you commit, not after.

**The customer promise is an operational commitment, not a marketing choice.** The distance between "nine minutes" and "two hours" is the distance between a promise derived from what you wished were true and one derived from what you can deliver at peak with real staffing. The second number performed better commercially, because a kept promise builds the confidence that drives repeat use and a broken one destroys it faster than any advertisement builds it.

**Watch what your operators do, not what they say.** The paper counts were the truth. They appeared five weeks in and were not escalated for six more. Build a channel through which frontline workarounds reach senior leadership within days, because a workaround is an unambiguous statement that the official system has failed at something specific, and the people building it usually know exactly what.

**The pause is the decision that saves the program.** Blue Ridge spent $260,000 and two months on a diagnostic in the middle of a visibly failing pilot, and that decision converted a likely write-off into a working capability. The instinct under pressure is to accelerate — more training, more resources, more urgency — because acceleration looks like leadership. When the failure is in a precondition rather than in execution, acceleration makes it worse. Ask first whether you are executing badly or executing on a foundation that is not there.

**Budget for the operating model, not just the technology.** The $1.4 million in dedicated picker labor was not an overrun. It was the correct and permanent cost of the capability, discovered late. When you evaluate a system that changes how stores work, model the labor first and let the software follow.

**The unglamorous foundation work is the whole game.** Blue Ridge's outcomes — 94 percent accuracy, 6 percent stockouts, $34 million in new channel revenue, an eight-point comparable sales swing — were purchased with a six-month cycle counting program that any competent retail operator could have designed in a week and that nobody was willing to fund as a standalone initiative. The company eventually paid $10.5 million to arrive at a place a $400,000 counting program and a year of patience would have taken it more directly. That gap is the case.
