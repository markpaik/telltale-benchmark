# Annual Operations Report

**Report Title:** FY2026 Annual Operations Report — Susquehanna Rising Charter Schools
**Reporting Period:** July 1, 2025 – June 30, 2026 (with staffing and financial data current through June 2026)
**Prepared By:** Nasreen Qureshi, Chief of Staff
**Prepared For:** Board of Trustees, Susquehanna Rising Charter Schools, for adoption at the August 2026 meeting and inclusion in the charter renewal packet to be submitted to the Harrisburg School District Board of Directors
**Contributors:** Antoine Lemieux, Chief Executive Officer; Holly Steinmetz, Chief Financial Officer; campus principals
**Distribution:** Board of Trustees; authorizing district (upon adoption); network leadership team

---

## 1. Purpose and Reading Guide

This report is the foundation document for the network's October charter renewal hearing before the Harrisburg School District Board. Its purpose is to give the Board of Trustees an accurate, campus-by-campus account of where Susquehanna Rising stands at the close of FY2026, so that trustees walk into the renewal process with a shared and honest understanding of network performance — not a single blended narrative that obscures real variation across our four campuses.

At the direction of Mr. Lemieux, Ms. Steinmetz, and Chair Bishop, this report deliberately avoids averaging results across campuses that are, in practice, operating under very different conditions. Riverfront Elementary is performing at a level that will make a strong affirmative case to the authorizer. Cameron Street Middle and Allison Hill Academy show real strengths alongside specific, addressable problems. Steelton Prep is carrying a financial burden that the network cannot absorb indefinitely without a change in enrollment trajectory or facility terms. The Board needs all four pictures, not a composite score.

The report proceeds as follows: Section 2 summarizes the network as a whole. Sections 3 through 6 cover each campus in turn — enrollment, staffing, attendance, and academic results. Section 7 presents the network's financial position, including cash position and the loss of federal relief funding. Section 8 addresses staff turnover systemwide and by campus. Section 9 is a focused treatment of the Steelton Prep deficit, since it is the single largest risk factor the Board will need to speak to at the hearing. Section 10 closes with what trustees should understand and be prepared to address going into the renewal hearing, including likely questions from the authorizer and the network's proposed responses.

## 2. Network Summary

Susquehanna Rising Charter Schools ended FY2026 serving 3,120 students across four campuses in Harrisburg, with 380 staff and a total operating budget of $47 million. The network's overall enrollment figure conceals significant divergence at the campus level: Riverfront is essentially at capacity and performing well above city averages academically; Cameron Street has lost enrollment and is struggling with staffing continuity; Allison Hill is graduating and placing students at respectable rates but lost critical counseling capacity mid-year; and Steelton Prep, still in its third year of operation, remains far below the enrollment level its financial model requires.

The table below gives the headline figures for each campus, which are unpacked in detail in the sections that follow.

| Campus | Enrollment (2025–26) | % of Enrollment Target | Attendance / Chronic Absenteeism | Notable Academic Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Riverfront Elementary | 780 | 96% | 92% attendance | 41% third-grade reading proficiency (city avg. 29%) |
| Cameron Street Middle | 640 (down from 720) | Below target; declining | 22% chronic absenteeism | 6 of 44 teaching positions vacant Jan–June, covered by long-term substitutes |
| Allison Hill Academy | Not separately reported below | — | — | 84% graduation rate; 61% of graduates with documented postsecondary plans; 210 in dual credit |
| Steelton Prep | 690 | 71% of 970-student model | — | Operating in its third year under founding principal Yesenia Duarte |

Systemwide, the network closed the year with 38 days of unrestricted cash on hand, eliminated 14 positions that had been funded by expiring federal relief dollars, and recorded teacher turnover of 31 percent. Each of these figures is addressed in full in Sections 7 and 8.

The overall picture the Board should carry forward is one of a network with a genuinely strong flagship campus, two mid-performing campuses each facing a distinct operational problem, and one campus whose financial structure does not yet match its enrollment reality. The renewal case should be built on the strength of Riverfront and the credible, specific plans the network can present for the other three — not on a claim that all four campuses are performing uniformly well, which the data does not support and which an authorizer reviewing campus-level records would not accept.

## 3. Riverfront Elementary

Riverfront Elementary is the network's oldest and largest campus and, on the basis of this year's results, its strongest argument for renewal. Enrollment held at 780 students, or 96 percent of the campus's enrollment target, reflecting sustained family demand and low attrition during the school year. Attendance across the campus averaged 92 percent, comfortably above both the district's elementary average and the threshold the network uses internally to flag attendance concern (85 percent).

Academically, Riverfront's headline result is third-grade reading proficiency of 41 percent, against a city-wide average of 29 percent — a 12-percentage-point gap that has held or widened over each of the past three years and represents the clearest, most authorizer-legible evidence of the network's academic value proposition. Third-grade reading proficiency is a closely watched indicator both because it is a state-reported metric and because it is predictive of later academic trajectory; a campus performing 12 points above the city average on this measure is producing results that few Harrisburg elementary options, district or charter, can currently match.

Staffing at Riverfront was stable through the year. The campus did not carry vacancies of the kind experienced at Cameron Street, and principal leadership has been consistent, which the network's instructional team credits in part for the reading results. Riverfront's staff turnover rate, discussed further in Section 8, ran below the network average, reinforcing that the instructional gains are being produced by a largely intact teaching staff rather than a rotating one.

The Board should treat Riverfront as the campus whose data can be presented with the least qualification at the renewal hearing. It is also, however, a caution against complacency: Riverfront's results demonstrate what the network is capable of when a campus has stable enrollment, stable staffing, and consistent leadership — conditions that do not currently hold at the other three campuses. The gap between Riverfront and the rest of the network is itself a data point the authorizer will notice, and trustees should be prepared to speak to why that gap exists and what the network is doing to close it, rather than treating Riverfront's performance as evidence that the whole network is functioning at the same level.

## 4. Cameron Street Middle

Cameron Street Middle presents the sharpest single-year decline in the network and requires the most direct acknowledgment in the renewal narrative.

**Enrollment.** Cameron Street's enrollment fell from 720 students at the start of the prior year to 640 this year, a decline of 80 students, or just over 11 percent. This is the largest year-over-year enrollment loss of any campus in the network. The decline has multiple contributing factors that the operations team has documented through exit surveys and enrollment office records: families citing dissatisfaction with staffing continuity (discussed below), a smaller number citing transportation issues following a district route change, and a modest number of eighth-grade families choosing to enroll students directly in district high schools rather than continuing to Allison Hill Academy, which draws primarily from Cameron Street's graduating cohort.

**Attendance.** Chronic absenteeism at Cameron Street stood at 22 percent for the year, meaning that roughly one in five students missed 10 percent or more of the school year. This is a serious figure in absolute terms and is also the network's highest chronic absenteeism rate by a wide margin. Chronic absenteeism at this level is both a symptom and a driver of the enrollment decline: families whose students are already disengaged are more likely to withdraw, and persistent absenteeism among students who remain enrolled depresses the academic and culture indicators the campus can otherwise post.

**Staffing.** The most operationally significant fact about Cameron Street this year is that 6 of its 44 teaching positions — roughly 14 percent of the instructional staff — sat vacant from January through June, covered by long-term substitutes for the back half of the school year. This is not a one- or two-week coverage gap; it is a full-semester staffing shortfall in core teaching roles, and it touches nearly every grade and subject team at the campus given its size. Long-term substitute coverage, even when substitutes are experienced and engaged, does not provide the continuity of instructional relationship, curriculum pacing consistency, or classroom management that a permanent teacher provides, and principal and instructional leadership at Cameron Street have flagged the vacancy period as a direct contributor to both the attendance figures and to unfinished curriculum pacing in several grade levels going into the summer.

The network's human resources team attempted three separate hiring cycles between January and June to fill these positions and made offers in four of the six roles; two offers were declined in favor of district positions offering higher base pay, and two hires who accepted did not complete onboarding before the school year ended and will start in the fall. The remaining two positions were actively recruited through June without a successful hire.

**Academic results.** Cameron Street's academic results for the year are mixed and, honestly, harder to interpret cleanly than Riverfront's, given the disruption described above. Campus-level proficiency data is being finalized by the assessment team and will be appended to this report before it goes to the Board for adoption; principal-reported classroom-level data indicates that grade teams with stable teacher assignment for the full year performed in line with prior-year Cameron Street results, while the two grade-subject combinations most affected by the vacancy period (seventh-grade math and eighth-grade science) show declines that instructional leadership attributes directly to the staffing gap.

**What the Board should understand.** Cameron Street's story this year is fundamentally a staffing story with enrollment and attendance consequences. The network's response — detailed in the staffing improvement plan Mr. Lemieux is bringing to the Board separately — centers on earlier-cycle hiring for the 2026–27 year (offers extended by April rather than June), a retention stipend for hard-to-staff subject areas, and restored transportation coordination with the district. Trustees should be prepared to describe this plan in specific terms at the hearing rather than characterizing the vacancy period as fully resolved, since two positions remain open going into the fall.

## 5. Allison Hill Academy

Allison Hill Academy, the network's high school campus, produced results this year that are genuinely strong in several respects and compromised in one specific, addressable way.

**Graduation and postsecondary outcomes.** Allison Hill graduated 84 percent of its senior class, a figure that compares favorably to district and citywide high school graduation rates and represents a continuation of the campus's multi-year upward trend. Of graduates, 61 percent had documented postsecondary plans on file at the time of graduation — meaning a confirmed college enrollment, military enlistment, workforce credential program, or documented employment plan. The campus also enrolled 210 students in dual credit coursework this year, a substantial figure for a school of its size and one that reflects real investment in college-preparatory pathway-building, including articulation agreements with two regional community college partners.

These figures should be read together: an 84 percent graduation rate paired with dual-credit enrollment at this scale indicates that Allison Hill is not simply moving students across a graduation stage but is building meaningful academic pathways for a substantial share of its student body.

**The counselor gap.** Set against these results is a staffing loss that the network has not yet fully mitigated. Allison Hill lost its only school counselor to a district position in February. For a high school of its enrollment, operating with a single counselor was already a thin staffing model relative to comparable schools, and the loss of that position mid-year left the campus without dedicated counseling capacity for the final four-plus months of the school year — precisely the period covering second-semester course selection, financial aid deadlines, spring testing administration, and, most consequentially, the period in which many seniors finalize postsecondary plans.

The 61 percent postsecondary-plan documentation rate should be read in this context. It is a respectable figure on its own terms, but it was achieved without dedicated counseling support for the back half of the year, through a combination of teacher advisory coverage, principal involvement, and support from the network's central college-and-career team, which the network deployed to Allison Hill on a partial basis after the vacancy occurred. It is reasonable to expect that a fully staffed counseling office for the full year would have produced a higher documentation rate, and the network's own internal projection, based on the trajectory before the vacancy, had targeted 68 to 70 percent.

The network has posted the counselor position for the 2026–27 year and, at Mr. Lemieux's direction, has approved a salary adjustment intended to make the role more competitive against district compensation, which was the primary factor cited by the departing counselor. As of this report, the position remains open going into August, and the Board should be aware that there is real risk of beginning the fall semester without a permanent counselor in place, which would extend the same coverage gap into a second year.

**What the Board should understand.** Allison Hill's outcomes this year are a genuine strength for the renewal case — an 84 percent graduation rate and 210 dual-credit enrollments are not marginal figures — but trustees should not present the 61 percent postsecondary-documentation figure without the context of the counselor vacancy, both because the authorizer's review team is likely to ask about staffing continuity at every campus given the Cameron Street pattern, and because presenting the figure without context would overstate the network's confidence that it can sustain or improve on 61 percent without addressing the underlying staffing gap.

## 6. Steelton Prep

Steelton Prep is the network's newest campus, opened in 2023 under founding principal Yesenia Duarte, and it is the campus that carries the most direct financial risk into the renewal conversation. A full financial treatment appears in Section 9; this section addresses enrollment, staffing, and operating conditions at the campus level.

**Enrollment.** Steelton Prep enrolled 690 students this year against a financial and staffing model built for 970 students — a shortfall of 280 students, or roughly 29 percent below the model's design enrollment. This is the campus's third year of operation, and while enrollment has grown from its opening-year base, the growth curve has not kept pace with the ramp assumed in the original charter application and financial model, which projected the school reaching approximately 85 to 90 percent of design enrollment by year three.

**Leadership and staffing.** Principal Duarte has led the campus since its opening and has built a staff and instructional program that campus-level walkthrough data and family survey results describe positively; the operating problem at Steelton Prep is not, on current evidence, a leadership or instructional-quality problem in the way that Cameron Street's is a staffing-continuity problem. It is an enrollment and facility-cost problem, which is a different kind of problem for the Board to understand and to explain to the authorizer.

**Facility.** The campus operates in a leased building sized for the 970-student model, at an annual lease cost of $1.4 million. Because the lease was structured around the enrollment projection rather than around current actual enrollment, the facility cost functions largely as a fixed cost: the network is paying for physical capacity for roughly 280 students it does not currently enroll. This fixed-cost structure is the direct mechanical driver of the deficit detailed in Section 9.

**What the Board should understand.** Steelton Prep is not a campus in academic or leadership distress; family and staff feedback about the program itself has been positive, and Principal Duarte's team has met most of the instructional benchmarks the network tracks for a school in its third year. The issue the Board must be prepared to address at the renewal hearing is structural: a facility commitment sized for an enrollment level the campus has not yet reached, producing a deficit that the rest of the network is currently absorbing. This is treated in full in Section 9, including the options the finance team has modeled for closing the gap.

## 7. Network Financial Position

Ms. Steinmetz's office has prepared the financial detail underlying this section; what follows is a summary sufficient for Board discussion, with the full financial statements to be presented separately as part of the August adoption meeting.

**Cash position.** The network closed the year with 38 days of unrestricted cash on hand. Days of cash on hand is calculated as unrestricted cash divided by average daily operating expenses, and it is one of the standard measures authorizers and rating agencies use to assess a school's short-term financial resilience. Thirty-eight days is below the 60-day threshold that the network's own board-adopted financial policy identifies as the minimum target, and well below the 90-day threshold that many authorizers, including Harrisburg's, treat as a benchmark of financial health in charter renewal reviews. This figure is likely to draw direct questions from the authorizer's finance review team and should not be minimized in the renewal narrative; it is more credible, and ultimately more persuasive, for the network to present the cash position candidly alongside a specific plan to rebuild reserves than to have the authorizer identify the shortfall independently.

The cash position reflects the combined effect of the Steelton Prep deficit, the loss of federal relief funding described below, and Cameron Street's enrollment decline, which reduces per-pupil funding flowing to the network under the state's charter funding formula. It does not, per Ms. Steinmetz's analysis, reflect any single one-time or unusual expense; it is the accumulated effect of structural pressures that will persist into FY2027 absent specific corrective action.

**Federal relief expiration.** The network eliminated 14 positions this year that had been funded by expiring federal pandemic-era relief funding (ESSER and related sources). This is a planned and, in the network's judgment, responsibly managed transition rather than a reactive layoff: the finance office identified these positions as relief-funded as early as the FY2024 budget cycle and communicated to affected staff and to campus leadership well in advance of the funding cliff. The 14 eliminated positions were distributed across instructional support, tutoring, and central-office roles added during the relief period; no core classroom teaching positions were eliminated as part of this reduction. The Board should understand this reduction as the network absorbing an anticipated funding cliff, not as an emergency cost-cutting measure, though it is fair to note that the loss of 14 positions' worth of support capacity — particularly tutoring and instructional support roles — removes some of the intervention capacity that had been available to campuses, including Cameron Street, during the relief period.

**Budget summary.** The table below presents the network's FY2026 budget at a summary level.

| Category | FY2026 (approx.) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Total operating budget | $47.0 million | Network-wide, all campuses and central office |
| Steelton Prep operating deficit | $2.3 million | Driven primarily by facility lease cost against under-enrollment |
| Steelton Prep annual facility lease | $1.4 million | Fixed cost, sized for 970-student model |
| Positions eliminated (federal relief expiration) | 14 positions | Instructional support, tutoring, central office; no classroom teachers |
| Unrestricted days cash on hand | 38 days | Below internal 60-day policy target and typical 90-day authorizer benchmark |

**Outlook.** Absent a change in Steelton Prep's enrollment trajectory, a renegotiation of facility terms, or an offsetting reduction elsewhere in the network budget, the finance office projects that the FY2027 budget will require either a further drawdown of reserves (worsening the cash position below 38 days) or a network-wide expense reduction exercise. Ms. Steinmetz is preparing a set of scenario options for Board review at the August meeting; those options are summarized at a high level in Section 9 and will be presented in full detail separately.

## 8. Staffing and Turnover

Teacher turnover across the network was 31 percent for the year, meaning roughly three in ten teaching positions turned over between the start and end of the year. This figure is elevated relative to the network's three-year historical average, which has run between 19 and 23 percent, and it is also elevated relative to typical turnover benchmarks for comparable urban charter networks, which commonly fall in the 15 to 20 percent range for stable, well-established systems (first-year and second-year schools often run higher).

Turnover is not evenly distributed across the network, and the Board should resist treating 31 percent as a single, network-wide condition requiring a single response. Cameron Street's staffing pattern — six vacancies held open for a full semester — is functionally a turnover and hiring-pipeline problem distinct from, for example, ordinary attrition at Riverfront, where turnover ran below the network average and where the positions that did turn over were filled before the start of the school year without disruption to instructional continuity. Allison Hill's most consequential loss this year was a single, hard-to-replace specialist position (the counselor) rather than a broad pattern of classroom-teacher attrition. Steelton Prep's staffing situation is driven primarily by the enrollment-facility mismatch discussed in Sections 6 and 9 rather than by voluntary attrition, though as a newer campus it has also experienced somewhat higher first-generation staff turnover than the network's established campuses, which is consistent with typical patterns for schools in their first several years of operation.

The exit-interview data the human resources team has compiled points to three recurring factors cited by departing staff, in order of frequency: compensation relative to district and comparable charter pay scales, particularly in hard-to-staff subject areas such as middle school math and science; workload and coverage burden in schools carrying vacancies, which creates a self-reinforcing dynamic at campuses like Cameron Street where remaining staff absorb additional responsibilities; and, for a smaller number of departing staff, dissatisfaction with facility conditions, cited primarily by Steelton Prep staff in connection with a building that is larger and more costly to maintain than its current enrollment requires.

The network's response for FY2027, which Mr. Lemieux will present to the Board in fuller form, includes an earlier hiring cycle across all campuses (targeting offers extended by April rather than June, based on the Cameron Street experience this year), targeted retention stipends in hard-to-staff subject areas, and a review of the counselor and specialist staffing model at Allison Hill and Steelton Prep to reduce single-point-of-failure risk in critical support roles — the kind of risk that materialized when Allison Hill's sole counselor departed in February.

The table below summarizes turnover and staffing continuity indicators by campus, to the extent data is available at this stage of the report's preparation.

| Campus | Teacher Turnover | Notable Staffing Event |
|---|---|---|
| Riverfront Elementary | Below network average | Stable; positions filled before school year began |
| Cameron Street Middle | Above network average | 6 of 44 positions vacant Jan–June, covered by long-term substitutes |
| Allison Hill Academy | Near network average | Sole counselor departed February; position unfilled as of this report |
| Steelton Prep | Above network average (newer campus) | Staffing tied to enrollment-facility mismatch; see Section 9 |
| **Network overall** | **31%** | 14 relief-funded positions eliminated (non-classroom) |

## 9. Steelton Prep Deficit — Detailed Treatment

Because the Steelton Prep shortfall is the single largest financial risk factor the Board will need to address at the renewal hearing, this section treats it separately and in more depth than the summary in Section 6.

**Mechanics of the deficit.** Steelton Prep's $2.3 million operating deficit is, at its core, a facility fixed-cost problem layered on an enrollment shortfall. The campus's per-pupil funding — the state and local dollars that follow each enrolled student under Pennsylvania's charter funding formula — is calculated against actual enrollment of 690 students, not the 970-student model the campus was built for. That means the campus's revenue is roughly 29 percent below what the building, staffing model, and program were designed to support, while the largest single fixed cost in the campus budget — the $1.4 million annual facility lease — does not scale down with enrollment. The lease was negotiated at the time of the campus's founding, based on the enrollment projections in the original charter application, and it runs on a term that does not permit renegotiation of space or rate based on current enrollment.

Put simply: Steelton Prep is paying for a building sized for 970 students while collecting revenue for 690, and the gap between those two numbers, once staffing and program costs are accounted for at a scale appropriate to actual enrollment, produces the $2.3 million shortfall. This deficit is currently being absorbed by the network's central budget — in effect, cross-subsidized by the other three campuses and by network reserves — which is a direct contributor to the 38-days-cash position described in Section 7.

**Why enrollment has lagged the model.** The finance and enrollment teams have identified several contributing factors. Steelton Prep opened in 2023 in a section of Harrisburg with less established charter-school presence than the neighborhoods served by Riverfront and Cameron Street, meaning the campus has had to build family awareness and trust from a lower starting base. The campus also competes directly with two other school options within a reasonable commuting distance for target families, which has slowed the enrollment ramp relative to the original projection. Family survey data collected by the enrollment office indicates generally positive sentiment among currently enrolled families about the instructional program, which suggests the shortfall is more a marketing, outreach, and time problem than a program-quality problem, but the finance office cannot responsibly assume the campus will close the 280-student gap on its own without a specific enrollment growth plan.

**Options under consideration.** Ms. Steinmetz's office has modeled several paths, which will be presented to the Board in full detail separately but are summarized here so trustees have the shape of the decision in advance of the August meeting:

*Accelerated enrollment growth plan.* A targeted marketing and community-outreach investment aimed at closing the enrollment gap over two to three years rather than allowing it to persist indefinitely. This is the network's preferred path and the one most consistent with the campus's positive family feedback, but it requires upfront investment at a time when the network's cash position is already strained, and it does not close the deficit in the near term.

*Facility renegotiation.* Network leadership has opened preliminary conversations with the building's landlord about either a partial subletting of unused space or a modification to the lease term. These conversations are early and have not produced a firm proposal; the landlord's initial response has not been favorable, but the finance office believes there is room to continue pursuing this option, particularly if the network can present a multi-year enrollment growth plan alongside the renegotiation request.

*Program or footprint consolidation.* A more difficult option under consideration involves consolidating Steelton Prep's program into a smaller footprint within the current building, if partial subletting proves unworkable, or, as a longer-horizon and last-resort option, evaluating whether the campus's enrollment trajectory can sustain its current independent facility at all. Network leadership does not recommend this path at this time and raises it here only so the Board has visibility into the full range of options being evaluated; pursuing this path would have significant implications for the renewal case and for Principal Duarte's team and should not be treated as a near-term decision.

*Continued cross-subsidy.* The network can continue absorbing the deficit through the central budget and reserves, as it has this year, but Ms. Steinmetz's projections indicate this is not sustainable beyond one additional year without either the cash position deteriorating below levels the network's financial policy — and likely the authorizer — would consider acceptable, or a reduction in spending elsewhere in the network to offset the Steelton Prep gap.

**What the Board should understand.** The Steelton Prep deficit is the item most likely to draw sustained attention from the authorizer's finance review team, both because $2.3 million is a large figure relative to a $47 million total budget and because it connects directly to the network's overall cash position. Trustees should be prepared to present the deficit not as a surprise or a crisis being discovered for the first time, but as a known, tracked, structural issue with a set of options under active evaluation and a timeline for a Board decision. The credibility of the renewal case depends significantly on the Board demonstrating that it understands the mechanics of this deficit and has a governance process for resolving it, rather than presenting Steelton Prep as a campus that will grow into its model on its own.

## 10. What Trustees Should Understand Going Into the Renewal Hearing

This closing section translates the preceding material into the specific points trustees should be prepared to make, and the specific questions trustees should expect, at the October hearing.

**The core narrative the Board should carry forward** is that Susquehanna Rising is a network with one campus performing at a genuinely strong level (Riverfront), two campuses with real strengths and specific, identified, addressable problems (Cameron Street's staffing continuity; Allison Hill's counseling capacity), and one campus (Steelton Prep) whose academic and family-satisfaction indicators are reasonable but whose financial structure does not currently match its enrollment, producing a deficit that the network is managing but has not yet resolved. This is a more defensible and more credible position before an authorizer than a narrative that treats all four campuses as performing at a uniform level — and Mr. Lemieux, Ms. Steinmetz, and Chair Bishop have all been explicit that the report and the Board's public presentation should not flatten these differences.

**Likely authorizer questions and the network's position on each:**

*On Riverfront's strength:* the authorizer is likely to ask why the network cannot replicate Riverfront's conditions — stable enrollment, stable staffing, consistent leadership — at the other campuses. The Board's answer should be specific to each campus rather than general: Cameron Street's staffing plan (earlier hiring cycles, retention stipends), Allison Hill's counselor recruitment, and Steelton Prep's enrollment growth plan are the concrete mechanisms, not an abstract commitment to "raise the bar."

*On Cameron Street:* expect direct questions about the six-position vacancy and its effect on students during the vacancy period. The Board should be able to state plainly that the network does not consider a full semester of long-term substitute coverage in 14 percent of a campus's teaching positions to be an acceptable staffing condition, and should be able to describe the hiring-cycle change concretely, including that two of the six positions remain unfilled going into the fall.

*On Allison Hill:* expect questions about counseling capacity and whether the 61 percent postsecondary-documentation rate would have been higher with a counselor in place for the full year. The Board should present the internal 68–70 percent projection candidly as context, rather than allowing the authorizer to interpret 61 percent as the network's ceiling.

*On Steelton Prep:* expect the most sustained questioning here, likely including a direct question about whether the network can sustain the campus at its current enrollment trajectory and facility cost. The Board should be prepared to walk through the option set in Section 9 and to indicate, if a decision has been reached by the Board before October, what the network intends to do; if no decision has been reached, the Board should be able to describe the decision timeline and process rather than leaving the impression that the deficit is unaddressed.

*On cash position:* 38 days of unrestricted cash is likely to draw a direct question about the network's short-term financial resilience. The Board should be prepared to connect this figure explicitly to the Steelton Prep deficit and the relief-funding cliff, and to describe the finance office's plan for rebuilding reserves, rather than treating the cash figure as a standalone concern disconnected from its causes.

*On turnover:* a network-wide rate of 31 percent, well above the network's historical average and above comparable-network benchmarks, is likely to prompt questions about instructional continuity and student impact. The Board should resist a single network-wide response and instead describe the distinct drivers at Cameron Street, Allison Hill, and Steelton Prep, consistent with Section 8.

**Governance actions the Board should consider before October.** Trustees may wish to take, or be prepared to describe as taken or in progress, the following before the renewal hearing: formal Board review and decision on at least a preliminary direction for the Steelton Prep facility question (Section 9); confirmation of the FY2027 hiring-cycle timeline for Cameron Street and the Allison Hill counselor search; and a Board-adopted plan, even if preliminary, for rebuilding cash reserves toward the network's 60-day policy target. Authorizers in Board renewal reviews generally give significant weight not only to current performance but to evidence that a governing board understands its own network's problems in specific terms and has an active process for addressing them. This report, and the trustee discussion it is intended to support at the August adoption meeting, is written to give the Board that specificity.

**Final note.** Every figure in this report reflects the network's actual, current operating condition as understood by network leadership at the time of writing. Where data remains in finalization — principally the Cameron Street campus-level proficiency results referenced in Section 4 — this report identifies that explicitly rather than presenting placeholder or projected figures as final. The Board should expect a short supplemental memo from the CEO's office before the August adoption meeting completing any sections marked as pending, so that the version submitted with the renewal packet in October is complete and fully sourced.
