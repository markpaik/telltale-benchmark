## Statement of Need

Sorrel Creek is a city of 31,240 residents in north-central Oklahoma built along the confluence of two waterways that have overtopped their banks twice in five years, in 2019 and again in 2023. These were not marginal events. Together they damaged 214 structures across the city, the large majority of them in or adjacent to the downtown core, and generated $18.6 million in insurance claims. For a community of this size, that figure represents a catastrophic and recurring drain on household wealth, business capital, and municipal tax base.

The pattern is repetitive-loss, not one-time bad luck. Properties damaged in 2019 were, in a substantial number of cases, damaged again in 2023, meaning the same households and business owners have absorbed the cost of flood recovery twice in half a decade, with FEMA and private insurers effectively subsidizing reconstruction in a floodplain that has already demonstrated it will flood again. The city's flood insurance claims history, its substantial-damage determinations following both events, and its current repetitive-loss property list — developed jointly by Public Works and the county assessor's office — document 34 structures that meet or exceed FEMA's repetitive-loss or severe repetitive-loss criteria. These 34 properties are the core target of this application.

The economic consequence of two floods in five years is visible on every block of downtown Sorrel Creek. Downtown commercial vacancy stands at 29 percent, nearly triple what city staff consider a healthy urban core vacancy rate. Sales tax receipts, the single largest source of discretionary municipal revenue, are down 8 percent from 2019 levels — not adjusted for inflation, meaning the real decline in taxable economic activity is larger still. Businesses that might otherwise expand or relocate into downtown Sorrel Creek decline to do so because ground-floor space floods, insurance is expensive or unavailable, and utilities have failed in both events, shutting down entire blocks for weeks at a time during cleanup and restoration.

This is not an abstract or evenly distributed hardship. Twenty-four percent of Sorrel Creek residents are Native American, and the city sits within a jurisdictional area requiring government-to-government consultation with a tribal environmental office on any project affecting certain parcels — two of which are included in this application's acquisition footprint. Nineteen percent of residents live below the federal poverty line, meaning flood damage falls disproportionately on households with the least capacity to self-finance repair, relocate, or absorb repeated insurance deductibles. Thirty-two percent of downtown businesses are minority-owned, and Main Street Association surveys conducted after the 2023 flood indicate these businesses are less likely to carry adequate flood coverage and more likely to have exhausted personal or family capital in the 2019 recovery, leaving less capacity to withstand a second loss.

The need, then, is threefold and interlocking: first, to permanently remove the highest-risk structures from the floodplain so that the same households and public dollars are not recycled through disaster recovery a third time; second, to restore downtown's physical capacity to convey and store floodwater so that the businesses that remain can operate, insure, and expand with confidence; and third, to do both in a manner that respects tribal consultation requirements, protects a low-income and minority-heavy business community, and rebuilds the sales tax base that funds basic city services for all 31,240 residents.

Sorrel Creek's Public Works Superintendent, Otis Brumley, has managed both recovery efforts with a department that has operated without a filled engineer position for fourteen months, relying on contract engineering support and mutual aid from county emergency management for technical review. That vacancy is disclosed in full in the Organizational Capacity section below, but it is relevant here as evidence of the need: the city's technical capacity has been stretched by the very disasters this application seeks to prevent from recurring.

## Project Description

This project has three physically and administratively linked components, each addressing a distinct point of failure exposed by the 2019 and 2023 floods.

**Component One: Acquisition and Demolition of 34 Repetitive-Loss Properties.** The city will use grant and match funds to complete voluntary, FEMA-consistent acquisition of the 34 structures identified on the repetitive-loss and severe repetitive-loss list, all located within the 100-year floodplain as mapped in the current effective Flood Insurance Rate Map. Acquisition will proceed on a willing-seller basis at fair market value, using pre-flood or current appraised value as required by program rules, followed by demolition and deed-restriction of the resulting parcels as permanent open space. Two of the 34 parcels lie within an area subject to tribal environmental office consultation; the city has already initiated informal contact with that office and will complete formal consultation, addressed further under Partner Roles, before any ground-disturbing activity occurs on those parcels. All acquisition and demolition work under this grant will be sequenced to follow, not precede, completion of environmental review — a sequencing commitment made necessary by the city's prior experience described under Prior Deobligation below.

**Component Two: Eleven Acres of Stormwater Detention with a Floodable Greenway.** The acquired and demolished parcels, together with several adjoining publicly owned lots, will be regraded and engineered into an eleven-acre detention complex designed to hold floodwater during high-water events and to function as a public greenway — walking paths, native plantings, and passive recreation space — during normal conditions. The detention design will be engineered to reduce peak flood elevations on the downtown blocks immediately downstream, directly protecting the commercial core that generates the city's sales tax base. The greenway design has been developed in consultation with the university engineering extension partner named below, using a floodable-park model already implemented in comparable Oklahoma communities.

**Component Three: Utility Elevation on Three Downtown Blocks.** Electrical, water, and select mechanical utilities serving the three downtown blocks that experienced the longest service outages in both 2019 and 2023 will be elevated above the base flood elevation, consistent with current building code and utility company specifications. This component directly targets the outage-driven business closures that Main Street Association members have identified as the single largest driver of tenant turnover and vacancy following each flood event, independent of direct structural damage.

Together, these three components form a single integrated strategy: remove the highest-risk structures permanently, replace them with water storage capacity that protects what remains, and harden the utility infrastructure that keeps downtown businesses operating through the next event rather than shuttered for weeks afterward.

The project will be implemented over the four-year grant period in three overlapping phases. Year one is dedicated to completing environmental review and tribal consultation, finalizing appraisals, and beginning voluntary acquisition offers. Years two and three cover the bulk of acquisition closings, demolition, and detention-basin construction, sequenced parcel-by-parcel to ensure no demolition activity outpaces environmental clearance. Utility elevation on the three downtown blocks will proceed in year three, coordinated with the Main Street Association to minimize disruption to operating businesses. Year four is reserved for greenway landscaping, trail construction, final punch-list work, and the first full cycle of post-construction monitoring against the objectives listed below.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

**Goal 1: Remove high-risk structures from the 100-year floodplain.**
Objective 1.1: Complete voluntary acquisition and demolition of at least 30 of the 34 identified repetitive-loss properties by the end of grant year three, with the remaining structures under active offer.
Objective 1.2: Record permanent open-space deed restrictions on 100 percent of acquired parcels within 90 days of demolition.

**Goal 2: Reduce recurring flood insurance losses.**
Objective 2.1: Achieve a minimum 40 percent reduction in the dollar value of flood insurance claims filed by downtown property owners in the first full flood season following construction completion, measured against the 2019–2023 average annual claims baseline.
Objective 2.2: Reduce the number of substantial-damage determinations issued in any future flood event affecting the project area by at least half, relative to the 2023 event.

**Goal 3: Restore downtown economic activity.**
Objective 3.1: Reduce downtown commercial vacancy from the current 29 percent to 18 percent or lower by the end of grant year four.
Objective 3.2: Return sales tax receipts from downtown businesses to at least 100 percent of 2019 levels, in real terms, by the end of grant year four.
Objective 3.3: Retain at least 90 percent of currently operating downtown businesses, with particular tracking of the minority-owned businesses that make up 32 percent of the downtown business base, through the construction period.

**Goal 4: Protect jobs during and after construction.**
Objective 4.1: Retain at least 200 downtown jobs, as counted in the city's current business license registry, through the full construction period with no net loss attributable to construction disruption.

**Goal 5: Complete construction in a manner consistent with tribal consultation and environmental compliance requirements.**
Objective 5.1: Complete formal consultation with the tribal environmental office on both affected parcels prior to any ground disturbance on those parcels, with written concurrence documented in the project file.
Objective 5.2: Complete 100 percent of demolition activity only after documented environmental review clearance for each individual parcel, with no exceptions.

## Evaluation Plan

Evaluation will track directly to the funder's five stated performance measures — structures removed from the 100-year floodplain, reduction in flood insurance claims, downtown occupancy, sales tax receipts, and jobs retained — supplemented by the consultation and compliance measures the city considers essential given its own history.

Grants Coordinator Renata Duchamp will serve as the project's data lead, compiling a semiannual performance report to the Trust that tracks each measure against the baseline and targets above. Structures-removed data will come directly from city demolition permits and recorded deed restrictions, cross-checked against the county assessor's parcel database, providing an auditable count independent of self-report. Flood insurance claims data will be obtained through the city's existing relationship with the state insurance department's catastrophe reporting unit and through direct outreach to the National Flood Insurance Program's claims office, comparing pre- and post-project claims for the defined project area. Downtown occupancy will be measured twice yearly through a physical survey of ground-floor commercial space conducted by Main Street Association staff, using the same methodology applied to generate the current 29 percent baseline so that year-over-year comparisons are valid. Sales tax receipts will be pulled from the city treasurer's monthly sales tax allocation reports, isolated to the downtown taxing zone, and compared against the documented 2019 baseline. Jobs retained will be tracked through the city business license registry, cross-referenced with Main Street Association's direct contact with member businesses, with particular attention to disaggregating outcomes for minority-owned businesses given their documented vulnerability.

Tribal consultation and environmental compliance will be evaluated through a simple, binary compliance checklist maintained by the Public Works Superintendent's office and reviewed by the city attorney before any demolition permit is issued: has consultation been completed and documented for this parcel, and has environmental review clearance been documented for this parcel. No demolition permit will be issued absent both. This checklist directly addresses the failure that produced the 2021 deobligation described below and will be made available to the Trust upon request at any point during the grant period.

The city will submit an interim evaluation report at the eighteen-month mark, coinciding with the mid-point of acquisition activity, and a comprehensive final evaluation report at grant closeout, comparing actual outcomes against every objective listed above. Both reports will be prepared by Renata Duchamp in coordination with City Manager Sundberg and will be shared with the Sorrel Creek City Council in open session, ensuring public accountability alongside funder accountability.

## Organizational Capacity

The City of Sorrel Creek brings direct, relevant, and hard-won experience to this project, having managed two federally declared flood disasters in the past five years. That experience includes strengths the city intends to build on and one significant weakness the city discloses openly below.

City Manager Ingrid Sundberg has overseen both disaster recovery efforts and will serve as executive sponsor for this grant, with authority to direct staff and resolve interdepartmental conflicts throughout the four-year project period. Emergency Manager Hector Villalpando, who coordinated FEMA public assistance and individual assistance applications following both floods, will serve as project co-lead, bringing direct working relationships with FEMA regional staff and the state emergency management agency into this mitigation-focused effort. Grants Coordinator Renata Duchamp will manage day-to-day grant administration, reporting, and financial tracking, and has managed the city's federal and state grant portfolio, including disaster recovery grants, for the past six years.

Public Works Superintendent Otis Brumley will lead the technical execution of acquisition, demolition, and detention-basin construction. The city discloses that the Public Works engineer position has been vacant for fourteen months. This vacancy has been managed through a standing contract with a regional civil engineering firm that has provided design review and construction oversight support for both flood recovery efforts and is already under contract for preliminary detention-basin design work for this project. The city has posted the engineer position twice in the past year without a successful hire, reflecting a regional shortage of municipal engineering talent common to smaller Oklahoma cities. Should the position remain unfilled, the city commits to expanding its contract engineering support to fully cover the technical oversight functions the position would otherwise perform, at a cost the city has built into the budget narrative below as a contingency line. The city views this as manageable risk rather than a barrier to successful project delivery, given the existing contract relationship's demonstrated performance through two prior disaster responses.

Mayor Franklin Standing Bear provides political leadership for the project and has publicly committed to bringing the local match bond issue to voters, discussed fully under Sustainability and the match contingency below.

**Prior Deobligation Disclosure.** The city discloses, as required, that FEMA deobligated $1.1 million of a 2021 hazard mitigation award after the city began demolition activity on a prior acquisition project before environmental review had cleared. This occurred because of a breakdown in internal sequencing: demolition permits were issued by Public Works before the Environmental Historic Preservation review had been documented as complete, a step that at the time depended on informal email confirmation rather than a formal sign-off process. The city has since implemented the binary compliance checklist described in the Evaluation Plan above, requiring documented, dated sign-off from both environmental review and, where applicable, tribal consultation before any demolition permit is issued, with the city attorney's office holding independent authority to halt permit issuance if that documentation is incomplete. This checklist has been in place since early 2024 and has governed all subsequent city demolition activity without incident. The city offers this disclosure not to minimize the seriousness of the 2021 error, which cost the city federal funding and delayed recovery for affected property owners, but to demonstrate that the underlying process failure has been identified and corrected, and that this application's sequencing commitments under Component One are a direct, structural response to that history rather than a general assurance.

## Sustainability

The physical improvements proposed under this grant are, by design, low-maintenance and structurally durable, but the city addresses sustainability at three levels: the greenway's long-term maintenance, the broader detention and utility infrastructure, and the fiscal sustainability of the project's local match.

**Greenway Maintenance.** The eleven-acre detention greenway will be maintained by the city's Parks and Recreation division under a maintenance plan developed jointly with the university engineering extension partner, ensuring that landscaping, trail surfaces, and detention-basin grading are maintained in a manner consistent with the basin's flood-storage function rather than being treated as ordinary parkland. The city will fund ongoing greenway maintenance through its existing Parks and Recreation operating budget, supplemented by a dedicated stormwater utility fee the city council adopted in 2024 specifically to fund stormwater infrastructure operations and maintenance citywide. This fee, currently generating approximately $310,000 annually, is projected to be more than sufficient to cover the incremental maintenance cost of the new detention greenway, estimated by the engineering extension partner at approximately $45,000 per year, once the basin is complete and the fee's revenue base is fully phased in during grant year four. The city commits to a minimum twenty-year maintenance horizon for the detention basin's flood-storage function, formalized through a council resolution to be adopted prior to grant execution.

**Utility and Acquisition Sustainability.** Elevated utilities require no ongoing maintenance beyond standard utility company practice already budgeted in existing rate structures. Acquired and demolished parcels will carry permanent deed restrictions prohibiting future structural development, removing them from future maintenance or flood-risk liability entirely and converting a recurring disaster-recovery cost into a one-time capital expenditure.

**Fiscal Sustainability of the Project as a Whole.** Beyond direct infrastructure maintenance, this project is designed to be fiscally self-reinforcing: by reducing downtown vacancy and restoring sales tax receipts to 2019 levels, the project is projected to generate approximately $340,000 in additional annual sales tax revenue by grant year four, based on Finance Department projections tied to Objective 3.2. The city intends to dedicate a portion of this recovered revenue to a capital reserve fund supporting future flood mitigation work, reducing the city's dependence on future federal mitigation grants for the next phase of repetitive-loss property acquisition beyond the 34 parcels covered here.

**Match Contingency.** The city discloses that $800,000 of the required local match depends on a general obligation bond issue scheduled to go before Sorrel Creek voters in September 2026, one month after this application's August 22 deadline. Mayor Franklin Standing Bear has publicly committed to the bond initiative and has secured city council authorization to place the measure on the ballot; the measure has been drafted, reviewed by bond counsel, and is on track for certification to the county election board in early August, ahead of the statutory filing deadline for a September election. The city offers the following contingency plan to address the timing gap between application submission and the vote. First, the city requests that the Trust structure any award with a phased disbursement schedule under which grant funds are drawn down against completed acquisition and design milestones in year one, activity that requires match funds well below the full $800,000 and can be covered by approximately $180,000 in city general fund reserves the council has already earmarked as bridge match pending bond passage. Second, should the September bond measure fail, the city commits to returning to voters with a revised measure in the next available election cycle, while simultaneously pursuing a combination of Community Development Block Grant Disaster Recovery funds and a private placement loan through the Oklahoma Water Resources Board's infrastructure financing program, both of which the city has preliminarily discussed with the relevant agencies as fallback match sources. Third, the city's finance department has modeled a scaled-back version of Component Three, the utility elevation work, as the most severable element of the project should match funding fall short, allowing full delivery of the higher-priority acquisition and detention components even in a reduced-match scenario. The city believes the bond measure is likely to pass, given strong public support documented in a spring 2026 community survey in which 68 percent of respondents rated flood mitigation as a top-three municipal priority, but provides this contingency plan in full recognition that certainty cannot be guaranteed a month ahead of an election.

## Budget Narrative

**Total Project Cost:** $4,000,000
**Requested from Tallgrass Civic Trust:** $3,200,000
**Local Match:** $800,000 (20 percent)

**Component One — Acquisition and Demolition (34 properties): $2,050,000**
This includes appraisal and title work for all 34 parcels ($120,000), acquisition purchase costs based on current assessed and comparable-sale values ($1,650,000), demolition and site clearing ($230,000), and deed restriction recording and legal costs ($50,000). Acquisition costs are based on the county assessor's current valuations for the 34 identified parcels, adjusted for post-flood condition, and reviewed by the city's contract engineering firm for reasonableness.

**Component Two — Eleven-Acre Detention Basin and Floodable Greenway: $1,150,000**
This includes final engineering design, building on preliminary work already funded by the university engineering extension partnership ($140,000), site grading and detention-basin construction ($720,000), trail construction, native plantings, and greenway amenities ($210,000), and construction management and inspection ($80,000).

**Component Three — Utility Elevation, Three Downtown Blocks: $580,000**
This includes electrical infrastructure elevation in coordination with the regional utility provider ($320,000), water and mechanical utility elevation ($180,000), and construction coordination with affected downtown businesses through the Main Street Association ($80,000), covering temporary signage, customer communication, and minor relocation logistics during construction.

**Project Administration and Evaluation: $170,000**
This includes Grants Coordinator staff time allocated to this project across four years ($90,000), semiannual and final evaluation reporting, including data collection support from Main Street Association and the county assessor's office ($40,000), tribal consultation coordination costs, including any required cultural resource survey work on the two affected parcels ($30,000), and audit and financial compliance costs ($10,000).

**Contingency: $50,000**
Reserved specifically to cover potential expansion of contract engineering support in the event the Public Works engineer position remains unfilled, as described under Organizational Capacity.

**Local Match Sources ($800,000 total):**
- General obligation bond proceeds (pending September 2026 voter approval): $620,000
- City general fund bridge reserve (available immediately, applied to year-one activity pending bond passage): $180,000

The city requests that disbursement of grant funds follow a milestone-based schedule tied to completed acquisition closings, design deliverables, and construction phases rather than a flat quarterly draw, both to align with the phased local match availability described under Sustainability and to allow the Trust visibility into concrete deliverables — parcels acquired, basin acreage graded, utility blocks completed — at each disbursement point. The city's Finance Department, under Grants Coordinator Duchamp's direct oversight, will maintain project accounting segregated from other city funds, consistent with the Trust's audit requirements, and will provide quarterly financial reports alongside the semiannual performance reports described in the Evaluation Plan.

## Partner Roles

Four partners join the City of Sorrel Creek in executing this project, each with a distinct and necessary role.

**County Emergency Management** will continue the coordination role it has played through both prior flood events, providing mutual-aid technical support to Emergency Manager Villalpando during construction, assisting with public communication during any weather events that occur mid-construction, and serving as a secondary reviewer on the city's environmental and floodplain compliance checklist to add an additional layer of oversight given the history disclosed above.

**The Tribal Environmental Office** holds formal consultation authority over two of the 34 acquisition parcels. The city has initiated preliminary contact and will complete formal government-to-government consultation, including any required cultural resource review, before ground disturbance begins on either parcel, as committed under Objective 5.1. The tribal environmental office's letter of commitment, included as Attachment E, confirms its willingness to engage in this process on the timeline the project requires and identifies a primary point of contact for ongoing coordination through Grants Coordinator Duchamp.

**The University Engineering Extension** has already provided preliminary hydrology and grading concept work for the eleven-acre detention basin, reflected in Attachment D, and will continue as the project's technical design partner through final engineering, construction oversight support alongside the city's contract engineering firm, and development of the long-term greenway maintenance plan described under Sustainability. The extension's involvement also provides a cost-efficient supplement to the city's fourteen-month engineer vacancy, discussed candidly under Organizational Capacity.

**The Main Street Association** will lead business-relocation coordination for any downtown tenant affected by utility elevation construction under Component Three, maintain the twice-yearly occupancy survey used to track Objective 3.1, and serve as the primary communication channel to the 32 percent of downtown businesses that are minority-owned, ensuring outreach materials and relocation assistance reach that population directly rather than through general public notice alone.
