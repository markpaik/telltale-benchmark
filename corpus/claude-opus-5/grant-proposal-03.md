## Statement of Need

Fort Smith and the surrounding Arkansas River Valley have a manufacturing base that is hiring and a working-age population that cannot reach it. Both halves of that sentence are documented, and the gap between them is widening.

On the demand side, the Consortium's 34 member manufacturers reported 310 unfilled production openings in our January employer census, to which 29 of 34 members responded. Forty-one percent of those openings had been posted more than 90 days. Nine members reported declining or deferring customer orders in the past eighteen months for staffing reasons alone. The openings are concentrated in exactly the three occupations we train for: CNC machining (94 openings), industrial maintenance and mechatronics (118), and welding and fabrication (98). Median posted starting wage across the three is $21.40 per hour, with second- and third-shift differentials of $1.50 to $2.75. These are not marginal jobs. They carry benefits at 27 of 34 member firms from day one or after 60 days, and they sit at the entry point of career ladders that reach $34 per hour for journey-level maintenance technicians.

On the supply side, 41 percent of Sebastian County adults hold no postsecondary credential of any kind — no associate degree, no certificate, no industry credential. That is roughly 26,000 working-age adults in the county alone, before counting Crawford, Franklin, and Logan counties, from which we draw about a third of our enrollment. This population is not idle. Most of it is employed in retail, food service, warehousing, and healthcare support at wages between $12 and $16 per hour. The barrier is not willingness; it is that the on-ramp to a $21 job requires twelve to eighteen weeks out of the full-time labor market, and almost no one in that wage band can absorb that.

Two structural barriers compound the problem, and our own data now demonstrates their weight. Transportation: the industrial corridors where our members operate — Chaffee Crossing, the Fort Smith Industrial Park, the Van Buren riverfront — are poorly served by fixed-route transit, and no route reaches them before the 6:00 a.m. first-shift start. In our 2024 exit interviews, 34 percent of non-completers cited transportation as a primary or contributing reason for withdrawal. Childcare: licensed capacity in Sebastian County covers an estimated 38 percent of children under five with all available parents in the labor force, and virtually none of it opens before 6:00 a.m. or extends past 6:00 p.m. Twenty-two percent of our 2024 non-completers were custodial parents citing childcare.

A third population is both the largest untapped source of labor in the region and the one our current model serves worst. Approximately 1,300 people return to the River Valley each year from Arkansas Division of Correction custody. Arkansas's three-year return-to-custody rate is roughly 46 percent, and the single most consistent predictor of not returning is stable employment above subsistence wage. We have enrolled 71 people with felony records since 2017. They completed at rates comparable to the general cohort (78 percent versus 81 percent) but placed at 90 days at only 38 percent — half the rate of everyone else. That is not a training failure. It is a failure of case management, employer policy, and the absence of anyone whose job it was to work the gap between a completed credential and a hiring manager's background screen.

**Why the Consortium's own record is part of the need.** We have trained 1,240 people since 2017. Ninety-day placement was 79 percent in program year 2022. It fell to 68 percent in 2023 and to 58 percent in 2024. Interim figures show partial recovery — 63 percent in 2025 and 66 percent through June 30, 2026 — but we are still eleven to thirteen points below where we were, at a moment when member demand is the highest it has been in the Consortium's history. Rosa Delgadillo, our Employer Engagement Manager, led a placement decomposition with analyst Tomasz Wierzbicki in late 2025. We present its findings here rather than in an appendix, because the foundation is entitled to know whether it is funding a program that works, and because our project design is a direct response to what the decomposition found.

The 21-point decline from 2022 to 2024 breaks down as follows:

| Driver | Points | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Closure of Talbert Cordage and displaced-worker competition | 8 | Talbert hired 27% of our 2022 completers (38 of 141). Its 2023 closure eliminated those slots and released 210 experienced production workers into the local market, who out-competed entry-level completers for roughly two hiring cycles. |
| Change in placement definition | 4 | Beginning in PY2023 we counted all completers rather than only those instructors designated "job-ready," which had excluded roughly one in eight. The correction was right; it is not improvement or decline, it is measurement. |
| Support-service gap in a shifted referral population | 5 | Workforce center referrals rose from 18% to 44% of enrollment across 2023–2024 without corresponding case management. This subgroup completed at 71% and placed at 49%. |
| Industrial maintenance instructor vacancy | 2 | The position was open seven months in 2024; two cohorts were taught by adjuncts. IM placement fell to 47% against 66% for welding. |
| Member hiring freezes | 2 | Two members paused headcount pending capital decisions in 2024, removing an estimated 14 hires. |
| **Total** | **21** | |

Four of these five drivers are addressable by program design. One — the Talbert closure — is not, but its lesson is. Talbert Cordage was 14 percent of employer cash support and 27 percent of hiring in a single year. We built a pipeline with a single point of failure and did not notice until it failed. The Talbert closure also cost us its $250,000 annual match commitment, which is the immediate reason this application exists and the reason we address concentration risk explicitly in Sustainability below.

The need, stated plainly: the region has 310 open jobs paying a living wage, 26,000 adults without a credential to reach them, 1,300 people a year returning from custody whom almost no one is serving, and a training provider whose placement performance degraded exactly when the barriers facing its students changed and its support model did not. This proposal funds the support model.

## Project Description

Valley Works will train 480 people over three years in CNC machining, industrial maintenance, and welding, place at least 294 of them in unsubsidized manufacturing employment within 90 days of completion, and hold 70 percent of them with their placing employer at twelve months. It does this by adding four things our current model lacks: paid work experience, wraparound support that removes the barriers our own exit data identifies, dedicated navigation, and a reentry cohort designed with the people who actually know how to serve it.

### Enrollment structure

We will enroll 160 participants per year in twelve cohorts:

| Track | Length | Hours | Cohorts/yr | Seats/cohort | Annual enrollment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Welding & Fabrication | 12 weeks | 360 | 5 | 14 | 70 |
| CNC Machining | 16 weeks | 480 | 4 | 12 | 48 |
| Industrial Maintenance & Mechatronics | 18 weeks | 540 | 3 | 14 | 42 |
| | | | **12** | | **160** |

Thirty seats per year (90 over three years) are reserved for the reentry cohort, distributed across all three tracks rather than segregated into a separate class. Participants with records train alongside everyone else; what differs is the support and employer-side work behind them, not the instruction.

### Instruction and credentials

Each track ends in stacked, portable industry credentials, not a certificate of attendance:

- **Welding:** AWS D1.1 3G and 4G qualification in SMAW and GMAW, blueprint reading, OSHA-10 General Industry.
- **CNC Machining:** NIMS Level I in Measurement/Materials/Safety, Job Planning & Benchwork, Turning Operations, and Milling Operations; Mastercam fundamentals; OSHA-10.
- **Industrial Maintenance:** MSSC Certified Production Technician, Siemens Mechatronic Systems Level 1, industrial electrical fundamentals, hydraulics and pneumatics, PLC basics, OSHA-10.

All three tracks articulate to the University of Arkansas at Fort Smith. Under the articulation agreement executed with UAFS's College of Applied Science and Technology, completers receive credit toward the A.A.S. in Manufacturing Technology: 8 credit hours for welding, 9 for CNC machining, and 12 for industrial maintenance. This matters for two reasons. It converts a terminal short-term credential into a first step, and it gives participants a reason to stay in the region rather than treating the credential as a ticket out.

### Paid work experience

Classroom instruction is followed by a 200-hour structured work experience at a member firm, at the firm's own production wage (currently $18.00 to $21.50 per hour depending on track and employer). The member firm is the employer of record from day one; the participant is on its payroll, in its safety program, under its supervision. The Consortium reimburses the employer for 50 percent of the first 120 hours as a hiring-risk offset. The employer bears the remaining hours in full.

This design does three things. It pays participants during the period when our exit data says they leave. It gives the employer 200 hours of observed performance before a hiring decision, which is worth far more than an interview. And it converts employer wage expenditure into documented match, which is how we are financing half of this project.

Work experience placements are brokered by a full-time Work Experience Coordinator against employer commitments secured in advance by Rosa Delgadillo. We are not enrolling participants into training and hoping a slot appears. Each cohort's work experience slots are committed in writing before the cohort's first day of class.

### Support services

Support is not an amenity here; it is the intervention. Based on 2024 exit interviews:

- **Transportation:** $50 per week during instruction, delivered as a fuel card or Fort Smith Transit pass at the participant's choice, plus a one-time $250 vehicle-repair allowance available once per participant.
- **Childcare:** up to $150 per week paid directly to a licensed provider, for participants with children under 13 and no other daytime care. Budgeted for an estimated 120 participants over three years based on current intake demographics.
- **Tools and PPE:** boots, safety glasses, hood or FR clothing, and a starter tool set the participant keeps. Average $450.
- **Credential testing fees:** paid in full, including one retest.
- **Emergency fund:** up to $500 per participant, single approval by the Training Director, for the utility shutoff or eviction notice that otherwise ends a training career in week seven.

### Navigation and case management

Two full-time Career Navigators carry an active caseload of approximately 65 each. Navigators run intake and barrier assessment before enrollment, meet each participant biweekly during instruction, conduct the 30-, 90-, and 180-day follow-ups, and own the retention relationship through twelve months. Navigator contact is documented in our case management system and is itself an evaluated variable — we will test whether contact frequency predicts retention, because if it does not, we should spend the money differently.

For the reentry cohort, case management is subcontracted to our reentry nonprofit partner, whose staff already carry these participants for housing, supervision compliance, treatment, and transportation. Duplicating that infrastructure would be wasteful and worse. The subcontract funds 0.6 FTE of dedicated case management plus a reentry employment specialist's time, with a data-sharing agreement and a shared case conference every two weeks with our navigators.

### Employer-side work on fair-chance hiring

The 38 percent placement rate for participants with records is not fixed by better training. Twelve of our 34 members currently interview candidates with felony convictions; six have written fair-chance policies. Delgadillo's target is 22 members interviewing and 14 with written policy by the end of Year 3. The tools are concrete: the Arkansas Federal Bonding Program, the Work Opportunity Tax Credit, a standardized individualized-assessment template developed with member HR leaders, and — most persuasive in our experience — a member firm that has already done it presenting to one that has not.

### Referral and intake redesign

The support-service gap identified in the decomposition was partly a referral problem. Under a revised memorandum with the state workforce center, referrals will move through a joint intake protocol: a structured barrier assessment, a shop-floor aptitude screen, and an employer expectations session before enrollment, rather than volume-based referral to fill seats. This is not a screen for advantage; the reentry cohort is proof that we are not selecting for ease. It is a screen for the right track, the right start date, and the right support package.

## Evaluation Plan

### Metric definitions

The foundation requires six measures. We define each here so that our reports and the foundation's expectations do not diverge in Year 2.

- **Credential attainment:** a third-party industry credential verified in the issuing body's registry (NIMS, AWS, MSSC, Siemens, OSHA). Denominator is completers. Reported separately for one credential and two or more.
- **Placement at 90 / 180 days:** unsubsidized employment of 30 or more hours per week with any employer, confirmed in Arkansas Division of Workforce Services UI wage records or by signed employer confirmation, within 90 or 180 calendar days of program completion. Denominator is completers. We will also report against all enrollees, because the completer denominator flatters every workforce program including ours.
- **Median wage at placement / 12 months:** hourly wage at the placement job, and quarterly UI earnings annualized at the fourth quarter following placement. Medians, not means, with interquartile range reported.
- **Employer retention:** employed by the same employer 12 months after placement. Reported alongside sector retention (any manufacturing employer) because a completer who leaves Member A for a $3 raise at Member B is a program success and an employer-retention failure, and the two numbers together say more than either alone.
- **Cost per placement:** total project expenditure for the period divided by 90-day placements in that period, reported three ways — foundation funds only, all project funds, and marginal cost excluding one-time startup.

### Data systems and verification

Participant records live in our Apricot case management system, which we migrated to in 2025 specifically to fix the follow-up data quality problems the 2024 decomposition exposed. Wage outcomes come from a quarterly UI wage record match executed under an executed data-sharing agreement with the Arkansas Division of Workforce Services, supplemented by employer confirmation forms for participants who fall outside the match (out-of-state employment, self-employment, contract work). Credential attainment is verified against issuing registries quarterly, not self-reported. Tomasz Wierzbicki, at 0.5 FTE on this project, owns the data pipeline and reconciles all three sources before any figure leaves the building.

### Comparison and attribution

We will not claim causal impact from a single-arm program. We will report three comparisons:

1. **Historical baseline.** Our own 2017–2026 cohorts, 1,240 participants, on the corrected placement definition applied retroactively where records permit.
2. **Matched comparison group.** Workforce center applicants who were assessed as track-eligible but did not enroll (scheduling, capacity, or choice), matched on age, prior credential, referral source, and prior four-quarter UI earnings. Estimated pool of 200 to 260 over three years. This is a quasi-experimental design with known selection limitations, which we will state in every report rather than in a footnote.
3. **Internal subgroup contrasts.** Reentry versus general cohort; workforce center referral versus employer or self-referral; participants receiving childcare support versus not; work experience completed versus not.

### External evaluation

We have budgeted $54,000 over three years for an independent evaluator, to be selected by competitive RFP within 60 days of award, with a preference for an Arkansas-based evaluator with workforce-outcome experience. Scope: validate our definitions and data pipeline in Year 1; produce interim reports at the end of Years 1 and 2; produce a summative report in Year 3 addressing whether Valley Works recovered placement performance, whether the support package was the operative ingredient, and what it cost per outcome. The evaluator reports to the board's Program Committee, not to program staff.

### Continuous improvement

A monthly dashboard goes to Ostrander, Whitfield, and Delgadillo covering enrollment against plan, attendance, work experience slots committed versus filled, and 30-day contact completion. A quarterly cohort review with the same group plus Wierzbicki examines completed cohorts against target.

We are adopting a **placement variance protocol**: if any cohort's 90-day placement falls more than 8 points below target, Whitfield and Delgadillo produce a root-cause memo within 30 days identifying whether the cause is instructional, employer-side, participant-support, or market, with a corrective action and an owner. The memo goes to the board Program Committee and is included verbatim in the next foundation report. The reason we are writing this protocol into the proposal is that in 2023 our placement rate fell eleven points and no one wrote anything down for fourteen months. That will not recur.

### Reporting to the foundation

Quarterly narrative and data file within 30 days of quarter end. Annual report within 60 days of each project year, with audited financials when available. One structural honesty note: twelve-month wage and retention outcomes for Year 3 completers do not exist until twelve to fifteen months after the grant period closes. We commit to a final outcomes true-up report delivered by December 31, 2031, funded from Consortium operating funds at no cost to the foundation, covering 12-month wage and retention for all Year 3 completers.

## Sustainability

The foundation's question — how does this continue when member dues cover 30 percent of operating cost — is the right question, and we do not have a costless answer. Here is the honest one.

**First, what went wrong.** Talbert Cordage was 14 percent of our employer cash and 27 percent of our hiring in 2022. When it closed, we lost a $250,000 annual match commitment and a quarter of our placement capacity simultaneously. The lesson is not that we need more money; it is that we built concentration risk and did not measure it. In November 2025 the board adopted a written concentration policy: no single member may exceed 10 percent of total employer-derived revenue, and Wierzbicki reports the concentration ratio to the Finance Committee quarterly. As of the last report, the largest single member accounts for 7.8 percent.

**Second, changing what members pay for.** Flat dues are a weak instrument: they are priced on membership, not value, and they are the first line cut in a bad quarter. On January 14 the board approved, by an 11–2 vote and contingent on this award, a restructured member revenue model with three components:

1. **Tiered dues** by employee count, replacing the current largely flat schedule. Modeled increase: $780,000 to $918,000 annually by Year 3.
2. **A dedicated Pipeline Assessment** of $138,000 per year across the 34 members, restricted to Valley Works and pledged for the full three years. Signed commitments from 31 of 34 members are in the appendix; the three outstanding are in process.
3. **A per-hire placement fee** of $1,500 per completer hired, phased in at $750 in Year 2 and $1,500 in Year 3. At 100 placements per year this yields $150,000 annually. This is the most important structural change in the proposal: it prices our product at the moment the member receives value, and it means that when placement performance improves, our revenue improves. In 2024, when placement fell 21 points, our member revenue did not move at all. That is a broken incentive and we are fixing it.

**Third, growing earned revenue.** Fee-for-service incumbent-worker training carries a 22 percent contribution margin and is our most reliable non-grant revenue. Target: $884,000 to $1,350,000 by Year 3, driven by robotics and automation content that member capital investment is creating demand for. Delgadillo's employer engagement time is split accordingly, and this growth is what allows a $128,000 annual internal transfer to support Valley Works after the grant.

**Fourth, public funding.** We are on the Arkansas Eligible Training Provider List for all three tracks. WIOA Individual Training Account and On-the-Job Training reimbursements through the workforce center, plus Arkansas Office of Skills Development grants, contribute $486,000 of match during the project and are projected at $310,000 annually thereafter. This is the least controllable revenue stream in the plan and we do not treat it as reliable; it is the first place a shortfall would appear.

**The facility question.** Our lease expires December 31, 2028 — six months into Year 2. The board constituted a Facility Committee in December, chaired by Sung-Min Park, with three options under evaluation: exercise the renewal option at market rate (estimated 18 to 26 percent increase); purchase a building, for which two members have indicated willingness to underwrite a portion; or co-locate a portion of instruction at UAFS's technology facilities under an expanded partnership. The committee will present a recommendation to the full board by September 30, 2027 and execute by June 30, 2028, so that no cohort start date is affected. Contingency space commitments from two member firms cover welding and CNC instruction for one term if a transition slips.

**The number we will not overstate.** At the end of three years, Valley Works will not be fully self-sustaining from earned revenue, and any applicant who tells you otherwise is describing a program with weaker support services or a wealthier region. Our modeled steady state at 160 participants per year is approximately $1,150,000 annually:

| Source | Annual | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Employer-paid work experience wages | $234,000 | 20% |
| Public workforce funds (WIOA, state) | $310,000 | 27% |
| Per-hire placement fees | $150,000 | 13% |
| Member Pipeline Assessment (continued) | $138,000 | 12% |
| Incumbent-worker training margin transfer | $128,000 | 11% |
| Philanthropy | $190,000 | 17% |

That is 56 percent member and earned revenue, 27 percent public, 17 percent philanthropic. Our commitment is to reach that mix by Year 3 and to reduce the philanthropic share by roughly two points per year thereafter. We are also targeting a 2 percent annual operating surplus to move reserves from 2.4 to 4.0 months by the end of the grant period, which is what would have absorbed the Talbert shock without an emergency appeal.

### Cost per placement

Blended all-funds cost per 90-day placement is $12,245, declining from $13,661 in Year 1 to $11,241 in Year 3 as fixed costs spread across a rising placement count. Foundation-only cost per placement is $6,122. Marginal cost at steady state, excluding startup, is approximately $9,800.

This is higher than our 2022 figure of roughly $7,400 per placement. The difference — about $4,800 — is precisely the paid work experience, the stipends, and the navigation that our 2024 data says were missing. We are asking the foundation to pay more per placement in exchange for a placement rate that recovers to 80 percent, a reentry cohort that reaches 66 percent rather than 38 percent, and twelve-month retention we can actually verify.

Against outcomes: median placement wage of $21.50 against an estimated $13.50 median for Sebastian County adults without a postsecondary credential produces roughly $16,600 in additional annual earnings per placed participant. The full investment is recovered in incremental participant earnings in approximately nine months, and the participant keeps earning it for a career.
