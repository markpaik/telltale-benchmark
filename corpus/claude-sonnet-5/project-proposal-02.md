## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Alvarado Family Markets stands at an inflection point. Same-store sales have declined for six consecutive quarters, most recently by 3.1 percent, while discount competitors have opened eleven new stores inside our trade areas over the past eighteen months. Doing nothing is not a neutral option; it is a decision to continue ceding share in a shrinking window of time.

We are proposing a $62.3 million, 30-month initiative with three linked components:

1. **Format conversion** of eleven existing stores to a smaller, prepared-foods-forward layout designed to compete on convenience and meal-solution value rather than price alone.
2. **Distribution consolidation**, closing the Porterville and Lindsay distribution points and moving to a single 240,000-square-foot facility in Goshen.
3. **Financing** through a $40 million expansion of our existing revolving credit facility and a $22.3 million sale-leaseback of four owned store properties.

This is not the 2024 prepared-foods pilot repeated at larger scale. That pilot lost $2.4 million in fourteen months, and we take full responsibility for the design flaws that produced that loss. Sections III and IV of this proposal detail specifically what went wrong and how this plan corrects it. We would not bring this proposal back to the board without confidence that the underlying model — not merely the ambition — has changed.

We understand the board's concerns, including those raised previously by Chair Alvarado and Mr. Alvarado regarding the Porterville facility. We address those concerns directly in Section VI and have built a modified transition schedule and severance structure in response to them.

**The specific action requested of the board on May 21** is authorization to: (1) approve capital expenditure of $62.3 million as outlined in Section V; (2) approve the financing structure described in Section V, including board authorization for Mr. Renner to execute the revolver expansion and sale-leaseback agreements; (3) approve the 30-month store conversion and distribution consolidation schedule in Section VI; and (4) approve the staffing plan in Section VII, including the associated workforce transition and severance commitments. A resolution for each element is provided in Section X for the board's vote.

## I. STATE OF THE BUSINESS

Alvarado Family Markets operates 34 stores across Tulare, Fresno, and Kern counties from our Visalia headquarters, employing 4,100 people and generating $538 million in sales last year. We remain the largest independent grocery operator in the Central Valley south of Fresno proper, and our brand carries three generations of trust with customers who have shopped with us since before some of our current store managers were born.

That trust is not, by itself, sufficient protection against the structural pressure we now face.

**Same-store sales trend.** Comparable-store sales have declined for six consecutive quarters:

| Quarter | Same-Store Sales Change |
|---|---|
| Q2 2025 (two years ago) | -0.4% |
| Q3 2025 | -0.9% |
| Q4 2025 | -1.6% |
| Q1 2025 (this cycle) | -2.0% |
| Q2 2025 | -2.4% |
| Q1 2026 (most recent) | -3.1% |

The trend is not merely negative; it is accelerating. A single bad quarter can be explained by weather, a competitor promotion, or a calendar shift. Six consecutive quarters of worsening decline describes a structural erosion of our customer base and basket size.

**Competitive intrusion.** Two discount chains — one hard-discount format, one extreme-value format — have opened eleven stores combined inside our existing trade areas since early last year, with at least four more sites under permit review in Tulare and Kern counties. These operators compete on shelf-stable center-store pricing where our cost structure, built around full-service perishables and a broader assortment, cannot match them unit-for-unit. Every dollar of center-store commodity volume we lose to these entrants is a dollar we are unlikely to recover through price competition alone. We do not have the scale to win a pricing war against national discount networks.

**Where we can still win.** What discount-format competitors do not offer, and structurally cannot offer within their real estate and labor models, is fresh prepared food, made-to-order deli and hot bar programs, and the kind of meal-solution convenience that increasingly drives trip frequency for time-constrained households. Our own transaction data shows that customers who purchase prepared foods visit 1.7 times more often per month and carry a 22 percent higher basket size on non-prepared-food purchases in the same trip, compared to customers who do not purchase prepared foods. This is the commercial logic behind the proposal: we cannot out-discount the discounters, but we can out-convenience them, and convenience is where our remaining structural advantages — full-service departments, established real estate in dense population centers, and workforce experience in fresh categories — actually apply.

**Why now.** Three factors converge to make this the right moment, and delay costly:

- The Goshen facility lease option, on which the entire distribution consolidation depends, **expires August 1, 2026**. If we do not exercise it before that date, we lose access to the only facility in our footprint that meets our square footage and dock-door requirements at an economically viable lease rate. The next-best alternative identified by Mr. Achterberg's team is 40 percent more expensive per square foot and would add an estimated $3.1 million annually to occupancy cost.
- Our UFCW contract, which governs the majority of our distribution and store workforce, does not reopen until 2027. Structural workforce changes made now, ahead of the reopener, allow us to negotiate the 2027 contract from a position where the transition is already substantially complete rather than negotiating changes and implementation simultaneously.
- Competitive intrusion is accelerating, not stabilizing. The four additional sites under permit review suggest this is the early phase of a longer buildout, not an isolated event. Every quarter we delay a response is a quarter in which trade-area customers form new shopping habits that become harder to reverse.

## II. WHAT WE ARE PROPOSING

We propose to convert eleven stores from our current full-size, full-assortment format to a smaller prepared-foods-forward format, reducing selling square footage per store by an average of 28 percent while expanding prepared-foods, deli, bakery, and hot-bar square footage by an average of 140 percent. Center-store dry grocery and general merchandise assortment will be reduced to the highest-velocity SKUs, with the freed square footage and freed capital redirected toward fresh, prepared, and grab-and-go categories.

This is a repositioning of eleven stores away from head-to-head competition with discount-format center-store pricing and toward the meal-solution and convenience trip occasions where we retain a structural advantage.

The eleven stores were selected using three criteria developed jointly by Ms. Cepeda's and Mr. Achterberg's teams: (1) proximity to at least one new discount-chain opening within the trade area, (2) above-median existing prepared-foods attachment rate, indicating the customer base is already receptive to the category, and (3) sufficient population density within a two-mile radius to support the higher trip-frequency model the format depends on. Store-by-store selection detail is provided as Appendix A, distributed separately to board members ahead of this meeting.

To support this store-level change, distribution must also change. The current two-facility structure at Porterville and Lindsay was built for a broader, slower-turning dry-grocery assortment. It is not configured for the higher-frequency, shorter-shelf-life replenishment cycle that a prepared-foods-forward store network requires — deli and bakery inputs, fresh proteins for hot-bar programs, and grab-and-go components need same-day or next-day cross-dock capability that neither existing facility was built to provide efficiently. Consolidating into a single, modern 240,000-square-foot facility in Goshen, centrally located within our existing store footprint, allows us to run the shorter, more frequent replenishment cycles the new format requires while eliminating duplicate fixed costs across two aging facilities.

## III. THE 2024 PILOT: WHAT WENT WRONG

The board approved a prepared-foods pilot at three stores in early 2024. It was pulled after fourteen months having lost $2.4 million. We have conducted a full post-mortem, led jointly by Ms. Cepeda and Mr. Baek, and we present it here without euphemism because the credibility of this proposal depends on the board's confidence that we understand why the pilot failed.

**1. The pilot added prepared-foods square footage without reducing anything else.** We treated prepared foods as an addition to the existing store, not a repositioning of it. This meant new capital, new labor, and new inventory risk layered on top of an unchanged cost base, with no offsetting reduction anywhere in the store. The current proposal explicitly reduces center-store footprint and assortment as prepared foods expands; it is a reallocation of capital and labor, not an addition to them.

**2. Staffing was drawn from existing store labor without dedicated management.** We used existing grocery and deli staff, cross-trained on the fly, without a dedicated kitchen manager accountable for food cost, waste, and execution quality. Food cost variance in the pilot stores averaged 340 basis points worse than budget, and mystery-shop quality scores were 30 percent below our full-service flagship locations. Nobody owned the outcome. The current proposal creates a dedicated kitchen manager position in every converted store — nineteen new positions total across the eleven-store conversion — with food cost and waste explicitly in their performance metrics, reporting through Mr. Baek's store operations structure.

**3. Supply chain was not adapted to support the format.** Prepared-foods inputs were routed through the existing Porterville and Lindsay distribution network on the same replenishment cadence as dry grocery — twice weekly. This produced both stockouts on fast-moving fresh components and excess waste on slower-moving ones, because the cadence didn't match the category. Waste as a percentage of prepared-foods sales in the pilot averaged 11.2 percent against an industry benchmark of 6 to 7 percent. The Goshen consolidation is designed specifically to correct this, with daily cross-dock capability for fresh and prepared-foods inputs built into the facility design from the outset rather than retrofitted.

**4. The pilot never reached the trip-frequency threshold needed for the category to earn its space.** Because it was three stores added to a company-wide marketing and merchandising calendar built around center-store promotion, the pilot stores never received dedicated marketing support to build trip frequency around the new category. Customers didn't know to come for it. The current proposal includes a dedicated marketing and promotional budget, detailed in Section V, specifically to build awareness and trial in the converted stores during the transition window.

**5. Fourteen months was not enough time, but the losses were not sustainable either.** We do not believe the pilot's underlying concept was wrong — the trip-frequency and basket-size data cited in Section I predates and postdates the pilot and remains consistent. We believe the execution was under-resourced and under-designed. This proposal is priced and staffed at a level we believe reflects what full execution actually requires, rather than a minimum-viable test that saved capital at the cost of the format's ability to succeed.

We want to be direct with the board: the pilot's failure is the single strongest argument against this proposal, and we do not dismiss it. Our judgment is that the pilot failed for identifiable and correctable reasons, not because the underlying customer demand doesn't exist. The board should weigh that judgment against the fact that we are asking for a substantially larger commitment on the strength of that judgment.

## IV. OPTIONS CONSIDERED

We evaluated four paths before settling on the recommendation before the board today.

**Option 1: Standing pat — no format change, no distribution consolidation.**
Under this option we would continue current operations, likely accompanied by increased promotional spending to defend share. Our modeling suggests this slows but does not reverse the same-store sales decline, and the promotional spending required to slow it further compresses already-thin margins. We project this path results in continued same-store sales decline in the range of 2 to 4 percent annually over the next three years, with operating margin erosion of approximately 60 to 90 basis points per year as promotional spending increases without corresponding volume recovery. This is the path of least immediate risk and least long-term viability.

**Option 2: Full-chain format conversion, all 34 stores.**
We rejected converting the entire chain immediately as financially and operationally reckless. It would require capital well beyond our current financing capacity, would eliminate our ability to learn from early conversions before committing the full network, and would repeat — at ten times the scale — the pilot's core error of moving too fast without proven execution capability. The eleven-store, 30-month approach allows sequenced learning and course correction that a full conversion does not.

**Option 3: Third-party prepared-foods fulfillment.**
We evaluated partnering with a regional commissary/fulfillment provider to supply prepared-foods programs to stores without building internal kitchen and distribution capability. Two vendors were evaluated in detail. This option carries substantially lower upfront capital cost — estimated at $8 to $12 million for equipment and store buildout versus $62.3 million for the full proposal — but comes with three disqualifying drawbacks. First, gross margin on third-party-supplied prepared foods runs 18 to 22 percentage points lower than internally produced product, because the margin is shared with the fulfillment provider; over a five-year horizon this gap alone exceeds the capital cost differential. Second, neither vendor could commit to daily delivery windows compatible with the freshness standard we believe is necessary to compete credibly against in-store-made offerings at other regional grocers, let alone to differentiate from the discount entrants. Third, and most importantly, this path builds no internal capability — we would remain permanently dependent on a third party for the one category we believe is central to our long-term competitive positioning, with no ability to control quality, cost, or innovation in that category over time. We do not believe this path solves the underlying strategic problem; it defers it while paying a recurring margin penalty to do so.

**Option 4 (Recommended): Sequenced eleven-store conversion with distribution consolidation.**
This is the proposal before the board. It commits meaningful but bounded capital, builds internal capability rather than renting it, sequences the rollout over 30 months to allow monitoring and adjustment, and directly addresses the distribution constraint that undermined the 2024 pilot. It is the only option that we believe both solves the strategic problem described in Section I and is financially disciplined enough to survive board and lender scrutiny.

These savings begin accruing in month 19, following the Porterville closure, and are incorporated into the payback calculation above.

## VI. STORE AND DISTRIBUTION SCHEDULE

The conversion is sequenced over 30 months in three waves to allow operational learning between phases and to avoid overwhelming Mr. Baek's store operations team or Mr. Achterberg's supply chain team with simultaneous transitions across all sites.

| Phase | Timing | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| **Phase 0 — Preparation** | Months 1–3 | Goshen lease exercised (before Aug. 1 deadline); facility design finalized; kitchen manager recruitment begins; UFCW notification and consultation begins |
| **Phase 1 — Wave A (4 stores)** | Months 4–10 | First four store conversions; Goshen facility construction; Lindsay volume begins shifting to Goshen as capacity comes online |
| **Phase 1 review** | Month 11 | Formal board update on Wave A performance against projections; go/no-go checkpoint for Wave B |
| **Phase 2 — Wave B (4 stores)** | Months 12–19 | Second four store conversions; Goshen facility reaches full operational capacity; Porterville distribution closes at month 19, coinciding with Goshen's readiness to absorb full volume |
| **Phase 3 — Wave C (3 stores)** | Months 20–26 | Final three store conversions, incorporating lessons from Waves A and B |
| **Phase 4 — Stabilization** | Months 27–30 | Full network operating under new format and distribution structure; final performance assessment against original projections |

The Goshen lease option must be exercised before August 1, 2026, regardless of the board's decision on the broader store-conversion timeline, if the board wishes to preserve the distribution consolidation option at all. We flag this explicitly because it is the one element of this proposal with a hard external deadline independent of our own sequencing choices.

**On the Porterville closure specifically:** we recognize that Chair Alvarado and Mr. Alvarado have previously expressed opposition to closing the Porterville facility, and we have not brought this proposal back with the closure unchanged from earlier discussions. Three modifications respond directly to concerns raised in prior board conversations:

First, the closure timing has been pushed to month 19 rather than early in the schedule, specifically to allow the Goshen facility to be fully operational and tested with real volume from Lindsay before Porterville volume transitions, reducing the risk of a service disruption during the transition.

Second, we have built a more generous severance and placement structure for Porterville's 61 warehouse positions than standard practice, described in Section VII, reflecting the community and workforce tenure considerations raised previously.

Third, we have asked Mr. Achterberg's team to evaluate, and will report back to the board within 90 days of approval, whether a smaller cross-dock or regional pickup function could remain at the Porterville site for local last-mile efficiency even after primary distribution moves to Goshen — this would not restore the facility's current function or workforce but may partially preserve a Porterville-based operation if it proves economically supportable. We present this as a commitment to study, not a commitment to a specific outcome, because we do not want to promise something Mr. Achterberg's team has not yet fully costed.

We understand these modifications may not fully resolve the board's concerns, and we welcome further discussion at the meeting on whether additional accommodation is warranted.

## VII. STAFFING PLAN

The staffing changes run in two directions simultaneously — additions in store-level food service, reductions in Porterville distribution — and we address both directly rather than netting them against each other, because they affect different people in different roles with different timelines.

**Additions.** Across the eleven converted stores, Mr. Baek's plan adds:

- **19 kitchen manager positions** (one per converted store, plus an additional two float/training managers to support Waves A through C sequentially), reporting through store management with dotted-line accountability to a newly designated Director of Prepared Foods within Mr. Baek's organization. This director-level position is itself new and is included in the $4.8 million workforce transition budget in Section V.
- **240 hourly food-service positions**, phased in with each store's conversion wave rather than all at once, covering hot-bar, deli, bakery, and grab-and-go production and service roles. Approximately 60 percent of these positions are expected to be filled through internal transfer or promotion from existing store staff, with the remainder as new hires.

All new hourly food-service positions fall within the existing UFCW bargaining unit and job classifications; Mr. Baek has confirmed with our labor relations team that no new job classifications requiring separate negotiation are created by this staffing plan, which we believe reduces — though does not eliminate — labor relations risk ahead of the 2027 contract reopener, discussed further in Section VIII.

**Reductions.** The Porterville distribution closure eliminates 61 warehouse positions, effective at month 19 of the schedule. Mr. Achterberg and our labor relations team have developed the following transition provisions, which exceed our standard severance policy:

- Severance calculated at three weeks' pay per year of service, versus our standard policy of two weeks, reflecting the workforce tenure concentrated at Porterville, where average seniority is 11.4 years.
- First right of transfer to open positions at the Goshen facility or any of the eleven converted stores, for any Porterville employee willing to relocate or commute, with relocation assistance for those who transfer to Goshen. Based on preliminary interest surveys conducted by Mr. Achterberg's team, we estimate 18 to 24 of the 61 affected employees may take up this option, meaning realistic net job losses are likely in the range of 37 to 43 positions rather than the full 61.
- Extended healthcare continuation at company expense for four months beyond standard COBRA-eligible severance, and funded outplacement/job placement services through a third-party provider, with a dedicated on-site coordinator during the final three months before closure.
- Advance notice of 120 days before closure, exceeding the 60-day requirement under the WARN Act, to maximize the window for affected employees to pursue the transfer option or external opportunities.

We do not present these provisions as eliminating the hardship of job loss for the roughly 40 employees likely to be affected net of transfers. We present them as the most substantial accommodation we believe is financially sustainable within the overall project budget, and as a direct response to the board's prior concerns about how this workforce would be treated.

## APPENDIX B: FINANCIAL MODELING AND SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS

**Base case assumptions.** The return projections in Section V rest on the following core assumptions, derived from a blend of chain-wide prepared-foods performance data (excluding the three 2024 pilot stores, whose execution issues we do not believe are representative of a properly resourced conversion) and industry benchmarks provided by our category management consultant, Hollis Retail Advisory:

- Average prepared-foods category sales per converted store reaching $1.42 million annually by month 24 post-conversion, phased in gradually from a starting run rate of approximately $340,000 in the first full quarter after conversion.
- Prepared-foods gross margin of 38 percent at maturity, reflecting a blended rate across hot bar (higher margin, higher labor cost), deli (moderate margin), and bakery (higher margin, lower labor intensity).
- Trip-frequency lift of 1.7x among prepared-foods purchasing customers, applied conservatively at 60 percent of the full observed chain-wide effect to account for the possibility that converted stores' customer base does not respond as strongly as our highest-attachment existing stores.
- Basket-size lift of 22 percent on non-prepared-food purchases within the same trip, applied at the same 60 percent conservative discount.
- Center-store sales reduction of approximately 18 percent per converted store, reflecting the reduced assortment and footprint, partially offsetting the prepared-foods and basket-lift gains.
- Occupancy cost increase of $1.8 million annually chain-wide from the sale-leaseback structure, fully loaded into the model beginning in the quarter each leaseback closes.
- Distribution consolidation savings of $2.9 million annually, phased in beginning month 19 per the schedule in Section VI, ramping to full run rate by month 24.

**Base case output.** Under these assumptions, the eleven-store conversion program reaches breakeven on incremental annual operating cash flow at month 22, with cumulative payback of the full $62.3 million capital investment at approximately 5.5 years following program completion (i.e., roughly 8 years from the May 21, 2026 approval date).

**Sensitivity analysis.** We tested the model against variation in the trip-frequency and basket-lift assumptions, which we consider the two variables with the greatest uncertainty given the limited comparable data available following the 2024 pilot's early termination.

| Scenario | Trip-Frequency/Basket Lift vs. Base Case | Cumulative Payback Period | Month 30 Cumulative Program NPV (8% discount rate) |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Downside | 70% of base case assumption | 8.1 years | $4.2M |
| Base case | 100% of base case assumption | 5.5 years | $19.6M |
| Upside | 130% of base case assumption | 4.2 years | $34.8M |

Even under the downside scenario — trip-frequency and basket lift performing at only 70 percent of our already-conservative base case assumption — the program is projected to generate positive net present value over the 8-year analysis window, though payback extends beyond the initial 5.5-year projection. We regard the downside scenario as a meaningful stress test rather than a remote tail case, given that it approximates the level of underperformance that would have been required for the 2024 pilot's core demand thesis to still hold despite its operational execution failures.

We did not model a scenario in which trip-frequency and basket-size effects are entirely absent, because we do not believe that outcome is consistent with the chain-wide transaction data described in Section I, which reflects actual observed customer behavior across our full store base over multiple years, not a hypothesis derived solely from the pilot.

**Break-even sensitivity to construction cost.** We separately modeled the effect of construction cost overruns on the store conversion and Goshen buildout, given that these categories together represent $48 million, or 77 percent, of total project capital.

| Construction Cost Variance | Effect on Total Project Capital | Effect on Payback Period |
|---|---:|---:|
| On budget | $62.3M | 5.5 years (base case) |
| 10% overrun | $67.1M | 6.0 years |
| 20% overrun | $71.9M | 6.6 years |
| Covered by contingency reserve only (5.4%) | $65.5M | 5.8 years |

The $3.2 million contingency reserve covers approximately a 6.7 percent overrun on the combined store conversion and Goshen buildout budget before requiring return to the board for additional authorization. Mr. Renner has structured the revolver expansion with sufficient headroom to accommodate draws up to a 20 percent overrun scenario without breaching covenant thresholds, should the board wish to authorize contingency spending beyond the reserve without a separate financing action; this would require a separate board resolution at the time, as Resolution 1 in Section X caps authorized capital at $62.3 million.

**Comparison to Option 3 (third-party fulfillment) economics.** For reference, we modeled the same eleven-store footprint under the third-party fulfillment structure described as Option 3 in Section IV, using indicative terms from the two vendors evaluated.

| Metric | Recommended Proposal (Option 4) | Third-Party Fulfillment (Option 3) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Upfront capital required | $62.3M | $9.6M (midpoint estimate) |

The lower upfront capital cost of Option 3 is, in our judgment, more than offset over any horizon beyond approximately three years by the substantially lower margin retained and the strategic cost of remaining dependent on external fulfillment for a category central to our competitive response. We include this comparison in the appendix rather than the body of the proposal because we recognize board members may wish to examine the underlying numbers independently of our recommendation in Section IV.

## APPENDIX C: PROJECT GOVERNANCE AND REPORTING STRUCTURE

To ensure the accountability gaps identified in the 2024 pilot post-mortem (Section III) do not recur at this larger scale, we propose the following governance structure for the duration of the 30-month program, effective upon board approval:

**Program Steering Committee**, meeting biweekly through month 11 and monthly thereafter, chaired by Ms. Cepeda and comprising Mr. Achterberg, Mr. Baek, Mr. Renner, and the newly created Director of Prepared Foods position referenced in Section VII. This committee holds direct accountability for schedule, budget, and performance against the projections in Appendix B, and is the body that will prepare the month-11 Wave A review for the board described in Section IX and Resolution 5.

**Monthly management reporting**, distributed to the full executive team, tracking capital spend against budget by category, construction and conversion schedule status by store and by wave, and the performance metrics listed in Section IX for any store that has completed conversion.

**Quarterly board reporting**, aligned to the board's existing quarterly meeting schedule, consisting of a standing agenda item summarizing program status, financial performance against the base case in Appendix B, and any material variance requiring board attention outside of the formal month-11 checkpoint.

**Escalation threshold.** Any single-category cost variance exceeding 15 percent of budgeted amount in Section V, or any schedule slippage exceeding 60 days on a wave boundary, triggers a special report to the board chair outside the standard quarterly cycle, rather than waiting for the next scheduled board meeting. We propose this explicit escalation threshold in direct response to the pilot post-mortem finding that no single party was accountable for surfacing problems as they emerged; this structure is intended to make surfacing problems early a defined obligation rather than a discretionary judgment call.

We believe this governance structure, combined with the phased capital release and month-11 checkpoint described in Section IX, gives the board meaningfully more visibility and control over this program than existed during the 2024 pilot, notwithstanding the larger scale of capital and stores involved.
