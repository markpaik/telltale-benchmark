# Predictable Schedules in Retail: What the Evidence Says About Turnover, Sales, Cost, and Worker Well-Being

*A literature review prepared for the Pinal Ridge Markets January capital meeting*

*Prepared by Dr. Hyun-Woo Chang, with data support from Camille Deschamps-Iyer*

---

## 1. Introduction

Pinal Ridge Markets is considering a twelve-store pilot of predictive scheduling: schedules posted two weeks in advance, a guaranteed weekly core of hours, and the elimination of on-call shifts. Chief People Officer Delia Marchbanks argues the policy will cut turnover among front-end clerks, currently running at 87 percent annually against a replacement cost of roughly $2,100 per departure. Chief Financial Officer Priyansh Bhatt projects the policy will add $1.9 million a year in labor cost across the pilot stores if scaled, primarily through the guaranteed-hours provision. The question before the capital committee is not whether predictable scheduling is popular with workers — the qualitative literature settles that quickly — but whether it pays for itself, or comes close enough to be justified on other grounds, for a mid-sized regional grocery chain operating in a Sun Belt labor market with a large part-time and caregiving workforce.

This review asks four linked questions. First, does advance posting of schedules, a guaranteed hours floor, or the removal of on-call shifts reduce voluntary turnover among hourly retail workers, and by how much. Second, what happens to sales, labor productivity, and customer-facing metrics when schedules stabilize. Third, what does implementation actually cost employers, and why do published cost estimates vary by an order of magnitude. Fourth, what happens to workers themselves — hours, earnings volatility, sleep, and the specific circumstances of workers with caregiving obligations — and how much of that literature is generalizable to a 61-store grocery operation in Arizona and southern Nevada.

The scope is bounded deliberately. The review privileges evidence from grocery and general retail over other low-wage service sectors, evidence from firms with 40 or more locations over single-site studies, and evidence published or circulated between 1998 and 2026, the period spanning the earliest scheduling-flexibility experiments and the diffusion of local Fair Workweek ordinances in the United States. It does not attempt to resolve debates in the broader flexible-work literature that concern salaried or professional workers, nor does it take a position on the legislative merits of scheduling ordinances as policy; the question here is narrower and operational — what should Pinal Ridge Markets expect if it runs this pilot.

## 2. Search and Screening

The search covered EconLit, PsycINFO, and the working paper archives of four labor research centers, for the period January 1998 through June 2026. Search terms combined variants of "predictive scheduling," "advance notice," "on-call shift," "just-in-time scheduling," "scheduling ordinance," "Fair Workweek," and "core hours guarantee" with retail-sector terms — "grocery," "supermarket," "apparel retail," "big-box," "hourly workers" — and outcome terms covering turnover, separation, quit rate, sales, labor productivity, absenteeism, sleep, income volatility, and food security.

The search returned 1,120 unique records after deduplication. Titles and abstracts were screened against three inclusion criteria: the study had to examine a scheduling intervention or ordinance rather than scheduling preferences in the abstract; it had to report at least one outcome from the four domains above with enough methodological detail to assess design; and it had to concern hourly, non-exempt retail, food service, or comparable low-wage service employment, since evidence from manufacturing shift work or from salaried employees was judged not to transfer cleanly to a grocery front end. Two hundred seventeen records passed to full-text review. Fifty-nine were included in extraction: nine field experiments in which an employer or research team randomized scheduling practices across stores or shifts; 21 quasi-experimental evaluations exploiting the staggered adoption of municipal Fair Workweek ordinances, mostly in Seattle, San Francisco, Chicago, New York, and Emeryville; 16 studies using employer or state administrative payroll and separations data without an experimental or quasi-experimental design, generally correlational; and seven qualitative studies built on interviews or ethnographic fieldwork with hourly retail workers, several focused specifically on workers with children or elder-care responsibilities.

Excluded records fell into predictable categories: studies of scheduling preference surveys with no behavioral outcome, studies of professional or salaried flexible work, studies from outside retail and food service with no plausible transfer to a grocery front end, and a large number of consulting white papers that did not disclose sample construction or effect-size calculation and could not be assessed for risk of bias. Effect sizes, outcome definitions, sample sizes, and firm characteristics for all 59 included studies were entered into a shared extraction file, coded by author, year, design, sector, firm size, and outcome, and cross-checked by Camille Deschamps-Iyer against internal Pinal Ridge turnover, staffing, and payroll data to flag where firm characteristics diverged sharply from Pinal Ridge's own profile — a 61-store chain, 8,400 employees, 61 percent part time, operating in two states with no scheduling ordinance in effect.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Turnover

The turnover literature is the largest body of evidence and the most internally contradictory. The strongest causal claim comes from a field experiment run across 28 apparel and general-merchandise stores, where randomly assigned stable, employee-informed scheduling reduced voluntary turnover among hourly associates by roughly 20 log points over ten months relative to control stores using algorithmic just-in-time scheduling (Hartley & Osei 2016). A separate field experiment embedded within a national footwear retailer's chain, using a similar design but adding a guaranteed-hours floor rather than notice alone, found a comparable reduction concentrated among workers who had been scheduled fewer than 20 hours a week before the intervention (Pruitt & Kowalski 2022) — a detail relevant to Pinal Ridge, where the bulk of turnover sits among clerks scheduled well below full time.

Set against these, the ordinance evaluations tell a less clean story. Seattle's Secure Scheduling Ordinance, evaluated using a difference-in-differences design comparing covered large retailers and restaurants to uncovered smaller firms, found improved measures of schedule predictability and self-reported sleep but no statistically distinguishable effect on turnover, and a modest negative effect on measured job satisfaction attributable to compliance-related scheduling rigidity (Chen & Abernathy 2019). San Francisco's Retail Workers' Bill of Rights produced a similar pattern in an independent evaluation: better notice, no clear movement in separations, and qualitative reports of manager frustration with reduced flexibility to accommodate worker-requested swaps (Ruiz & Bergström 2020). A pooled analysis of four West Coast ordinances using synthetic control methods for each city found average turnover effects close to zero and not distinguishable from pre-ordinance trends, though the confidence intervals were wide enough to be consistent with either the field-experiment magnitude or with a null (Volkov & Naidoo 2018).

Administrative-data studies split along firm-size and sector lines. Using five years of payroll data from a mid-sized Midwestern grocery chain that adopted core-hours guarantees voluntarily, Okafor & Lindqvist (2020) found a 14-point reduction in annualized front-end turnover, comparable in magnitude to the apparel field experiment, but noted the adopting chain had simultaneously raised starting wages, confounding attribution. A larger administrative study across a national drugstore chain found essentially no turnover effect from notice requirements alone, attributing the null to the fact that most quits in that chain were driven by wage competition from nearby employers rather than schedule instability (Bassi & Fung 2020). Grocery-specific administrative evidence remains thin: only four of the sixteen administrative studies used grocery payroll data, and none came from a chain smaller than 150 stores, raising a generalizability concern addressed further below.

The qualitative literature, while not designed to estimate turnover effects, is worth setting against the quantitative findings because it identifies mechanism. Interview-based work with retail clerks in the Pacific Northwest found that workers described leaving jobs not primarily because of unpredictable schedules per se, but because unpredictability combined with insufficient guaranteed hours made budgeting and childcare arrangement impossible — a compound condition that notice-only ordinances do not fully address (Emerson & Castellanos 2021). This distinction between notice and hours guarantees recurs across the literature and is central to interpreting why ordinance evaluations, which typically mandate notice and premium pay for late changes but not a guaranteed hours floor, produce weaker turnover effects than the field experiments and administrative studies that bundle notice with guaranteed hours, closer to what Marchbanks has proposed.

### 3.2 Sales and Productivity

The sales evidence is thinner and more sector-specific than the turnover evidence, and it is dominated by a single widely cited result. The retail field experiment most often invoked in these discussions randomized stable, employee-informed scheduling across a subset of apparel stores and found sales increases of approximately 7 percent in treated stores, driven by improved labor-hour matching to customer traffic and reduced absenteeism (Lambrecht et al. 2021). This result has proven difficult to replicate outside apparel. A grocery-sector field experiment testing advance-notice scheduling alone, without a guaranteed-hours component, in a 40-store Midwestern chain, found no measurable sales effect and a small, statistically marginal increase in labor cost per transaction, attributed by the authors to reduced managerial flexibility to shift labor toward unexpected demand spikes such as weather-driven grocery runs (Sørensen & Whitfield 2017) — a scenario with obvious relevance to Arizona summer demand patterns.

Two further studies complicate the picture. An administrative-data study using scanner and payroll data from a regional supermarket chain found a positive association between schedule stability and same-store sales growth, but the design was observational, comparing stores that had adopted stable scheduling for operational reasons unrelated to any policy change, and the authors were explicit that reverse causation — better-performing stores adopting stable scheduling because they could afford to — could not be ruled out (Adeyemi & Sørensen 2019). A qualitative study of grocery store managers found that stable scheduling improved perceived customer service quality through greater staff familiarity with regular shifts and customers, but managers also reported that the loss of on-call flexibility made it harder to cover unplanned absences, a tradeoff not well captured in any of the quantitative sales studies (Tarrant 2016).

Taken together, the sales literature supports a conditional claim rather than a general one: schedule stability appears to help sales in settings where labor-hour matching was previously poor and where the intervention includes enough flexibility for managers to respond to genuine demand shocks, and appears neutral to mildly negative where a hard no-on-call rule removes the employer's ability to respond to short-notice demand variation of the kind grocery experiences more than apparel. No study in the sample examined a grocery chain implementing the specific three-part bundle Pinal Ridge is proposing — two-week notice, guaranteed core hours, and no on-call shifts — simultaneously.

### 3.3 Cost

Cost estimates in the literature range across roughly an order of magnitude, and reconciling them requires attention to what is being counted. At the low end, ordinance-compliance cost studies commissioned in connection with Seattle's and San Francisco's evaluations estimated employer compliance costs — scheduling software, manager training, predictability-pay penalties for late changes — at under $200 per employee per year, because those ordinances did not mandate a guaranteed-hours floor and predictability pay was triggered only occasionally (Chen & Abernathy 2019; Ruiz & Bergström 2020). At the high end, the Midwestern grocery chain that voluntarily adopted core-hours guarantees alongside wage increases reported an average labor cost increase of roughly $1,400 per employee per year, though this figure bundled the wage increase with the scheduling change and cannot be cleanly attributed to scheduling alone (Okafor & Lindqvist 2020). An administrative study isolating the cost of a guaranteed-hours floor specifically, using a national restaurant chain's rollout, estimated added labor cost at $340 to $980 per employee per year depending on how tightly the guarantee was set relative to historical average hours, with cost rising sharply as the guarantee approached the store's median historical schedule rather than its 25th-percentile low-demand schedule (Kessler & Amara 2021).

This last finding bears directly on Bhatt's $1.9 million projection. If Pinal Ridge sets its guaranteed core near typical scheduled hours for front-end clerks, the Kessler and Amara evidence suggests costs toward the higher end of the range; if the guarantee is set closer to the lower end of currently scheduled hours — closer to a floor than to an average — published costs run substantially lower, and several studies found that turnover-cost savings offset a meaningful share of the added labor cost even when they did not fully cover it (Hartley & Osei 2016; Pruitt & Kowalski 2022). Applying Pinal Ridge's own $2,100 replacement cost and 87 percent turnover rate to the front-end clerk population, even a turnover reduction in the 15-to-20-point range found in the strongest field experiments would offset a material fraction, though not all, of Bhatt's projected figure — a calculation elaborated in Section 5.

A separate and consistent finding across nine of the sixteen administrative studies is that employers facing new scheduling requirements frequently respond by cutting total scheduled hours, shifting toward a smaller core of more fully scheduled employees and reducing headcount at the margin, which lowers the visible per-employee cost of guarantees but shows up instead as reduced total hours worked and, in three studies, reduced total store labor budget rather than an increase (Blanchard & Ibarra 2017; Volkov & Naidoo 2018; Bassi & Fung 2020). This response pattern means published "cost" figures sometimes understate what a firm intending to preserve total scheduled hours, as Marchbanks' proposal appears to, should expect to pay, since the cost-saving version of compliance available to other employers — trimming hours elsewhere — is not part of the design being piloted.

### 3.4 Worker Outcomes

The worker-outcome literature is the most consistent of the four bodies, though consistency here means agreement on direction, not magnitude. Every ordinance evaluation in the sample that measured sleep or self-reported stress found improvement associated with advance notice, typically in the range of 15 to 20 additional minutes of sleep per night and reduced scores on standardized stress scales (Chen & Abernathy 2019; Ruiz & Bergström 2020; Nakamura & Feldstein 2020). Income volatility, measured as month-to-month variation in take-home pay, fell in every study that examined it when a guaranteed-hours floor was part of the intervention, but did not fall meaningfully under notice-only ordinances, again underscoring the distinction between notice and hours guarantees that recurs through the turnover literature (Dubois & Talwar 2019).

The seven qualitative studies, several built around retail workers with caregiving responsibilities, are where the practical stakes of the two-week-notice provision specifically become visible. Interview studies with single parents working retail schedules found that two weeks of notice was described repeatedly as the minimum threshold at which childcare could reliably be arranged, with anything shorter producing a cascade of missed shifts, informal childcare costs, or reliance on unstable arrangements with relatives (Villanueva & Strand 2018; Renner & Achebe 2016). A related ethnographic study of grocery clerks in the Southwest — one of the few studies in the sample situated in a labor market resembling Pinal Ridge's — found that workers balancing multiple part-time jobs valued the guaranteed-hours floor more than the notice period itself, because it let them commit predictable blocks of availability to a second employer without risking under-hours at either job, a pattern with direct relevance given that a majority of Pinal Ridge's workforce is part time (Okoro 2014).

Against this consistency sits one persistent finding that complicates a simple welfare story: in six of the studies that measured total hours as well as predictability, workers whose hours fell as an indirect effect of employer adjustment to new scheduling rules reported net declines in earnings satisfaction despite improved predictability, suggesting that predictability without adequate hours is not sufficient and can in some cases leave workers worse off financially even as satisfaction with notice improves (Blanchard & Ibarra 2017; Sato & Marchetti 2020). This finding argues for treating the guaranteed-hours-floor and no-on-call provisions of Marchbanks' proposal as load-bearing, not incidental, since notice alone appears capable of improving sleep and stress without improving — and in some documented cases while worsening — income security.

## 4. Why the Literature Splits

Four methodological differences account for most of the disagreement summarized above, and each has direct bearing on how much weight Pinal Ridge should place on any single number in this review.

**Design and what it can detect.** The nine field experiments randomize scheduling practices within a firm that controls implementation fidelity, and they tend to find the largest effects on turnover and, in one case, sales. The 21 ordinance evaluations rely on quasi-experimental comparisons across firms of varying size and compliance quality, capture a policy that mandates notice but rarely mandates a guaranteed-hours floor, and tend to find smaller or null turnover effects. This is not simply a matter of one design being more rigorous than another; the interventions being evaluated are different in kind. Ordinances regulate the process of scheduling — notice periods, predictability pay — while the strongest field experiments and the administrative case studies with the largest effects (Hartley & Osei 2016; Okafor & Lindqvist 2020; Pruitt & Kowalski 2022) test a bundle that includes an hours guarantee, closer to what Pinal Ridge is proposing. Readers who cite ordinance-evaluation nulls as evidence against Marchbanks' proposal are, in a meaningful sense, citing evidence about a different intervention.

**Sector and demand variability.** Grocery retail differs from apparel retail in ways that matter for the sales and cost findings specifically. Grocery demand has sharper, more weather- and event-driven short-notice spikes than apparel, which likely explains why the apparel field experiment's 7 percent sales gain (Lambrecht et al. 2021) did not replicate in the grocery-sector experiment that removed on-call flexibility (Sørensen & Whitfield 2017). Any extrapolation from apparel-sector sales gains to a grocery chain in Arizona, where extreme heat drives sudden demand shifts, should be treated with caution in the direction of the more conservative grocery-specific finding.

**Firm size and market position.** Every grocery-specific administrative study in the sample came from a chain of 150 or more stores, roughly two and a half times Pinal Ridge's footprint, and larger chains typically have more scheduling software infrastructure, deeper management layers, and more slack in labor budgets to absorb a guarantee without cutting total hours. The consistent finding that some employers respond to scheduling mandates by cutting total hours elsewhere in the store (Blanchard & Ibarra 2017; Bassi & Fung 2020) is itself a firm-size-contingent behavior likely to be harder for a 61-store chain to execute quietly than for a national chain with more scheduling levers, which cuts against assuming Pinal Ridge could offset costs the way some studied firms did.

**How outcomes are defined.** "Turnover" is not measured consistently across studies: some use annualized voluntary separations among all hourly staff, others restrict to front-end or first-year employees, and a few include involuntary separations without disaggregating. Pinal Ridge's 87 percent figure is specific to front-end clerks, a population closer to the samples in the strongest field experiments (Hartley & Osei 2016; Pruitt & Kowalski 2022) than to the whole-store turnover figures used in several ordinance evaluations, which is one reason this review weights the field-experiment findings more heavily for the turnover question specifically than the ordinance-evaluation nulls, while still flagging the latter as the more externally valid design for estimating compliance cost under a notice-only regime.

## 5. Gaps

Several questions directly relevant to Pinal Ridge's decision have not been studied, or have been studied too thinly to support confident extrapolation.

No study in the sample examines a grocery chain of Pinal Ridge's specific size implementing the full three-part bundle — two-week notice, guaranteed core hours, and elimination of on-call shifts — simultaneously; the closest analogues combine two of the three elements, not all three, and none is in grocery. No study examines a labor market resembling Phoenix and southern Nevada specifically, where the seasonal Snowbird population, extreme summer heat driving abrupt demand shifts, and a large Spanish-speaking hourly workforce all plausibly affect both baseline turnover drivers and the feasibility of two-week advance scheduling. Only one qualitative study addresses Southwest grocery workers directly (Okoro 2014), and it was not designed to estimate cost or turnover magnitude. The interaction between a scheduling-guarantee policy and a state minimum-wage change has not been studied in any design in this sample, a gap relevant given ongoing Arizona minimum-wage indexing. No administrative study tracks cost trajectories beyond the second year of implementation, so whether initial cost estimates like Bhatt's hold, rise, or fall as managers learn to schedule under the new constraints is unknown — several qualitative studies suggest manager adaptation over time reduces friction, but no quantitative study has tested whether this translates into lower marginal cost in years two and three. Finally, no study quantitatively isolates the caregiving-worker subpopulation's turnover response to a guaranteed-hours floor specifically; the qualitative literature establishes that caregiving workers value hours guarantees highly, but no field experiment or administrative study has produced an effect size for this subgroup, leaving Marchbanks' argument that the policy will disproportionately retain caregiving-obligated clerks — plausible, and consistent with the qualitative record — without a quantitative estimate behind it.

## 6. Implications for a Chain This Size

The literature does not resolve the question of whether Pinal Ridge's pilot will pay for itself in the pilot's first year, and any claim to the contrary should be treated skeptically regardless of which side of the capital-meeting debate it supports. What the evidence supports is a narrower and more useful set of conclusions.

First, the bundled design Marchbanks has proposed — notice plus guaranteed hours plus no on-call — sits closer to the interventions that produced the largest turnover reductions in this literature than to the notice-only ordinances that produced null results, and the mechanism identified in the qualitative literature (that unpredictability combined with insufficient guaranteed hours, not unpredictability alone, drives quits) supports treating the guaranteed-hours component as necessary rather than optional. A pilot that implemented notice alone, on this evidence, would be unlikely to move turnover materially and should not be substituted for the full bundle.

Second, the cost projection should be treated as sensitive to where the guaranteed core is set. Evidence from Kessler & Amara (2021) indicates cost rises sharply as the guarantee approaches historical median hours rather than a lower-percentile floor; Bhatt's $1.9 million figure should be checked against the specific hours threshold used in Pinal Ridge's model, since the same policy label can produce costs varying by a factor of two or more depending on this parameter alone.

Third, applying Pinal Ridge's own figures to the more conservative end of the turnover-reduction range found in comparable field experiments and administrative studies (roughly 14 to 20 points, per Okafor & Lindqvist 2020 and Hartley & Osei 2016) against an 87 percent baseline and a $2,100 replacement cost suggests turnover-related savings that would offset a meaningful share, though on current evidence not the full amount, of the projected labor cost increase. The gap between offsetting savings and full cost recovery is the range within which the sales evidence — inconsistent between apparel and grocery, and entirely untested for this specific policy bundle in this sector — would need to contribute for the pilot to be cost-neutral, and this review cannot responsibly project a sales effect for a 61-store grocery chain from a single apparel-sector 7 percent result.

Fourth, the pilot's twelve-store scale and the twelve-month or longer horizon typically recommended before a scale decision would address two of the sharpest gaps identified above: it would generate the first grocery-specific, Southwest-specific evidence on this exact policy bundle, and, if tracked past twelve months, would begin to speak to whether costs decline as managers adapt, a question no existing study answers. Structuring the pilot's internal evaluation to disaggregate turnover and cost by whether clerks report caregiving responsibilities, and to track total scheduled hours per store rather than only per-employee guarantees, would close two of the specific gaps this review identifies and would let the January capital committee revisit this question in twelve months with company-specific rather than borrowed evidence.

On balance, the literature supports proceeding with a bounded pilot rather than either an immediate full rollout or a rejection of the proposal, and supports designing that pilot's measurement plan around the guaranteed-hours threshold and total-hours tracking specifically, since those are the two levers this review identifies as most responsible for the spread in published cost and turnover findings.
