# Six Patients a Day
## How Ogeechee Valley Health spent $7.25 million to fix an access problem, got the first year wrong, and salvaged the program

## The organization

Ogeechee Valley Health sits in Valdosta, Georgia, about fifteen miles north of the Florida line, and serves a catchment of roughly 190,000 people across Lowndes and five surrounding counties. It runs a 146-bed acute care hospital, six outpatient clinics, a home health agency, and a small hospice. It employs 1,300 people, which makes it the third-largest employer in the county. In fiscal 2023 it booked $210 million in total revenue.

The payer mix tells most of the story. Medicaid accounts for 29 percent of the system's gross revenue, Medicare for 41 percent, commercial insurance for 23 percent, and self-pay and charity for the remainder. Roughly a fifth of the adult population in the primary service area is uninsured. Two of the three surrounding counties are federally designated primary care health professional shortage areas. The nearest tertiary referral centers are in Tallahassee, ninety minutes southwest, and Macon, two and a half hours north, which means Ogeechee Valley is the last full-service hospital for a large and economically fragile territory. It cannot easily shrink its service lines, and it cannot easily raise prices.

By late 2023, its operating margin was minus 4.1 percent — an operating loss of about $8.6 million on $210 million of revenue. The system had run negative in 2022 as well, at minus 2.8 percent. Days cash on hand had fallen from 141 to 88 over eighteen months. The board's finance committee had begun meeting monthly instead of quarterly.

---

## The problem: two failures, braided together

Chief Executive Beatrice Nwachukwu had been in the job for four years when the 2023 numbers closed. Her read, which she gave the board in a September 2023 session, was that the system was not suffering from one problem but from two that were feeding each other.

The first was access. Nine of the system's 31 employed primary care physician positions were vacant. Some had been vacant for more than two years. Recruiting into rural south Georgia was hard before the pandemic and had become harder after it; the system had lost three physicians in 2022 alone to larger systems in Jacksonville and Macon offering both higher compensation and call schedules that did not include weekends. The consequence showed up in the schedule. A patient who called the system's primary care line asking to establish care waited an average of 47 days for a new-patient appointment. Established patients seeking an acute visit — a sore throat, an infected toe, a blood pressure check — often could not be seen the same week.

The second was financial, and the two met in the emergency department. Chief Medical Officer Vikas Deshpande commissioned a chart review in November 2023 that pulled 400 randomly selected ED encounters from the prior twelve months. Working with the ED medical director, he classified encounters as non-emergent if they were triaged at Emergency Severity Index level 4 or 5, discharged home, and required no diagnostic imaging beyond plain film and no intravenous therapy. Thirty-eight percent of the department's roughly 33,800 annual visits met that definition — nearly 12,850 encounters a year that, clinically, belonged in a primary care office.

The financial logic of that was unforgiving. Those visits consumed ED capacity, contributed to a left-without-being-seen rate that had climbed to 4.9 percent, and drove boarding times up for the patients who genuinely needed a bed. Meanwhile the encounters themselves were poorly compensated: a disproportionate share came from Medicaid and self-pay patients, and the system's collection rate on self-pay ED balances was under 9 percent. Ogeechee Valley was, in effect, delivering primary care at emergency department cost structure and collecting primary care reimbursement or less.

The no-show rate compounded everything. Across the six clinics, 24 percent of scheduled appointments were missed. Every no-show was a slot the system had staffed and could not refill on short notice, and every no-show was also a patient who, days or weeks later, would present somewhere less appropriate.

Deshpande's summary to the board was blunt: "We have built a system where the fastest way to see a clinician is to go to the most expensive place we own."

## The decision

On January 11, 2024, the board approved $5.4 million over 24 months to open two "access clinics" — a term the leadership team chose deliberately over "urgent care," to signal continuity of care rather than episodic treatment. The design had four features:

**Nurse practitioner staffing.** Rather than compete for physicians the system had already failed to recruit, the clinics would be staffed by family nurse practitioners working under collaborative agreements with employed physicians. Georgia's scope-of-practice rules permitted this, and the labor market for FNPs, while tight, was not as brutal as the physician market.

**Same-day scheduling.** Each clinic would hold a majority of its daily capacity open for same-day and next-day booking, with a small panel of empaneled patients for continuity.

**Two sites.** One in Hahira, a town of about 3,300 roughly ten miles north of Valdosta on the I-75 corridor, chosen because a large share of ED non-emergent volume originated from the northern county and the Berrien County line. The second in Valdosta proper, initially sited at Baytree Medical Park, a professional office complex near the hospital where the system could get below-market rent on space a departing orthopedic group had vacated.

**Locum bridge staffing.** Because the clinics needed to open before permanent nurse practitioners could be recruited, credentialed, and started, the system contracted with Halcyon Clinical Staffing to place five nurse practitioners on temporary assignment. Halcyon's billed rate was $312 per hour, inclusive of travel and housing. The plan assumed locums would cover roughly nine months while permanent hires landed.

The business case projected each clinic reaching 26 visits per day by month four — 52 visits per day combined — against a modeled breakeven of 19 per day per clinic. At target volume, the two clinics would contribute approximately $1.6 million annually and divert an estimated 4,500 to 5,500 non-emergent ED visits per year.

### The assumptions underneath it

Every capital request rests on assumptions, and Ogeechee Valley's rested on six. It is worth listing them plainly, because the first year of the program was essentially a stress test of each one.

1. **Demand was latent and would surface on its own.** The business case reasoned that if 12,850 people a year were going to the ED for non-emergent care, a fraction of them would go to a clinic instead once one existed. No mechanism was designed to route them there. The assumption was that awareness would follow from opening the doors.

2. **Locums were a finite bridge.** The plan assumed Halcyon would fill all five placements within 60 days and that permanent nurse practitioners would be hired within nine to twelve months, capping locum spend at approximately $1.2 million.

3. **The ramp curve was reliable.** The 26-visits-per-day-by-month-four figure came from a consulting dataset of retail-adjacent primary care launches, most of them in suburban markets with commercial payer mixes and dense drive-time populations.

4. **Site selection was second-order.** Rent was treated as the primary site variable. The implicit assumption was that patients would travel to wherever the clinic was, because they were already traveling to the ED.

5. **Revenue cycle would scale automatically.** No incremental revenue cycle staffing was included. The new clinics were assumed to fold into existing billing workflows, since they used the same electronic health record and the same tax ID.

6. **Nurse practitioner care would be accepted by patients and referred to by physicians.** The plan assumed the employed medical staff would actively direct overflow to the access clinics.

Assumptions one through five proved wrong. The sixth proved partly wrong and largely fixable.

## Opening: April 2024

The Hahira clinic opened on April 8, 2024, in a converted 3,900-square-foot retail bay on a state highway at the north edge of town. Buildout had run $980,000 against a $760,000 budget, mostly on mechanical and electrical work the pre-lease inspection had not caught.

In its first full month it averaged six patients a day.

Not six patients per provider per day — six patients total, against a clinic staffed with one nurse practitioner, one medical assistant, one front desk coordinator, and a shared phlebotomist, and against a target of 26.

The second clinic did not open at all that spring. Halcyon had filled only three of five placements. Two of the three were assigned to Hahira on alternating schedules and to float coverage at two existing primary care sites; the third covered weekend hospital rounding for the employed physician group, which was itself short-staffed. The Baytree site sat with a signed lease and a partially completed fit-out.

The volume did not move much. May averaged 6.4 patients a day. June, 7.1. By October, with a modest print and radio campaign behind it, Hahira reached 8.2 patients a day. In the same period, the ED's non-emergent share of visits moved from 38 percent to 37 percent — inside the noise.

### Why nobody came

The reasons were discoverable, and in fact were discovered later, in a series of patient interviews Latrice Bonnaire commissioned in December 2024. They were not mysterious.

Hahira residents with a primary care need who were willing and able to drive did not stop at the new clinic; they drove twelve more minutes into Valdosta, where their existing physician, pharmacy, and family were. Hahira residents without reliable transportation did not come to the clinic either, because the site was on a highway with no sidewalk, no bus service, and a 45-minute walk from the residential core.

More consequential: the people generating non-emergent ED volume did not know the clinic existed, and nothing in their pathway told them. A patient who arrived at the Ogeechee Valley ED at 9 p.m. with a urinary tract infection was triaged, treated, and discharged with a printed instruction to "follow up with your primary care provider." No one offered an appointment. No one mentioned the access clinic. The ED registration system and the ambulatory scheduling system did not talk to each other in any way a triage nurse could use at 9 p.m.

And the clinic's hours were 8:00 a.m. to 5:00 p.m., Monday through Friday — precisely the hours during which the working uninsured population it was designed to serve could not leave a job.

## The staffing failure

Halcyon's contract, signed in February 2024, carried terms that were standard for the industry and unfavorable to a program with uncertain volume. The billed rate of $312 per hour was inclusive of housing and travel, which made it look competitive against the alternative of separate stipends. But the agreement guaranteed each placed clinician a minimum of 45 billable hours per week regardless of scheduled patient volume, carried a 90-day minimum engagement per clinician, required 30 days' notice for cancellation, and imposed a $22,500 conversion fee if Ogeechee Valley hired a placed clinician directly.

Over seven months — April through October 2024 — Halcyon billed $1.4 million.

The number that eventually appeared in the October board packet was the one that ended the debate. Of that $1.4 million, approximately $612,000 was attributable to coverage at the Hahira clinic. The Hahira clinic saw approximately 1,050 patients in that period.

**Agency staffing cost per Hahira visit: $583.**

Average net collected revenue per Hahira visit in the same period, before accounting for the billing problem described below: $164.

The minimum-hour guarantee was the mechanism. The system was paying for 45 hours of clinical capacity per clinician per week and using, at six to eight visits a day, perhaps a third of it. Nobody had modeled locum cost against actual volume; the model had assumed target volume, and once the contract was signed there was no term that allowed the system to flex hours down without terminating the placement altogether — which would have left the clinic dark.

There was a second, quieter failure. Halcyon had filled three of five placements, and the two unfilled placements were exactly the ones designated for the second clinic. The program's opening sequence had made the second site's launch dependent on an agency's ability to fill roles in a market where that agency had already demonstrated it could fill only 60 percent of them. There was no contingency plan. So the Baytree lease ran, and the fit-out sat.

Meanwhile, permanent recruitment for the nurse practitioner roles had not meaningfully begun. The program reported to a director of physician services who was simultaneously responsible for filling nine vacant physician positions and running a compensation redesign for the employed group. The access clinics were the third priority of a person with three priorities, and NP recruitment — which required a different pipeline, different sourcing, and a different value proposition than physician recruitment — got the residue of the attention.

## The billing failure

In August 2024, Revenue Cycle Director Emiliano Cortez ran a routine variance report on accounts receivable by department and found that the new access clinic cost center showed almost no cash posting against four months of charge activity.

What he found underneath took three weeks to fully reconstruct.

When the three Halcyon nurse practitioners were onboarded in March and April 2024, they were credentialed through the system's medical staff office and enrolled with Medicare, Georgia Medicaid, and the four principal commercial payers under the system's group tax identification number. That part was done correctly and on time.

What was not done was the addition of the new practice locations to the group's provider enrollment records with each payer, and the linkage of each clinician's individual enrollment to those service locations. In the payers' systems, the three nurse practitioners were enrolled providers rendering services at addresses where the group was not enrolled to render them.

Claims went out. Claims came back denied — mostly under denial codes indicating that the rendering provider was not eligible at the billed service location, with a subset denied for missing collaborative practice agreement documentation that two of the four commercial payers required to be filed in advance for NP-rendered services.

The denials went into a work queue. The work queue was not worked.

This was the part that made the failure a four-month failure rather than a two-week one. Ogeechee Valley's denial management team assigned queues by cost center. The access clinics had been set up with a new cost center in January. Nobody had mapped that cost center to a follow-up team. Denials accumulated in an orphaned queue that appeared on no analyst's daily worklist and on no supervisor's dashboard. The queue had a name and an owner field, and the owner field was blank.

The total was $780,000 in denied claims across the three clinicians' full scope of billed activity — the Hahira clinic, float coverage at two existing primary care sites, and weekend hospital rounding — spanning April through July 2024.

Cortez escalated on September 3. By then, five weeks of the earliest claims were past the 90-day timely filing deadline for one commercial payer and approaching it for another.

## October 2024: the reckoning

The October 17 board meeting was the decision point. Nwachukwu presented the position without softening it.

Seven months into a program budgeted at $5.4 million over two years, the system had spent $2.9 million. One clinic was open and running at 31 percent of its volume target. The second clinic was not open, had consumed $340,000 in design and partial fit-out, and had no staffing path. The agency staffing model was costing $583 per visit at Hahira. Four months of claims were denied, $780,000 in value, with a portion already unrecoverable. The ED's non-emergent share had moved one point, within measurement error. And the system's operating margin for the first three quarters of 2024 stood at minus 3.6 percent — better than 2023, but for reasons unrelated to the access program.

Three options were on the table: close the program and take the loss; pause and reassess for six months; or restructure it under new leadership with a revised budget and a hard set of milestones.

The board's finance chair argued for closure. The argument was reasonable: the program had failed its first-year test on every dimension, the system could not afford to fund experiments, and the $2.9 million was sunk regardless.

Deshpande argued the counterfactual. The access problem had not gone away. Nine physician vacancies were still nine physician vacancies. Closing the clinics would return the system to a state in which the fastest route to a clinician was the emergency department, and would also mean writing off the Hahira buildout entirely and paying out the remaining lease term. The failures, he argued, were failures of execution — siting, staffing structure, hours, referral pathway, revenue cycle ownership — and every one of them was a thing that could be fixed by someone whose full-time job was fixing it.

Nwachukwu's position was that the board should approve restructuring on the condition that it come with named accountability, a re-baselined budget, and quarterly milestones with a pre-committed shutdown trigger. If the clinics did not clear 24 combined visits per day by June 2025, the program would close, and that decision would not be relitigated.

The board approved on that basis.

## The correction: November 2024 through June 2025

Latrice Bonnaire, the system's ambulatory director, took over the program on November 4, 2024. She had run the system's specialty clinic operations for six years and had spent four years before that as a practice manager for a multi-site family medicine group in Albany, Georgia. Her first act was to ask for and receive the program as her sole responsibility, with the specialty clinic portfolio temporarily reassigned.

Her first six weeks were diagnostic. She sat in the Hahira waiting room. She rode with the ED charge nurses on three evening shifts. She pulled the address data on twelve months of non-emergent ED encounters and mapped them. She commissioned 60 structured patient interviews. She read the Halcyon contract line by line with the system's general counsel.

Then she made seven changes.

### 1. She moved the second clinic

The Baytree Medical Park site was abandoned. Bonnaire's mapping showed that the density of non-emergent ED origination was not near the hospital; it was concentrated in the northern and eastern residential corridors, and it correlated strongly with the trade areas of two supermarkets. She negotiated out of the Baytree lease for a $115,000 termination payment and signed a new lease in Bemiss Crossing, a grocery-anchored plaza on the northeast side of Valdosta with a regional bus stop at the entrance and 340 parking spaces already there.

The new site cost more per square foot — $1.06 million in buildout against $340,000 already sunk at Baytree — and Bonnaire defended it on a single argument: the previous site had been selected on cost and the new one was selected on where the patients were. The clinic opened February 10, 2025.

She also discovered, in the course of the relocation analysis, something the original business case had never examined: whether either site qualified for Rural Health Clinic certification, which would have converted Medicare and Medicaid reimbursement to a cost-based, per-visit encounter rate materially above the fee schedule the clinics were being paid. Neither did. The Hahira site, despite the town's rural character, sat inside the Census-defined Valdosta urbanized area boundary. A site eight miles further north, in Berrien County, would have qualified. That analysis had never been run before the lease was signed.

### 2. She ended the agency dependency

Working with human resources, Bonnaire converted two of the three Halcyon nurse practitioners to employed positions, paying $45,000 in conversion fees. Both had built local ties and both accepted salaries well below their agency billing rate. She released the third placement at the end of its term and did not replace it.

She then ran NP recruitment as a distinct campaign rather than an adjunct to physician recruitment: a partnership with the FNP program at a regional university, a rotating preceptor site at the Hahira clinic, a sign-on structure weighted toward retention rather than arrival, and a schedule commitment of no more than one Saturday morning in four. Two hires landed in February and April 2025 — one of them a former preceptee.

The last locum rolled off March 21, 2025. Agency spend from November 2024 through March 2025 was $610,000 during the wind-down, bringing total Halcyon spend to $2.01 million against a $1.2 million plan.

Fully loaded cost per employed nurse practitioner: approximately $148,000 per year, or roughly $71 per hour against Halcyon's $312.

### 3. She changed the hours

Both clinics moved to 8:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m. Monday through Friday, plus 8:00 a.m. to noon on Saturday. The evening block was staffed by extending the NP schedule with a four-day, ten-hour rotation rather than by adding headcount, which is why it cost almost nothing.

By June 2025, 34 percent of all access clinic visits occurred after 5:00 p.m. or on Saturday.

### 4. She built the referral pathway that had never existed

This was the single change with the largest effect on volume, and it required almost no capital.

Bonnaire worked with the ED medical director and the informatics team to build a discharge workflow: any patient triaged at ESI level 4 or 5, discharged home, and without an established primary care relationship in the system would be flagged. Before discharge, a care coordinator or, after hours, the discharge nurse would book them an appointment at an access clinic — an actual appointment, with a date, time, and address, printed on the discharge instructions and confirmed by text.

The system called it a warm handoff. It was, functionally, converting a discharge instruction into a transaction.

In the first full month of operation, February 2025, the ED booked 412 access clinic appointments. Show rate on those appointments was 61 percent — low by clinic standards, high for a population that had previously received a piece of paper. By June, monthly ED-originated bookings had risen to 690 with a 68 percent show rate.

She built the same pathway in reverse for the existing primary care clinics: any request for an acute appointment that could not be accommodated within 48 hours at the patient's home clinic was offered an access clinic slot, with a note routed back to the patient's regular physician. This required negotiating with the employed medical staff, several of whom were initially resistant to NP-rendered care for their empaneled patients. Deshpande handled that conversation, and the compromise was a standardized note-back protocol and a monthly quality review of access clinic encounters presented at the medical executive committee.

### 5. She attacked no-shows

Text-message appointment reminders at 72 hours, 24 hours, and two hours, with a one-touch confirm or cancel. A cancellation released the slot to an automated waitlist. A live outbound call for any patient who had missed two consecutive appointments, made by a medical assistant rather than a call center, asking what had gotten in the way. In about 30 percent of those calls, the answer was transportation, and the system began issuing rideshare vouchers for a capped number of visits per patient per year at an average cost of $18 per round trip.

The access clinic no-show rate, which had run at 26 percent under the original operating model, fell to 11 percent by June 2025. Across all clinics, the system rate fell from 24 percent to 13 percent, because Bonnaire pushed the same reminder and waitlist infrastructure out to the other six sites in April 2025 at a marginal cost of about $31,000.

### 6. She recovered the money

Cortez and Bonnaire ran the denial recovery jointly. The enrollment records were corrected with all six payers, which itself took eleven weeks because two payers required the corrections to be processed sequentially rather than in parallel. Claims within the filing window were rebilled. Claims outside it were appealed on the basis that the provider was properly enrolled and the deficiency was a location registration matter, not an eligibility matter — an argument that succeeded with Medicare and Georgia Medicaid and with two of the four commercial payers.

Of the $780,000 denied, **$520,000 was recovered in cash** by May 2025. The remaining $260,000 was written off: approximately $150,000 forfeited to timely filing on the earliest five weeks of claims, and approximately $110,000 uncollectible after balances shifted to patient responsibility on accounts with no realistic collection probability.

The structural fix cost less than the recovery effort. Every cost center in the system was audited for work queue ownership; four others were found unmapped. A rule was added to the revenue cycle checklist requiring that no new department could be activated in the billing system without a named denial queue owner, and a weekly exception report was built to flag any cost center with charges posted and no cash posted for more than 21 days.

### 7. She re-baselined the numbers and told the board the truth

In December 2024, Bonnaire presented a revised budget of $7.3 million total authorized program spend — a $1.9 million supplemental — and a revised volume target of 24 to 34 combined visits per day by June 2025, against the original 52. She revised breakeven downward to approximately 28 combined visits per day, which was possible only because the employed staffing model had cut the cost structure so far below the locum model.

She also told the board, in writing, that the original target of 52 visits per day was not achievable in this market with this payer mix and this catchment density, and that anyone who told them otherwise was selling something.

The clinics saw 31 combined visits per day in the June 2025 quarter — 40 percent below the original business case target, and above the re-baselined breakeven of 28. They contributed $210,000 to the system in the quarter, or roughly $89 per visit, against net revenue of approximately $181 per visit and direct operating cost of approximately $92 per visit.

An honest footnote belongs here, and Bonnaire put it in the board packet. That $210,000 is a contribution margin: net revenue less direct clinic operating expense. It excludes allocated corporate overhead and it excludes amortization of the $2.4 million in leasehold and equipment investment. On a full absorption basis, the two clinics were still approximately $95,000 negative for the quarter. They were cash-positive and not yet profitable, and the distinction mattered.

The ED effect was the larger financial story and the harder one to measure precisely. Total ED volume fell modestly, from about 33,800 to about 32,600 annualized, while the non-emergent share fell from 38 percent to 29 percent — a reduction of roughly 3,390 non-emergent encounters per year. The system's finance office estimated the value of that diversion at approximately $1.9 million annually, composed of avoided variable cost on low-margin encounters, reduced boarding-driven capacity loss, and improved throughput on the higher-acuity encounters that remained. That estimate carried a wide confidence interval and Bonnaire said so.

The access improvements also had a recruiting effect nobody had modeled. Two of the four physicians hired in 2025 cited the existence of the access clinics in their decision — specifically, that acute overflow would not land on their schedules and that they would not be the only route into the system. Physician vacancies fell from nine to five.

Against $5.4 million originally authorized, the program cost $7.25 million — a 34 percent overrun.

The overrun is almost entirely traceable to four items, and each one maps to a failed assumption: $810,000 in excess agency spend (assumption two), $455,000 in abandoned site cost (assumption four), $260,000 in permanently lost revenue (assumption five), and roughly $600,000 in extended ramp-period staffing cost caused by eleven months of volume that never materialized (assumptions one and three).

Put differently: the program's execution failures cost approximately $2.1 million, which is roughly what the corrected program will take two and a half years of contribution to earn back.

## What the access program did not do

Ogeechee Valley's operating margin improved from minus 4.1 percent to minus 0.6 percent — a swing of about $7.3 million on a slightly larger revenue base. It would be dishonest to attribute that to two clinics contributing $210,000 a quarter.

The finance office's decomposition of the improvement, presented alongside the program results, attributed roughly $2.6 million to the normalization of contract labor costs in nursing as travel rates fell across the market, roughly $1.9 million to a renegotiated commercial contract with the system's largest payer that took effect in October 2024, roughly $1.1 million to a supply chain and pharmacy formulary initiative unrelated to ambulatory operations, and roughly $800,000 to the elimination of two service lines. The access program's total contribution — clinic margin plus estimated ED diversion value plus recovered denials — was estimated at $1.4 million on an annualized basis, of which $520,000 was a one-time recovery of money the program itself had lost.

The program was, on the numbers, a meaningful but secondary driver of the financial turnaround. Nwachukwu insisted on presenting it that way. Her reasoning, stated at the June 2025 board meeting, was that a program which had already been rescued once from an over-optimistic business case should not be rehabilitated by an over-optimistic attribution.

What the program did do, decisively, was fix the access problem. A 47-day wait became a 12-day wait. A 24 percent no-show rate became 13 percent. Nearly 3,400 people a year who would have received primary care in an emergency department received it in a clinic instead. Those are the outcomes the program was authorized to produce, and it produced them — fourteen months late and $1.85 million over budget.

## What Ogeechee Valley would do differently

Bonnaire, Cortez, Deshpande, and Nwachukwu each wrote a retrospective memo in July 2025. The board consolidated them into eight items.

**Screen sites for reimbursement designation before signing a lease.** The failure to evaluate Rural Health Clinic eligibility before committing to the Hahira location was, in pure dollar terms, probably the most expensive single omission in the project. A site eight miles north would have qualified for cost-based encounter reimbursement, worth an estimated $290,000 to $400,000 annually at current volume. That analysis takes a week and costs nothing.

**Select sites on patient origin data, not on rent.** The Baytree decision cost $455,000 to reverse. The mapping analysis that led to Bemiss Crossing used data the system already possessed in January 2024 and could have run before either lease was signed.

**Never sign a minimum-hour guarantee for a program with unproven volume.** The Halcyon contract was not predatory; it was standard. It was also structurally incompatible with a launch whose volume was uncertain. The system now requires that any temporary staffing agreement supporting a new service include a volume-linked flex provision or a 30-day hour reduction right, and that the finance office model agency cost per visit at 40, 70, and 100 percent of projected volume before signature.

**Do not make a launch dependent on a single vendor's fill rate.** Two unfilled placements delayed the second clinic by ten months. The contingency — open one clinic well before opening two — was never considered because the plan had no branch points.

**Assign a single accountable owner with no competing priorities.** The most consequential change in the entire program was putting Bonnaire on it full-time. The failures of the first seven months were not failures of intelligence or effort; they were the predictable output of a program that was the third priority of a person with three priorities.

**Build the referral pathway before opening the doors.** The assumption that demand would surface on its own was the deepest error in the business case, because it treated a clinic as a destination rather than as the endpoint of a route. The ED warm handoff cost approximately $60,000 to build and drove more incremental volume than the entire marketing budget.

**Require a named denial queue owner as a precondition of activating any new cost center.** Four months and $780,000 went by because a field was blank. The control that prevents recurrence is one line on a checklist and one weekly exception report.

**Re-baseline publicly and early.** The original 52-visits-per-day target was drawn from suburban benchmark data that did not describe this market. Carrying it for eleven months meant every status report was a report of failure, which had a corrosive effect on the team and delayed the honest conversation with the board. Bonnaire's December 2024 memo revising the target downward was the moment the program became manageable.

---

## What a reader in a similar position should take from it

**The business case is the risk, not the project.** Ogeechee Valley's clinics were fundamentally a good idea, executed by competent people, in a market that needed them. What nearly killed the program was a set of six unexamined assumptions carried from a slide deck into a $5.4 million commitment. If you are approving capital for a new access point, spend the extra two weeks writing down every assumption the model depends on, and next to each one write what you would observe in the first 90 days if it were false. Most of Ogeechee Valley's assumptions would have failed that test by May 2024.

**Access is a routing problem before it is a capacity problem.** Building capacity and waiting for demand is the single most common failure mode in ambulatory expansion. Patients do not reallocate themselves; systems reallocate them. The clinic that saw six patients a day and the clinic that saw fifteen-plus were the same clinic with the same staff — what changed was that somebody built a road to it. If your plan does not specify, concretely, the mechanism by which a specific patient at a specific decision point ends up at your new door, you do not have a plan.

**Locum staffing at scale will consume a startup.** The $583-per-visit figure is the number to remember. Agency clinicians are a reasonable bridge for a service with known, stable volume. They are a very poor bridge for a service whose volume is the thing you are trying to prove, because the cost is fixed and the revenue is not. If you must use them, model the cost per visit at your downside volume case, not your target case, and put that number in front of the board before signing.

**Revenue cycle failures are ownership failures.** The credentialing error itself was routine and would have been caught in two weeks under normal circumstances. What made it a $780,000 event was that a queue existed with no owner. New services generate new cost centers, new provider enrollments, new payer requirements, and new denial patterns, and every one of those is an opportunity for something to land in a place nobody looks. Before a new department bills its first claim, someone should be able to name the person who will see its first denial.

**Sunk cost is not an argument for continuation, and it is not an argument for closure either.** The October 2024 board meeting was the right meeting to have, at roughly the right time, with the right options on the table. The board did not continue the program because it had already spent $2.9 million; it continued the program because the underlying need was unchanged and the failures were diagnosable and structural. It also attached a shutdown trigger, which is what made continuation a decision rather than a drift. Both halves of that matter.

**Beating a re-baselined target is not the same as succeeding.** The clinics cleared their revised breakeven and contributed $210,000 in a quarter. They also came in at 60 percent of the volume the board originally funded, and they are not yet profitable on a fully loaded basis. Organizations that revise targets downward and then celebrate hitting them lose the ability to learn from anything. Ogeechee Valley reported both numbers side by side, every quarter, and that discipline is why the retrospective produced eight usable changes rather than a press release.

**Attribute honestly, especially when the news is good.** The system's margin improved by 3.5 points during the period the access program ran, and the program accounted for a minority of that improvement. Saying so cost nothing and preserved something valuable: the board's willingness to believe the next set of numbers Bonnaire brought them. A rural health system with 88 days cash on hand cannot afford many more $7 million experiments, which means it cannot afford to lose its ability to tell which ones worked.

## Postscript

As of the March 2026 quarter, the two access clinics were averaging 38 combined visits per day and contributing $340,000 for the quarter, turning positive on a fully loaded basis for the first time in February. Non-emergent ED share had settled at 27 percent. The system's operating margin for fiscal 2025 closed at plus 0.4 percent, its first positive year since 2021.

In January 2026 the board approved a third access clinic, in Nashville, Georgia, in Berrien County. The site was screened for Rural Health Clinic eligibility before the letter of intent was signed. It qualified. The ED referral pathway was built and tested before the lease was executed. No agency staffing was contracted; the clinic opened in May 2026 with two employed nurse practitioners, one of whom had precepted at Hahira as a student two years earlier.

Budget: $1.9 million. Actual at opening: $1.94 million.
