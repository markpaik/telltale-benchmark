# The Ferryman's Notebook

There is a job I think about often, though it no longer exists in most places: the ferryman on a small river crossing. Not the mythic one who takes coins from the dead — the ordinary one, who poled a flat boat between two banks a few hundred times a day, carrying farmers and letters and once, maybe, a bride.

What interests me is what he knew and didn't know. He knew the river completely: where the current turned mean in April, which sandbar migrated after storms, how the light fell on the water at every hour. And he knew almost nothing about his passengers. They stepped on mid-story. A woman crying, a man rehearsing a speech under his breath, two children arguing about a dog. He received them at their most transitional — between the errand and the outcome, the decision and the consequence — and then they stepped off and became invisible to him forever.

He never learned whether the speech went well. Whether the woman stopped crying. Whether the dog, when they got home, was fine.

---

I want to defend the ferryman's kind of knowing, because I think we've come to treat it as a lesser kind.

We are taught that real understanding is longitudinal. To know someone is to accumulate them: their history, their patterns, the callbacks and running jokes, the slow archive of a shared life. And this is true, and it is one of the great human achievements, this filing system of the heart.

But there is another way of knowing, and it happens in crossings. The stranger on the night train who tells you the thing they have told no one. The nurse who holds your hand for four minutes that you will remember for forty years, and whose name you never catch. The conversation at the funeral reception with your great-aunt's neighbor, who somehow says the one sentence that unlocks your grief. These encounters are not shallow because they are brief. Sometimes they are deep *because* they are brief — because nothing is being banked, nothing invested toward a future, and so the whole of the attention arrives at once, unhedged.

The ferryman could listen the way no husband could, precisely because he would never have to hear the second version of the story, the revised one, the one adjusted for consequences.

---

Here is a thing I have noticed about middles. Nearly everything we encounter, we encounter in the middle of it. You never meet a person at their beginning. The city you visit was mid-sentence before you arrived and continues after you leave. Even your own life, examined honestly, is a series of entrances into rooms where the conversation started long ago. We are all of us always catching up.

The usual response to this is anxiety — the fear of missing context, of not having read the earlier chapters. But the ferryman's response is different. He does not need to know how the river started in the mountains or where it goes past the bend. His stretch of it is complete. The crossing has a shape: a near bank, a far bank, a middle where the water is deepest and the passengers, for a moment, are nowhere at all — belonging to neither side, suspended, sometimes strangely honest.

A whole life can be lived well in a stretch of river. A whole meaning can fit inside a crossing.

---

I think about what he might have written down, if he kept a notebook. Not names — he didn't have them. Something more like: *Man with a lame horse, third time this month, lies about where he's going. Girl who reads while walking, nearly fell in again. Old woman who always pays with the same rubbed-smooth coin and takes it back as a joke.* A ledger of fragments. A book of middles.

And I think that notebook, if it survived, would tell us something the parish records never could. The records hold the beginnings and endings — baptisms, marriages, burials, the official punctuation. The ferryman's notebook would hold the sentences themselves: people in motion, mid-errand, mid-hope, being briefly and completely seen by someone with no stake in their story except the safe delivery of it to the other side.

That is a kind of love, I think, though it has no good name. The love of the crossing-keeper. Attention without accumulation. Care without keeping.

---

The bridge, when it came, was better in every measurable way. Faster, safer, open in all weather. No one mourns a ferry for practical reasons.

But a bridge does not know you are on it. That is the whole of its virtue and the whole of its lack. It gets you across without the crossing ever happening — without the moment in the middle of the water when you were held by someone, briefly, between one bank of your life and the other, and were, for the length of that passage, the only thing he was thinking about.

The river is still there. It always is. And somewhere on it, in whatever form, there is still the work of taking people across: receiving them mid-story, giving them the whole of the attention, and letting them go without needing to know how it ends.

It is not the lesser knowing. It is just the kind that leaves no records — only, on the far bank, someone walking away a little lighter, already forgetting the boat.
