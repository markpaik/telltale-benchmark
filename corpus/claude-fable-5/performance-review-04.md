## Section 1: Performance Against Annual Goals

### Goal 1: Sustain Press Three OEE at or above the plant goal of 72 percent

**Result: Exceeded.** Wanda closed the review period at 81 percent overall equipment effectiveness on press three, the best sustained OEE figure in the plant across all three shifts. This is not a number she hit in a good month and coasted on; her twelve-month trailing average never dropped below 77 percent, including the two months when the reorganization added presses five and six to her area of responsibility and split her attention across three crews instead of one. For context, the plant average this year was 69 percent, and no other press on any shift finished above 74 percent. Press three's performance was the single largest reason second shift held its throughput commitments during a year when the plant as a whole missed its on-time delivery gate two quarters running. When corporate asks why second shift's numbers held up while the plant's did not, the honest answer starts with Wanda Chee.

### Goal 2: Train and qualify a minimum of two new operators to independent status

**Result: Exceeded.** Wanda trained six operators to independent qualification this year, three times her goal. Two of those six now run presses on first and third shift, which means her training work is directly propping up crews she does not supervise and will never get OEE credit for. ### Goal 3: Reduce scrap attributable to press three to below 2.0 percent

**Result: Exceeded.** Press three finished at 1.7 percent scrap against the plant's 2.5 percent target and her stretch goal of 2.0 percent, in a year when the plant overall ran 4.2 percent. The single most significant event under this goal was the April die-temperature drift catch. Wanda noticed a thermal profile trending out of family across three consecutive billets — a drift subtle enough that the alarm limits never tripped — and shut the run down on her own authority. Metallurgy later confirmed the profile would have produced surface defects across the full order. The avoided scrap was valued at approximately $40,000, and I would add that the order was destined for the Phoenix integrator during the corrective action window; a second major defect escape to that customer during an open CAPA could have put the $22 million annual account at risk in a way no scrap dollar figure captures.

### Goal 4: Support the plant corrective action response to the June customer parts submission failure

**Result: Met.** Although the failed submission did not originate on press three, Wanda served on the cross-functional containment team, ran the sorting and re-inspection protocol on second shift, and rewrote the visual inspection standard that the customer's auditors reviewed and accepted in the closure package. The quality manager's summary to the customer specifically cited the revised standard work as objective evidence of systemic correction.

## Section 1: Performance Against Annual Goals

The full-year average lands at roughly goal, but the average is the least informative number on this page. The 29-hour figure is real, sustained, and driven by identifiable behavior changes: Rick began working the preventive maintenance schedule instead of running purely reactive, started closing work orders with actual root-cause notes instead of "repaired, tested OK," and built a bearing and seal kit stocking list for presses one and four that cut mean repair time on the two most common failure modes nearly in half. **Result: Exceeded.** This is the strongest line in Rick's review, and I want it stated without qualification. He designed the fix, installed it, and validated it. The quality manager's own words in the CAPA file: *"Root cause identification credited to R. Dombrowski. That said, I told Jaylen directly what I will also write here, because it is the developmental lesson inside the systemic failure: an experienced operator develops the reflex to distrust any instrument nobody has explicitly qualified them on, and to ask. He did not yet have that reflex in May. He has it now — his response to the escapes, described below, demonstrates it — and building that skeptical instinct into everything he touches is a named goal for the coming year.

Jaylen's response to the escapes deserves its own paragraph. He did not deflect, even though the root cause would have let him deflect fairly. He asked the quality technician to walk him through the full gauge calibration procedure on his own initiative before the corrective action training was even scheduled, wrote up a one-page gauge-verification quick reference that quality has since posted at press two, and asked me whether the other presses had the same gap. They did — press four's checklist had the identical omission — and his question is the reason it got fixed there before it produced an escape. A ten-month operator whose reaction to his own escape is to hunt the same failure elsewhere in the plant is showing exactly the instinct this plant is short of.
