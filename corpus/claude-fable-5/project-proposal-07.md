## 1. Executive Summary

Ozark Precision Components requests approval of an $18.9 million investment to reorganize the Springfield plant from a functional department layout into five product-aligned machining cells, install nine CNC machines and two robotic load stations, and stand up a second shift of 84 employees over 20 months. The first two cells go live in month seven; the full program completes in month 20.

The proposal exists because three problems have converged and none of them will wait.

**Commercially**, Vantage AgWorks — 38 percent of our revenue, roughly $47.9 million a year — has told us in writing that the 2027 rebid will require 6 percent annual price reductions for three years, a cumulative concession of approximately 16.9 percent, or it will dual-source. At current cost structure, absorbing that concession out of margin would reduce plant EBITDA from approximately $15.1 million to roughly $7.0 million by 2030 and leave us pricing below the cost floor on several high-volume part families. We cannot concede our way through this rebid, and we cannot afford to lose the account.

**Operationally**, scrap runs at 6.1 percent against a 2.5 percent target — an annual cost of approximately $6.9 million — and Quality Director Ivan Bogdanov's Pareto analysis ties 60 percent of that scrap to setup variation that a cellular layout with standardized fixturing removes structurally, not incrementally. The plant runs at 91 percent capacity with twelve-week lead times, which means we have no room to grow, no room to recover from disruption, and a lead-time position that is itself a competitive liability in the rebid.

**Demographically**, our average machinist is 54 years old and 61 machinists are retirement-eligible within three years. If we wait, we will attempt this transformation with a workforce that is walking out the door, and the tribal knowledge that currently compensates for our process weaknesses will leave with them. The cells and robotic load stations reduce dependence on scarce setup expertise and create roles that the local training pipeline can actually fill.

**The return.** At full run rate, the program delivers approximately $8.3 million in annual EBITDA improvement: $3.7 million from scrap reduction, $1.8 million from setup and flow productivity, and $2.8 million in net contribution from second-shift volume — enough to fund the Vantage price concession, service the $14.0 million of holding-company debt, and still improve plant EBITDA. Simple payback is 2.8 years on net benefits; project IRR is approximately 24 percent over a seven-year horizon; NPV at a 12 percent discount rate is approximately $13.6 million.

**Governance.** We are aware of the committee's experience with the 2023 finishing line, and we have structured this proposal so that the committee is not asked to trust a forecast. The capital releases in two tranches. Tranche 1 ($9.2 million, Cells 1 and 2 plus site preparation) is approved now. Tranche 2 ($9.7 million, Cells 3 through 5 and the second-shift ramp) releases only after a month-nine gate review confirms Cells 1 and 2 have hit defined scrap, changeover, and utilization thresholds. If the gates are missed, the committee retains $9.7 million of unspent capital and a plant that is still better than it is today.

**The decision requested on November 6** is approval of the full $18.9 million program with staged release as described in Section 10.

## 2. The Commercial and Operational Problem

### 2.1 The Vantage AgWorks rebid

Vantage AgWorks purchases hydraulic manifolds and valve bodies representing $47.9 million of our $126 million in revenue. In August, Vantage's commodity management team informed us that the 2027 rebid — supplier selections expected to be finalized in the third quarter of 2027 — will carry two conditions: 6 percent annual price reductions in each of 2028, 2029, and 2030, and demonstrated lead-time improvement. Suppliers who do not commit will see the business dual-sourced, with Vantage explicitly naming two competitors, one domestic and one in Monterrey, Mexico, that have already quoted.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. A 6 percent annual reduction compounds to a 16.9 percent cumulative price decline: on constant volume, roughly $2.9 million of revenue in year one, $5.6 million by year two, and $8.1 million annually by year three. Our current EBITDA margin on the Vantage book is approximately 13 percent. Conceding the full reduction with no cost action turns our largest account marginally profitable in 2028 and loss-making by 2030.

Losing the account is worse. Vantage volume absorbs a disproportionate share of plant fixed cost; a dual-sourcing outcome that takes even half the book would strand overhead across the remaining business and trigger a restructuring far more expensive than this proposal.

The only durable answer is to take cost out faster than Vantage takes price out. This proposal does that.

### 2.2 Scrap and quality

Scrap runs at 6.1 percent of production value against a 2.5 percent target — approximately $6.9 million per year in material, machine time, and rework labor. Ivan Bogdanov's twelve-month Pareto analysis attributes 60 percent of scrap events to setup variation: parts routed across five functional departments, refixtured at each operation by whichever machinist and whichever fixture happens to be available, with datum shifts and tool-offset errors accumulating across handoffs. This is not an operator-discipline problem. It is a layout problem. A part that crosses the plant four times has four opportunities to be scrapped; a part that completes in one cell with standardized, kitted fixturing has one.

The February OSHA citation — $47,000 for machine-guarding deficiencies on three legacy machining centers — is a related symptom. The affected machines date from the 1990s, were guarded to the standards of their era, and are among the units this program retires. The citation has been abated, but the underlying equipment vintage remains, and a repeat citation would carry willful-violation exposure.

### 2.3 Capacity and lead time

The plant runs at 91 percent of demonstrated capacity on a single production shift, with twelve-week quoted lead times against an industry standard of six to eight. At 91 percent, every machine breakdown, every quality escape, and every rush order cascades directly into customer delivery. We are declining quotable work today — approximately $19 million in the last twelve months by sales team tracking — because we cannot promise credible dates. The lead-time position also weakens our hand in the Vantage rebid, where responsiveness is an explicit evaluation criterion.

### 2.4 Workforce demographics

Our average machinist is 54. Sixty-one of our machinists — roughly 40 percent of the direct machining workforce — are retirement-eligible within three years. The plant's current functional layout is uniquely dependent on the deep setup expertise of exactly this cohort: complex, undocumented setups on aging machines are the glue holding 6.1 percent scrap from being worse. HR Director Tameka Sallis reports that the local pipeline — principally Ozarks Technical Community College's machining program — produced 34 graduates last year for the entire regional labor market. We will not replace 61 master machinists with 61 master machinists. We can, however, redesign the work so we do not have to: cells with standardized fixturing, conversational controls on new equipment, and robotic load stations on the highest-volume operations create operator roles that a two-year graduate can fill productively within months rather than years.

### 2.5 Why now

Each of these problems is individually manageable for another year or two. Together, they are not. The Vantage rebid concludes in the third quarter of 2027; we need Cells 1 and 2 demonstrably running — with the scrap and lead-time evidence to present to Vantage's sourcing team — by mid-2027, which requires equipment orders in December 2026 given 22-to-28-week machine tool lead times. The retirement window means every year of delay increases execution risk, because the experienced machinists we need to lead cells and train new hires are the same people who are eligible to leave. A November 6 approval supports a December order placement and a month-seven (July 2027) go-live for Cells 1 and 2. A deferral to the next committee cycle pushes go-live past the rebid decision and forces us to negotiate with Vantage on promises rather than evidence.

## 3. The Proposed Solution: Cellular Reorganization

### 3.1 Concept

The plant today is organized functionally — turning, milling, drilling, deburring, wash, and inspection in separate departments — with parts traveling an average of 2,900 feet and queuing between every operation. The proposal reorganizes the floor into five machining cells, each aligned to a product family and each completing parts from raw casting or bar to finished, inspected component:

| Cell | Product family | Share of revenue | Key equipment | Robotic load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cell 1 | High-volume Vantage manifolds | 22% | 2 new 5-axis HMCs, 1 retained HMC | Yes |
| Cell 2 | Vantage valve bodies | 16% | 2 new turning/milling centers, 1 retained VMC | Yes |
| Cell 3 | Construction-equipment manifolds | 19% | 2 new 5-axis HMCs, 2 retained machines | No |
| Cell 4 | Low-volume / high-mix valve bodies | 14% | 2 new multi-axis turning centers, 3 retained machines | No |
| Cell 5 | Prototype, aftermarket, and short-run | 9% | 1 new VMC, 4 retained machines | No |

The remaining 20 percent of revenue (finishing-intensive and outside-processed parts) flows through the existing finishing line, which — as discussed in Section 8 — the cell layout will finally load properly.

### 3.2 Equipment

**Nine CNC machines.** Six horizontal five-axis machining centers, two multi-axis turning/milling centers, and one vertical machining center, all specified with pallet changers, probing, and tool-life management. The five-axis capability is central to the scrap case: it collapses three or four separate setups into one, eliminating the datum-shift errors that drive 60 percent of current scrap. Fourteen legacy machines are retired, including all three units cited by OSHA; their disposal value (est. $0.4 million) is treated as contingency offset and not counted in the return.

**Two robotic load stations**, one each in Cells 1 and 2, tending the highest-volume Vantage part numbers. Each station runs lights-out for the last two hours of each shift and through breaks, adding approximately 11 percent effective spindle utilization on the plant's most valuable machines. The stations carry integrated, interlocked guarding that permanently resolves the machine-guarding exposure on these operations. They also decouple our highest-volume output from the demographic risk: a robot-tended cell needs one operator overseeing three machines, not three machinists.

**Fixturing and metrology.** Standardized zero-point workholding across all cells so that every setup locates identically regardless of operator; kitted, documented setups for the top 120 part numbers; and two shop-floor CMMs (one per high-volume cell) so inspection happens inside the cell rather than in a queue across the plant.

### 3.3 What the cells deliver

- **Scrap from 6.1 percent to a validated 2.9 percent** (we hold 0.4 points of conservatism against the 2.5 percent target): worth approximately $3.7 million annually. Bogdanov's team has piloted the standardized fixturing approach on one Vantage manifold family since June; scrap on that family has fallen from 5.8 percent to 3.1 percent in ninety days with fixturing changes alone, before any new equipment.
- **Setup and changeover time down approximately 55 percent** on cellularized families, worth $1.8 million annually in recovered machine hours and reduced overtime.
- **Lead times from twelve weeks to five** on cellularized product, because parts no longer queue between departments. This is the operational evidence we will put in front of Vantage.
- **Capacity headroom** for the second shift described in Section 4, moving the plant from 91 percent utilization on one shift to approximately 68 percent across two — restoring resilience and the ability to quote the roughly $19 million in demand we currently turn away.

## 4. Staffing, Hiring, and Training

### 4.1 The second shift

A second production shift of 84 employees — 58 machine operators, 10 setup/cell leads, 6 quality technicians, 4 material handlers, 4 maintenance technicians, and 2 shift supervisors — is stood up in three waves aligned to cell go-lives, reaching full strength in month 20. Tameka Sallis has costed the shift at $5.4 million per year fully loaded, including a $1.75/hour second-shift differential benchmarked against the Springfield labor market.

### 4.2 Where 84 people come from

We do not assume the local pipeline solves this alone. The sourcing plan:

- **Ozarks Technical Community College partnership (est. 30–35 hires over 20 months).** We have signed a letter of intent with OTC to sponsor a dedicated evening cohort beginning January 2027, with Ozark Precision funding tuition for committed hires and providing two retired machinists as adjunct instructors. This grows the pipeline rather than competing for the existing 34 annual graduates.
- **Internal upskilling (est. 20 hires).** Current material handlers, deburr operators, and assemblers move into operator roles on the new, easier-to-run equipment through a 12-week internal certification program.
- **Regional recruiting (est. 20–25 hires).** Second-shift operator roles on new five-axis equipment with robotic tending are genuinely attractive postings in this market; our current recruiting problem is that we are offering second-tier work on thirty-year-old machines.
- **Retiree phase-back (est. 8–10 part-time).** Retirement-eligible machinists are offered phased part-time roles as trainers and setup mentors for up to 24 months, converting the demographic problem into the training solution. Six machinists have already expressed interest in Sallis's soundings.

### 4.3 Training

Each cell go-live is preceded by an eight-week training block: four weeks of vendor-led programming and maintenance training (included in equipment purchase contracts), and four weeks of supervised production on the cell before rate commitments apply. Every cell operates from documented standard work — setups, offsets, inspection frequencies — created during commissioning, so that the process knowledge currently held in 61 heads is captured on paper and in the control programs before those heads retire. Training and ramp costs of $0.5 million are inside the capital budget.

### 4.4 Retention

The retirement cliff makes retention of mid-career machinists the single most important workforce variable. Concurrent with this proposal (funded from operations, not this capital request), we are implementing a skills-based pay progression for cell leads and a stay-incentive for the 18 machinists identified as critical to commissioning. Cost is approximately $0.3 million annually and is reflected in the financial model.

## 5. Implementation Phasing

The program is phased so that production commitments are never dependent on unproven cells, and so that the committee's Tranche 2 capital is never at risk before Tranche 1 has demonstrated results.

| Phase | Months | Scope | Production protection |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Order & prepare | 1–4 (Dec 26–Mar 27) | Equipment orders placed month 1; foundations, electrical, and coolant infrastructure installed in the vacant northeast bay; fixturing design and standard work for Cells 1–2 | No disruption — new cells built in currently unused floor space |
| 1. Cells 1–2 | 5–7 (Apr–Jun 27) | Machines installed and commissioned; robotic load stations integrated; first training wave (28 second-shift hires) | Legacy departments run at full rate in parallel; Vantage parts transfer to cells only after first-article and 30-day capability approval |
| Gate review | Month 9 (Aug 27) | Committee gate: release of Tranche 2 (see §10) | — |
| 2. Cells 3–4 | 9–14 (Aug 27–Jan 28) | Legacy machines relocated or retired in sequence; second training wave (34 hires); banked inventory (6 weeks, cost included in working-capital line) covers each machine move | Each machine moves only after its cell successor is qualified; no customer sees a missed shipment due to installation |
| 3. Cell 5 & full ramp | 15–20 (Feb–Aug 28) | Final cell; third training wave (22 hires); second shift at full 84; legacy departments decommissioned | Full-rate qualification complete before decommissioning |

Cells 1 and 2 — the Vantage cells — go live in month seven, July 2027, in time to demonstrate scrap and lead-time performance to Vantage's sourcing team before rebid selections are finalized in the third quarter of 2027. This sequencing is deliberate: the committee's capital directly arms the commercial negotiation on our largest account.

Yusuf Abdirahman is the accountable executive for execution, supported by a dedicated program manager (an internal appointment, backfilled), the equipment vendor's turnkey installation team, and a cell-design consultant retained for Phase 0 (fees inside the budget). Weekly program reviews run at the plant; Chelsea Nakagawa will provide the committee a one-page dashboard monthly and a formal review at the month-nine gate.

## 7. Alternatives Considered

The committee should approve this proposal only if the alternatives are genuinely worse. We evaluated four.

### 7.1 Do nothing

Retain the functional layout and current cost structure. Vantage dual-sources or exits; scrap continues at $6.9 million annually; 61 retirements over three years progressively degrade even current performance; the OSHA exposure on legacy equipment persists. We model plant EBITDA declining from $15.1 million to below $6 million by 2029 with the loss of half the Vantage book, at which point Kestrel Bridge faces a restructuring decision rather than an investment decision. Rejected.

### 7.2 Concede the price out of margin

Accept the 6/6/6 concession with no cost action. This holds the account and requires no capital, and it is the correct comparison base case for the return math in Section 6.3. But it reduces plant EBITDA by $8.1 million annually by 2030, makes the Vantage book loss-making in year three, does nothing for scrap, lead time, the retirement cliff, or safety exposure — and, critically, positions us to face the *2030* rebid, where further concessions are certain to be demanded, with no cost improvements banked and no capital appetite remaining. It converts a solvable 2027 problem into an unsolvable 2030 one. Rejected as a strategy; retained as the financial base case.

### 7.3 Outsource finishing and redeploy that capital

Sell or idle the 2023 finishing line, outsource finishing to regional processors, and free floor space and management attention. We examined this seriously given the line's 40 percent utilization. It fails on three grounds. First, quoted outside-processing costs add $1.9 million annually versus in-house cost even at current poor utilization, and add 8–12 days of lead time — directly contrary to the Vantage requirement. Second, the finishing line's underutilization is a symptom of the upstream problem this proposal fixes: the functional layout starves it with erratic, batched flow. Our modeling shows the cell layout lifts finishing-line utilization to approximately 70 percent by feeding it level, sequenced work — which is the best available answer to the committee's legitimate question about that 2023 investment. Third, outsourcing surrenders a differentiator: single-source machining-through-finishing is explicitly valued in Vantage's supplier scorecard. Rejected, with the finishing-line recovery incorporated into this program's benefits (conservatively excluded from the return math in Section 6.3; any finishing-line contribution is upside).

### 7.4 A smaller program — Cells 1 and 2 only

Invest $9.2 million in the two Vantage cells and stop. This addresses the rebid but leaves 44 percent of revenue on the legacy layout, captures only $4.1 million of the $8.3 million benefit, forgoes the second shift (the legacy departments cannot support one), and leaves the plant at 91 percent utilization with no growth path. It also produces a hybrid plant — half cellular, half functional — that is operationally harder to run than either pure state. The staged-release structure in Section 10 gives the committee this alternative *as a downside protection* without choosing it as the plan: if the month-nine gate fails, the committee has, in effect, executed alternative 7.4 and retained $9.7 million.

## 8. The 2023 Finishing Line: What We Learned

We owe the committee a direct answer on the finishing line before asking for further equipment capital. The 2023 investment was approved on a capacity forecast without committed demand, installed as a standalone asset into a functional layout that could not feed it consistently, and lacked defined utilization gates or a named accountable owner for its ramp. It runs at 40 percent.

This proposal differs on every one of those points. Demand is not forecast; it is standing in front of us — a customer representing 38 percent of revenue with a written price ultimatum, twelve-week lead times, and $19 million of tracked declined quotes. The equipment is not a standalone asset; it is a reorganization of flow, validated by a ninety-day pilot already showing results. Accountability is named (Abdirahman), reviewed monthly by the committee, and enforced by a capital gate with published thresholds. And the program is the most credible path to fixing the finishing line itself, by finally giving it the level upstream flow it was designed for.

## Appendix B: Cell Layout (Narrative Summary; Drawings in Binder)

The plant floor is 214,000 square feet, of which the northeast bay (31,000 sq ft, vacated when assembly was consolidated in 2021) is currently used for overflow storage. Cells 1 and 2 are built there, which is what allows Phase 1 to proceed with zero disruption to current production.

Key layout facts:

- **Average part travel distance** falls from 2,900 feet (current state, measured across the top 40 part numbers by spaghetti-diagram study, March 2026) to 310 feet in the future state.
- **Cells 1–2** occupy the northeast bay: 14,800 sq ft combined, with shared raw material staging, a shared shop-floor CMM room between them, and direct forklift access to shipping.
- **Cells 3–5** are built progressively into floor space vacated as legacy departments retire, per the Phase 2–3 sequence. The final layout releases approximately 22,000 sq ft, which is reserved for the finishing line's level-flow staging lanes (Appendix B-4 drawing) — the physical mechanism by which finishing utilization recovers.
- **Material flow** is one-directional (receiving, east, to shipping, west) in the future state; the current layout has 14 documented backflow loops.
- All new machine foundations are standard 8-inch reinforced slab except the two largest HMCs, which require isolated 24-inch foundations (included in the $1.9M facility line; geotechnical survey completed September 2026, no adverse findings).

### C.1 Twelve-month scrap Pareto (October 2025 – September 2026)

Total scrap cost: $6.94M on $126M revenue (6.1 percent of production value). Root-cause attribution from 2,340 logged scrap events:

| Root cause category | % of scrap cost | $M | Addressed by program? |
|---|---|---|---|

The 60 percent setup-variation figure cited in the body is the sum of the first three categories. The model's claimed $3.7M annual benefit assumes elimination of 80 percent of setup-variation scrap plus half of the legacy-machine-condition scrap, discounted a further 10 percent for conservatism.
