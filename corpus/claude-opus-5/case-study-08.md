# When the Machine Arrived Before the Plan
## Saluda Textile Works and the $11.4 Million Automation of a Single-Plant Business

---

### I. The Company

Saluda Textile Works occupies about 340,000 square feet on a site off Highway 221 outside Spartanburg, South Carolina, on land the company has held since the late 1960s. It is a single-plant business. There is no second facility to absorb a shutdown, no sister operation to borrow capacity from, and no corporate parent to backstop a bad quarter. Everything the company makes, it makes there.

By 2022 the product mix had drifted a long way from the commodity woven goods that built the place. Saluda made technical fabrics: coated and laminated substrates for automotive seating and door panels, flame-retardant and abrasion-rated materials for commercial furniture, and a smaller book of filtration and industrial goods. Sales were $132 million. Headcount was 480, of whom roughly 375 were hourly production employees running three shifts, with the balance in maintenance, quality, engineering, scheduling, sales, and administration. Gross margin ran near 30 percent. Cost of goods sold was about $92 million, and direct labor accounted for 31 percent of it — roughly $28.5 million, including $2.4 million in overtime and the payroll burden that came with it.

Two operating numbers defined the plant's character in 2022, and both were bad.

The first was defects. Saluda's internal defect rate — material that failed final inspection and had to be scrapped, downgraded, reprocessed, or credited back to a customer — was 3.8 percent. In technical fabrics that is not catastrophic, but it is not competitive either. The all-in annual cost of that 3.8 percent, once the company counted raw material, the conversion cost already sunk into rejected yardage, reprocessing labor, expedited freight to cover shortfalls, and customer chargebacks, was approximately $4.4 million a year. Final inspection was performed by human inspectors on light tables, on rolling goods, at the end of an eleven-hour shift. Inspector-to-inspector variation was well documented internally and had been for years.

The second was absenteeism, which stood at 9 percent. It was worst on second and third shift and worst in the finishing department, where the work was hot, repetitive, and physically demanding. Absenteeism drove overtime, overtime drove fatigue, fatigue drove defects, and defects drove more rework hours, which drove more overtime. Everyone in the building understood this loop. Nobody had broken it.

Against that backdrop sat a structural exposure that the leadership team had discussed for years and never resolved: one customer, an automotive interiors manufacturer supplying seat and door-panel assemblies to two North American OEM platforms, accounted for 44 percent of Saluda's revenue. That was roughly $58 million a year from a single account. President Dagmar Ellingson had raised customer concentration in every annual planning cycle since she took the role. Every year the answer was the same — the work was good work, the relationship was thirty years old, and diversification would take capital and time that the business was already spending elsewhere.

---

### II. The Demand

In November 2022, the customer's purchasing director asked for a meeting in Spartanburg rather than the usual video call. Ellingson and VP of Operations Malik Rasheed took the meeting in the front conference room.

The message was direct and, by the standards of automotive supply, not unusual. The customer's own OEM contracts had been repriced downward. Its interiors business was under pressure from lower-cost sourcing in Mexico and from two Asian suppliers who had recently qualified on competing platforms. The customer needed 12 percent out of Saluda's price, phased over three years — four points a year, effective at each model-year changeover. If Saluda could not commit, the customer would begin resourcing the business at the next platform refresh, which meant a wind-down starting in roughly eighteen months.

Twelve percent of $58 million is about $7 million a year at full run-rate. Saluda's gross margin on that account was thinner than the company average — approximately 24 percent, or about $13.9 million of gross profit. A $7 million price reduction would remove half of it. The company's operating income in 2022 was around $8 million. On a straight pass-through, the demand consumed most of the company's profit.

The alternative was worse. Losing 44 percent of revenue from a single-plant operation is not a revenue problem; it is a fixed-cost problem. Saluda's overhead structure — the coaters, the finishing line, the boiler house, the wastewater treatment, the salaried staff — was sized for $132 million. At $74 million it would have been catastrophically oversized. Rasheed's rough modeling in December 2022 showed the plant going cash-negative within two quarters of the customer's departure and requiring a workforce reduction on the order of 200 people to stabilize. There was no version of losing the account that the business survived intact.

So the question was never whether to accept the price reduction. The question was whether Saluda could take 12 percent out of its own cost base faster than the customer took 12 percent out of its price.

---

### III. Building the Case

Ellingson and Rasheed spent December 2022 and January 2023 building an investment case with the controller and with Plant Engineer Josefina Huerta, who had joined Saluda in 2019 from a converting operation in North Carolina.

The analysis focused on two areas where cost was demonstrably being destroyed rather than merely spent.

**Finishing.** The finishing line — tentering, heat-setting, and roll handling — was manually loaded and manually doffed. It required 22 operators per shift across three shifts, plus relief. It was the department with the worst absenteeism, the highest overtime, and the highest injury rate. Automating material handling and roll transfer at the back end of finishing would eliminate an estimated 38 to 42 direct positions and reduce the overtime that absenteeism in that department generated.

**Inspection.** Replacing human final inspection with an optical inspection system — line-scan cameras, controlled lighting, and defect-classification software — was projected to take the defect rate from 3.8 percent to somewhere between 1.0 and 1.5 percent, because the system would catch defects earlier in the process rather than at final roll-out, allowing intervention before hundreds of yards were committed. That was the larger of the two prizes.

The capital request that went to the board in February 2023 totaled $9.2 million:

- $6.4 million in equipment (automated finishing handling, three optical inspection stations, conveyance and roll-handling infrastructure)
- $1.4 million in installation, electrical, and civil work
- $0.9 million in software, integration, and MES connection
- $0.5 million in contingency

The projected annual savings, as written in Rasheed's memo, were $4.6 million: $2.4 million from direct labor elimination, $1.5 million from overtime reduction, and — conservatively stated, the memo said — $700,000 net from defect reduction after accounting for the volume the plant would now need to run. Simple payback: 24 months.

The controller pushed back on double-counting. A meaningful share of the $1.5 million overtime saving came from the same finishing positions being counted in the $2.4 million labor line. Her revised figure was $3.8 million of annual savings and a 29-month payback. Rasheed did not contest it. The board approved on the revised number.

What the case did not contain, and this became the central issue eighteen months later, was any line item, schedule, or named owner for what happened to the 38 to 42 people whose jobs the equipment would eliminate. The memo used the word "attrition." The plant's voluntary turnover was running about 18 percent a year, and the assumption — never tested, never modeled by department, never converted into a hiring freeze or a transfer matrix — was that natural turnover would absorb the displacement over the commissioning period.

---

### IV. The Money

Financing closed in March 2023.

A regional bank provided a $6 million equipment term loan at seven years, secured by the new assets and a second position on the real estate, with covenants on fixed-charge coverage and minimum tangible net worth. The bank's credit committee spent most of its diligence on exactly the right question — customer concentration — and conditioned the facility on Saluda providing quarterly evidence of the automotive relationship's status.

The South Carolina jobs and development authority approved a $1.5 million grant tied to capital investment and job retention. The retention covenant was straightforward: Saluda would maintain no fewer than 460 positions at the Spartanburg site through December 31, 2024, verified against quarterly wage filings. Falling below the floor triggered clawback provisions.

Ellingson signed that covenant knowing the project's own business case called for eliminating 38 to 42 positions from a base of 480. The arithmetic — 480 minus 40 is 440, which is below 460 — was not hidden. It was simply assumed away on the theory that the plant would backfill into growth, that some displaced operators would move into other departments, and that revenue expansion from improved throughput would justify hiring in the second half of 2024. Nobody wrote that assumption down as a condition, and nobody was made accountable for tracking it.

The remaining $1.7 million came from cash and the revolver.

---

### V. Procurement and the Long Quiet

Saluda selected Brandywine Automation, a mid-sized integrator based in eastern Pennsylvania, over two competitors. Brandywine's bid was neither the cheapest nor the most expensive. It won on two grounds: it proposed a single point of accountability for both the finishing handling and the optical inspection scope, rather than requiring Saluda to integrate two vendors; and it had installed a similar inspection package at a coated-fabrics plant in Georgia that Huerta visited in January 2023 and found impressive.

The contract was signed in March 2023. Mechanical completion was scheduled for August 2023, with run-off and acceptance testing in September and full production release in October. Liquidated damages for late completion were capped at $275,000.

The Georgia reference visit, in hindsight, was the most consequential forty-five minutes of the entire program, and it was misread. The Georgia plant ran a narrow range of substrates in a small number of colors under stable lighting in a purpose-built inspection hall. Saluda ran 140 active SKUs across coated, laminated, brushed, and napped constructions in colorways from bone white to carbon black, in a converted 1970s bay with mixed high-bay lighting, skylights on the north wall, and forty years of accumulated ambient reflection. The optical challenge was not comparable. Nobody on either side raised it, because nobody framed the reference visit as a technical due-diligence exercise. It was framed as a confidence-building exercise, and it built confidence.

Spring and summer 2023 were quiet. Brandywine reported progress against milestones in monthly written updates. Huerta made two trips to Pennsylvania to witness factory acceptance testing on the handling equipment, which went well. The inspection system was not tested at Brandywine's facility with Saluda's actual materials; the plan was to tune it on site during commissioning, using production material, over an estimated three weeks.

In late July, Brandywine advised that a control-cabinet supplier had slipped and mechanical completion would move to late September. In September it moved to November. In November it moved to January 2024. The final commissioning milestone was achieved in February 2024 — five months late against a contract that had promised October 2023 production release.

The delay itself, Rasheed later said, was survivable. What was not survivable was that Saluda ran its side of the plan on the original schedule anyway.

### VI. Cutover

Here is the sequence that produced the crisis, and it is worth reading slowly, because the individual decisions were each defensible and the aggregate was a disaster.

The finishing handling equipment was mechanically complete and functional in October 2023, ahead of the inspection system. It worked. Once it was commissioned and running, the 38 manual roll-handling and doffing positions on the finishing line had no work content. Rasheed made the call to stand them down at the end of October rather than pay 38 people across three shifts to watch automated equipment for an indeterminate period.

Two things had gone wrong before that decision was made, and both were invisible in the monthly project report.

First, the attrition assumption had not held. Voluntary turnover at Saluda over the eighteen months prior had run below the historical average — the local labor market had cooled, and people were staying. Turnover in the finishing department specifically had run at about 11 percent, not 18. Instead of the roughly 40 natural vacancies the plan assumed would open up somewhere in the plant, there were 14, and 9 of those were in departments requiring skills the finishing operators did not have. Nobody had tracked this. There was no monthly report on it, because there was no owner for it.

Second, no retraining program existed. Not a delayed one, not an underfunded one — none. Human Resources Director Clarence Boatwright had raised the question in a May 2023 staff meeting and had been told the timing was premature because the equipment schedule was still moving. The item did not appear on an action list. It did not reappear.

So on October 30, 2023, 38 operators — average tenure just over eleven years, some of them second-generation Saluda employees — were told at shift change that their positions were being eliminated. Twelve were offered transfers into open roles, seven of which were on third shift at lower classification. Nine accepted. The remaining 29 were offered severance of two weeks per year of service and told the company would "work to identify opportunities."

Within eleven weeks, 21 of the 38 had left the company entirely, including six of the nine who had accepted transfers.

Meanwhile the optical inspection system came online in December for pilot running and was catastrophically miscalibrated for Saluda's actual product mix. It falsely rejected 14 percent of good material.

### VII. The 14 Percent

A false-reject rate of 14 percent on a system installed to reduce a 3.8 percent defect rate is not a tuning issue. It is a system that is actively destroying value.

The mechanics were straightforward once anyone looked. The defect-classification models had been trained during commissioning on a limited sample set — mostly mid-weight coated goods in medium colorways, run over a period of weeks when the plant happened to be building a particular platform's inventory. Presented with dark napped material under the inspection hall's mixed lighting, the system read surface texture variation as defect. Presented with high-gloss coated goods, it read specular reflection off the skylights as defect. Presented with anything at the throughput rates finishing was now capable of, it read motion artifact as defect.

The operational consequences compounded. Every false reject pulled a roll out of flow to a hold area for manual adjudication — but manual adjudication capacity had just been eliminated, because those were the inspectors and handlers who had been stood down. Hold inventory built. Finishing throttled back to keep the hold area from overflowing. Throughput fell 18 percent below the 2022 baseline and stayed there for four months, from December 2023 through March 2024.

Falling 18 percent below baseline while carrying the full fixed-cost load of a plant that had just spent $9.2 million to go faster is an expensive way to spend a winter. Saluda missed shipments. It expedited freight at its own cost. It outsourced finishing on two SKUs to a converter in Georgia at a per-yard cost roughly 40 percent above internal. Overtime went *up*, not down, because the remaining workforce was covering both the manual adjudication that had no staffing plan and the recovery of a schedule that was falling behind.

The customer noticed. In January 2024 Saluda's on-time delivery to the automotive account fell to 91 percent from a trailing average of 98. The customer's supplier quality engineer opened a corrective action. This was the account whose price demand had triggered the entire investment.

### VIII. The January Meeting

Saluda's plant committee is not a union body. It is an elected employee committee that has existed at the site since the 1990s, with a standing monthly meeting with plant leadership and a charter covering safety, scheduling, and grievances. Its chair in 2023–24 was Rosario Villanueva, a 16-year employee working in quality, who had been elected the previous spring.

Through the fall of 2023 Villanueva had raised the displacement issue repeatedly, in the standing meeting and in writing. The written record later became important. Her October 12 note asked what the retraining plan was for the finishing operators and received a reply that the company was "evaluating options." Her November 9 note listed by name six operators who had been told nothing about their status and asked for a timeline. Her December 7 note stated that people were leaving and that the plant was losing operators it would need. She received an acknowledgment.

The January 18, 2024 meeting was different because Villanueva had spent the holidays doing arithmetic.

She brought three sheets of paper. The first listed the 21 departures by name, hire date, and department, with a total of 238 years of accumulated service. The second was a hand-built tally, assembled from the quality department's own hold logs, showing 4,100 rolls put on hold since December 1 for false reject, and the labor hours the plant had spent adjudicating them — 2,900 hours, which she noted was more inspection labor than the eliminated positions had represented. The third was a petition signed by 214 employees stating that the plant's operators had no confidence in the company's management of the automation program and that they were prepared to withhold labor.

She read the third sheet last. The threat of a walkout was explicit and it was credible, in a plant already running 18 percent below baseline with a customer corrective action open.

Ellingson was in the room. She has since described the meeting as the point at which she understood that the project had two failure modes, technical and human, and that the company had been managing only one of them, badly.

She did three things in the meeting, which is worth noting, because the alternative — taking it under advisement — would probably have produced a walkout.

She acknowledged, without qualification, that the company had eliminated the positions before it had a plan for the people, and that this was a management failure and not a consequence of the vendor delay. She committed to a written retraining and placement program within fourteen days, with Boatwright named as owner and a standing weekly review with the committee. And she committed that no further position would be eliminated at the site until the inspection system was accepted and stable, in writing, that week.

The walkout did not happen.

### IX. Ten Weeks

The recovery ran on two tracks in parallel from late January through early April 2024.

**The technical track** was Huerta's. Her first act was to stop treating the false-reject problem as a vendor warranty matter to be escalated through commercial channels and start treating it as an engineering problem to be solved on the floor. She demanded — and Ellingson backed with a formal notice of default under the contract — that Brandywine place a vision engineer on site continuously rather than flying one in for three-day visits. Brandywine, facing a default notice, a liquidated-damages claim, and a reference customer about to become an anti-reference, agreed. The engineer, a woman named on the site badge log for 51 of the next 70 days, worked out of a desk in the quality office.

The work took ten weeks and was unglamorous.

*Lighting first.* Before touching a single algorithm, Huerta's team rebuilt the optical environment. The north-wall skylights in the inspection bay were blacked out. The mixed high-bay fixtures over the three inspection stations were replaced with tuned LED arrays at controlled color temperature. Each station was enclosed with light-tight curtaining. This took three weeks and cost about $340,000, none of which had been in the capital plan, because the capital plan had treated the inspection hall as an existing facility rather than as part of the instrument.

*Then the training set.* The classification models were retrained on a deliberately constructed sample library spanning the actual product mix — Huerta pulled physical samples covering 96 of the 140 active SKUs, including every dark and every high-gloss construction, and ran them through the system under the new lighting with known defects seeded in and catalogued. The library took four weeks to assemble and became a permanent asset; Saluda still adds to it when a new construction is introduced, and no new SKU is released to production until it is in the library.

*Then thresholds by product family.* Rather than a single global sensitivity setting, the team built family-specific threshold profiles — eleven of them — that the system selected automatically from the production order. This was the change that broke the back of the problem. False rejects on the worst-performing family fell from 22 percent to under 2 percent.

*Then the second look.* Huerta and Rasheed made a decision that ran against the entire premise of the investment: they installed a manual second-look station downstream of automated inspection, permanently staffed, where any roll flagged by the system is reviewed by a human inspector before disposition. It cost about $265,000 to build and roughly $190,000 a year to staff.

The purchase of the system had been justified on the elimination of manual inspection. Reinstating manual inspection looked like an admission of failure, and internally, some people called it that. It was not. The second-look station does three things the automated system cannot: it adjudicates edge cases at a cost far below the cost of scrapping good material; it generates a continuous labeled data stream — every human override is a training example — that has kept the models improving for two years; and it gives the plant a human being who can see a problem developing upstream and pick up a phone. Huerta's position, stated at the time and vindicated since, was that a 98-percent-accurate machine plus a human on the exceptions beats a hypothetical 100-percent-accurate machine that does not exist.

**The human track** was Boatwright's, and he had fourteen days to produce a program.

What he produced was narrower and better than what would have been produced with more time. He did not build a general upskilling curriculum. He identified the specific roles the automated plant actually needed and could not fill — equipment technician, vision-system operator, controls maintenance support — and he built a path into those roles for people already in the building.

Forty-four operators entered the program between February and August 2024. The structure was 120 hours of classroom instruction delivered in partnership with Spartanburg Community College, paid at straight time, combined with a structured on-the-floor progression under a named mentor and a competency sign-off at three stages. Completion moved an operator from the production classification to the technician classification at $3.10 more per hour — roughly a 13 percent increase against the plant's average production wage, or about $6,450 a year per person. The full-cohort wage impact was approximately $284,000 annually. The program itself, including curriculum development, the college partnership, instructor time, and the backfill hours to cover people in class, cost about $520,000.

Boatwright made two design choices that mattered more than the curriculum. First, priority admission went to the operators displaced from finishing who were still employed, and the company reached out individually to all 21 who had left, offering re-entry at prior seniority. Six came back. Second, the weekly review with Villanueva's committee was not a briefing; the committee reviewed the selection list, and disputes about who got a seat were resolved in that room. Boatwright has said that ceding that control was the only reason the program had credibility with the floor within its first month.

### X. Coming Back

Throughput crossed the 2022 baseline in May 2024 and did not go below it again. The four-month trough — December through March — cost the company approximately $480,000 in expedited freight, outsourced conversion, and premium labor, on top of the margin on volume it simply did not produce.

The customer's corrective action was closed in June 2024 after Saluda delivered a containment plan, the retuning documentation, and four consecutive months of on-time delivery above 98 percent. Ellingson has said that the single most useful thing the company did during that period was to tell the customer the truth in February — that the inspection system was miscalibrated, that the plant had a specific ten-week plan with a named engineer on site, and that Saluda was absorbing the recovery cost rather than seeking relief. The customer's supplier quality engineer, she said, had seen a hundred suppliers explain a problem away and very few show up with a Gantt chart and a badge log.

By the fourth quarter of 2024, the defect rate had fallen below 1.5 percent. By the second quarter of 2025 it was 0.9 percent, comfortably below the 1.0 to 1.5 percent range the original business case had projected. Throughput ran 23 percent above the 2022 baseline. Overtime for the trailing twelve months was $700,000, down from $2.4 million. Absenteeism had fallen from 9 percent to 5.4 percent — the automation removed the physically punishing finishing work that had generated the worst attendance, and the technician track gave people something to show up for.

Cost per yard, measured on a consistent product-mix basis against 2022, was down 14 percent.

That number is the whole case. The customer had demanded 12 percent. Saluda took out 14 and absorbed roughly two points of input-cost inflation in the same period. It gave the price back and held its margin. It did not win — it broke even against a demand that would otherwise have cost it half the gross profit on nearly half its revenue — but breaking even was the objective.

In July 2025 the customer signed a five-year renewal. The agreement covers eleven part numbers across two platforms with committed minimum volumes valued at $58 million over the term — roughly $11.6 million a year of guaranteed floor, against total annual business with the account that continues to run substantially above the committed minimum. The floor is the part that mattered. Saluda's bank had spent two and a half years asking quarterly about customer concentration; a contractual minimum through 2030 changed the conversation, and the company refinanced the equipment loan in September 2025 at 140 basis points inside the original rate.

### XI. The Numbers

**Program cost.** The approved capital was $9.2 million. Total spend was $11.4 million, an overrun of $2.2 million, or 24 percent. The overrun consisted of:

- $410,000 — extended vendor engineering presence on site during the ten-week recovery, including travel and living, above contract scope
- $340,000 — lighting, skylight blackout, and enclosure work in the inspection bay, unscoped in the original plan
- $520,000 — the retraining program, curriculum, college partnership, and backfill hours
- $265,000 — construction and equipping of the manual second-look station
- $480,000 — expedited freight, outsourced conversion, and premium labor during the four-month throughput trough
- $185,000 — legal costs on the vendor default notice and advisory costs on the grant renegotiation

This figure is net of $275,000 recovered from Brandywine Automation under the liquidated-damages provision — the full contractual cap. Saluda's internal estimate of costs directly attributable to the five-month delay and the commissioning failure was approximately $1.9 million. The company recovered 14 percent of it, because that is what the contract said.

**Annual benefit at mid-2025 run rate**, net of the new costs the recovery created:

- Overtime reduction: $1.7 million
- Defect cost reduction: approximately $2.9 million, volume-adjusted
- Net direct labor: approximately $1.6 million, after subtracting $284,000 in technician wage increases and $190,000 to staff the second-look station
- Less incremental maintenance, service contracts, and software licensing on the new equipment: approximately ($610,000)

Net annual benefit: approximately $5.6 million, against an original case of $3.8 million. The equipment, once it worked, substantially outperformed the projection.

**Payback**, honestly stated two ways. Measured from the point of stable operation in mid-2024, the $11.4 million returns in about 25 months. Measured from the first dollar spent in February 2023 — which is the only measure that reflects what the company actually experienced — payback lands around 40 months, against the 29 months the board approved. The delta is almost entirely the cost of the commissioning failure and the fourteen months of value the plant did not capture while it was being fixed.

### XII. The Grant

Headcount settled at 441. The retention floor was 460 through December 31, 2024. Saluda missed it, and knew it would miss it from roughly March 2024 onward.

Ellingson's decision was to disclose early rather than wait for the quarterly filings to reveal it. In April 2024 she wrote to the state agency describing the shortfall, the cause, and the remediation, and requested a meeting. The negotiation ran from June 2024 to February 2025 and consumed a great deal of executive attention at a moment when there was very little to spare.

The settlement modified rather than voided the agreement. Saluda repaid $210,000 of the $1.5 million, accepted a revised retention floor of 435 positions extended through December 2027, and committed to a documented annual training investment at the site of not less than $250,000 through the same period. The state's willingness to modify rested on three arguments Ellingson made and documented: that average wages at the site had risen, materially, through the technician conversion; that the five-year customer commitment preserved a $132 million manufacturing operation in Spartanburg County that would otherwise have been at genuine risk; and that the company had disclosed the shortfall itself, eight months before it was obligated to.

Ellingson's own assessment is blunter. The covenant should never have been signed. The business case in front of the board in February 2023 explicitly contemplated eliminating 38 to 42 positions from a base of 480. The floor of 460 was arithmetically incompatible with the plan the same executives were approving in the same month, and the incompatibility was resolved by optimism about growth hiring that nobody was accountable for delivering. The $210,000 repayment was cheap. The nine months of leadership time was not.

### XIII. What Saluda Would Do Differently

Ellingson, Rasheed, Huerta, and Boatwright have each presented some version of this project externally, and their lists converge on six things.

**Sequence the people plan ahead of the equipment, not alongside it.** The single decision that generated the crisis was standing down 38 positions in October 2023 when the finishing equipment worked and the inspection system did not. The plant needed those people — desperately, within sixty days, to adjudicate false rejects. Saluda's rule now is that no position is eliminated until the *entire* system it belongs to has been accepted and has run stable for ninety days, and until the individual holding it has a written destination. The carrying cost of keeping 38 people on payroll for four extra months would have been roughly $920,000. The company spent more than twice that on the consequences of not doing so, and lost 238 years of institutional knowledge in the bargain.

**Fund the environment, not just the machine.** The optical inspection system did not fail. Its installation environment failed. Nobody scoped the lighting, the skylights, the ambient reflection, or the enclosure, because those were features of an existing building rather than line items on a quotation. A vision system is an instrument, and an instrument requires controlled conditions. Saluda now requires every capital request involving sensing or measurement to include an explicit environmental scope with its own budget line.

**Test on the hard cases before signing.** The Georgia reference visit persuaded everyone and proved nothing, because Georgia ran a narrow product range and Saluda runs 140 SKUs including dark napped goods and high-gloss coated goods that are the hardest possible optical targets. Saluda's revised procurement standard requires that any inspection or measurement system be demonstrated, before contract award, on the ten most difficult items in the actual product mix, using the buyer's material, with a documented pass criterion. Had that test been run in February 2023, the false-reject problem would have surfaced during selection rather than during production.

**Write liquidated damages that reflect the exposure.** Brandywine's delay and miscalibration cost Saluda approximately $1.9 million. The contract capped recovery at $275,000, a figure negotiated as a percentage of contract value with no reference whatsoever to the operational consequence of failure at a single-plant business with a concentrated customer. Saluda's current contracting standard ties delay damages to modeled downtime cost and requires vendor personnel to be on site continuously during commissioning as a contractual obligation rather than a negotiated favor extracted under a default notice.

**Do not sign covenants your own plan contradicts.** The grant floor of 460 against a plan to eliminate 40 positions was a known contradiction resolved by assumption. Saluda now requires that any financing covenant be tested against the base case of the project it is financing, in writing, before signature, and that a named executive own the covenant as a tracked monthly metric.

**Keep the humans on the exceptions.** The manual second-look station was reinstated reluctantly and has become, in Huerta's view, the most valuable single element of the installed system. It is the mechanism by which the machine keeps learning, and it is the reason the defect rate kept falling from 1.5 percent in late 2024 to 0.9 percent by mid-2025 without further capital. Saluda now designs for the machine-plus-human exception loop from the outset rather than treating human intervention as a failure state.

Rasheed adds a seventh, which is less a process than a caution: the project's monthly reporting was accurate and useless. Every report through the summer of 2023 tracked equipment milestones, spend against budget, and vendor schedule. None tracked departmental attrition against the displacement assumption, the status of a retraining plan that did not exist, or the number of open positions that displaced operators could actually fill. The information that would have prevented the crisis was not hidden — it was simply not on anyone's report, because reports track what someone decided to track, and nobody had decided.

### XIV. What to Take From This

If you are running a single-site business facing a price demand from a customer who represents a large share of your revenue, several things in this case generalize and one thing does not.

What does not generalize is the outcome. Saluda recovered because the underlying technical premise was sound — the equipment, once correctly installed and tuned, beat its business case by a wide margin, delivering $5.6 million a year against a projected $3.8 million. If the premise had been wrong, no amount of recovery management would have saved it. Do not read this as a story about resilience overcoming a bad investment. It is a story about competent execution rescuing a good investment from bad implementation.

What generalizes is the shape of the failure. The company treated a socio-technical transformation as a capital purchase. It budgeted for machinery and not for the conditions machinery requires, human or physical. It assigned an owner to the equipment and no owner to the workforce transition. It approved a plan whose people implications contradicted a covenant it signed the same month and reconciled the contradiction with an assumption. And it built a reporting system that faithfully tracked the parts of the project that were going fine.

Three specific lessons are worth carrying.

First, in an automation program, the commissioning period is the period of maximum labor need, not minimum. This is counterintuitive and it is the trap Saluda fell into. New equipment is unstable, generates exceptions, and requires parallel manual capability precisely during the months when the business case says the manual capability is redundant. Plan to carry the cost of both for a defined stabilization window and put that cost in the capital request where the board can see it.

Second, the people you displace are the people who know how to fix what you installed. Twenty-one operators with an average of eleven years' experience walked out of Saluda in eleven weeks, at exactly the moment the plant needed people who understood, viscerally, what a defect in a napped dark fabric actually looks like under a light table. That knowledge was the raw material of the sample library Huerta had to build from scratch. Some of it walked into the parking lot and never came back.

Third — and this is the part Villanueva's three sheets of paper demonstrate — the floor usually knows before the office does. The hold logs Villanueva used to build her tally were the quality department's own records, available to anyone. The 21 departures were in the HR system. The false-reject rate was on a screen in the inspection bay. None of it was secret and none of it was on a management report. The plant committee did not discover information that leadership lacked; it assembled information leadership had and was not looking at. A company that has to be told what is happening in its own building by a walkout threat has a reporting problem before it has a labor problem.

Saluda absorbed a 12 percent price reduction, holds a five-year contract with a $58 million floor, runs at 0.9 percent defects and 23 percent above its old throughput, and employs 441 people at higher average wages than it paid 480 in 2022. It got there. It got there fourteen months later and $2.2 million more expensively than it needed to, and every dollar of that overrun is traceable to a plan that specified what the machines would do and left blank what the people would do.
