# Cash, Conflict, and the Limits of Evidence: A Review of Unconditional Cash Transfer Pilots for the Alamo Basin Community Foundation

Prepared by Tomas Iribarren, Senior Research Officer, with Chidinma Okorafor, Research Associate, and Landon Yazzie, Program Officer
Alamo Basin Community Foundation, San Antonio, Texas
July 30, 2026

---

## 1. Introduction: The Question Before the Board

The Alamo Basin Community Foundation's board is considering a six-million-dollar, three-year commitment to an unconditional cash pilot serving 500 households across three San Antonio-area zip codes where median household income falls below $32,000. The proposal draws on a decade of guaranteed-income experimentation across American cities, several rural and international trials, and a growing but contested academic literature. It also draws political weight it did not ask for. County Commissioner Odalys Fuentes-Nabors has said she will not bring matching public funds to the table without evidence that the model changes outcomes rather than simply redistributing income for three years. Two donor-advised fund holders, meanwhile, have told program staff they will move their accounts elsewhere if the foundation proceeds without a rigorous evidentiary basis — a threat that has less to do with ideology than with a sense, shared by several trustees, that the field's claims have outrun its data.

This review was commissioned to settle, or at least clarify, what the accumulated pilot literature actually supports. The mandate from the board was narrow and specific: lay out what is known about unconditional cash transfers on employment, health, housing, and child development; explain where studies disagree and why; identify which populations the evidence does not cover; and translate all of this into a judgment usable by a foundation weighing a pilot of this size and duration. This is not a review built to justify a decision already made. Both program staff advocating for the pilot and board members skeptical of it agreed on this scope before the search began.

The stakes of getting the synthesis right are not abstract. A poorly calibrated brief prepared for donor communications, or a commissioner's floor speech, could either overstate the field's confidence in employment or health gains that later trials have not replicated, or understate the more durable finding — housing stability — that may be the strongest available argument for the pilot as designed. The foundation's $340 million endowment can absorb a six-million-dollar bet financially; what is less certain is whether the pilot's design, drawn from a literature built mostly on urban populations studied in relatively short windows, will produce evidence Commissioner Fuentes-Nabors or the donor community will accept as decisive, favorable or not.

We organized the review around four questions the board asked us to answer directly: What do the strongest studies show, and where do they conflict? What methodological and contextual differences explain the conflict? Whose experience is absent from this evidence base, including populations present in the three target zip codes? And what, given all of that, should a foundation of this size reasonably expect, and reasonably promise, from a pilot of 500 households over three years?

## 2. Search and Screening Methods

The search covered EconLit, the Social Science Citation Index, and PsycINFO, supplemented by a gray-literature search of pilot evaluation reports, working papers, and program-office publications not indexed in academic databases — a necessary supplement given that several major guaranteed-income demonstrations release findings first, and sometimes only, through nonprofit research offices rather than peer-reviewed journals. The date range ran from January 1997, chosen to capture the tail end of the negative-income-tax experiments and their long-delayed re-analyses, through June 2026.

Search terms combined variants of "unconditional cash transfer," "guaranteed income," "basic income," and "cash assistance" with outcome terms — employment, labor supply, mental health, housing stability, child development, cognitive outcomes — and with study-design terms to help identify randomized and quasi-experimental designs during initial screening. We supplemented database searches with citation chaining from eleven existing systematic reviews and meta-analyses and with direct outreach to program offices administering active or recently concluded pilots.

The search returned 1,410 unique records after de-duplication. Two reviewers (Okorafor and Yazzie) independently screened titles and abstracts against inclusion criteria: the study had to evaluate an unconditional or minimally conditioned cash transfer to households or individuals, report at least one outcome in employment, health, housing, or child development, and include either a comparison group or a pre-post design with a defined follow-up window. Disagreements were resolved by Iribarren. This screening yielded 268 records for full-text review, from which 63 met inclusion criteria for the final synthesis.

The included set comprises 14 randomized controlled trials, 19 quasi-experimental studies (difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, and matched comparison designs), twelve qualitative studies, seven cost or fiscal-offset analyses, and eleven existing syntheses or meta-analyses, which we used for cross-checking rather than as primary evidence. These studies evaluate twelve distinct pilot or natural-experiment programs, ranging from a 1997–2000 re-analysis of a 1970s income-maintenance experiment to a large randomized trial published in 2024 and several programs still reporting interim results as of this year. Effect sizes, transfer amounts, durations, comparison conditions, and outcome-measurement methods for every included study are recorded in the project extraction workbook, cited throughout this review by author and year and available to board members on request.

We want to flag one limitation in the search itself before turning to findings: gray-literature searches are inherently less systematic than database searches, and pilot programs with disappointing or null results are, by the admission of several program directors we contacted, less likely to produce a public report at all. The synthesis below should be read with that asymmetry in mind — if anything, the tensions we describe understate the field's uncertainty rather than overstate it.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Employment and Labor Supply: Divergent Findings Across Cohorts

The single largest source of disagreement in this literature concerns what unconditional cash does to work. The earliest wave of city-level pilots reported encouraging results. The Meridian Economic Mobility Demonstration, a $500-per-month, two-year unconditional pilot for low-income families in a mid-sized California city, found a nine-percentage-point increase in full-time employment among recipients relative to controls at the eighteen-month mark, attributing the gain to reduced job-search friction — recipients could afford application fees, car repairs, and child care that had previously kept them tethered to unstable part-time work (Lindqvist et al., 2021). The Rossmoor Guaranteed Income Project, evaluating a similar transfer to formerly incarcerated residents, reported comparable employment gains alongside reduced reliance on informal and off-book income (Thibodeaux & Okonkwo-Reyes, 2020). The Bellwood Trust for Working Mothers, which targeted Black mothers in a mid-sized Southern city with a $1,000 monthly unconditional transfer, found increases in full-time employment concentrated among mothers who had been working part-time at baseline, alongside qualitative accounts of women turning down exploitative shift arrangements once the transfer offered a floor (Achebe, 2019; Oyelaran, 2019, qualitative companion study).

These findings anchored several years of public and philanthropic advocacy for guaranteed income as a labor-market intervention, not merely a poverty-alleviation one. That framing has not held up well against more recent and more rigorously powered evidence. The Nordholm Employment Trial, a three-year randomized study published in 2024 with a substantially larger sample and administrative wage-record outcomes rather than self-report, found a reduction of approximately 1.3 weekly work hours among recipients relative to controls, concentrated among households with children under six, and no significant change on several secondary health outcomes examined in the same trial (Vollmer et al., 2024). The Cascade Basic Income Trial, which randomized households to unconditional versus work-conditioned cash arms alongside a pure control, found no significant difference in employment or hours between the unconditional and control arms at thirty months, though the conditioned arm showed a small positive employment effect relative to both (Sundaram & Baek, 2023).

The disagreement is not simply a matter of one study being wrong. Read together, the pattern that emerges is one of moderation rather than contradiction: early pilots studying two-year transfers in relatively tight urban labor markets with self-reported employment found modest gains, plausibly reflecting a reduction in job-search constraints for people already close to full-time work. Later, larger, and longer trials using administrative wage data and following households for three years found either no effect or a small reduction in hours, concentrated among parents of young children — consistent with cash easing the pressure to work rather than enabling more work, once the sample includes enough households where a parent's time with a young child is a live alternative to a marginal work hour. The Rift Basin Universal Basic Income Trial, run in a rural East African setting with a much longer time horizon and a substantially lower opportunity cost of local employment, found sustained increases in self-employment and microenterprise activity over five years with no reduction in wage labor (Deshpande et al., 2022) — a result that travels poorly to San Antonio zip codes with a functioning formal labor market but is frequently cited, sometimes uncritically, in domestic policy debates.

The employment literature, in short, does not support a claim that cash reliably increases full-time work, nor does it support the opposite claim that cash reliably depresses it. It supports a narrower and less satisfying conclusion: effects depend heavily on baseline labor-market attachment, presence of young children in the household, local labor-market conditions, and how long the study followed households before measuring the outcome.

### 3.2 Mental Health and Wellbeing: Fragile Gains

Early pilots again reported the more favorable results. The Meridian Economic Mobility Demonstration found a moderate reduction in depression and anxiety symptom scores (self-reported, PHQ-9 and GAD-7 instruments) at twelve months, sustained but attenuated at eighteen months (Lindqvist et al., 2021). The Rossmoor project reported similar reductions on a locally adapted psychological distress measure, alongside qualitative reports of reduced hypervigilance and improved sleep among formerly incarcerated recipients navigating housing and employment discrimination (Thibodeaux & Okonkwo-Reyes, 2020; Farraday, 2020, qualitative companion).

The Nordholm trial's three-year, administratively verified follow-up found no significant change on several standardized mental health measures, including a clinical anxiety screener and a validated life-satisfaction index, despite the same trial finding a modest reduction in self-reported financial stress (Vollmer et al., 2024). The Cascade trial found a small, statistically significant improvement in self-reported life satisfaction in both cash arms relative to control, but no corresponding change on a clinician-administered depression screen administered to a sub-sample (Sundaram & Baek, 2023). The Harborview Rental Stability Cash Pilot, discussed further below, found mental health gains that tracked housing stability closely — recipients whose housing situation stabilized showed sustained improvement, while recipients who remained housing-insecure despite the transfer showed no measurable change (Kowalczyk-Diaz, 2022).

Two patterns are worth naming. First, mental health gains appear more consistently in studies relying on self-report than in studies using clinician-administered or standardized clinical instruments — a measurement-method gap that recurs across nearly every outcome domain in this literature and is discussed further in Section 3.5. Second, where mental health gains do appear in longer or more rigorous studies, they appear mediated by a concrete change in material circumstance — housing stability, debt reduction — rather than as a freestanding effect of income security itself. This matters for how the foundation should describe expected benefits: mental health improvement is plausible but appears contingent on cash actually resolving a specific material stressor, not guaranteed by the transfer alone.

### 3.3 Housing Stability: The Steadiest Signal

Housing outcomes are the one domain in which the literature agrees more than it disagrees. The Harborview pilot, designed explicitly around housing stability as a primary outcome, found a 34 percent relative reduction in evictions and a 28 percent reduction in moves attributable to nonpayment among recipients over two years, using county eviction-filing records rather than self-report (Kowalczyk-Diaz, 2022). The Bellwood Trust found reduced housing instability among recipient mothers, measured by both self-report and a locally matched administrative housing-court dataset, with effects holding through a 24-month follow-up (Achebe, 2019). The Nordholm trial, despite its null and negative findings on employment and mental health, found a statistically significant reduction in self-reported housing instability and a smaller but still significant reduction in administratively recorded eviction filings (Vollmer et al., 2024) — making it notable as a study whose employment and health results diverged from earlier pilots while its housing results converged with them.

The Cascade trial found housing-stability effects concentrated in the unconditional arm rather than the work-conditioned arm, the reverse of its employment pattern, and attributed this to the unconditional transfer's flexibility in covering irregular housing costs — security deposits, back rent, utility arrears — that a work-conditioned transfer's delayed or interrupted disbursement schedule could not reliably cover (Sundaram & Baek, 2023). A 2021 quasi-experimental study using the Bellwood program's administrative rent-ledger data found that housing-stability effects were largest for households within striking distance of stable housing at baseline — those one or two months of arrears from losing a lease — and smallest for households already facing eviction proceedings, suggesting a threshold effect rather than a uniform benefit (Bassiouni, 2021).

Two qualitative studies, one following Rossmoor participants (Farraday, 2020) and one following a set of Cascade trial households (Golshan, 2023), independently identified housing security as the outcome participants themselves named most often as life-changing, ahead of employment or health changes — a convergence between what administrative data shows and what recipients report that does not recur elsewhere in this literature.

Taken together, housing stability is the domain where the evidence across designs, populations, and measurement methods points in the same direction most consistently. It is also, notably, the outcome least contingent on local labor-market conditions or family structure, which may explain its portability across the varied urban settings in the included studies. Whether it will hold for San Antonio's target zip codes, where rental-market conditions and eviction-filing practices differ from the cities studied, is addressed in Section 4.

### 3.4 Child and Infant Development: Early Promise, Uncertain Persistence

No domain in this review illustrates the risk of overclaiming more clearly than infant and early childhood development. The Cradle Study, a randomized trial providing unconditional monthly cash to mothers of infants beginning shortly after birth, reported in its first wave of results a measurable difference in infant brain activity — specifically, higher-frequency EEG power in regions associated with cognitive development — among infants in high-transfer households relative to low-transfer controls at twelve months (Provenzano et al., 2022). This finding drew substantial attention in philanthropic and policy circles as neurological evidence that cash transfers causally affect brain development in ways that could plausibly translate into long-run gains in school readiness and adult earning capacity.

The study's second wave, following the same children to 36 months, did not find a corresponding difference on standardized cognitive assessments or on a follow-up EEG measure; the initial neurological difference had narrowed to a magnitude no longer statistically distinguishable from zero (Provenzano et al., 2025). The study's authors were candid about the limits of the original finding, noting in the follow-up report that early neurological differences of this kind are not well established as predictive of later cognitive or academic outcomes, and that the initial result should have been reported with more caution about its downstream implications than it in fact received in public communications at the time.

A separate, older natural-experiment study offers a partial counterpoint on longer time horizons. The Great Smoky Casino Dividend Study, examining the long-run effects of a per-capita cash disbursement to households on a tribal reservation beginning in the late 1990s, found that children in households receiving the dividend during early childhood showed higher educational attainment and lower rates of adult criminal-justice involvement by their mid-twenties, relative to children in comparable households who aged out of dividend eligibility before the payments began (Karimi & Ilić, 2019, reanalysis of program data collected 1998–2016). This is the strongest evidence in the included set for a durable, long-run developmental effect, but it differs from the Cradle Study and from the proposed Alamo Basin pilot in several respects relevant to interpretation: the dividend was sustained over the child's entire minority rather than for three years, it was combined with broader community-level investment from the same casino revenue, and outcomes were measured in the child's mid-twenties rather than in infancy.

The qualitative literature offers a more modest but consistent picture. Parents across three included qualitative studies (Oyelaran, 2019; Farraday, 2020; Golshan, 2023) described using cash to reduce the number of caregivers a young child cycled through, to secure more stable child care arrangements, and to reduce the household chaos associated with utility shutoffs and moves — plausible developmental mechanisms that are harder to capture in a brain scan or standardized test at twelve or thirty-six months, but that align conceptually with the Great Smoky study's much longer-run findings.

The board should treat the infant-brain-development finding as an instructive caution rather than as supporting evidence: an early, biologically striking result that did not survive a longer follow-up window, in a literature that otherwise suggests developmental benefits, if they exist, may take considerably longer than three years to become measurable or may operate through pathways — housing stability, caregiver stability — that a three-year pilot is better positioned to measure than infant neurology is.

### 3.5 What Drives the Disagreement: Design, Measurement, and Context

Four factors recur across the conflicts documented above and deserve explicit treatment because they are the factors most within the foundation's control in designing its own pilot.

**Transfer size and duration.** Included studies range from $250 to $1,000 in monthly transfer value and from twelve months to open-ended duration. Larger, longer transfers (Bellwood, Cascade) show more consistent housing and mental-health effects than smaller or shorter ones; but the largest and longest domestic trial in the set (Nordholm) is also the one that found null or negative employment and health effects, suggesting duration and size alone do not explain the pattern and may interact with local labor-market and family-composition factors described above.

**Self-report versus administrative measurement.** This is the single most consistent predictor of effect size in the set. Every outcome domain shows larger, more favorable effects in studies relying primarily on participant self-report than in studies using administrative records — wage data, eviction filings, standardized clinical instruments — for the same outcome category. This is not evidence that self-reported effects are false; participants may accurately perceive improvements that are real but too small, too delayed, or too unevenly distributed to register in aggregate administrative data. It is evidence that the two measurement approaches are answering somewhat different questions, and that a pilot relying primarily on participant survey data, which is administratively far cheaper to collect, should expect to find more favorable results than one paired with administrative record linkage, independent of the transfer's actual effect.

**Comparison-group design.** Randomized trials with a pure control arm (Cascade, Nordholm, Cradle) are more conservative in their findings than quasi-experimental studies using matched comparison groups (Bellwood, Rossmoor, the Great Smoky reanalysis), which are more susceptible to selection into and out of the comparison pool. This does not mean quasi-experimental findings are unreliable, but it means the more favorable early results in this literature disproportionately come from designs more vulnerable to positive bias, while the more recent, larger randomized trials — better powered to detect true null or small negative effects — have found less to report.

**Local context.** Labor-market tightness, rental-market and eviction-filing regimes, baseline family composition, and whether the transfer displaces an existing benefit (several studies note recipients losing SNAP, housing-assistance, or Medicaid eligibility as income rose, blunting the transfer's net effect — see Rasmussen, 2021, on benefit-cliff interactions in the Rossmoor sample) all vary substantially across the twelve programs in this set. None of the twelve programs studied a Texas market, a market with San Antonio's specific eviction-filing practices, or a sample drawn from majority-Latino, mixed-status, or colonia populations. This is the subject of Section 4.

### 3.6 Costs and Fiscal Offsets

The seven cost analyses in this set offer a more consistent, if narrower, picture than the outcome literature. Program administrative costs, including navigation staff, financial coaching add-ons where offered, and evaluation infrastructure, ranged from 9 to 22 percent of total program spending, with leaner pilots (direct transfer via existing benefits infrastructure) at the low end and pilots layering case management or coaching services at the high end (Chandrasekaran, 2021, synthesis of five pilot budgets). Fiscal offset analyses — attempts to net out reduced use of homelessness services, emergency-room utilization, or criminal-justice system contact against program cost — found modest but real offsets in the Harborview and Rossmoor samples, on the order of 8 to 14 cents of offset per transfer dollar over the study window, concentrated in avoided shelter and emergency-service costs rather than avoided criminal-justice costs (Mokoena & Sennhauser, 2022). No study in the set found offsets sufficient to make the program self-financing within its evaluation window; every cost analysis characterized the program as a net public or philanthropic expenditure whose justification rests on the value assigned to the primary outcomes rather than on fiscal savings.

## 4. Gaps: Who Is Not in the Evidence Base

Three populations directly relevant to the Alamo Basin proposal are essentially absent from the 63 studies reviewed here, and the board should treat this as a first-order limitation rather than a footnote.

**Mixed-status immigrant families.** None of the twelve programs studied explicitly recruited or reported outcomes for households with mixed documentation status, and several pilot eligibility rules — tied to program-specific identity verification or tax-filing requirements — would have functionally excluded such households from participation even where not formally barred. Given that mixed-status households are a substantial share of low-income households in the proposed San Antonio zip codes, the evidence base offers no direct guidance on take-up, disclosure risk, or outcome patterns for this population, and no study addresses whether fear of program visibility affects participation or self-report accuracy.

**Rural colonia residents.** All twelve programs in this set operated in incorporated urban areas or, in the Rift Basin case, a rural international setting with different infrastructure and labor-market conditions than South Texas colonias. No included study addresses populations living in unincorporated settlements with limited water, sewer, or road infrastructure, where cash's effect on housing stability, employment access, or health may operate through entirely different mechanisms — for instance, where transportation cost rather than rent arrears may be the binding constraint on employment, or where housing stability is not meaningfully measured by eviction filings because tenure arrangements are informal.

**Households with a disabled adult.** Only two of the sixty-three included studies report disability status as a moderator (Rasmussen, 2021; Bassiouni, 2021), and both note small subsample sizes insufficient for confident inference. Given that unconditional cash income can interact with SSI and Medicaid eligibility rules in ways that materially change a disabled household member's benefit exposure, and that several studies note benefit-cliff effects as a general concern, this is a population for whom program design questions are especially consequential and especially unaddressed by the existing evidence.

If the three target zip codes contain meaningful concentrations of any of these populations — plausible, given the income threshold and the San Antonio metropolitan area's demographic composition — the foundation should expect its own pilot to generate evidence the field does not currently have, for better or worse. This is a genuine contribution the Alamo Basin pilot could make to the field, and a reason the board might value rigorous, published evaluation of its own pilot beyond the immediate local decision.

## 5. Implications for the Alamo Basin Pilot

Four implications follow directly from this synthesis and respond to the board's specific questions.

**On the employment case Commissioner Fuentes-Nabors is asking about:** the literature does not support promising that the pilot will increase full-time employment, and the strongest recent evidence — the 2024 Nordholm trial, methodologically the closest analog to a well-resourced three-year pilot with administrative outcome data — found a small reduction in work hours, concentrated among parents of young children. If matching public funds are conditioned on demonstrating an employment gain, the board should either revise that expectation before securing the commissioner's support or select outcome measures and messaging that do not stake the case on employment. Overpromising here risks a credibility cost with the commissioner's office that would outlast this single funding decision.

**On the strongest available case:** housing stability is the outcome most consistently supported across study designs, measurement methods, and program contexts, and it is also the outcome most plausibly connected, through the mechanisms described in Section 3.4, to whatever child-development benefits a three-year pilot might realistically hope to detect. A pilot designed and communicated around housing stability as its primary hypothesis, with employment and health treated as secondary and appropriately hedged, is better supported by the evidence than one built primarily around employment or mental health claims.

**On measurement, directly relevant to the donor-advised fund holders' concerns:** the gap between self-reported and administrative findings across this literature is large enough that the pilot's credibility will depend heavily on securing administrative data linkage — eviction filings, wage records where available, benefit-enrollment data — rather than relying primarily on participant surveys. This adds cost and complexity but is the single design choice most likely to produce findings the field, and skeptical funders, will find credible rather than dismissible as self-report bias.

**On populations and generalizability:** the pilot's three target zip codes should be assessed now for their likely concentration of mixed-status, colonia, and disability-affected households, both because the existing evidence offers little guidance for these populations and because a pilot that documents outcomes for them would be a genuine and citable contribution to a field that currently cannot speak to their experience. The board should not assume San Antonio effects will replicate the urban pilots reviewed here; it should design the evaluation to test that assumption directly rather than import it.

Finally, the board should be candid, including with the commissioner and the donor-advised fund holders, that this is a genuinely unsettled literature rather than a settled one awaiting local confirmation. The honest case for the pilot is not that the evidence guarantees success on employment, mental health, or child development, but that housing stability effects are reasonably well established, that the population and geographic gaps identified here make this pilot independently valuable to the field regardless of outcome, and that a rigorously measured three-year pilot — administrative data, pre-registered hypotheses, appropriately modest employment expectations — is a defensible use of $6 million from a $340 million endowment precisely because it is designed to produce evidence rather than to confirm a conclusion already reached.
