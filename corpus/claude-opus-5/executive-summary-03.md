# 1. Executive Summary — K–2 Structured Literacy Rollout

**To:** Superintendent Imani Blackshear; CFO Petra Lindqvist; Board Finance Committee
**From:** Rosalind Ferrer-Guzman, Director, Office of Research and Accountability
**Date:** September 9, 2025
**Subject:** Summative evaluation of the K–2 structured literacy rollout, 2024–25; recommendation on continuation funding ahead of the September 30, 2025 grant expiration

## Finding and Recommendation

The K–2 structured literacy rollout is the strongest-performing initiative in the 2024–25 evaluation cycle, and it is the one closest to lapsing. Across twelve elementary schools serving 1,900 students, the share of children reading at or above the spring benchmark on the state-approved universal screener rose from 41 percent to 58 percent in a single year. Comparison schools moved five points over the same period, from 44 to 49 percent, which places the rollout's net advantage at roughly twelve points. First-grade gains held across all four subgroups this office tracks — an unusual result, and the one that most argues against treating this as a fluke of a favorable cohort.

**The Office of Research and Accountability recommends that the Board absorb the recurring cost of the program into the general fund beginning October 1, 2025, at an estimated $1.25 million per year, and that it do so by action at the September 9 meeting rather than at a later date.** The award that funded year one, a competitive state early literacy grant of $2.4 million, ends September 30. Nine coaching positions carry contracts through June 30, 2026 that were issued contingent on continuation funding. Whatever the Board decides about the program, it has already incurred most of the liability for the current year; the practical choice is between funding a working program and paying for staff whose program has been dismantled.

## What the Evaluation Found

The $2.4 million award covered a curriculum adoption, nine literacy coaches, universal screening and progress-monitoring licenses, and roughly forty days of professional learning per school. Spending worked out to $1,263 per participating student. Of that total, approximately $690,000 was non-recurring — the curriculum purchase itself, initial training contracts, and the assessment platform build-out — which is why the continuation cost is roughly half the first-year cost.

Results were measured against the nine elementary schools that did not participate, serving about 1,420 K–2 students with comparable demographic profiles. Rollout schools and comparison schools began the year within three points of each other and ended it nine points apart. The gain was not evenly distributed by grade. Kindergarten moved from 44 to 63 percent at or above benchmark, first grade from 39 to 59 percent, and second grade from 40 to 52 percent. The weaker second-grade result is consistent with what the literature would predict: second graders entered the year having already spent two years under the prior approach, and the program is designed to work best when students receive it from the start.

The subgroup results are the reason this office is recommending continuation rather than a further year of study. Among first graders, the share at or above benchmark moved as follows:

- Students with disabilities: 22 to 36 percent
- Multilingual learners: 26 to 44 percent
- Students eligible for free or reduced-price meals: 34 to 52 percent
- Black students: 31 to 48 percent

No subgroup was flat, and no subgroup's gain came at the visible expense of another. Gaps narrowed modestly in absolute terms while every group improved. This is the pattern that distinguishes an instructional change from a measurement artifact or a composition shift.

Two cautions belong in the record. First, this is a single year of data against a non-randomized comparison group, and the comparison schools were not selected — they are simply the schools the grant did not reach. Second, implementation fidelity varied. Classroom observation logs show that three of the twelve schools completed fewer than 70 percent of scheduled coaching cycles, and those three schools produced the smallest gains in the group. That variation is itself an argument for the coaching model rather than against it.

## What the District Should Do Before September 30

The award closes September 30, 2025, and the final expenditure report and performance narrative are due to the state within thirty days of that date. Three actions need to happen before the deadline.

The district should complete final drawdown of the remaining $47,000 in unliquidated funds, applying it to consumable student materials for the fall semester so that classrooms are not stranded mid-unit. It should file the final performance report on time; the state has signaled that a second competitive round will open in spring 2026, and prior-award compliance is a scored element. And it should adopt the continuation appropriation. Continuation at $1.25 million represents 0.43 percent of the $291 million general fund. If that figure cannot be found in full, the office's fallback recommendation is to preserve the nine coaching positions and the screening licenses — about $854,000 — and reduce professional learning days from forty to twenty-two per school, accepting a slower rate of improvement rather than a discontinuity.

What the district should not do is extend the program at partial funding while leaving the coaching model intact on paper but unstaffed. The three low-fidelity schools already demonstrate what that produces.

## Consequences If the Program Stops

**Staff.** Nine literacy coaches and one half-time project coordinator are funded entirely from the expiring award. Their contracts run through June 30, 2026. If the Board declines continuation, the district faces approximately $587,000 in salary and benefit obligations from October through June for positions with no program to support, and it will need to reassign ten people into classroom or interventionist vacancies mid-year. Seven of the nine coaches were promoted from Piedmont Vale classrooms; six of those seven classroom positions have since been filled. Reabsorbing them will require displacing or reassigning newer hires, at a moment when the district is carrying eleven elementary vacancies.

**Students.** The 1,900 students in the rollout would return in October to whatever their building can improvise. The curriculum materials are purchased and would remain, but the diagnostic screening licenses, the progress-monitoring cycle, and the small-group intervention scheduling all depend on staff who would no longer be assigned to the work. The most exposed group is the roughly 640 students who ended the year below benchmark and are currently receiving tiered intervention; they are the students for whom coaching capacity is not an enhancement but the delivery mechanism. In addition, this year's kindergarten class — approximately 630 students — would enter a program that its predecessors received and it would not, which is both an equity problem and a measurement problem, since it would break the cohort comparison the office needs to evaluate any future round of funding.

# 2. Executive Summary — Online Credit Recovery Program

**To:** Superintendent Imani Blackshear; CFO Petra Lindqvist; Board Finance Committee
**From:** Rosalind Ferrer-Guzman, Director, Office of Research and Accountability
**Date:** September 9, 2025
**Subject:** Summative evaluation of the online credit recovery program, 2024–25; recommendation on non-renewal and replacement design ahead of the September 30, 2025 grant expiration

## Finding and Recommendation

The online credit recovery program did not work as designed, and the evaluation is unambiguous enough that this office is recommending against renewal in its current form. Of 1,240 high school students enrolled, 46 percent completed a course. Among completers in subjects with a state end-of-course examination, 31 percent passed that examination. The same courses taught in person produced a 68 percent pass rate. The program awarded credit at more than twice the rate at which it produced demonstrated subject mastery.

**The Office of Research and Accountability recommends that the district decline to renew the platform license, allow the $890,000 grant to close on September 30, 2025, and reinvest approximately $505,000 in a facilitated, teacher-supervised recovery lab beginning in January 2026.** The decision must be made this week: the platform contract contains an automatic annual renewal clause requiring written notice of non-renewal by September 15, 2025. Silence on September 15 obligates the district for a second year.

## What the Evaluation Found

The $890,000 award covered a district-wide platform license at $412,000, three full-time lab facilitators at $268,000, a half-time coordinator at $41,000, proctoring stipends of $54,000, headsets and lab hardware at $38,000, and $77,000 in contracted online tutoring. The 1,240 enrolled students generated 1,588 course seats, meaning the average enrollee attempted slightly more than one course.

Completion, defined as finishing all required units and the platform's summative assessment, reached 570 students. That is a per-completion cost of $1,561. The district's summer seat-based recovery program, for comparison, has historically produced credits at roughly $385 apiece. Cost alone would not condemn the program if the credits were sound. They were not.

Of the 1,588 seats, 812 were in Algebra 1, Biology 1, English 2, or U.S. History and the Constitution — courses carrying a state end-of-course examination that counts toward the school's report card and, in most of these cases, toward the student's course grade. Four hundred eighty-six students completed a seat in one of those four subjects. One hundred fifty-one passed the corresponding examination. Face-to-face sections of those same four courses, taught the same year in the same buildings, produced a 68 percent pass rate. The gap of thirty-seven points is not attributable to the recovery population being weaker: when the office restricted the in-person comparison to students who had previously failed the course and were repeating it in a seated section, the in-person pass rate was 54 percent — still twenty-three points above the online result.

The mechanism is visible in the platform's own usage data. Median time-on-task per completed course was eleven hours against a design specification of sixty. Median elapsed time from enrollment to final login was nineteen days. Seniors completed at 63 percent, ninth and tenth graders at 31 percent, which suggests the program functioned less as instruction than as a compliance channel for students approaching a graduation deadline. Unit assessments were unproctored; the end-of-course examinations were not. That single difference explains most of the divergence between the completion rate and the pass rate.

Three hundred sixty-one students who enrolled never opened a second unit. The district paid a per-seat license fee for each of them.

## What the District Should Do Before September 30

The immediate action is the non-renewal notice, due in writing to the vendor by September 15. The office recommends that the Superintendent transmit it under her signature the week of the Board briefing.

Before the award closes, the district should obligate the remaining $63,400 in unliquidated funds to two purposes: a transcript reconciliation audit covering all 570 credits awarded in 2024–25, and a teach-out arrangement for students with coursework in progress. The transcript audit matters beyond this program. Credits awarded through an unproctored platform and recorded on official transcripts create exposure with the state education agency, with the district's accreditation, and with post-secondary institutions. The office recommends the audit be completed before the October graduation cohort file is certified, and that any credit awarded on fewer than fifteen documented hours of platform activity be flagged for principal review rather than silently voided.

For the replacement, the office recommends a facilitated recovery lab: enrollment capped at 500 seats, a certified teacher of record assigned per subject, a minimum of five hours weekly in a staffed lab during the school day, proctored unit assessments, and a reduced-tier content license rather than a district-wide seat license. Estimated cost is $505,000 annually with 2.0 FTE facilitators, which is $385,000 below the current program and, on the office's projection from comparable district models, would produce fewer credits of substantially higher value. This should be funded from the at-risk allocation rather than from a new grant, so that the program's survival does not again depend on a funding cycle rather than on results.

## Consequences If the Program Stops

**Staff.** Three full-time lab facilitators and one half-time coordinator are grant-funded. Two facilitators hold South Carolina certification and are placeable into the district's current secondary vacancies without difficulty; one is a paraprofessional with eleven years of district service whose position has no obvious equivalent, and the office recommends she be given first consideration for the facilitator roles in the replacement lab. The half-time coordinator is a retiree on contract and would simply not be renewed. If the replacement lab is approved for January, two of the four positions carry forward with a gap of roughly ten weeks, which the district should plan to bridge rather than absorb through attrition.

**Students.** Three hundred thirty-seven students had coursework in progress as of August 2025. Platform access terminates when the license lapses, and partial progress is not portable to another vendor. Without a teach-out plan, those students lose the work they have done. The office recommends that the district negotiate a ninety-day read-only extension as part of the non-renewal notice — vendors routinely grant this — and that counselors contact all 337 students before October 1.

The larger student consequence runs the other way. Of the 402 seniors who enrolled last year, 118 needed a recovered credit to graduate and 96 received one. Under a stricter replacement model, some meaningful fraction of those students will not clear the bar on the first attempt. That is the intended effect, but the Board should adopt it knowingly: a program that awards fewer credits will, in the first year, be visible in the four-year graduation rate. The office's position is that a diploma backed by an eleven-hour online course is a liability the district has been quietly accumulating, and that absorbing a rate change now is preferable to defending those transcripts later.

# 3. Executive Summary — Community School Wraparound Model

**To:** Superintendent Imani Blackshear; CFO Petra Lindqvist; Board Finance Committee
**From:** Rosalind Ferrer-Guzman, Director, Office of Research and Accountability
**Date:** September 9, 2025
**Subject:** Year-two evaluation of the community school wraparound model at three sites; recommendation on site-level restructuring ahead of the September 30, 2025 continuation filing

## Finding and Recommendation

The community school wraparound model produced one clear success and two null results, and the difference between them is explained by implementation rather than by design. At Redbud Elementary, chronic absenteeism fell six points, from 34.1 to 28.0 percent. At Amity Ridge Elementary it moved half a point, from 29.6 to 29.1. At Thorne Hollow Middle it worsened, from 38.2 to 39.5 percent, in a year when staff turnover at that site reached 44 percent. District-wide chronic absenteeism was 21.4 percent.

**The Office of Research and Accountability recommends that the district file its year-three continuation application on schedule, retain all three sites, and condition the Amity Ridge and Thorne Hollow allocations on ninety-day corrective action plans that redirect spending toward the specific students the model exists to reach.** This is a mid-course correction, not a termination decision. The federal award runs through September 2027 and the current budget period closes September 30, 2025; the continuation application and carryover request are due to the program office by that date.

## What the Evaluation Found

The model is funded at $1.6 million annually — $1.15 million from a five-year federal Full-Service Community Schools award and $450,000 from the Cavender Family Foundation as local match. The three sites serve approximately 1,410 students combined.

Redbud Elementary is the site that worked, and it is worth being precise about why. Its site coordinator has been in place twenty-six months. The school convened a weekly attendance team — principal, coordinator, nurse, counselor, and the attendance clerk — that reviewed a named list of students at four or more absences and assigned a specific adult to each. The coordinator conducted 41 home visits. A partnership with Calloway Health placed a nurse practitioner on site two days a week, which eliminated the same-day medical absence for 63 identified students. The site funded 214 individual transportation rides for students outside bus zones or living in temporary housing. Every one of those interventions was targeted at a student on a list. The six-point drop concentrated almost entirely in the students who had been on that list in September.

Amity Ridge had a stable coordinator and no attendance team. Its principal investment was a food pantry that served 340 families and is, by any humane standard, worth keeping. It is not, however, an attendance intervention, and the evaluation found no relationship between pantry use and absence patterns. The site delivered real services to a population defined by need rather than by absence, and its attendance result reflects that.

Thorne Hollow is the site that failed, and the failure is structural. Forty-four percent of grant-funded staff turned over during the year: the site coordinator position turned twice, both family engagement aides departed, and one of two contracted mental health counselors resigned in February. The site coordinator salary is $52,400 on a 190-day teacher calendar, which is below what the same skill set commands in area nonprofits and below what the two elementary sites' coordinators earn in effective hourly terms. Sixty-one percent of Thorne Hollow's allocation went to after-school programming that reached 88 students, of whom 12 were chronically absent. The school spent most of its money on students who were already coming to school. Meanwhile 143 chronically absent students received no case-managed contact at all.

The evaluation's central conclusion is that the wraparound model is not self-executing. It works when a stable adult maintains a named list and assigns accountability for each name. Where that function was absent — whether because no one built it, as at Amity Ridge, or because the person who would have built it kept leaving, as at Thorne Hollow — the money purchased services that did not touch the outcome.

## What the District Should Do Before September 30

The continuation application and the carryover request are due to the federal program office by September 30. Four things should be settled before it is filed.

First, the district should reprogram approximately $310,000 of Thorne Hollow's year-three allocation from after-school programming to case-managed attendance intervention, with a caseload target of no more than thirty students per staff member and the same weekly attendance-team structure Redbud uses. Second, it should reclassify the site coordinator role from a 190-day teacher position at $52,400 to a 220-day administrative position at $61,000, funded within the existing award. The turnover rate at Thorne Hollow is a compensation problem before it is anything else, and the district cannot correct implementation with a position it cannot keep filled. Third, the Office of Research and Accountability should document the Redbud model as a written operating playbook — attendance team composition, meeting cadence, escalation thresholds, home visit protocol — and make adherence to it a condition of site funding. Fourth, the district should confirm with the Cavender Family Foundation that the restructuring satisfies the maintenance-of-effort provision in the match agreement. That provision ties the $450,000 to continuous operation at all three sites; reducing or suspending Thorne Hollow, rather than restructuring it, would put the match at risk and with it the federal award's local-share requirement.

The office also recommends that the Board receive a ninety-day progress report on the two corrective plans at its January meeting, with the explicit understanding that a second flat year at either site should trigger a site-replacement decision rather than a third corrective plan.

## Consequences If the Program Stops

**Staff.** Twelve and a half positions depend on this award: three site coordinators, 1.5 family engagement specialists, two mental health counselors contracted through Ridgeline Behavioral Health, and six part-time program aides. None are general-fund positions and none have a district equivalent to fall back on. The counselors are contracted rather than employed, so their loss would be immediate; the coordinators and specialists hold annual grant-funded contracts and would require either reassignment or non-renewal notice. Because the federal award runs through 2027, a stoppage would also mean returning unobligated funds and forgoing roughly $2.3 million in remaining federal support and $900,000 in foundation match — a loss the district could not recover by reapplying, since the competition is closed to prior withdrawals for one cycle.

**Students.** The three sites serve 1,410 students. Ending the model would close the Redbud health partnership that has been keeping 63 students in school, end 214 annual transportation rides, close a pantry serving 340 families, and dissolve a mental health caseload of 96 students at the two schools with the district's highest referral rates. Redbud's six-point gain would be expected to reverse within a year, since nothing in it is self-sustaining; the interventions are the effect. At Thorne Hollow, where the model has not yet worked, stopping would remove services from the school with the district's worst absenteeism — 39.5 percent, nearly double the district rate — on the grounds that the school failed to use them well. That is a defensible position about management and an indefensible one about children, which is why this office recommends restructuring rather than withdrawal.

# 4. Executive Summary — One-to-One Device Refresh

**To:** Superintendent Imani Blackshear; CFO Petra Lindqvist; Board Finance Committee
**From:** Rosalind Ferrer-Guzman, Director, Office of Research and Accountability
**Date:** September 9, 2025
**Subject:** Evaluation of the one-to-one device refresh; recommendation on cost reconciliation, procurement controls, and grant closeout ahead of the September 30, 2025 liquidation deadline

## Finding and Recommendation

The one-to-one device refresh was approved by the Board at $5.9 million and has spent $8.43 million, an overrun of $2.53 million, or 43 percent. Deployment finished seven months behind schedule, in March 2025 rather than August 2024. First-year breakage reached 19 percent against a peer benchmark of six to eight percent. The project delivered devices to students, which is the outcome that matters most, and it did so at a cost and under a set of controls that this office cannot certify as adequate.

**The Office of Research and Accountability recommends four immediate actions: freeze the $218,000 in remaining unencumbered purchase authority; commission an independent reconciliation of project expenditures; reclassify approximately $310,000 in likely-disallowed charges from the state instructional technology grant to local funds before the September 30 liquidation deadline; and bring to the Board in October a device stewardship policy and a revised change-order threshold.** The recommendation is not that the district stop putting devices in students' hands. It is that the district stop doing so through a mechanism the Board cannot see.

## What the Evaluation Found

The approved scope covered 19,600 student devices for grades 3 through 12, 1,750 staff devices, and mobile device management licensing, at a bid unit price of $263. Funding came from $4.5 million in capital reserve and lease-purchase authority and a $1.4 million state instructional technology grant. Grants Manager Chandra Pillai administered the project.

The $2.53 million overrun decomposes as follows:

- **Breakage replacement, $1,110,000.** Nineteen percent of deployed student devices — approximately 3,724 units — required replacement in year one at $263 plus $34 per unit in imaging and handling.
- **Expedited freight and reorder premiums, $412,000.** Incurred after the original manufacturer missed its delivery window by four months and a partial substitution was arranged.
- **Protective cases, $372,000.** Not included in the original specification. Purchased in January 2025, five months into deployment, at $19 per unit.
- **Additional units, $368,000.** Approximately 1,400 devices for enrollment growth and to replace inventory not returned at year-end.
- **Extended warranty and insurance, $184,000.** Purchased mid-year in response to breakage.
- **Temporary technicians and overtime, $86,000.**

Two of these lines are ordinary. Enrollment growth and warranty coverage are legitimate scope additions that a Board would likely have approved. The other four describe a project that was specified incompletely and then repaired at premium cost. Devices were deployed for five months without protective cases; 41 percent of first-year damage was screen damage, and 62 percent of it occurred in grades 6 through 8. The district had no student device fee, no damage tier schedule, and no insurance program for the first two quarters of deployment, which meant that every broken screen was a full district cost.

The governance finding is more consequential than the cost finding. The project generated fourteen change orders. Eleven were executed at staff level under the district's $50,000 board-approval threshold. Three exceeded that threshold and were processed as multiple sequential orders that individually fell beneath it. The office is not asserting that this was done to evade Board review, and Ms. Pillai's project files are complete and well organized. It is asserting that the district's current threshold and change-order policy permitted a $2.53 million variance to accumulate without a single Board action, and that this will happen again on the next capital project unless the policy changes.

## What the District Should Do Before September 30

The $1.4 million state instructional technology grant requires final liquidation and a performance report by September 30, 2025. Reviewing the charge detail against the award's allowable-cost provisions, this office identifies approximately $310,000 in expedited freight, out-of-period charges, and temporary staffing that is unlikely to survive state audit. The district's interest is served by reclassifying those charges to local funds before filing rather than defending them afterward. A voluntary reclassification is an administrative correction; a disallowance found on audit is a finding that follows the district into the next competitive cycle.

Beyond closeout, the district should freeze the $218,000 in unencumbered purchase authority pending reconciliation, and engage an outside firm to reconcile project expenditures against the approved scope at an estimated cost of $45,000 to $60,000. The office recommends this be commissioned by the CFO rather than by the department that managed the project.

The FY26 budget must also be amended to recognize the overrun. A $2.53 million draw against fund balance reduces the district's reserve from $53.2 million to approximately $50.7 million against a policy floor of two months' operating expenditures, or $48.5 million. The district remains compliant at roughly 2.09 months, but the cushion above the floor narrows to $2.2 million, which is thin enough that the Board should be told plainly rather than left to discover it in the audited financials.

Finally, the district faces a liability it has not yet budgeted. Devices deployed in 2024–25 reach end of useful life in 2028–29, implying a refresh obligation of roughly $5.2 million with no identified funding stream and no sinking fund. A device stewardship policy — cases required, a graduated damage fee schedule, a summer collection and inventory protocol, and an annual per-student technology fee with hardship waiver — would both reduce breakage toward the peer benchmark and begin generating replacement revenue. The office recommends this come to the Board in October.

## Consequences If the Program Stops

This initiative differs from the other three in that it cannot meaningfully stop. The devices are deployed and 19,600 students are using them as their primary instructional tool; five of the district's high school course sequences and the entire secondary assessment calendar now assume one-to-one access. Stopping does not return the district to a prior state. It means the fleet degrades without replacement.

**Staff.** Three and a half full-time device support technicians and two temporary technicians are funded from the project rather than from the general fund, along with the portion of the Grants Manager's time allocated to it. If project funding ends without a successor budget line, those positions disappear and the district retains 21,000 devices with no one to repair them. The support ratio would move from roughly 1 technician per 5,600 devices — already thin — to zero. The office recommends that the 3.5 technician positions be converted to general-fund operating positions in the FY27 budget regardless of what is decided about further hardware purchases.

**Students.** With 19 percent annual breakage and no replacement funding, the practical result is that access becomes a function of how careful a student was, and then of whether a family can pay for a repair. The office's projection is that within eighteen months of a funding stop, between 3,000 and 4,500 students would be without a working device, concentrated in the middle grades where breakage is highest and in the schools where families are least able to absorb a repair cost. That is a worse equity outcome than the district had before the refresh began, because instruction has since been redesigned around universal access.

The recommendation, then, is corrective rather than terminal. The project's cost controls failed and its procurement governance was inadequate to the dollar amount involved. Both are fixable, and both should be fixed before the district undertakes another capital initiative of this size. The devices themselves should stay where they are.
