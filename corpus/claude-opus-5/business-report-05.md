## 1. Purpose and Scope

This review covers the first half of fiscal 2026 across all six operating businesses of Fairmount Software Group. It is written for two audiences at once, which shapes how it is organized. The board needs to understand where growth is coming from, where it is not, and what decisions require capital in the second half. The lender group needs to understand the covenant position, the durability of the recurring revenue base, and whether the second-half plan carries execution risk that could move leverage in the wrong direction.

The document proceeds business by business, then consolidates. Section 3 covers each of the six units with its own results and its own central problem. Section 4 presents the consolidated financial picture and the covenant calculation. Section 5 addresses the Everlight platform decision, which is the largest discretionary capital request in front of the board this year. Section 6 covers the VetLedger integration, which is behind plan. Section 7 sets out the second-half outlook, including what has to go right and what happens if it does not.

Nothing in this review is presented as settled that is not settled. Where management holds a view but has not yet committed, that is stated. Where a number is an estimate, the basis of the estimate is given.

## 2. Company Position at Mid-Year

Fairmount ended H1 FY2026 with $146 million in annual recurring revenue across six vertical software businesses and 780 employees. The portfolio was assembled through acquisition beginning in 2019; the most recent addition, VetLedger, closed in November 2025 and has been owned for approximately five months as of the period close.

The consolidated ARR growth rate for the half was 7.4 percent year over year. That number is the arithmetic result of a portfolio in which two businesses are growing well above the mean, two are growing modestly, one is flat, and one is contracting. Treating the consolidated figure as a description of company performance obscures more than it reveals, which is why the business-level detail in Section 3 carries most of the weight in this review.

Net leverage closed the quarter at 4.61 times against a maximum permitted 4.75 times. Total funded debt stands at $310 million. The 0.14 turns of headroom is the tightest position the company has held since the credit agreement was executed, and it constrains the range of actions available in the second half more than any operating factor does. Section 4 works through the calculation and the sensitivities.

Three matters require board attention and are not resolved by this document alone:

**The Everlight platform decision.** Everlight runs on a third-party application framework whose vendor has announced end of support in March 2027. A rewrite has been scoped at $4.2 million. The business is contracting and lost its founder and two engineers in April. The board must decide whether to fund the rewrite, pursue an alternative, or plan an exit.

**The VetLedger billing migration.** The migration is four months behind schedule and 190 practices are still invoiced manually. This is a cash conversion problem, a customer experience problem, and a diligence-credibility problem in roughly that order of urgency.

**RxCounter's competitive position.** RxCounter lost 340 of 4,100 pharmacy customers in the half to a competitor bundling wholesaler ordering. The business is flat at $27 million. Management has not yet brought a strategic recommendation to the board and commits to doing so in Q3.

## 3. Business Unit Review

### 3.1 DentalPath — Practice Management Software

| Metric | H1 FY2026 | Prior Year | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual recurring revenue | $38.0M | $35.8M | +6.1% |
| Gross revenue retention | 91% | 92% | −1 pt |
| Share of consolidated ARR | 26.0% | — | — |
| Headcount | 198 | 191 | +7 |

DentalPath is the largest business in the portfolio by revenue and the most stable. Six percent growth on a $38 million base is not exciting, but it is predictable, and the business generates the most reliable cash contribution in the group. Gross retention of 91 percent is consistent with the four-year average and reflects a customer base of independent and small-group dental practices whose principal churn driver is practice closure or sale rather than competitive displacement.

**The problem: growth is entirely price-driven and the price lever is close to exhausted.**

Decomposing the 6.1 percent growth reveals the issue. Net new logo contribution was approximately 1.8 points. Expansion within the existing base — additional operatories, added modules — contributed roughly 2.4 points. Price increases contributed the remaining 1.9 points. Against that, gross churn of 9 percent was partially offset within these figures.

The company has raised list price on DentalPath in each of the last three renewal cycles, cumulatively 14 percent. Renewal-cycle escalation is now being met with resistance. The customer success team logged 61 escalations in H1 in which price was the stated reason for a renewal delay, against 34 in the prior-year half. None of these have converted to churn at material rates yet, but the leading indicator is unambiguous.

New logo acquisition is the constraint. DentalPath adds approximately 90 net new practices per quarter against a serviceable addressable market that management estimates at 41,000 practices in its target segment. The business holds roughly 4,700 practices. Penetration is low enough that the market is not the limit; the go-to-market motion is. DentalPath sells through a six-person inside sales team and a small channel of dental service organization referral partners. There has been no material investment in demand generation in three years.

Aaron Kleinfeld's assessment is that DentalPath's product is competitive but undifferentiated in the mid-market and that the fastest available growth lever is not product but distribution. The H2 plan includes a proposal to add four inside sales representatives and a demand generation manager at an annualized cost of approximately $780,000. Under the current covenant position, this investment competes directly with the Everlight rewrite and with VetLedger remediation. Section 7 addresses the sequencing.

**Second-half expectation:** DentalPath finishes FY2026 at approximately $39.2 million ARR, growing 6 to 6.5 percent. Retention holds at 90 to 91 percent. No material downside risk is identified within the fiscal year; the risk is to FY2027 if the price lever is pulled a fourth time without distribution investment behind it.

### 3.2 RxCounter — Pharmacy Point of Sale

| Metric | H1 FY2026 | Prior Year | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual recurring revenue | $27.0M | $27.1M | −0.4% |
| Pharmacy customers | 3,760 | 4,100 | −340 |
| Gross revenue retention | 87% | 93% | −6 pts |
| Share of consolidated ARR | 18.5% | — | — |
| Headcount | 142 | 149 | −7 |

RxCounter is flat, and flat is a generous description of what is happening underneath the number. The business lost 340 of 4,100 pharmacy customers during the half — 8.3 percent of the installed base — and held revenue only because the departing accounts skewed toward smaller single-location pharmacies while price increases and modest expansion in the retained base offset the loss.

**The problem: a competitor is bundling wholesaler ordering, and Fairmount cannot match the bundle.**

The displacement is concentrated and attributable. A competitor has integrated pharmaceutical wholesaler ordering directly into its point-of-sale product, allowing a pharmacist to manage inventory replenishment and purchasing in the same system that handles the register. For an independent pharmacy operating on thin margins with one or two staff members handling both functions, the workflow consolidation is worth more than the software itself. Win/loss interviews conducted by the RxCounter team across 74 of the 340 departures identified wholesaler integration as the primary or contributing reason in 68 cases.

RxCounter does not have this capability and cannot build it quickly. Wholesaler ordering integration requires commercial agreements with the major pharmaceutical distributors, each of whom controls its own ordering interface and has no particular incentive to open it to a point-of-sale vendor of Fairmount's scale. The competitor achieved this through a relationship that predates its software business. Aaron Kleinfeld's engineering assessment is that the technical work is on the order of nine to twelve months once agreements are in place, and that securing the agreements is the binding constraint, not the code.

Three paths exist and management has not yet chosen among them:

**Pursue the integrations.** Fairmount opens commercial discussions with two or three distributors. Timeline to a shippable capability is realistically eighteen to twenty-four months, during which churn continues. Cost is uncertain but the software portion is estimated at $1.6 to $2.1 million.

**Reposition RxCounter upmarket.** The 340 departures skewed small. Regional chains of eight or more locations churned at 4 percent, well below the segment average, because their workflows are more complex and their wholesaler relationships are already handled through separate enterprise systems. Refocusing sales and product on the 8-to-40 location segment concedes the single-location market and preserves a smaller but more defensible business. This path implies ARR contraction to perhaps $23 to $24 million over two years before stabilizing.

**Prepare RxCounter for divestiture.** At $27 million ARR and flat growth, RxCounter would not attract a strong multiple, but the customer base has value to a strategic buyer with wholesaler relationships already in place — including, plausibly, the competitor taking the customers. A sale would generate proceeds applicable to debt reduction, which given the covenant position has value beyond the headline price.

Management commits to bringing a recommendation to the board in Q3 FY2026 supported by a full segment analysis and, in the divestiture case, a preliminary market read from a banker. The recommendation will not be deferred past that meeting.

**Second-half expectation:** RxCounter finishes FY2026 at $26.4 to $27.0 million. Customer count declines a further 260 to 320. Gross retention does not recover within the fiscal year. Revenue holds approximately flat only if the H2 price increase is executed as planned, which carries its own churn risk in a base already under pressure.

### 3.3 CivicDesk — Municipal Permitting

| Metric | H1 FY2026 | Prior Year | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual recurring revenue | $22.0M | $18.5M | +18.9% |
| Gross revenue retention | 96% | 95% | +1 pt |
| Implementation backlog | 14 months | 8 months | +6 months |
| Services gross margin | −4% | +6% | −10 pts |
| Share of consolidated ARR | 15.1% | — | — |
| Headcount | 121 | 96 | +25 |

CivicDesk is the fastest-growing business in the portfolio on a percentage basis excluding VetLedger, and it has the best retention. Municipal customers do not churn; the procurement cycle that brought them in is the same cycle that makes them expensive to replace. Ninety-six percent gross retention on a municipal base is close to a structural floor.

**The problem: the company is selling faster than it can implement, and the services organization is now losing money.**

The implementation backlog has gone from eight months to fourteen months in a year. A municipality that signs a contract in April will not be live until the following June. During that period Fairmount recognizes no subscription revenue, the customer's enthusiasm decays, and the reference the sales team wants for the next deal does not exist.

The services margin has gone from positive 6 percent to negative 4 percent. This is the more serious of the two figures because it indicates the backlog is not simply a staffing shortfall but a delivery model problem. Three factors drive it:

**Scope variance.** CivicDesk implementations involve migrating historical permit records from whatever system the municipality previously used, which in a meaningful number of cases is paper, spreadsheets, or a mainframe application maintained by a retired employee. Fixed-fee implementation contracts have been signed against scope assumptions that hold roughly 60 percent of the time. When they do not hold, the overage is absorbed.

**Configuration depth.** Every municipality's permitting workflow reflects its own ordinance history. CivicDesk has treated this as a configuration exercise, and configuration has grown to consume an average of 240 hours per implementation against an original model of 120. The product supports the flexibility; the services model was never repriced for it.

**Staffing composition.** The team added 25 people in the last year, most of them into implementation roles. New implementation consultants are not productive on municipal projects for roughly five months. The team is currently carrying an unusually high proportion of ramping staff, which depresses realized utilization to 64 percent against a 78 percent target.

The remediation plan Aaron Kleinfeld and the CivicDesk general manager have developed has three components. First, repricing: implementation fees rise approximately 35 percent for new contracts signed after 1 August, and fixed-fee arrangements are replaced with a fixed-fee base plus a scoped data migration line item priced on record volume. Second, productization of configuration: the six most common ordinance patterns are being built as pre-configured templates, targeting a reduction of configuration hours from 240 to 150 per implementation by the end of Q4. Third, a partner channel: two regional government-technology consultancies have been engaged to take overflow implementations at a referral margin, targeting 20 percent of the backlog by Q4.

Management expects services margin to return to breakeven by Q4 FY2026 and to positive 8 to 10 percent during FY2027. The backlog is expected to fall to ten months by fiscal year end and to six months by mid-FY2027. These targets depend on the partner channel producing, which is the least proven of the three components.

**A note on growth quality.** Nineteen percent growth at CivicDesk is real but should be understood correctly. Municipal software sales are lumpy and procurement-driven, and H1 benefited from three contracts above $400,000 in annual value that landed in the same quarter. The underlying run-rate growth is closer to 15 percent. The board should not extrapolate 19 percent forward.

**Second-half expectation:** CivicDesk finishes FY2026 at approximately $23.6 million ARR, growing 16 to 18 percent for the full year. Services margin at or near breakeven in Q4. Backlog at ten to eleven months.

### 3.4 StoreVault — Self-Storage Management

| Metric | H1 FY2026 | Prior Year | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual recurring revenue | $31.0M | $27.9M | +11.1% |
| Gross revenue retention | 93% | 93% | — |
| Payments attach rate (tenants) | 34% | 26% | +8 pts |
| Share of consolidated ARR | 21.2% | — | — |
| Headcount | 134 | 128 | +6 |

StoreVault is the best-performing business in the portfolio on a combined growth-and-quality basis. Eleven percent ARR growth on a $31 million base, stable retention, and an embedded payments business that is compounding faster than the software beneath it.

The payments attach rate — the percentage of storage tenants whose rent payments flow through Fairmount's embedded payments product rather than a third-party processor or a check — reached 34 percent, up from 26 percent a year ago. This matters disproportionately. Payments revenue carries a different economic profile than subscription revenue: it scales with tenant count and rent levels rather than with facility count, it does not require a new sale to grow, and its gross margin after interchange and processor costs runs in the high fifties. Attach-rate expansion has contributed approximately 3.4 points of StoreVault's 11.1 percent growth.

**The problem: the payments economics are excellent and the concentration risk underneath them is not being managed.**

StoreVault's embedded payments product runs through a single payment facilitator relationship. That facilitator handles underwriting, settlement, and compliance for every transaction. The commercial agreement runs through August 2027 and contains a 180-day termination-for-convenience provision on the facilitator's side.

This is a $10.5 million annualized revenue stream inside a $31 million business, growing faster than anything else in the portfolio, dependent on a counterparty that can exit with six months' notice. It is also, from the lender group's perspective, a revenue stream whose durability is materially different from subscription ARR and which the covenant calculation currently treats identically.

Management is taking three actions. A second facilitator relationship is being negotiated to establish redundancy, with a target of a signed agreement by Q4 FY2026 and initial volume routing in Q1 FY2027. The existing agreement is being renegotiated ahead of its term to extend the notice period and add a transition-services obligation. And the finance team, at Simone Baptiste's direction, is separating payments revenue from subscription revenue in internal reporting so that the two are visible independently to the board and can be discussed on their own terms with the lender group.

A second, smaller issue deserves mention. StoreVault's growth is partly a function of self-storage industry construction, which added supply at an elevated rate through 2023 and 2024 and has since slowed considerably. New facility openings in StoreVault's markets are running roughly 30 percent below the 2023 peak. The business has not yet felt this because the sales pipeline reflects facilities that broke ground eighteen months ago. It will feel it in FY2027. Management's planning assumption is that StoreVault's subscription growth moderates to 7 to 8 percent in FY2027 with payments attach continuing to carry the total to 10 percent or so.

**Second-half expectation:** StoreVault finishes FY2026 at approximately $32.5 million ARR. Payments attach reaches 38 to 40 percent. Retention holds at 93 percent.

### 3.5 Everlight — Funeral Home Management

| Metric | H1 FY2026 | Prior Year | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Annual recurring revenue | $16.0M | $16.5M | −3.0% |
| Gross revenue retention | 89% | 92% | −3 pts |
| Share of consolidated ARR | 11.0% | — | — |
| Headcount | 68 | 79 | −11 |
| Engineering headcount | 9 | 14 | −5 |

Everlight is contracting, and it has the most severe structural problem in the portfolio. The full decision is treated in Section 5; this section establishes the operating position.

Revenue fell 3 percent to $16 million. Gross retention declined three points to 89 percent. The business serves approximately 2,900 funeral homes, predominantly independent single-location operators, with case management, arrangement documentation, state filing, and pre-need contract administration.

**The problem: the platform reaches end of vendor support in March 2027, the founder has left, and the engineering team that knew the system has been halved.**

Everlight was built on a commercial application framework whose vendor announced in January 2026 that support ends March 2027. After that date there are no security patches, no compatibility updates, and no vendor recourse. The framework underpins Everlight's data access layer, its user interface, and its reporting engine. It is not a component that can be swapped; it is the substrate.

Warren Sikes, Everlight's founder, who joined Fairmount with the 2020 acquisition and stayed on as general manager, departed in April 2026. Two engineers left with him. Combined with three earlier departures, Everlight's engineering team has gone from fourteen to nine in twelve months. Of the nine remaining, four have been with the product more than three years.

The knowledge loss is material and specific. Sikes wrote the original pre-need contract engine, which is the most regulatorily complex part of the product — pre-need funeral contracts are governed by state trust and insurance law that varies substantially across the forty-one states Everlight operates in. Documentation of the state-specific rule logic is incomplete. The two engineers who left with Sikes were the two most familiar with the framework-dependent portions of the data layer.

**Interim actions taken.** An interim general manager has been appointed from the DentalPath organization. Retention arrangements have been extended to the four longest-tenured remaining engineers, at a total cost of $340,000 payable in two tranches through March 2027. A knowledge capture project is underway to document the pre-need rule logic before further attrition; it is approximately 40 percent complete and expected to finish in Q3.

**On the retention decline.** The three-point retention drop is not yet attributable to the platform issue — Everlight has not communicated the end-of-support date to customers and there is no evidence of competitive exploitation. The decline appears driven by funeral home consolidation, with larger acquirers migrating acquired locations to their own standardized systems. Eleven of the nineteen accounts lost in H1 were consolidation-driven. This is a secular trend and will continue regardless of the platform decision.

**Second-half expectation:** Everlight finishes FY2026 at approximately $15.7 million ARR, down 3 to 4 percent. Retention at 88 to 89 percent. These figures assume the platform decision is made in Q3 and does not itself become a customer-facing event within the fiscal year.

VetLedger was acquired in November 2025 for $61 million, representing approximately 6.5 times ARR at close. It is the fastest-growing business in the portfolio at 27.7 percent and the newest. ### 4.5 The FY2027 Step-Down

The credit agreement steps the maximum permitted leverage ratio down to 4.50 times effective with the Q2 FY2027 compliance certificate. At current adjusted EBITDA and debt levels, the company would be at 4.61 times against a 4.50 times maximum — a breach.

Compliance at the stepped-down level requires trailing adjusted EBITDA of at least $64.8 million with debt constant, or approximately $3.5 million of debt paydown at constant EBITDA, or a combination. Additionally, the $2.9 million VetLedger integration add-back will have rolled out of the TTM calculation by that date, meaning the underlying EBITDA improvement required is larger than the headline gap suggests.

The company's FY2027 plan must therefore deliver approximately $6 to $7 million of adjusted EBITDA improvement over the current TTM figure, or an equivalent combination of EBITDA growth and debt reduction. This is achievable — the CivicDesk services recovery alone is worth $2.4 to $2.8 million annualized, and VetLedger's growth at current margin contributes $1.5 to $1.8 million — but it does not leave room for the Everlight rewrite to be funded from operations, and it does not leave room for the RxCounter situation to deteriorate further.

Management's recommendation, developed with Simone Baptiste, is that the company open a conversation with the administrative agent in Q3 FY2026 rather than Q1 FY2027. The subjects would be a covenant amendment providing relief on the step-down in exchange for a fee and tightened reporting; a carve-out permitting the Everlight platform investment to be added back as a non-recurring modernization expense up to a capped amount; and confirmation of the treatment of divestiture proceeds should RxCounter proceed to a sale. Approaching the lender group from the current compliant position with a credible plan is a materially better posture than approaching them from a projected breach.

## 5. The Everlight Platform Decision

### 5.1 The Situation

Everlight's underlying application framework loses vendor support in March 2027 — eleven months from the date of this report and five months into FY2027. After that date the framework receives no security patches. Everlight processes payment information, personally identifiable information about deceased individuals and their families, and pre-need contract data subject to state trust regulation. Operating unsupported software against that data profile is not a position the company can hold indefinitely.

The rewrite has been scoped by Aaron Kleinfeld's team at $4.2 million and fourteen months, with the caveat that both figures assume a team that no longer exists in full.

### 5.2 Options

**Option A — Fund the rewrite at $4.2 million.**

Rebuild Everlight on a modern, internally supported stack. The estimate comprises approximately $2.6 million of engineering labor including contract augmentation, $700,000 of data migration and testing, $500,000 of infrastructure and tooling, and $400,000 of contingency at roughly 10 percent.

The schedule risk is significant. A fourteen-month estimate produced by a team that has lost five of fourteen engineers, including the two most familiar with the framework-dependent layers and the author of the pre-need contract engine, should be treated as optimistic. Kleinfeld's own confidence interval runs fourteen to twenty months. Twenty months exceeds the support deadline by six.

The financial treatment matters. A portion of the spend qualifies for capitalization as internally developed software, which softens the P&L impact but does not soften the cash impact. Preliminary assessment suggests roughly 65 percent capitalizable, leaving approximately $1.5 million flowing through the P&L across two fiscal years. Against 0.14 turns of headroom, even $1.5 million spread over two years is not comfortable, and the cash draw of $4.2 million against $23.6 million of effective liquidity is the harder constraint.

**Option B — Extended support or a bridge.**

Negotiate paid extended support from the framework vendor. Vendors commonly offer this, typically at a substantial premium and typically covering security patches only. Initial contact has been made; no pricing has been received. If extended support can be secured through, say, March 2029 at a cost of $400,000 to $700,000 annually, it buys two years to execute the rewrite on a sane schedule, rebuild the team, or run a sale process.

This option does not solve the problem. It sequences it. But sequencing has real value given the covenant step-down in FY2027 — moving $4.2 million of cash outlay out of the constrained window is worth a meaningful premium.

**Option C — Migrate Everlight customers onto an adjacent Fairmount platform.**

Everlight's core case management workflow shares structure with elements of DentalPath's scheduling and records architecture. Kleinfeld's assessment is that this is a superficial resemblance and that the pre-need contract engine, state filing integrations, and merchandise inventory functions have no analogue anywhere in the portfolio. The estimated effort to extend an existing platform to cover Everlight's requirements is not materially less than the rewrite, and it introduces risk to a $38 million business to protect a $16 million one. Management does not recommend this option.

**Option D — Sell Everlight.**

Everlight is a $16 million ARR business contracting at 3 percent with 89 percent retention, a platform end-of-life eleven months out, no general manager, and a halved engineering team. This is not a strong selling position. A buyer would discount for all of it.

However, there are strategic acquirers in death-care software with their own platforms onto which Everlight's customer base could be migrated, for whom the platform problem is irrelevant because they would not run the platform. For such a buyer, the asset is 2,900 funeral home relationships. Management's rough estimate of value in that scenario is $28 to $38 million — 1.75 to 2.4 times ARR — which is below what Fairmount paid in 2020 but which would reduce net leverage by roughly 0.45 to 0.60 turns and eliminate the capital requirement entirely.

### 5.4 Management's Position

Management does not bring a single recommendation to this meeting and states plainly why: Option B's pricing is unknown and Option D's value is untested, and committing to Option A before either is known would be a decision made in the absence of the two most relevant facts.

The recommended course is a sixty-day parallel process, running to the end of September 2026:

**Track one.** Obtain firm extended-support pricing from the framework vendor. Owner: Aaron Kleinfeld. Due: 31 August 2026.

**Track two.** Engage a boutique software banker for a confidential market read on Everlight, without a formal process launch. Owner: Noor Haddad, with Simone Baptiste. Due: 30 September 2026.

**Track three.** Complete the pre-need knowledge capture project and re-baseline the rewrite estimate with the actual current team. Owner: Aaron Kleinfeld. Due: 30 September 2026.

**Track four.** Stabilize Everlight operationally regardless of outcome — retain the interim general manager, hold the four retained engineers, maintain the product. Owner: interim GM, reporting to Patrick Molnar.

A decision paper will be brought to the board at the November 2026 meeting with a single recommendation and full supporting analysis. Management's current lean, held loosely, is toward a combination of B and D: secure extended support to remove the deadline pressure, then run a proper sale process from a stable position in FY2027 rather than a distressed one in FY2026.

The board is asked to approve the sixty-day process, authorize up to $150,000 for banker and advisory fees, and confirm that management is not authorized to commit to the $4.2 million rewrite before the November meeting.

## 6. VetLedger Integration

### 6.1 Position

VetLedger closed in November 2025. Five months of ownership have produced a clear picture: the growth thesis is validated, and the integration execution is not.

| Workstream | Plan | Status | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Financial consolidation | Dec 2025 | Complete | On time |
| HR and payroll | Jan 2026 | Complete | On time |
| Billing platform migration | Mar 2026 | 86% complete | **4 months late** |
| CRM consolidation | Apr 2026 | Complete | On time |
| Support tooling | Jun 2026 | On track | — |
| Product integration assessment | Sep 2026 | On track | — |

Five of six workstreams are on or ahead of plan. The sixth is the one that touches customers and cash.

### 6.2 The Billing Migration

VetLedger operated a proprietary billing system that handled subscription invoicing, usage-based charges for its claims-processing module, and multi-location consolidated billing for veterinary groups. The migration plan assumed the multi-location logic could be represented in Fairmount's platform through existing parent-child account structures.

It could not. VetLedger's multi-location customers include groups where individual practices carry different contract terms, different module entitlements, and different billing cycles, but require a single consolidated invoice with per-location cost allocation. Fairmount's platform supports consolidated invoicing and supports differentiated terms, but not both simultaneously across a single parent.

This was discovered in February, three weeks before cutover, when the migration team ran the first full test. Approximately 1,150 of 1,340 practices — the single-location and simply-structured accounts — migrated successfully in March. The remaining 190 practices, representing the complex multi-location groups, could not, and are being invoiced manually.

**Why diligence missed it.** Billing systems diligence focused on revenue recognition accuracy and contract documentation rather than on the structural feasibility of platform consolidation. The integration plan's timeline was built on a technical assessment conducted after signing, over eleven days, by a team that had not seen the customer contract structures. Noor Haddad's recommendation, endorsed by Patrick Molnar, is that future acquisition diligence include a billing-architecture assessment conducted by the platform engineering team before signing, not after.

### 6.3 Impact

| Impact | Measure |
|---|---|
| Practices manually invoiced | 190 (14% of base) |
| ARR under manual invoicing | ~$3.4M |
| DSO, manual cohort | 61 days |
| DSO, automated cohort | 34 days |
| Working capital tied up | ~$0.25M |
| Manual invoicing FTEs | 3.0 |
| Annualized manual cost | ~$0.21M |
| Billing error rate, manual | ~4% |
| Credits issued from errors (H1) | $47,000 |
| Churn attributable to billing | 1 practice ($31K ARR) |
| At-risk accounts citing billing | 3 practices ($112K ARR) |

The financial impact is contained — roughly $460,000 of cost, credits, and working capital drag on an annualized basis. The reputational impact within the affected accounts is the greater concern. These 190 practices are the multi-location groups, which are the largest, fastest-growing, and most strategically valuable customers in the VetLedger base. They are the accounts through which the consolidation thesis is expressed. Sending them hand-typed invoices with a 4 percent error rate five months after acquiring their vendor is precisely the wrong impression.

### 6.4 Recovery Plan

| Milestone | Owner | Target Date |
|---|---|---|
| Platform enhancement spec complete | A. Kleinfeld | Complete (May 2026) |
| Development complete | Platform eng. | 31 Aug 2026 |
| Test migration, 20 pilot accounts | Integration team | 30 Sep 2026 |
| Pilot validation and sign-off | Integration + Finance | 15 Oct 2026 |
| Full migration, remaining 170 | Integration team | 30 Nov 2026 |
| Manual invoicing discontinued | Finance | 15 Dec 2026 |
| Post-migration audit | Internal audit | 31 Jan 2027 |

The platform enhancement — allowing differentiated contract terms beneath a consolidated invoice parent — has been specified and is in development. It has value beyond VetLedger: DentalPath and CivicDesk have both lost deals involving multi-entity billing structures, and the capability is on the platform roadmap independently. Approximately 60 percent of the $1.9 million cost is therefore properly attributed to platform development rather than integration remediation.

**Customer communication.** All 190 affected practices have received direct outreach from VetLedger's general manager. Communication has been specific about the cause and the timeline rather than general. Fee credits totaling $88,000 have been offered to the 34 accounts that experienced two or more billing errors. Three at-risk accounts have executive sponsors assigned from the Fairmount leadership team.

**Accountability.** Patrick Molnar's note for the record: the integration was managed to a schedule rather than to a set of validated technical assumptions, and the eleven-day post-signing assessment was inadequate to the complexity. The corrective process changes — pre-signing billing architecture review, mandatory full-population migration testing before any cutover commitment, and a monthly integration status report to the board for the first four quarters of ownership — are being applied to all future acquisitions and retroactively to the remaining VetLedger workstreams.

### 6.5 What Is Working

The growth is real. VetLedger added 84 net new practices in H1 against a plan of 71. Retention held at 94 percent despite the billing problem. The claims-processing module, which was the strategic rationale for the acquisition beyond core practice management, has attached to 41 percent of the base against 33 percent at close. Cross-sell of Fairmount's embedded payments product — leveraging the StoreVault facilitator relationship — begins pilot in Q3 and represents an estimated $1.4 to $1.9 million ARR opportunity within eighteen months.

At $12 million ARR growing 27.7 percent, VetLedger is tracking ahead of the acquisition model, which assumed 22 percent. The entry multiple of 6.5 times is looking reasonable. The integration has been mismanaged in one workstream; the acquisition has not been a mistake.

## 8. Summary

Fairmount enters the second half of fiscal 2026 with $146 million of recurring revenue growing 7.4 percent, 0.14 turns of covenant headroom, and a portfolio in which the businesses that grow and the businesses that have problems are largely different businesses.

CivicDesk, StoreVault, and VetLedger are working. Together they represent 44.5 percent of revenue and essentially all of the growth. Their problems — a services delivery model that needs repricing, a single-counterparty dependency, a botched billing migration — are execution problems with identified owners and dated plans.

DentalPath is stable and under-invested. It is the largest business in the portfolio and the one where a modest, well-directed investment in distribution would most reliably produce returns, and it is being asked to wait because capital is constrained elsewhere.

RxCounter and Everlight are structural problems. Neither will be solved by better execution. RxCounter has lost a feature war it did not know it was fighting; Everlight has lost its platform, its founder, and half its engineers within twelve months. Together they represent 29.5 percent of revenue and the entirety of the company's strategic uncertainty. Both require board decisions within the next two quarters, and this review commits management to bringing recommendations on both — RxCounter in Q3, Everlight in November.

The covenant is the frame around all of it. At 4.61 times against 4.75, with a step-down to 4.50 coming in FY2027 and the VetLedger add-back rolling off, the company has enough room to operate and not enough room to be wrong twice. That argues for the proactive lender conversation recommended in Section 4.5, for the disciplined capital sequencing in Section 7.5, and for resolving RxCounter and Everlight from a position of choice rather than waiting until the position chooses for us.
