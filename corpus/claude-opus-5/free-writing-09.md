# Still

**still**, *adj.* — Free from sound or movement; motionless; calm.

The Beaufort scale, which measures wind by what wind does to things, defines its zero this way: *Sea like a mirror. Smoke rises vertically.* Force zero is not described as an absence. It is described as a column of smoke going straight up, and a surface holding an image without distortion.

This is the first honest thing about stillness: you cannot see it directly. You see it in the smoke, which has to be moving in order to show you that nothing else is. You see it in the mirror of the water, which is only a mirror because there is a sky above it to be borrowed. Stillness is legible only through an intermediary — a candle flame, a dust mote in a shaft of light, the surface of tea in a cup on a train, that little trembling meniscus by which passengers know, without looking up, whether they are moving.

The word comes from Old English *stille*, and behind that a root meaning *to stand, to place, to set in order*. Which is worth pausing on. Stillness in this family is not emptiness. It is arrangement. Things put where they belong and left there.

---

**still**, *adv.* — Up to this time; even now; nevertheless.

Here is the strangest turn in the word's history, and nobody made it happen on purpose. *Motionless* became *continuously* became *up to now*. If a thing does not move, it stays; if it stays, it persists; if it persists, it is still here. The adjective walked into the adverb through a door that shouldn't have been there.

The two senses now sit so close that only word order keeps them apart.

*She is sitting still.*
*She is still sitting.*

Four words each. One says: she has stopped. The other says: she has not stopped. English asks you to tell them apart by rhythm alone, and — this is the remarkable part — you can. Nobody is confused by this. Every speaker of the language performs the distinction thousands of times without noticing it, the way you don't notice your tongue against your teeth.

But in certain rooms the two senses collapse back into each other, and you find that the language has been holding something you weren't ready for. A hospital corridor at four in the morning. Somebody says *she's still*, and the pause before the next word is the whole of it. *She's still here.* Two opposite mercies out of one syllable, and for a second, both of them are true, because the sentence hasn't ended yet.

I think this is why the word is so hard to use lightly in grief. It has already been there. It arrived before you did and worked out the arithmetic: that to endure is to remain unmoved; that the dead are still in both senses; that *still* is the word we use for whatever refuses to be finished, and also for whatever is finished entirely.

---

**still**, *n.¹* — An apparatus for distillation.

This one is not related. It's a foundling. It comes from *distill*, from Latin *destillare*, from *stilla*, a drop — to trickle down. Etymologically the still is not a stillness at all. It is a dripping.

And yet. Consider what the object asks of you. Copper, because copper takes the sulfur out. A pot, a head, a swan's neck bending over like something drinking, and then the worm coiled in a bath of cold water. The heat must be low. If you hurry it you scorch the wash and everything downstream tastes of it. There is no technique for going faster; there is only the decision not to.

And when the spirit begins to run, the distiller separates it into three parts, and the names are not technical, they are anatomical. First comes the *foreshot*, or heads — volatile, sharp, poisonous in quantity. Last comes the *tails* — oily, dull, wet cardboard. In between is the part you keep, and it is called *the heart*.

You cut the heads and the tails to keep the heart. That is the whole of it, and it takes a person standing there with a hydrometer and a nose, making a judgment that cannot be automated, at a moment that will not come again in that batch.

So the interloper turns out to have married well. A stranger came into this family of words carrying an unrelated meaning, and three hundred years later you cannot tell it apart from the others, because it, too, is about holding something over low heat until only what matters rises.

---

**still**, *v.* — To make quiet; to calm; to allay.

*Peace, be still* — spoken to weather. Whoever translated that in 1611 gave the storm the same imperative you'd give a frightened animal or a child at two in the morning: not *stop*, which is a command about behavior, but *be still*, which is a command about being. It assumes the storm has an interior.

Eight chapters back in the same book there is the more astonishing phrase. Elijah on the mountain: a great wind that broke the rocks, and God was not in the wind; an earthquake, and not in the earthquake; a fire, and not in the fire. And after the fire, in the King James, *a still small voice*.

The Hebrew is *qol demamah daqqah*. A sound of thin silence. A voice made of the material of quiet, beaten out fine, the way gold is beaten. Not the absence of the wind and the fire but a thing with its own substance, thinner than they were and therefore harder to break.

The English is a good translation. *Still* is exactly right, because it carries both the quiet and the continuing: the voice that is calm, and the voice that is *still* speaking, after everything loud has spent itself and gone.

---

**still**, *n.²* — A single photographic image; a frame taken from a moving picture.

Until 1878, no one had ever seen a horse gallop. People had watched horses gallop all their lives — for millennia — and had painted them wrong, legs splayed fore and aft like a rocking horse, because the eye cannot hold what the leg does at that speed. Then Muybridge put twelve cameras along a track in Palo Alto and stopped it. All four hooves leave the ground, and when they do, the legs are gathered underneath the body, not extended. The truth was available the whole time and required stopping to be seen.

So the mirror completes itself. Stillness is only visible through motion — the smoke, the flame, the tea in the cup. And motion is only visible through stillness — the frame, cut out of the twenty-four that go by every second, in which a face finally holds an expression long enough to be read.

One more: the Dutch called a painted arrangement of fruit and glass and a dead pheasant a *stilleven* — a still life. The French, translating, called it *nature morte*. Dead nature. Same table, same pheasant, same slant of light. One language looked at it and saw death; one looked at it and saw *life, held*. English took the Dutch. I have always thought that was a kindness, and that it was probably an accident, and that most kindnesses in a language are.

---

So: one syllable, doing this much work.

*Cease*, and *continue*. *Hold*, and *drip*. *Calm down*, and *carry on*. A word that can mean the sea is a mirror and can mean the dead are with us and can mean the heart of the run, the part you keep, cut carefully out of what came before it and what came after.

I don't think it's confusion. I think a very large number of people, over a very long time, kept reaching for the same sound in two opposite conditions — when everything had stopped, and when something wouldn't — and eventually the language stopped objecting.

Smoke rises vertically. The sea is like a mirror.

Still here.
