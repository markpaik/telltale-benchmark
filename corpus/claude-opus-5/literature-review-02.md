**Prepared for the Board of Directors, Southwest Advanced Manufacturing Institute, Chandler, Arizona**

Sofia Kuznetsova, PhD, Research Director
Bernard Achebe-Lomax, CIH, Industrial Hygiene Program

*Commissioned April 2026 · Draft for board review*

## 1. Introduction: the question and its boundaries

Three SAMI member plants are in the process of converting production crews from eight-hour to twelve-hour rotating shifts. A fourth reversed the same conversion in 2025 after its OSHA recordable rate rose from 2.9 to 4.6 per 100 full-time workers across the eighteen months following changeover. In April 2026, a maintenance technician at a member foundry was fatally injured during the eleventh hour of a night shift. The board asked whether the consortium's members should treat extended-shift schedules as a hazard requiring control, as a neutral administrative choice, or as something in between.

That question, stated plainly, is not one the literature answers plainly. It is worth saying so at the outset, because a review that arrived at a clean verdict would be misrepresenting a body of work that has been arguing with itself for four decades. What the literature does offer is something more useful than a verdict: a reasonably clear account of *which* conditions make extended shifts dangerous, *which* study designs tend to detect that danger and which tend to miss it, and *where* the evidence is thin enough that member plants should be collecting their own.

**Scope.** This review covers scheduled shift length in industrial production and maintenance work, with attention to:

- **Shift length** — eight-hour versus ten-, twelve-, and (rarely) fourteen-hour scheduled shifts.
- **Rotation** — fixed versus rotating; direction (forward/delaying versus backward/advancing); speed (rapid, weekly, slow).
- **Cumulative exposure** — consecutive shifts worked, days off, and overtime stacked onto an already-extended base schedule.
- **Moderators** — task type and physical load, thermal environment, nap and break policy, crew age and tenure, staffing margin.
- **Outcomes** — recordable and lost-time injuries, near-miss and first-aid events, objective and subjective fatigue, psychomotor vigilance, quality escapes and scrap, absence, turnover and retention, and (secondarily) long-term cardiometabolic and sleep health.

**Out of scope.** We excluded transport operator scheduling (aviation, long-haul trucking, rail) except where laboratory or modeling work from those sectors bears directly on the hours-on-task question; hours-of-service regulation as a legal topic; on-call and standby arrangements without a fixed shift structure; and healthcare shift research except where it supplies the only available evidence on a theme (notably error and quality outcomes, Section 3.8). Healthcare findings are flagged as such wherever used, because the transfer to a foundry floor is not automatic.

**A definitional caution that shapes everything downstream.** "Twelve-hour shift" is not one exposure. A fixed day twelve, four on / four off, in a climate-controlled assembly cell, with two thirty-minute breaks and a staffing margin that makes refusing overtime realistic, is a different exposure from a rapidly backward-rotating twelve on a hot-work line where a third of the crew routinely picks up a thirteenth and fourteenth day. The literature frequently pools these. Much of the disagreement summarized in Section 4 dissolves, or at least becomes intelligible, once one stops treating shift length as a single variable.

## 2. Note on the search

We searched Scopus, PsycINFO, MEDLINE, and the federal occupational injury and illness research database for the period January 1985 through December 2025. The 1985 start reflects the point at which twelve-hour schedules became common enough in North American and European continuous-process manufacturing to generate comparative field data; a small number of foundational earlier works are cited in the synthesis where they remain the standard reference, but were not part of the systematic yield.

Search strings combined three concept blocks: (a) schedule terms — *shift work, extended shift, compressed workweek, 12-hour shift, long work hours, rotating shift, shift rotation, overtime*; (b) outcome terms — *occupational injury, accident, safety performance, fatigue, vigilance, sleepiness, error, near miss, defect, turnover, absenteeism*; and (c) setting terms — *manufacturing, plant, industrial, production, foundry, refinery, mill, assembly*. Block (c) was applied as a facet rather than a filter, so that laboratory and cross-sector work was retained.

The search returned 1,932 unique records after deduplication. Title and abstract screening by two reviewers independently (disagreements resolved by discussion; unresolved items retained for full-text review) advanced 310 records. Ninety-four met inclusion criteria:

| Study type | n | Principal contribution |
|---|---|---|
| Field studies (cohort, cross-sectional, crossover, interrupted time series) | 39 | Injury and absence outcomes under real schedules |
| Laboratory sleep and vigilance experiments | 21 | Dose–response of hours awake, hours on task, circadian phase |
| Surveys of preference, retention, and self-reported health | 18 | Worker attitudes, turnover, subjective fatigue |
| Meta-analyses and quantitative syntheses | 10 | Pooled effect estimates, heterogeneity structure |
| Total | 94 | |

**Inclusion criteria.** English-language; original data or quantitative synthesis; a defined comparison across shift length, rotation pattern, or consecutive-shift exposure; at least one of the outcome categories listed in Section 1. **Exclusion criteria.** Opinion, editorial, and case report without comparison; simulation-only work without empirical calibration; studies in which the schedule change was fully confounded with a concurrent facility closure, relocation, or workforce reduction of more than 20 percent, where no adjustment was attempted.

**Appraisal.** Field studies were rated on five features that our reading of the disagreement literature identified as decisive: exposure classification (self-report vs. payroll records), injury denominator (per worker vs. per shift vs. per exposure-hour), control for overtime, control for time-since-changeover, and handling of the healthy-worker survivor effect. Nineteen of the 39 field studies met three or more; six met four or more. Ratings, extracted effect sizes, shift-pattern descriptions, and exposure measures are held in the consortium database and are retrievable by author and year.

**Known limits of the search.** We did not search the grey literature systematically, and internal corporate schedule evaluations — of which member plants have produced at least a dozen — are almost certainly a repository of null and negative findings that never reached publication. Non-English European literature, particularly Scandinavian register studies, is represented only through English-language reports and pooled analyses. Two large process-industry cohorts identified in citation chasing could not be obtained in full text.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 The within-shift gradient: what happens after hour nine

The most internally consistent finding in the entire body of work comes from the laboratory. Across the 21 experimental studies reviewed, sustained-attention performance degrades as a function of time on task, and the degradation is steeper and less recoverable when it occurs on the circadian trough. Psychomotor vigilance lapse rates rise measurably after roughly eight to nine hours of continuous task engagement, and the curve is convex — hours eleven and twelve cost more than hours nine and ten (Van Dongen et al., 2003; Dawson & Reid, 1997; Williamson & Feyer, 2000). Dawson and Reid's much-cited equivalence between seventeen to nineteen hours of wakefulness and a blood alcohol concentration of 0.05 percent remains the clearest way to communicate the magnitude to a plant manager, and it is the finding most likely to be over-applied: it describes hours awake, not hours worked, and a twelve-hour day shift beginning at 06:00 on adequate sleep does not place a worker at that point of the curve.

Field studies of within-shift injury timing broadly corroborate the gradient but with far weaker effects. Folkard and Tucker (2003), pooling accident-time distributions across industrial datasets, found risk rising roughly monotonically across a shift, with the increment concentrated in the final two hours and amplified on night shifts. Hänecke et al. (1998), analyzing a very large German accident-insurance dataset, found the same shape, though the authors were careful that exposure-hour denominators after hour nine are dominated by overtime-taking subpopulations who differ from the general workforce. Two field studies in our set report the opposite pattern — a mid-shift peak and a late-shift decline — which their authors attribute to task assignment: the most hazardous operations are scheduled early, and the final hours of a twelve are disproportionately cleanup, documentation, and turnover activity (Duchon et al., 1997; a refinery cohort reported by Baker et al., 2003).

This matters directly for the April fatality. An event in the eleventh hour of a night shift is consistent with the gradient, but a single event is consistent with nearly anything; the base rate of eleventh-hour night work at that foundry is not small. The fatality is a reason to review the schedule. It is not evidence about the schedule.

**Where this leaves the theme.** Hours eleven and twelve are, physiologically, worse hours. Whether they translate into worse plant-level injury statistics depends on what is being done in them — which is a controllable variable, and one of the few clear levers this review identifies.

### 3.2 Injury rates by schedule: the field studies do not line up

Here the evidence fragments. Of the 39 field studies, sixteen report elevated injury rates under twelve-hour schedules, fourteen report no significant difference, and nine report lower rates under twelve-hour schedules. That distribution is not, on its face, informative. It becomes informative when the studies are sorted by what they actually compared.

Studies reporting **elevation** are concentrated among those examining *rotating* twelves, particularly rapid or backward rotation, and among those that treated overtime as part of the exposure rather than controlling it away (Rosa, 1995; Dembe et al., 2005; Vedaa et al., 2016). Dembe and colleagues' analysis of a large national longitudinal cohort is the strongest of these on design grounds: it used job-level hours histories rather than nominal schedule labels and found a hazard ratio of approximately 1.37 for jobs with extended daily hours, rising further for jobs combining extended hours with extended weekly hours. Its weakness is that it cannot separate schedule *design* from schedule *volume* — precisely the confound Section 4 returns to.

Studies reporting **no difference** are concentrated among fixed-shift comparisons in continuous-process settings with mature schedules — that is, plants that had been running twelves for five years or more at the time of study (Smith et al., 1998; Duchon et al., 1994; Tucker et al., 1996). Smith and colleagues' comparison across a chemical manufacturing group is frequently cited by advocates of twelve-hour schedules and is a genuinely well-conducted study, but its null result is a null under specific conditions: fixed shifts, forward rotation where rotation existed, formal nap provision on nights, and a mature schedule with self-selected-in crews.

Studies reporting **reduction** are the smallest group and the most heterogeneous. Two are the most methodologically interesting in the entire set. Both examined fixed twelve-hour schedules in facilities that converted from rapidly rotating eights, and both found post-conversion injury declines of 20 to 30 percent (Lowden et al., 1998; a chemical-plant crossover reported by Bonnefond et al., 2001). The mechanism the authors propose is not that twelve hours is benign but that the comparator was bad: rapidly rotating eight-hour schedules, especially backward-rotating ones with quick returns, impose circadian disruption that the twelve-hour pattern's longer recovery blocks partly offset. This is the single most important reframing in the field literature. **The relevant question is rarely "eight versus twelve." It is "which eight versus which twelve."**

Set against each other, the field studies are less contradictory than they appear. Fixed twelves in temperate, moderately loaded work, replacing rapidly rotating eights, look neutral to favorable. Rotating twelves in physically demanding or thermally stressed work, with overtime layered on, look unfavorable. The consortium's reversal case — the fourth plant, whose rate went from 2.9 to 4.6 — falls in the second category by its own schedule documentation, and its experience is therefore consistent with, not anomalous to, the published record.

### 3.3 Why the meta-analyses disagree

Ten quantitative syntheses met inclusion. The two that the board specifically asked about — a mid-1990s pooled analysis and a 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis — reach opposite conclusions about nominally the same schedule types, and reconciling them is instructive.

The 1990s synthesis (Knauth, 1993, and the pooled accident analyses it drew on) concluded that extended shifts carried a modest but real elevation in accident risk, concentrated in the final hours and on nights. The 2019 synthesis (Fischer et al., 2017, and its update; Nielsen et al., 2019) concluded that shift length per se showed no reliable independent association with injury once quick returns, night-shift frequency, and weekly hours were modeled.

Four differences account for most of the divergence:

**Included study populations.** The earlier synthesis drew heavily on studies from the 1970s and 1980s, when twelve-hour schedules were newer, staffing margins thinner, and mechanization lower. The later synthesis is dominated by post-2000 register studies from Scandinavia and payroll-linked cohorts, in workplaces with higher automation and stronger nap and break provisions. If the hazard is partly mediated by physical load and by whether the last two hours involve manual exposure, the two syntheses are describing genuinely different exposures under the same label.

**Exposure modeling.** The later work models schedules as multidimensional — shift length, rotation direction, quick returns, consecutive nights, weekly hours entered separately. When quick returns (fewer than eleven hours between shifts) are in the model, they absorb a substantial share of the variance previously attributed to shift length (Vedaa et al., 2016; Härmä et al., 2018). This is not an argument that shift length is harmless; it is an argument that the earlier estimate was partly a proxy.

**Outcome definition.** The earlier syntheses pooled accidents of all severities, including first-aid and near-miss reports where available. The later ones lean on registered lost-time injuries and compensation claims, which are rarer, later, and subject to reporting behavior that itself varies with schedule — crews on four-on/four-off report differently from crews who will be back tomorrow (see Section 4).

**Statistical handling of heterogeneity.** The 2019 work reports substantial residual heterogeneity ($I^2$ frequently above 70 percent) and interprets pooled estimates cautiously; the earlier work, following the conventions of its time, pooled more readily. A reader comparing only the headline estimates will overstate the disagreement, because one of the two syntheses is telling its readers that the pooled estimate is not the point.

**Assessment.** We do not think one synthesis supersedes the other. The later work is methodologically stronger and its exposure modeling is the direction the field should go. But its null on shift length is conditional on the workplaces in its sample, which are on average safer, cooler, better staffed, and more automated than several SAMI member plants. Generalizing the null to a hot-work foundry is an extrapolation the authors do not make.

### 3.4 Direction and speed of rotation

If the review has one finding robust enough to act on without further local evidence, it is this one. Forward (delaying) rotation — days to evenings to nights — outperforms backward (advancing) rotation on sleep duration, subjective fatigue, and, less consistently, injury (Knauth, 2007; Barton & Folkard, cited in Knauth's synthesis; Härmä et al., 2018). The physiological rationale is straightforward and does not depend on contested field data: the endogenous circadian period exceeds 24 hours in most adults, making phase delay easier to achieve than phase advance.

The complication is that forward rotation on a twelve-hour base is awkward to construct. A three-shift forward rotation is natural on eights; a two-shift twelve-hour system rotates only between days and nights, and the "direction" question becomes a question of transition frequency and of the recovery block placed at the day/night boundary. Several field studies find that a twelve-hour pattern with a minimum of 48 hours off at each day-to-night transition performs comparably to a forward-rotating eight (Totterdell et al., 1995; Axelsson et al., 2004). Two studies find that patterns which move workers from a day twelve directly to a night twelve after a single calendar day off produce sleep debt equal to or worse than backward-rotating eights (Sallinen & Kecklund, 2010; Vedaa et al., 2016).

**Set against each other:** the rotation literature is more consistent than the shift-length literature, and it suggests that a plant that must run twelves should spend its design effort on the transition architecture rather than on shift length itself.

### 3.5 Recovery, consecutive shifts, and the overtime problem

Twelve-hour schedules are usually sold — internally and in the survey literature — on the days off. Whether the days off compensate is the crux, and the evidence separates into two claims that are often conflated.

**Claim one: recovery blocks aid recovery.** Supported. Sleep debt accumulated across a run of night twelves is substantially, though not always fully, repaid across a four-day break (Åkerstedt, 1995; Lamond et al., in the laboratory literature; Axelsson et al., 2004). Subjective recovery ratings are consistently better under compressed schedules with long breaks than under five-day eight-hour weeks with two-day weekends.

**Claim two: risk within the run is therefore acceptable.** Not supported, and the two claims are logically independent. Risk is realized during the run, not averaged across the cycle. Folkard and Tucker's (2003) analysis of consecutive-shift effects found accident risk rising across successive night shifts — roughly 6 percent by the second, 17 percent by the third, 36 percent by the fourth, relative to the first — and these increments are steeper on twelve-hour patterns because each successive shift adds more hours of accumulated deficit. Vedaa et al. (2016) and Härmä et al. (2018) find the same shape in register data.

The practical consequence is that four consecutive night twelves is a materially different exposure from two, and that the widely used four-on/four-off pattern places its highest-risk shifts — the third and fourth nights — before the recovery block that is supposed to justify the design.

**Overtime is where this becomes urgent.** Nine of our field studies examined overtime layered onto an extended base schedule. All nine found risk elevation, and the effect sizes are larger and more consistent than anything in the shift-length literature proper (Dembe et al., 2005; Caruso, 2014; Wagstaff & Sigstad Lie, 2011). Wagstaff and Sigstad Lie's meta-analysis found injury risk rising steeply beyond roughly 12 hours per day and beyond 55–60 hours per week, with the two exposures compounding rather than merely adding. A twelve-hour schedule has almost no headroom: a single stacked shift takes a crew from 48 to 60 hours in a week, which is at or past the point where the pooled evidence turns clearly unfavorable.

This is where the consortium's reversal case is most legible. That plant's own records show the recordable rate rising in parallel with a doubling of voluntary overtime uptake following changeover — a pattern that Section 4 will argue is a general feature of twelve-hour conversions, not an idiosyncrasy.

### 3.6 Breaks, naps, and the countermeasure literature

Twelve laboratory and eight field studies addressed within-shift countermeasures. The laboratory evidence for napping is strong: a 20- to 30-minute nap taken before the circadian trough measurably restores vigilance for two to four hours, and longer naps produce larger restoration at the cost of sleep inertia lasting 15 to 30 minutes (Bonnefond et al., 2001; Purnell et al., 2002; Sallinen & Kecklund, 2010). Prophylactic napping before a night shift outperforms mid-shift napping on some measures.

Field translation is weaker and more equivocal. Two field studies report reduced fatigue and error under formal nap policies (Purnell et al., 2002; a control-room study in the process-industry set). Three report no measurable safety benefit, attributing this to low uptake: where napping is permitted but not scheduled, uptake in production settings runs below 25 percent, and workers cite peer perception and the absence of a suitable location more than lack of sleepiness (Smith et al., 1998; and two of the survey studies). One study found that permitting naps without designating relief coverage produced *worse* outcomes on a hot-work line, because operators napped and then returned to full task load with sleep inertia unresolved.

Break structure has received less attention than it deserves given how cheaply it can be modified. The available evidence favors more frequent, shorter breaks in the second half of an extended shift over the standard two-break structure, particularly for physically loaded or thermally stressed work (Tucker et al., 1998; Macdonald & Bendak, 2000). Tucker and colleagues' finding that accident risk rises across the interval since the last break, and resets after it, is one of the more actionable results in the literature and is largely independent of the shift-length debate.

### 3.7 Heat, physical load, and task type: the moderator that keeps appearing

Several member plants perform hot work in an environment where ambient summer temperatures exceed 40°C for weeks at a time. The interaction between thermal load and extended shifts is undersudied, but what exists points one direction. Heat exposure independently elevates injury risk, with effects appearing above roughly 25–28°C WBGT and rising sharply thereafter (Xiang et al., 2014; Morabito et al., in the environmental set). Two studies in our field group examined the interaction directly and found it to be super-additive: the marginal risk of extended hours was larger under high thermal load than under moderate (one foundry cohort; one construction-sector analysis included for the interaction estimate). Physiological plausibility is high — thermal strain accumulates across a shift, hydration and electrolyte deficits are cumulative, and the compensatory sweat response degrades with sleep restriction.

Physical load moderates similarly. Duchon et al. (1994, 1997) — the most-cited work on twelve-hour shifts in physically demanding underground mining — found that workers self-regulated pace across the longer shift and that objective fatigue measures were manageable, but their setting had substantial task variety and self-paced work. Studies in machine-paced assembly find less favorable results, because the worker cannot redistribute effort across the shift (Rosa & Colligan, 1988; Rosa, 1991).

**Set against each other:** the twelve-hour schedules that perform well in the literature are disproportionately self-paced, cognitively loaded, thermally moderate, and monitoring-oriented. The ones that perform poorly are machine-paced, physically loaded, and hot. Several SAMI member plants are in the second category, and the more optimistic field literature was not conducted in them.

### 3.8 Quality escapes as an outcome distinct from injury

Injury is a poor proxy for fatigue-related failure, because injuries are rare, severity-truncated, and reported through a process that schedule itself perturbs. Quality escapes — defects reaching the customer — are more frequent, better instrumented in most member plants, and arguably a more sensitive fatigue indicator.

The literature here is thin in manufacturing and much richer in healthcare, where medication error, procedural error, and diagnostic error have been studied against shift length with reasonable rigor. Findings there are consistent in direction: error rates rise with shift duration beyond roughly nine hours, with a marked increase past twelve (Rogers et al., 2004; Landrigan et al., 2004; Barger et al., 2005; Scott et al., 2006). Trinkoff et al. (2006) find the same for extended weekly hours.

Manufacturing evidence is sparser and less consistent. Three studies in our set examined scrap, rework, or defect escape against schedule. Two found elevation in the final quarter of twelve-hour shifts, one substantial (a semiconductor fabrication study) and one modest. One found no association but reported that automated inspection had been introduced concurrently with the schedule change, which would mask exactly the effect being sought. A fourth study found *lower* defect rates under twelves and attributed this to reduced handoffs: two shift changes per day rather than three, with fewer opportunities for information loss at turnover.

That handoff argument deserves more weight than it usually gets. Turnover-of-control failures are a well-documented source of error, and a twelve-hour schedule eliminates one third of them. This is a genuine and underappreciated safety *benefit* of extended shifts, and it works in the opposite direction from the fatigue mechanism. Whether the net is favorable depends on how error-prone a given plant's handoffs are — which is locally measurable.

### 3.9 Preference, retention, and the self-selection problem

Eighteen surveys examined worker preference. The finding is close to unanimous: where workers have experienced both, large majorities prefer twelve-hour schedules, with reported preference typically between 65 and 90 percent, and the stated reason is almost always the block of consecutive days off (Smith et al., 1998; Baker et al., 2003; Tucker et al., 1996; Costa, 1996). Commuting reduction is a secondary reason and is not trivial in the consortium's geography — a crew working twelves makes roughly 40 percent fewer trips.

This finding is real, it is consistent, and it is the foundation of the operations council chair's position. It should not be dismissed. It also should not be treated as safety evidence, for three reasons the literature makes explicit.

**First, preference and risk are dissociable.** Workers reliably prefer schedules that concentrate work into fewer days, and reliably underestimate cumulative fatigue. Several studies find self-rated alertness and objective vigilance diverging across a run of nights, with the subjective measure recovering faster than the objective one (Van Dongen et al., 2003; Åkerstedt, 1995). Van Dongen's demonstration that chronically sleep-restricted subjects report stable sleepiness while their performance continues to degrade is the central finding here, and it undercuts any argument resting on crews feeling fine.

**Second, preference data are collected from survivors.** Workers who cannot tolerate extended shifts leave, transfer, or are selected out — the healthy-worker survivor effect. Cross-sectional preference surveys in plants running twelves for years are surveying the tolerant remainder. Two studies attempted to address this by surveying leavers; both found schedule cited as a departure reason at rates far above its prevalence in stayer surveys (one retention study in the survey set; Costa, 1996, in a longitudinal European sample).

**Third, retention effects can run either way and appear to be plant-specific.** Some studies find twelve-hour schedules improve retention, particularly where the labor market offers competing employers on eights (Baker et al., 2003). Others find elevated turnover among workers over 45 and among those with dependent-care responsibilities, in a pattern that would gradually reshape the workforce composition of an adopting plant (Costa, 1996; Fischer et al., 2017). Given the consortium's median production-worker age of 41 and rising, this is not a peripheral concern.

### 3.10 Longer-horizon health outcomes

Beyond injury, extended and rotating shifts have been examined against cardiometabolic outcomes, sleep disorder, and mental health. The evidence for night work as a risk factor for cardiovascular disease and metabolic syndrome is reasonably strong and largely independent of shift length (Kecklund & Axelsson, 2016; Vyas et al., in the cardiometabolic literature; Bambra et al., 2008). Evidence isolating shift *length* from night work is much weaker; the two are correlated in nearly every dataset. Two studies report worse cardiometabolic profiles under twelve-hour rotating schedules than under eight-hour rotating schedules at equal night-shift frequency, but both are cross-sectional and vulnerable to the survivor effect operating in the opposite direction from Section 3.9 — healthier workers may be the ones who remain on the more demanding schedule.

We flag this theme as relevant to the board's fiduciary and health-plan considerations but not decisive for the schedule question, and we do not rest any recommendation on it.

## 4. Design and measurement: what actually drives the disagreement

Five methodological differences account for most of the apparent conflict in Section 3, and understanding them changes how a member plant should read any study handed to it by a vendor or a union.

**4.1 The denominator.** Injury rates expressed per worker, per shift, and per exposure-hour give different answers to the same question, and the difference is not small. A worker on twelves works the same annual hours in fewer shifts. Per-shift rates therefore favor eights mechanically; per-worker annual rates are roughly neutral; per-exposure-hour rates are the most defensible but are reported by fewer than half the field studies in our set. Several of the "no difference" findings and at least two of the "reduction" findings are denominator artifacts. Conversely, at least one elevated finding used per-shift rates without adjustment.

**4.2 Exposure classification.** Studies using nominal schedule labels — "this plant runs twelves" — misclassify substantially, because actual hours worked diverge from posted schedules through overtime, shift swaps, absence coverage, and call-ins. Studies using payroll or time-clock records consistently find larger effects than studies using nominal labels, which is the signature of non-differential misclassification attenuating a real association. The register-based Scandinavian studies are strong precisely here.

**4.3 The overtime confound, in both directions.** Twelve-hour conversions systematically change overtime behavior. Workers with four consecutive days off have both the opportunity and, often, the financial motivation to accept additional shifts, and plants running twelves generally have fewer bodies available to cover absence, so pressure to accept rises. A study that "controls for" overtime is answering a counterfactual question — what would happen if twelves were adopted without the overtime change — that does not describe what actually happens in plants. A study that does not control for it cannot attribute its finding to shift length. Both approaches are defensible; they answer different questions, and readers routinely conflate them. For a board deciding whether to adopt a schedule, the *uncontrolled* estimate is closer to the decision-relevant quantity, because the overtime response is part of the intervention.

**4.4 Reporting behavior.** Injury reporting is not a fixed measurement instrument. Workers approaching a four-day break have a documented incentive to defer reporting minor injuries; workers who will be back tomorrow do not. Two studies in our set found the schedule effect on recordables larger than the effect on first-aid events or on near-miss reports, which is the wrong direction if the mechanism were purely physiological and the right direction if reporting thresholds shifted. This cuts against the reliability of recordable-rate comparisons specifically — including, we must note, the consortium's own reversal case, whose 2.9-to-4.6 movement we cannot fully validate against underlying event severity.

**4.5 Time since changeover, and the adaptation question.** Schedule changes produce disruption independent of the schedule's steady-state properties: training gaps, unfamiliar crew pairings, new handoff routines, and a period of sleep-schedule adjustment. Studies measuring within 12 months of conversion capture disruption and steady state together; studies measuring at five years capture steady state and a self-selected crew. Both are informative; neither is the other. The consortium's reversal plant reversed at eighteen months, which is inside the window where several studies find rates elevated and then partially receding. We cannot say whether that plant reversed a genuinely worse schedule or reversed during a transient — and neither can they, from the data they have.

**4.6 Statistical power, an underrated issue.** Injury outcomes are rare. A 700-person plant with a recordable rate near 3.0 experiences roughly 21 recordables per year. Detecting a 25 percent change in that rate at conventional power requires several years of data or pooling across plants. A substantial share of the "no difference" findings in the field literature are underpowered nulls, not evidence of equivalence — and only four of the 39 field studies reported a power analysis or equivalence bound. This is the single most common misreading of the literature we encountered in preparing this review: absence of a detected difference has been repeatedly cited by schedule advocates as evidence of safety.

## 5. Gaps in the evidence

**Age.** Workers over 55 are nearly absent. Only three field studies stratified results by age above 50, and none reported an age band above 60 with adequate cell sizes. This is a serious gap given both physiological evidence that circadian adaptability and sleep consolidation decline with age (Härmä et al., 2018; Costa, 1996) and the consortium's aging workforce. If the age interaction is real and positive, the published literature — drawn from younger cohorts — systematically understates the risk a member plant now faces.

**Sex.** Women constitute under 20 percent of pooled samples and are concentrated in the healthcare-derived studies rather than the manufacturing ones. Sex-stratified estimates for extended shifts in production work are effectively unavailable. Given differences in sleep architecture, dependent-care load, and thermal physiology, this is not a gap that can be assumed benign.

**Thermal environment.** Only two studies in our set examined the shift-length-by-heat interaction directly, neither in a foundry, neither in a desert climate. For a consortium in central Arizona with hot-work members, this is arguably the most consequential gap in the review.

**Nap policy as an experiment.** Despite consistent laboratory support, we found no adequately powered field trial of a *scheduled, covered* nap on a twelve-hour night shift in manufacturing, with injury or quality escapes as the primary endpoint. This is a tractable study that the consortium is unusually well positioned to run.

**Quality escapes.** Three manufacturing studies is not a literature. The handoff-reduction benefit (Section 3.8) has never, to our knowledge, been quantified against the fatigue cost in the same facility.

**Small plants.** Field studies cluster in facilities above 500 workers, because smaller sites cannot generate analyzable injury counts. Roughly half of SAMI members employ fewer than 300 production workers, and their staffing margins — the ability to absorb absence without mandating overtime — are structurally worse than those of the studied populations.

**Long-horizon follow-up.** Most field studies observe two to five years. Effects mediated by cumulative sleep debt, workforce composition change, or cardiometabolic health would appear over ten to twenty.

## 6. Implications for member plants

The board asked what follows. We offer conclusions at three levels of confidence, and we distinguish them explicitly, because a review that presented all of these as equally supported would be doing the board a disservice.

### 6.1 Supported well enough to act on now

1. **Overtime stacked on twelve-hour schedules is the clearest hazard in this literature.** The evidence for risk elevation beyond roughly 12 hours per day and 55–60 hours per week is more consistent than the evidence about shift length itself (Wagstaff & Sigstad Lie, 2011; Dembe et al., 2005; Caruso, 2014). Plants running twelves should treat weekly hour ceilings as an engineering control, not a preference, and should recognize that a twelve-hour base leaves almost no margin.

2. **Consecutive night shifts should be capped.** The risk gradient across successive nights is well-replicated. Patterns with four or more consecutive night twelves place their highest-risk shifts at the end of the run.

3. **Transition architecture matters more than shift length.** Minimum 48-hour breaks at day-to-night transitions; no quick returns; forward rotation where rotation exists. These are cheap and the evidence is comparatively clean.

4. **Break structure in the second half of a shift is modifiable and underused.** Shorter, more frequent breaks after hour eight, particularly under thermal or physical load (Tucker et al., 1998).

5. **Task scheduling within the shift is a real lever.** The most hazardous operations should not be scheduled in hours eleven and twelve, especially at night. Some member plants already do this informally; none, so far as we know, do it by policy.

### 6.2 Conditional, requiring local judgment

6. **Twelve-hour schedules are not uniformly worse than eight-hour schedules, and in some comparisons are better.** A plant replacing a rapidly or backward-rotating eight-hour system with a fixed or slowly-rotating twelve may well improve. A plant replacing a stable eight-hour day-heavy system with a rotating twelve is in the part of the evidence where risk elevation is most consistently observed. The comparator determines the answer.

7. **Plant characteristics move the estimate substantially.** Machine-paced, physically loaded, thermally stressed operations sit in the region of the literature where extended shifts look worst. Monitoring-oriented, self-paced, climate-controlled operations sit where they look neutral or favorable. Member plants should locate themselves honestly before importing a result from a study conducted somewhere else.

8. **The workforce a plant will have in ten years is affected by this decision.** Differential retention by age and caregiving status is documented, if imprecisely. A schedule that the current crew prefers may reshape who the crew is.

### 6.3 Not supported, and should stop being asserted

9. **"The studies show twelve-hour shifts are safe" is a misreading.** Many of the null findings are underpowered, denominator-artifacted, or conditional on schedule features that the asserting plant does not have.

10. **"The crews prefer it" is not a safety finding.** Preference is consistent, real, and legitimate as a labor-relations consideration and a retention asset. It carries no information about injury risk, and the divergence between subjective alertness and objective performance under cumulative sleep restriction is one of the better-established findings in this literature.

11. **The April fatality does not establish that twelve-hour schedules caused it,** and the review should not be used to imply otherwise. The eleventh-hour timing is consistent with the fatigue mechanism; a single event cannot distinguish that from the several other explanations the incident investigation is pursuing.

### 6.4 What the consortium should collect

The gaps in Section 5 are, unusually, gaps the consortium can close. Thirty-eight plants, 24,000 workers, and schedule variation across members constitute a research resource that does not exist in the published literature: a multi-site natural experiment in a hot climate with substantial hot-work exposure and an aging workforce. We recommend the board consider (a) a standardized schedule and hours registry drawing on payroll rather than nominal schedules, (b) quality escape and near-miss capture alongside recordables, given the sensitivity and reporting arguments in Sections 3.8 and 4.4, (c) age- and sex-stratified reporting from the outset, and (d) a scheduled-nap field trial at two or three volunteer night-shift operations.

A final note on the internal disagreement this review was written into. The position that crews at established twelve-hour plants would resist reversion is almost certainly correct, and the survey literature supports it strongly. Nothing in this review recommends reversion at plants already running well-designed twelve-hour schedules with controlled overtime, capped night runs, and adequate transitions. What the review does recommend is that the three plants currently converting treat the conversion as a hazard-introducing change requiring the same controls as any other, and that all members recognize the difference between a schedule that is tolerable and a schedule that has been demonstrated safe. The literature supports the first claim for many plants. It does not yet support the second for any of them.
