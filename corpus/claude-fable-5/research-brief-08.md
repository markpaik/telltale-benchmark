# Did the Transitional Care Program Reduce 30-Day Heart Failure Readmissions? A Research Brief for the September 15 Budget Committee

**Mountain Laurel Health System — Office of Clinical Outcomes Analysis**
**Prepared by:** Anjali Deshmukh, Senior Clinical Outcomes Analyst
**Commissioned by:** Loretta Sizemore, Vice President of Care Management
**Prepared for:** Kwabena Mensah, MD, Chief Medical Officer; Grant Pelletier, Chief Financial Officer; Budget Committee
**Date:** August 9, 2026

---

## The Question and What Was Found

In July 2024, Mountain Laurel Health System launched a transitional care program for patients discharged home after an index hospitalization for heart failure. The program pairs a pharmacist telephone medication review at 48 hours with an in-person follow-up visit within seven days of discharge. The Chief Medical Officer has asked a direct question: did the program lower 30-day all-cause readmissions? The answer bears on two decisions. First, the budget committee will vote on September 15 whether to fund six additional care coordinator positions at a combined cost of $840,000 per year. Second, the system faces a federal Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program penalty of $1.2 million against next year's Medicare payments, and leadership wants to know whether the program is a credible instrument for reducing future penalties.

The short answer is that the program is probably working, but the effect is smaller than the raw numbers suggest, and it is concentrated in a specific population. Across 2,308 index heart failure discharges between July 2024 and December 2025, enrolled patients were readmitted within 30 days at a rate of 14.1 percent, compared with 19.6 percent for unenrolled patients — a difference of 5.5 percentage points. That headline comparison overstates the program's effect. Enrollment was voluntary, coordinators systematically skipped the 19 percent of patients without a working telephone, and discharges to skilled nursing facilities — 22 percent of the total — were excluded by design. When the comparison is restricted to patients with a reachable telephone, the population in which the program could actually operate, and adjusted for observable differences between enrollees and non-enrollees, the gap narrows to 16.8 percent versus 18.9 percent: a 2.1 percentage point difference. Patients living more than 45 minutes from a Mountain Laurel hospital showed no difference at all between enrolled and unenrolled, which suggests the effect runs substantially through the seven-day in-person visit rather than the telephone call alone.

A 2.1 percentage point reduction is real and clinically meaningful, but it is not 5.5 points, and it should not be projected onto populations the program has never touched — patients without phones, patients discharged to skilled nursing facilities, and patients living beyond a 45-minute drive. Those three groups together account for roughly half of the heart failure discharge population and carry readmission rates at or above the system average. The staffing decision therefore turns on a question this analysis cannot fully answer from historical data: whether six additional coordinators would extend the observed effect into those harder-to-reach populations, or whether they would simply enroll more of the patients who were already the easiest to help. The body of this brief describes the data sources and their gaps, explains how voluntary enrollment complicates the comparison, presents the results overall and by subgroup, and connects the findings to the September staffing vote and the $1.2 million penalty.

## Program Background and Decision Context

Mountain Laurel Health System operates three hospitals and 512 licensed beds in and around Beckley, West Virginia, employs approximately 4,200 people, and serves a region in which 31 percent of residents are covered by Medicare. Heart failure is among the highest-volume and highest-readmission diagnoses in the system, and it is one of the conditions on which the federal Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program assesses penalties. The current penalty exposure of $1.2 million against next year's Medicare payments reflects excess readmission performance during a federal measurement window that predates or only partially overlaps the program period; this matters for interpretation and is discussed in the implications section.

The transitional care program has two components delivered to patients discharged home from an index heart failure hospitalization. At approximately 48 hours after discharge, a clinical pharmacist conducts a telephone review covering medication reconciliation, diuretic dosing, symptom recognition, and dietary counseling. Within seven days of discharge, the patient attends an in-person follow-up visit with a physician or advanced practice provider, scheduled before the patient leaves the hospital and tracked by a care coordinator who reschedules missed appointments. Care coordinators identify eligible patients on the inpatient unit, explain the program, and enroll those who agree. Two exclusions were built into the design at launch: patients discharged to skilled nursing facilities were not enrolled, on the reasoning that the facility assumes responsibility for medication management and follow-up, and patients without a working telephone could not receive the 48-hour call and were skipped by coordinators rather than offered a modified version of the program.

The proposal before the budget committee would add six care coordinator positions at $840,000 in annual fully loaded cost. As described by Care Management, the additional staff would be used to raise enrollment among currently eligible patients, extend outreach to patients without telephones through home visits or partnerships with community health workers, and potentially bring skilled nursing facility discharges into a modified program track. The Chief Financial Officer has asked that the analysis address not only whether the program reduced readmissions but what the observed effect implies about the return on that investment, both in direct cost avoidance and in future penalty reduction.

## Data Sources and Their Coverage

Two data sources feed this analysis, and their limitations shape what can and cannot be concluded.

**The electronic health record.** All 2,308 index heart failure discharges between July 1, 2024 and December 31, 2025 were identified from the system's electronic health record using principal discharge diagnosis codes, following the general logic of the CMS heart failure readmission measure: adult patients, alive at discharge, with transfers and same-day readmissions handled per measure specification. The record captures every readmission to a Mountain Laurel facility completely and promptly. It also supplies the covariates used in the adjusted analyses — age, sex, payer, prior-year inpatient utilization, a comorbidity index constructed from diagnosis history, discharge disposition, and documented telephone status. What the record cannot see is any readmission that occurs at a hospital outside the system.

**The state all-payer claims feed.** Out-of-system readmissions are identified through West Virginia's all-payer claims database feed. This source has three coverage gaps that must be stated plainly. First, it lags approximately five months behind the service date. Because this analysis was finalized in August 2026 and the last 30-day readmission window for a December 2025 discharge closed at the end of January 2026, the claims feed is now complete for the entire study period; earlier interim analyses circulated within Care Management were truncated and understated late-period readmissions, and those interim figures should no longer be cited. Second, the feed excludes several Medicare Advantage plans whose claims are not submitted to the state database. Third, even among covered payers, the feed captures claims for only about 78 percent of our discharge population. In practical terms, roughly one in five patients in this analysis could have been readmitted to a hospital in Charleston, Princeton, or across the Virginia line without that event appearing in our data at all.

In the merged data, approximately 17 percent of all detected readmissions occurred at non–Mountain Laurel facilities. Given the 78 percent claims coverage and the Medicare Advantage exclusions, the true out-of-system share is certainly higher, and both groups' true readmission rates are understated. The more important question is whether the undercount is differential — whether it biases the comparison between enrolled and unenrolled patients rather than merely lowering both rates. There is reason to think it might. Care coordinators actively direct enrolled patients back to Mountain Laurel facilities and clinics when problems arise, so an enrolled patient's readmission is more likely to occur inside the system, where we see it perfectly. An unenrolled patient with no coordinator relationship is comparatively more likely to present to whichever emergency department is nearest, including out-of-system hospitals whose claims we capture incompletely. If that pattern holds, the unenrolled readmission rate is understated by more than the enrolled rate, and the true program effect is somewhat larger than reported here. The opposite bias is also conceivable — Medicare Advantage patients, whose out-of-system events we miss most completely, may be distributed unevenly between the groups — but the payer mix of the two groups is similar enough (traditional Medicare and Medicare Advantage together account for roughly seven in ten discharges in both) that this is unlikely to dominate. We flag the direction of these biases in the limitations section; the honest summary is that the out-of-system undercount adds uncertainty of perhaps one to two percentage points around any rate reported in this brief, and it more plausibly causes us to understate than overstate the program's effect.

**Cost and financial inputs.** Direct cost figures for readmissions were supplied by Finance from the cost accounting system: the average variable direct cost of a heart failure readmission at Mountain Laurel facilities is approximately $13,400. The $1.2 million penalty figure and the staffing cost of $840,000 were provided by the Chief Financial Officer's office.

## How Enrollment Happened and What It Does to the Comparison

This was not a randomized trial, and the way patients entered the program is the single most important fact for interpreting the results. Three filters stood between an index heart failure discharge and program enrollment, and each one selects for patients who were likely to do better regardless of the program.

The population flow, described rather than drawn, runs as follows. Of the 2,308 index discharges in the study period, 508 (22 percent) were discharged to skilled nursing facilities and excluded by design, leaving 1,800 community discharges. Of those, 342 (19 percent) had no working telephone documented and were skipped by coordinators, leaving 1,458 reachable patients. Of the reachable population, 812 enrolled — about 56 percent of those who could be approached, and 45 percent of all community discharges — while 646 reachable patients either declined, were missed during a short stay, or were discharged before a coordinator reached them. The unenrolled comparison group in the raw analysis therefore mixes 646 reachable non-enrollees with 342 patients who had no phone and were never offered the program.

Each filter matters. The skilled nursing exclusion removes the frailest fifth of the population from the program entirely; SNF discharges in our data were readmitted at 24.3 percent, well above the community rate, and nothing in this analysis speaks to whether the program would help them. The telephone filter is not a neutral administrative detail: in southern West Virginia, lacking a working phone is a proxy for poverty, housing instability, and social isolation, all of which independently predict readmission. Patients without phones were readmitted at 21.1 percent, against 18.9 percent for reachable unenrolled patients. When the raw comparison lumps the no-phone group into the unenrolled denominator, it inflates the unenrolled rate and makes the program look better than it is. Finally, voluntary enrollment among reachable patients selects on engagement. Patients who agree to a pharmacist call and commit to a seven-day appointment are, on average, more activated, better supported at home, and more adherent than patients who decline — differences that reduce readmission risk through channels that have nothing to do with the program. Chart review of a sample of decliners found that documented reasons for declining included transportation barriers, caregiver unavailability, and cognitive impairment, all of which are themselves readmission risk factors.

The analytic consequence is that the raw 5.5-point difference (14.1 versus 19.6 percent) is a ceiling, not an estimate. It bundles the true program effect together with the phone filter and the self-selection of healthier, more engaged patients into enrollment. The analysis below strips out what can be stripped out — restricting the comparison to reachable patients and adjusting for measured differences — but no statistical adjustment can fully remove selection on unmeasured traits like motivation and caregiver support. The adjusted estimate of 2.1 points should therefore itself be read as an upper bound on the causal effect, partially offset by the out-of-system undercount described above, which pushes in the opposite direction.

## Analytic Approach

The primary outcome is all-cause inpatient readmission within 30 days of index discharge, at any hospital visible in either data source. Planned readmissions were not excluded, which is a departure from the CMS measure but was necessary because procedure-level detail in the claims feed is too thin to apply the planned readmission algorithm reliably outside the system; this affects both groups equally and is unlikely to bias the comparison.

Three comparisons were run. The first is the unadjusted comparison of all enrolled patients against all unenrolled community discharges, reported because it is the number leadership has already seen circulating. The second, and the one we treat as primary, restricts both groups to patients with a documented working telephone — the population in which enrollment was actually possible — and adjusts the comparison using logistic regression with inverse-probability weighting on age, sex, payer, prior-year admissions, comorbidity index, length of index stay, and drive time to the nearest system hospital. The third set of comparisons stratifies the restricted, adjusted analysis by drive time (45 minutes or less versus more than 45 minutes) and reports rates by discharge disposition for context. Drive time was computed from the patient's address of record to the nearest Mountain Laurel hospital. Program process measures — completion of the 48-hour pharmacist call and completion of the seven-day visit — come from care coordination logs and are used to interpret the distance findings.

Confidence intervals reported below are conventional 95 percent intervals and do not account for the out-of-system undercount, which adds uncertainty beyond what the intervals convey.

## Results

**Overall.** Enrolled patients (n = 812) were readmitted within 30 days at 14.1 percent (115 readmissions). All unenrolled community-discharged patients (n = 988) were readmitted at 19.6 percent (194 readmissions). The raw difference is 5.5 percentage points. As argued above, this comparison is confounded by the telephone filter and self-selection and should not be used for decision-making.

**Restricted and adjusted.** Confined to patients with a reachable telephone and adjusted for observable differences, enrolled patients' readmission rate is 16.8 percent against 18.9 percent for reachable unenrolled patients — a difference of 2.1 percentage points (95 percent confidence interval roughly 0.2 to 4.0 points, sensitive to specification). Note the direction of the adjustment: restricting and reweighting moves the enrolled figure up from 14.1 to 16.8, confirming that enrollees were a lower-risk group to begin with, and moves the unenrolled figure down from 19.6 to 18.9 by removing the no-phone patients. Roughly three-fifths of the raw gap was selection; roughly two-fifths survives as a plausible program effect.

**By subgroup.** The table below summarizes the restricted, adjusted comparison by drive time, alongside the populations outside the comparison.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| All community discharges (raw, unadjusted) | 1,800 | 14.1% | 19.6% | −5.5 pts |
| Reachable phone, adjusted (primary) | 1,458 | 16.8% | 18.9% | −2.1 pts |
| — Drive time ≤ 45 minutes | ~1,020 | 15.9% | 19.1% | −3.2 pts |
| — Drive time > 45 minutes | ~440 | 18.3% | 18.6% | −0.3 pts |

The distance result is the most informative finding in the analysis. Among patients living within 45 minutes of a system hospital, enrollment is associated with a 3.2-point reduction. Among patients living farther away, the difference is 0.3 points — statistically and practically indistinguishable from zero. The process data explain why: 91 percent of enrolled patients completed the 48-hour pharmacist call regardless of where they lived, but seven-day visit completion was 76 percent among nearby enrollees and only 44 percent among those beyond 45 minutes. The telephone component reaches everyone enrolled; the visit does not. The natural reading is that the pharmacist call alone does little to move 30-day readmissions and that the in-person visit is where the effect lives. This is consistent with the published literature on heart failure transitions, in which early follow-up visits show more consistent effects than telephonic outreach alone. It carries a direct operational implication: expanding the current program model into remote parts of the service area, without solving the visit-completion problem through telehealth follow-up, satellite clinic capacity, or transportation support, is unlikely to reduce readmissions there.

**Populations outside the program.** The two excluded groups readmit at the highest rates in the data. Patients without working telephones were readmitted at 21.1 percent and skilled nursing discharges at 24.3 percent. Together these 850 patients — 37 percent of all index discharges — generated roughly 195 readmissions during the study period, more than the entire enrolled group. Nothing in this analysis demonstrates that the program would work for them, because it was never tried on them. But it does establish that the largest concentration of preventable-readmission opportunity, if it is preventable, sits in populations the current program cannot reach.

**A figure, described.** If plotted, monthly 30-day readmission rates for enrolled and unenrolled reachable patients over the eighteen study months would show two noisy lines separated by two to four percentage points in most months, with the enrolled line below the unenrolled line in fourteen of eighteen months. The separation is visible but modest and does not widen over time, which argues against a strong learning-curve effect but also suggests the program's effect, such as it is, was present from early in implementation. A second described figure — the population flow from 2,308 discharges through the SNF exclusion, the telephone filter, and voluntary enrollment down to 812 enrollees — would make visually obvious the central point of this brief: the program was evaluated on the 35 percent of discharges that were easiest to serve.

## Limitations

The limitations here are not footnotes; several are large enough to change how the results should be used.

**Selection into enrollment.** The adjusted analysis controls for what the record measures. It cannot control for motivation, health literacy, caregiver presence, housing stability, or substance use, all of which plausibly differ between patients who accepted the program and patients who declined it, and all of which affect readmission independently. The 2.1-point adjusted difference should be treated as an upper bound on the causal effect of the program in the population it served. A conservative reading would place the true effect somewhere between roughly one and two points in the reachable, near-hospital population.

**Out-of-system readmission capture.** The claims feed covers about 78 percent of discharges and excludes several Medicare Advantage plans. Both groups' rates are understated. Because coordinators channel enrolled patients back into the system, the undercount likely falls more heavily on the unenrolled group, which would mean the reported comparison understates the program effect — a bias running opposite to the selection bias above. We cannot quantify either precisely, and to some unknown degree they offset. The five-month claims lag does not truncate this analysis but has corrupted earlier interim versions of these numbers; only the figures in this brief should be circulated.

**Generalizability.** The observed effect comes from telephone-reachable patients living mostly within 45 minutes of a hospital who volunteered. Extrapolating the 2.1-point effect to no-phone patients, distant patients, or SNF discharges is not supported by any evidence in this analysis. The proposal before the budget committee contemplates exactly those extensions, which is why the recommendation below treats expansion as a hypothesis to be tested rather than an effect to be assumed.

**Outcome definition and secular trends.** All-cause readmission without planned-readmission exclusion differs modestly from the federal measure. The analysis also cannot rule out that some portion of the enrolled–unenrolled gap reflects concurrent changes — order set revisions, cardiology staffing — that touched enrolled patients differentially, though we identified no specific candidate. Emergency department visits and observation stays that did not convert to inpatient admissions are not counted; if the program diverts inpatient readmissions into observation status, the true clinical benefit is smaller than the readmission numbers imply, and conversely CMS measure performance would still improve.

**Statistical precision.** With roughly 1,458 patients in the primary comparison, the confidence interval around the 2.1-point difference is wide, running from a trivial effect to a substantial one. The subgroup estimates, particularly the distance stratification, rest on smaller samples and should be read as directional.

## Implications for the September 15 Decision and the Penalty

**What the evidence supports.** The program plausibly reduces 30-day readmissions by about two percentage points among telephone-reachable patients living within 45 minutes of a system hospital, with the effect apparently driven by the seven-day visit. That is a defensible clinical result and justifies continuing the program at current scale for that population.

**The direct financial arithmetic.** The system generates approximately 1,540 index heart failure discharges per year at the study-period rate, of which roughly 970 are community discharges with a working phone. Current enrollment covers about 540 of those annually. Applying the adjusted 2.1-point effect to current enrollment implies roughly 11 readmissions avoided per year; at $13,400 in average variable direct cost per readmission, that is approximately $150,000 in annual direct cost avoidance from the program as it stands. If six new coordinators raised enrollment among currently eligible near-hospital patients from 56 percent toward 85 percent, the incremental enrollees — perhaps 250 to 300 patients per year — would yield on the order of five to six additional avoided readmissions and roughly $70,000 to $80,000 in incremental direct savings, and plausibly less, since patients who have so far declined are unlikely to respond as well as volunteers. On direct cost avoidance alone, the $840,000 staffing investment does not come close to paying for itself. Under fee-for-service payment, moreover, an avoided Medicare readmission also forgoes the associated revenue, so the net direct margin effect is smaller still. The financial case, if there is one, must rest on the penalty and on strategic positioning, not on unit cost savings.

**The penalty.** The $1.2 million Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program penalty warrants careful framing. The penalty is calculated from excess readmission ratios across up to six conditions — heart failure is one — over a rolling three-year federal performance window, and it is assessed against total base operating Medicare payments, not against heart failure payments alone. Two consequences follow. First, next year's $1.2 million penalty is already largely determined by performance in a window that only partially overlaps the program period; nothing decided on September 15 will change it materially. Second, the program can reduce future penalties, but only through its effect on the Medicare heart failure excess readmission ratio, only in proportion to heart failure's weight among the penalized conditions, and only with a lag of two to three years as the performance window rolls forward. A two-point improvement in heart failure readmissions among enrolled Medicare patients, sustained, would plausibly reduce the heart failure component of future penalties by a meaningful fraction — Finance's rough modeling suggests something in the range of $150,000 to $350,000 per year at maturity, with wide uncertainty — but it will not eliminate a $1.2 million penalty driven by multiple conditions and multiple years. The committee should not be told that funding six coordinators buys back the penalty. It should be told that the program is one of several levers on the heart failure portion of future penalties, and that penalty relief plus direct cost avoidance together might justify a substantial fraction of the staffing cost, but probably not all of it, under the current program model.

**Where the opportunity actually is.** The populations the program excludes — no-phone patients at 21.1 percent, SNF discharges at 24.3 percent, distant patients showing no benefit from the current model — hold most of the remaining readmissions. If the new positions are funded, their value depends almost entirely on whether the program can be adapted to work in those populations, which the existing evidence neither confirms nor rules out. Adaptations worth considering include home visits or community health worker outreach for patients without telephones, telehealth or satellite-clinic follow-up for distant patients to address the 44 percent visit-completion rate beyond 45 minutes, and a coordinated handoff protocol with the highest-volume skilled nursing facilities. Each is a different intervention from the one evaluated here, and each should carry its own evaluation plan.

---

## Recommendation

The analysis supports a conditional recommendation rather than a simple yes or no. First, continue the current program for reachable, near-hospital patients; the evidence of benefit, while smaller than the raw numbers suggested, is real. Second, if the budget committee funds the six positions, fund them with an explicit mandate and evaluation design: dedicate a defined share of the new capacity to structured pilots in the no-phone and long-distance populations, assign patients to outreach on a staggered or randomized rollout so that the next analysis is not crippled by the selection problems documented here, and set a twelve-month checkpoint with pre-specified enrollment, visit-completion, and readmission targets. Third, do not present the raw 14.1-versus-19.6 comparison to the committee or in penalty discussions; the defensible figure is the adjusted 2.1-point difference, bounded by the caveats in this brief. Fourth, direct Finance and Population Health to pursue completion of Medicare Advantage data-sharing agreements, since the 78 percent claims coverage is the largest measurement gap in every readmission analysis this office produces. A program that has shown a modest, real effect in its easiest population has earned continued investment; whether it has earned $840,000 of expansion depends on a commitment to test it where the readmissions actually are.

## Appendix A: Cohort Construction and Data Specifications

**Index discharge definition.** Index admissions were identified as inpatient discharges from any of the three Mountain Laurel hospitals between July 1, 2024 and December 31, 2025 with a principal discharge diagnosis of heart failure (ICD-10-CM I50.x, I11.0, I13.0, I13.2, and related codes per the CMS heart failure cohort specification). Patients under 18, patients who died during the index stay, patients discharged against medical advice, and patients transferred to another acute care hospital were excluded from the index count. A patient could contribute more than one index discharge if a subsequent heart failure admission occurred more than 30 days after a prior index discharge; 214 patients contributed two or more index events. Readmissions within 30 days of an index discharge were not themselves counted as new index events, consistent with measure logic. Sensitivity analysis limiting each patient to a single randomly selected index discharge produced an adjusted difference of 2.0 points, essentially unchanged from the primary estimate.

**Readmission ascertainment.** In-system readmissions were identified from the electronic health record as any inpatient admission within 30 days of index discharge. Out-of-system readmissions were identified in the state all-payer claims feed by matching on the database's encrypted patient identifier, admission date, and inpatient claim type. Match quality was audited on a 100-record sample against manual review of transferred records and payer notifications; three false negatives and one false positive were found, a level of error too small to affect the reported comparisons. Observation stays and emergency department visits without inpatient conversion were tabulated but excluded from the primary outcome, as noted in the limitations.

**Covariates.** The adjustment model included: age in five-year bands; sex; payer class (traditional Medicare, Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, commercial, uninsured/other); count of inpatient admissions in the 365 days before index; Elixhauser-based comorbidity score computed from two years of diagnosis history; index length of stay; ejection fraction category where documented (reduced, preserved, undocumented — undocumented in 23 percent); and drive time band. Missing covariate values were handled by category indicators rather than imputation given the low missingness outside ejection fraction. The inverse-probability weights were stabilized and trimmed at the 1st and 99th percentiles; post-weighting standardized mean differences were below 0.10 on all covariates, indicating acceptable balance on measured characteristics.

**Telephone status.** Working telephone status was taken from the care coordination log where a coordinator attempted contact, and from registration data otherwise. Registration data overstate phone access — a number on file may be disconnected — so the 19 percent no-phone figure is best understood as a floor. Misclassification here would move some effectively unreachable patients into the "reachable unenrolled" group, slightly inflating that group's readmission rate and modestly overstating the adjusted program effect; a sensitivity analysis reclassifying patients with two or more failed contact attempts as unreachable moved the adjusted difference from 2.1 to 1.8 points.

## Appendix B: Sensitivity Analyses

Five sensitivity analyses were run to test the stability of the primary estimate. Results are summarized in prose; full model output is available from the Office of Clinical Outcomes Analysis on request.

**1. In-system readmissions only.** Restricting the outcome to readmissions at Mountain Laurel facilities — abandoning the claims feed entirely — yields adjusted rates of 14.9 versus 16.4 percent, a 1.5-point difference. Because this specification is biased against the program (coordinators channel enrolled patients' readmissions into the system, where they are always counted, while unenrolled patients' out-of-system events vanish), the survival of a positive difference under it is reassuring.

**2. Claims-covered payers only.** Restricting both groups to patients whose payers are represented in the claims feed, so that out-of-system capture is as complete as the data allow, yields an adjusted difference of 2.4 points. This is the specification least affected by differential ascertainment and is modestly more favorable to the program than the primary estimate.

**3. Excluding the first program quarter.** Dropping July–September 2024 discharges, when coordinator workflows were still stabilizing, yields an adjusted difference of 2.2 points — no evidence that early implementation problems diluted the estimate.

**4. Per-protocol contrast.** Comparing enrolled patients who completed both program components against reachable unenrolled patients yields a difference of 3.6 points. This estimate is more contaminated by selection than the primary analysis — patients who complete the visit are the most adherent — and is reported only to bound the plausible effect of full program delivery. It should not be quoted as the program's effect.

**5. Falsification test.** As a check on residual confounding, the same enrolled-versus-unenrolled comparison was run on an outcome the program should not affect: 30-day readmission for trauma and injury diagnoses. The adjusted difference was 0.4 points and statistically indistinguishable from zero, which offers modest reassurance that the adjustment is not manufacturing effects from selection alone, though it cannot rule out confounding specific to cardiac outcomes.

Taken together, the sensitivity analyses bracket the plausible effect between roughly 1.5 and 2.4 points in the reachable population, consistent with the primary estimate of 2.1 and with the conservative reading of one to two points offered in the limitations section.

## Appendix C: Evaluation Design for Proposed Expansion

If the September 15 vote funds the six positions, the following evaluation structure is recommended so that the next version of this brief can answer causal questions the current data cannot.

**No-phone outreach pilot.** Identify no-phone patients at discharge and assign them on an alternating-week basis to home-visit outreach versus usual care for the first twelve months. Alternating-week assignment is administratively simple, difficult to game, and approximates randomization closely enough for credible inference at this volume (roughly 220 no-phone community discharges per year, yielding about 110 per arm). With a baseline rate of 21 percent, twelve months of data would detect a reduction of roughly seven points with conventional power — a large effect, but the right first question is whether the intervention moves this population at all. A smaller true effect would require a second year of accrual, which should be stated to the committee now rather than discovered later.

**Distance-adapted follow-up.** For enrolled patients beyond 45 minutes, substitute a telehealth visit with a heart failure advanced practice provider for the in-person seven-day visit, with in-person referral triggered by weight gain, symptom flags, or medication problems identified on the call. Track visit-completion as the leading indicator: if telehealth completion beyond 45 minutes does not exceed 70 percent within six months, the adaptation is failing on process grounds and readmission results will not need to be awaited.

**Skilled nursing facility track.** Rather than enrolling SNF discharges into the existing model, negotiate a structured handoff protocol with the three facilities receiving the largest share of our discharges (together about 60 percent of SNF volume), including medication reconciliation at facility admission and a cardiology telehealth touchpoint at day five. Evaluate against the remaining facilities as a comparison group. This is the population with the highest baseline rate and the least evidence, and it should be framed to the committee as exploratory.

**Governance.** Pre-register the outcome definitions, comparison groups, and analysis windows for all three pilots with the CMO's office before launch; report process measures quarterly and outcomes at twelve months; and hold the six positions' continuation review to the pre-registered targets. The central failure mode of the current program's evidence base — voluntary enrollment into an undefined comparison — is avoidable in the expansion, but only if the design is fixed before the first patient is approached.

## Appendix E: Glossary and Data Notes for Committee Members

**30-day all-cause readmission.** Any inpatient admission, for any reason, within 30 days of discharge from the index heart failure hospitalization, at any hospital visible in the record or claims data.

**Adjusted difference.** The gap between enrolled and unenrolled readmission rates after restricting to comparable patients (working telephone) and statistically reweighting so the groups match on age, payer, prior utilization, comorbidity, length of stay, and distance. It removes measured differences only; it cannot remove differences in motivation or home support.

**All-payer claims database (APCD).** The state feed capturing hospital claims across payers. For our population: five-month lag, roughly 78 percent coverage, several Medicare Advantage plans absent.

**Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program (HRRP).** The federal program that reduces Medicare payments to hospitals with excess readmissions across specified conditions, measured over a rolling three-year window. The $1.2 million figure is next fiscal year's assessed reduction and reflects performance largely predating this program.

**Excess readmission ratio.** The hospital's predicted-to-expected readmission ratio for a condition under the CMS model; values above 1.0 contribute to the penalty. The transitional care program can influence future heart failure ratios but not the already-determined penalty.

**Data currency note.** All figures in this brief reflect the claims feed as received through July 2026, which fully covers 30-day windows for the entire study period. Any figures circulated before June 2026 were drawn from incomplete claims and should be discarded.
