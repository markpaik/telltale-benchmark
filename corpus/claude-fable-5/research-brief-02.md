# Meals as Medicine? First-Year Hospital Utilization Findings from Riverbend Community Table's Medically Tailored Meal Program

**A Research Brief Prepared for the Renewal Deliberations of the Foundation's Program Committee**

Prepared by Adaeze Okoro, Evaluation Manager, with Tomas Villalobos-Reyes, Graduate Evaluation Fellow, at the direction of Harriet Lindqvist-Moore, Executive Director, Riverbend Community Table, Cincinnati, Ohio

August 2026

---

## The Question and What We Found

In the spring of 2024, Riverbend Community Table launched a medically tailored meal program for adults living with congestive heart failure or type 2 diabetes, supported by a $3.8 million, three-year grant from the foundation. As the grant approaches the end of its second full program year, the foundation's program officer, Renata Kowal, has posed a direct question ahead of the September renewal decision: did the meals reduce hospital use among the people who received them?

The honest answer this brief offers is a qualified yes — qualified in ways that matter for how the foundation should read the numbers, and qualified in ways that point toward specific improvements a renewed grant could fund.

Among the 1,412 enrolled participants whose hospital records we could observe through a data-use agreement with a partner health system, inpatient admissions in the twelve months following enrollment averaged 0.71 per person. A comparison group of 2,640 pantry clients with the same qualifying diagnoses, drawn from the same referral lists but never enrolled in the meal program, averaged 0.94 admissions over an equivalent period. That is a difference of 0.23 admissions per person, or roughly a 24 percent reduction relative to the comparison group's rate.

But the average conceals more than it reveals. The apparent benefit is concentrated almost entirely among participants who stayed with the program. Those who completed the full 26-week delivery course averaged 0.52 admissions — nearly half the comparison group's rate — while the 38 percent of participants who stopped receiving deliveries before week 10 averaged 0.98 admissions, statistically indistinguishable from, and numerically slightly above, the comparison group. The effect also varies sharply by who the participant is. Adults over 70 showed almost no difference from comparison clients. Participants living alone showed a reduction roughly twice the size of that observed among participants in households of three or more people.

Two structural problems shade every number in this brief. First, our utilization data cover only the four hospitals operated by the partner health system; admissions at the county's other two hospitals are invisible to us, and there is no strong reason to believe participants and comparison clients use those invisible hospitals at identical rates. Second, an eleven-week delivery interruption in two zip codes in early 2025, caused by the failure of a refrigerated van, means that some participants counted as receiving the intervention did not receive it continuously, and it degraded our ability to interpret the dose-response pattern cleanly.

What the foundation is deciding in September is whether to renew a program whose best available evidence suggests a meaningful reduction in hospital use for participants who complete it, an unclear or null effect for those who disengage early and for the oldest participants, and a data infrastructure that cannot yet support the causal claims a payer or policymaker would demand. This brief lays out the evidence, its construction, and its limits, so that the decision can be made with clear eyes.

## Background: The Program and the Grant

Riverbend Community Table has operated a twelve-site food pantry network in Cincinnati and surrounding Hamilton County neighborhoods for more than two decades. The pantry network serves a client population in which diet-sensitive chronic disease is endemic: internal intake surveys conducted in 2022 and 2023 found that roughly one in three adult pantry clients reported a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes, congestive heart failure, or both.

The medically tailored meal program, launched in March 2024, was designed to move the organization from general food assistance toward clinically targeted nutrition. Eligible adults — those with a documented diagnosis of congestive heart failure or type 2 diabetes, referred through partner clinics, hospital discharge planners, and the pantry network's own intake screening — were enrolled for a 26-week course of home-delivered meals. Each participant received ten meals per week, designed by a contracted registered dietitian to meet condition-specific specifications: sodium restriction and fluid-balance considerations for heart failure participants, carbohydrate consistency and glycemic targeting for participants with diabetes, and combined protocols for the substantial number of participants with both diagnoses. Deliveries ran weekly, by refrigerated van, to participants' homes.

The foundation's $3.8 million grant covers the full three-year program period, funding meal production, delivery logistics, dietitian services, enrollment staff, and the evaluation activity that produced this brief. The grant agreement anticipated an evaluation of health care utilization outcomes, and the data-use agreement with the partner health system was negotiated during the program's first six months specifically to make that evaluation possible.

By the close of the observation window for this analysis, 1,903 adults had enrolled in the program. The renewal decision before the foundation concerns the third year of the current grant and, implicitly, the case for a longer-term commitment beyond it.

## Data and Methods

### Enrollment and the Linkage Picture

The evaluation's foundation is the linkage between program enrollment records and hospital encounter data. Of the 1,903 enrolled participants, 1,412 — 74 percent — appear in the linked analytic file. The remaining 491 participants fall out of the analysis for one of two reasons: they declined to consent to the data linkage at enrollment or in a subsequent consent refresh, or they receive their hospital care primarily outside the partner health system, making linkage uninformative even where consent was granted.

This 74 percent linkage rate deserves scrutiny rather than celebration. Participants who declined linkage may differ systematically from those who consented — prior evaluations in comparable programs have found that linkage decliners skew toward greater privacy concern, more fragmented care relationships, and in some populations, worse baseline health. Participants who get care elsewhere are, by definition, patients of the two county hospitals we cannot see, a point taken up at length in the limitations section. For now, the essential caveat is that every result in this brief describes the linked 1,412, not the enrolled 1,903, and generalizing from the former to the latter requires an assumption we cannot fully test.

The linked participant population reflects the program's clinical targeting: all carry a qualifying diagnosis of congestive heart failure or type 2 diabetes, with a substantial share carrying both. Enrollment dates span March 2024 through the analytic cutoff, and each participant is observed for the twelve months following their individual enrollment date, or for as much of that window as had elapsed by the cutoff. Participants with fewer than twelve months of potential follow-up were retained with person-time adjustment; sensitivity checks restricting the analysis to participants with complete twelve-month windows produced substantively identical results.

### How the Comparison Group Was Built

No randomization occurred; the program enrolled every eligible person who accepted. The comparison group is therefore an observational construction, and its logic should be understood before its numbers are trusted.

The comparison pool was drawn from the same referral lists that fed program enrollment: partner clinic referrals, hospital discharge planner referrals, and pantry intake screenings. From those lists, we identified pantry clients who carried a matching qualifying diagnosis — congestive heart failure or type 2 diabetes, verified against the same referral documentation used for enrollees — but who never enrolled in the meal program. Some were referred and declined; some were referred during periods when enrollment capacity was full; some were flagged as eligible at pantry intake but never completed a referral. This yielded a comparison group of 2,640 individuals.

The design's strength is that comparison clients come from the same underlying population as participants: the same neighborhoods, the same pantry network, the same clinical referral streams, the same diagnostic criteria. They are not a convenience sample of the general public or a claims-based synthetic cohort; they are, in most observable respects, the people the program would have served had it enrolled them.

The design's weakness is the reason they were not served. People who decline a free meal program, or who never complete a referral, may differ from people who enroll in ways that also predict hospital use: motivation, health literacy, family support, housing stability, competing crises. If enrollees are systematically more engaged with their own care than non-enrollees, some portion of the observed admission gap reflects that engagement rather than the meals. The direction of this bias most plausibly inflates the apparent program effect, though the opposite is conceivable — discharge planners may preferentially refer, and successfully enroll, their sickest patients.

Comparison clients were assigned index dates matched to the enrollment-date distribution of participants, and their hospital utilization was observed through the same partner-system data feed for the twelve months following the index date. Critically, the same visibility limitation applies to both groups: comparison clients' admissions at the two non-partner hospitals are equally invisible. Whether that invisibility is *equally distributed* across the two groups is a separate and harder question, addressed in the limitations section.

### Outcome Measurement and Analytic Approach

The primary outcome is the count of inpatient admissions at the partner health system's four hospitals during the twelve months following enrollment or index date, expressed as a per-person average. Emergency department visits that did not result in admission, observation stays, and outpatient encounters were captured in the data feed and examined descriptively but are not the focus of this brief, both because the foundation's question centered on hospitalization and because admission records proved the most reliably coded field in the linked file.

The analytic approach is deliberately simple: unadjusted per-person admission averages, compared between groups and across pre-specified subgroups. We considered and constructed regression-adjusted estimates controlling for age, diagnosis category, and zip code, and those adjusted estimates fall within a few hundredths of an admission of the unadjusted figures reported here; we present the unadjusted numbers because they are transparent, because the adjustment set is thin enough that adjustment offers more reassurance than protection, and because we do not want the apparatus of modeling to lend the estimates a causal authority the design cannot support. Subgroups — delivery dose, age band, and household size — were specified in the evaluation plan filed with the foundation in the first grant year, not discovered after the fact.

Dose was measured from delivery logs: the number of weeks in which a participant received at least one delivery, out of the scheduled 26. Household size was taken from enrollment intake. Age was calculated at enrollment.

## Results

### The Overall Difference

In the twelve months after enrollment, the 1,412 linked meal recipients averaged 0.71 inpatient admissions at the partner system's four hospitals. The 2,640 comparison clients averaged 0.94 admissions over their matched twelve-month windows. The gap — 0.23 admissions per person — represents a roughly 24 percent reduction relative to the comparison rate.

To make that number concrete: across a population the size of the linked participant group, a difference of 0.23 admissions per person corresponds to roughly 325 avoided admissions over a year, if the difference is real and attributable to the program. At typical inpatient costs for this clinical population, the implied avoided expenditure would run into the millions of dollars annually — a figure we cite not as a claim but as an illustration of why the causal caveats in this brief are worth taking seriously. The stakes of over-reading the estimate are as large as the stakes of dismissing it.

A figure accompanying this brief in presentation form would show two horizontal bars — participants at 0.71, comparison at 0.94 — with the participant bar then decomposed into the dose strata described below. The visual point the figure makes is that the overall participant bar is an average of two very different populations, and the reader should carry that decomposition into every interpretation of the headline number.

### Results by Dose: Where the Effect Lives

The most important finding in this evaluation is not the overall gap but its distribution across levels of program exposure.

Participants who received all 26 weeks of scheduled deliveries averaged 0.52 admissions — 45 percent below the comparison group's 0.94, and 27 percent below even the overall participant average. At the other end, the 38 percent of participants who stopped receiving deliveries before week 10 averaged 0.98 admissions, a rate slightly above the comparison group's. Participants with intermediate exposure — those who continued past week 10 but did not complete the full course — fell between these poles, with admission rates declining roughly monotonically as completed weeks increased.

|---|---|---|
| Comparison group (never enrolled) | 2,640 | 0.94 |
| All linked participants | 1,412 | 0.71 |
| — Completed full 26 weeks | ~540 | 0.52 |
| — Stopped before week 10 | ~537 | 0.98 |
| — Intermediate exposure (10–25 weeks) | ~335 | between 0.52 and 0.98, declining with dose |

Two readings of this pattern are available, and the evaluation cannot fully adjudicate between them.

The optimistic reading is dose-response: more meals, delivered for longer, produce larger reductions in hospitalization, exactly as a nutritional mechanism would predict. Sodium-controlled meals plausibly reduce fluid-overload admissions in heart failure; glycemically consistent meals plausibly reduce hyperglycemic and hypoglycemic crises in diabetes. Under this reading, the program works, and the policy problem is retention — getting the 38 percent who leave early to stay.

The skeptical reading is selection: the same underlying factors that keep a participant in the program for 26 weeks — stable housing, a functioning stove and refrigerator, cognitive capacity, family support, the absence of intervening health collapse — independently predict lower hospitalization. Under this reading, completers were always going to be admitted less often, and the meals are a marker of stability rather than a cause of health. The fact that early leavers' admission rate (0.98) slightly exceeds the never-enrolled comparison rate (0.94) is consistent with this reading: early disengagement may itself signal deteriorating health or life circumstances. Indeed, a review of disenrollment reasons recorded by delivery staff found that a meaningful share of early exits followed hospitalizations, nursing facility placements, or moves — events that are themselves the outcome, or precursors of it.

The truth almost certainly contains both mechanisms. What the evaluation can say is this: the program's plausible benefit is confined to sustained participation, and any renewal case should be built on the completer population and on strategies to enlarge it, not on the overall average, which blends a possibly real effect with a possibly null one.

### Results by Age: Little to No Difference Over 70

Participants over 70 showed almost no difference in admission rates relative to comparison clients of the same age band. The gap that drives the overall result is generated almost entirely by participants under 70.

Several explanations are candidates, none confirmed. Older participants' hospitalizations may be driven by causes less responsive to diet — falls, infections, frailty cascades, and the accumulated multimorbidity of advanced age — so that even a nutritionally effective intervention moves their admission risk little. Older participants may also have their food environment more fully managed already, through family caregivers, home health aides, or congregate services, so that the meals substitute for existing support rather than adding to it. Delivery staff notes suggest a third possibility: some older participants received the meals but did not consistently eat them, whether due to appetite, taste preference, or the practical difficulty of reheating, and delivered-but-uneaten meals produce delivery-log "dose" without nutritional dose.

The finding does not necessarily argue for excluding older adults from a renewed program — the meals may deliver benefits this evaluation does not measure, including food security itself, reduced caregiver strain, and quality of life — but it does argue against projecting hospitalization savings from this age group, and it suggests that if hospital-use reduction is the foundation's primary objective, targeting and program design for the over-70 population need rethinking.

### Results by Household Size: The Meals Matter Most to Those Who Eat Alone

The reduction in admissions among participants living alone was roughly twice the reduction observed among participants in households of three or more people. Participants in two-person households fell in between.

This is, on its face, one of the more mechanistically coherent findings in the evaluation. A person living alone who receives ten medically tailored meals per week has the majority of their diet directly determined by the program; the intervention's "coverage" of their actual food intake is high. In a household of three or more, delivered meals compete with, are shared into, or are displaced by household cooking. Delivery staff reported anecdotally — and program design should treat this as a signal, not a flaw of character — that meals in larger households were sometimes distributed among family members, diluting the clinical dose reaching the enrolled participant. A tailored meal split three ways is a generous act and a diminished intervention.

An alternative explanation again involves selection: people living alone who enroll and persist in a delivery program may be an unusually organized subset of solo-dwelling adults. But the household-size gradient is less vulnerable to this critique than the dose gradient, because household size is fixed at enrollment rather than being an outcome of ongoing engagement, and because the mechanism — dietary coverage — is direct and specific.

The programmatic implication is significant: if the effect is real, participants living alone are the program's highest-yield population, and a household-level meal option (feeding the family so the tailored meal reaches the patient intact) is a testable design change for larger households.

### The Delivery Interruption

In early 2025, the failure of one of the program's refrigerated vans halted deliveries to two zip codes for eleven weeks while a replacement was procured. Participants in those zip codes whose 26-week course overlapped the interruption experienced a gap of up to eleven weeks in the middle of their intervention.

The interruption matters to this evaluation in three ways. First, it mechanically reclassified some participants in the dose analysis: individuals who would have completed 26 weeks instead show interrupted or truncated delivery records, blurring the completer/non-completer boundary in those zip codes. Where possible, we distinguished program-caused interruption from participant-initiated disengagement in the dose coding, but the delivery logs do not always permit a clean separation. Second, the interruption functions as a crude natural experiment: participants exposed to it were removed from treatment for reasons unrelated to their own health or motivation. Admission rates among interruption-affected participants during and shortly after the gap ran higher than among comparable uninterrupted participants — a pattern consistent with a real treatment effect that decays when treatment stops — but the affected group is small, the window short, and the estimate too noisy to bear real weight. We report it as suggestive, not probative. Third, the episode is operationally instructive: a program whose clinical logic depends on continuous weekly dosing had a single point of mechanical failure, and eleven weeks is a long time in the course of decompensating heart failure. A renewal budget should price cold-chain redundancy as a clinical necessity, not a logistics luxury.

A second described figure would plot, for the two affected zip codes, weekly deliveries and weekly admissions on a shared timeline: deliveries dropping to zero for eleven weeks in early 2025, with the admission series showing an elevated stretch beginning several weeks into the gap and subsiding after deliveries resumed. The figure's honest caption would note the small counts underlying the admission series and the impossibility of ruling out seasonal confounding, since the interruption coincided with winter months when admissions for this population run high regardless.

## Limitations

The findings above should be read through four limitations, in descending order of severity.

### The Invisible Hospitals

Our data-use agreement covers the partner health system's four hospitals. Admissions at the county's two other hospitals do not appear in our data — for anyone, participant or comparison client. If both groups used the invisible hospitals at the same rate, our estimates of the *difference* between groups would be attenuated in absolute terms but directionally sound. The problem is that we have reasons to doubt equal invisibility. Program participants were disproportionately referred through the partner system's own clinics and discharge planners, and referral source predicts where a patient is subsequently hospitalized; participants may therefore be *more* visible in our data than comparison clients, some of whom entered the comparison pool through pantry intake with no partner-system care relationship at all. If comparison clients' admissions are more likely to occur at invisible hospitals, the comparison group's true admission rate is higher than the 0.94 we observe, and our estimated effect is understated. The bias could also run the other way for specific subgroups. The essential point is that we are measuring hospital use through a partial window whose partiality may differ by group, and no amount of statistical adjustment within the observed data can fix a difference we cannot see. Regional health information exchange data or an expanded multi-system data agreement — both plausible uses of renewal funds — would close this gap.

### Selection Into Enrollment and Into Completion

As detailed in the methods and dose sections, neither enrollment nor completion was random. The comparison group consists of people who, offered or eligible for the same program, did not take it up; the completer group consists of people who, having started, stayed. Both selections plausibly correlate with unmeasured determinants of hospitalization. The overall estimate of 0.23 avoided admissions per person should therefore be treated as an upper-bound-leaning estimate of the program's causal effect, and the completer estimate more so. The interruption-based natural experiment offers a small counterweight — an exposure change uncorrelated with participant characteristics, showing effects in the expected direction — but it is far too underpowered to anchor a causal claim on its own.

### Linkage Loss

The 26 percent of enrollees absent from the linked file are not a random sample of enrollees. If linkage decliners and out-of-system patients are sicker, more transient, or less engaged than linked participants, the results describe the program's effect on its most measurable participants, who may also be its most reachable ones. All statements in this brief apply to the linked 1,412; extension to the full 1,903 is an extrapolation.

### The Interruption and Other Measurement Noise

The eleven-week delivery gap contaminates dose classification in two zip codes; delivery logs measure meals delivered, not meals eaten; household size was self-reported at enrollment and may have changed; twelve-month follow-up windows were incomplete for late enrollees. None of these is likely to reverse the qualitative pattern of results, but each adds imprecision, and together they counsel against treating any single point estimate as exact.

What this evaluation is not: it is not a randomized trial, it does not measure total cost of care, it does not capture emergency department use or mortality with confidence, and it says nothing about outcomes beyond twelve months. What it is: the strongest inference available from the data infrastructure the program could build in its first two years, presented with its seams showing.

## Implications

### For the Foundation's September Decision

The foundation is deciding whether to fund the program's third year and, in effect, whether the program has earned the runway to prove itself properly. The evidence supports the following characterization: the program is associated with substantially lower hospitalization among participants who complete it, with the strongest and most mechanistically credible effects among adults under 70 and those living alone; the association is compatible with a real causal effect of meaningful size, but selection into completion prevents a definitive causal claim; and the null findings among early leavers and the over-70 population are as informative as the positive ones, because they identify where the current model does not work.

In our judgment, this is the evidence profile of a program worth renewing with conditions, not the profile of either a proven intervention or a failed one. A renewal that simply continues current operations would, in a year, return to the foundation with the same causal ambiguities. A renewal structured around the following commitments would not.

### Retention as the Central Programmatic Priority

Thirty-eight percent of participants leave before week 10, and this group shows no benefit. If even the optimistic reading of the dose gradient is only partly correct, the single largest available gain is converting early leavers into completers. Renewal-year investments should include a structured retention protocol: outreach at the first missed delivery, a dietitian or community health worker touchpoint in weeks 2 through 6 when disengagement concentrates, menu flexibility responsive to the taste and cultural-preference complaints that surface in disenrollment notes, and systematic recording of disenrollment reasons so that program-caused, health-caused, and preference-caused exits can be distinguished in the next evaluation.

### Targeting and Design by Subgroup

The household-size finding argues for piloting a household meal supplement in larger households, so the tailored meal reaches the enrolled patient rather than being shared thin. The age finding argues for a redesigned over-70 offering — smaller portions, texture and reheating accommodations, or pairing meals with home-visit support — evaluated separately, and for honest accounting in any cost projections that hospitalization savings should not currently be claimed for this group. Enrollment targeting toward adults under 70 living alone would maximize measured impact, though the organization and foundation should deliberate openly about whether measured hospital impact is the sole criterion by which a food-security organization should ration a food program.

### Fixing the Measurement Infrastructure

Three investments would transform the third-year evaluation. First, expand utilization visibility beyond the partner system, through the regional health information exchange or direct agreements with the county's other two hospitals, eliminating the invisible-hospital problem that currently undermines every estimate. Second, raise the linkage rate by revisiting the consent process, so the analytic file better represents the enrolled population. Third, if enrollment demand continues to exceed capacity — as it did during periods of the first two years — implement a randomized waitlist: when more eligible people are referred than can be served in a given month, random selection from the waitlist is both an equitable rationing rule and, incidentally, the design that would let the next evaluation make the causal claim this one cannot. No additional burden falls on participants; the program simply formalizes a scarcity that already exists.

### Operational Resilience

The refrigerated van failure interrupted a clinical intervention for eleven weeks in two zip codes. Renewal budgeting should treat cold-chain redundancy — a backup vehicle, a standing rental contract, or a mutual-aid arrangement with a peer organization — as part of the intervention's clinical integrity, and should fund it accordingly.

### The Longer Arc

If the third year delivers a waitlist-randomized design, expanded hospital visibility, and improved retention, the program will be positioned by the end of the grant period to make the kind of evidence-backed case — to Medicaid managed care organizations, to hospital community-benefit programs, to the state's emerging food-is-medicine initiatives — that could sustain it beyond philanthropy. The foundation's $3.8 million will then have purchased not only two-plus years of meals but a credible answer to the question Ms. Kowal asked. The present brief offers the best answer the current data allow: the meals appear to keep people out of the hospital when the meals actually, consistently, reach the people who need them most — and the work of the next year is to make that sentence true more often, and to prove it beyond reasonable doubt.
