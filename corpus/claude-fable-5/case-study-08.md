# Threading the Needle: Saluda Textile Works and the Automation That Nearly Broke Before It Paid

## A Case Study in Automation, Workforce Transition, and Customer Retention at a Mid-Sized Manufacturer

---

## The Company and Its Situation

Saluda Textile Works occupies a long, low-slung plant on the industrial fringe of Spartanburg, South Carolina, in a region that once defined American textile manufacturing and then, over two brutal decades, watched most of it leave. The company survived that exodus by refusing to compete in commodity fabric. Instead, it moved upmarket into technical textiles — engineered fabrics with demanding specifications for abrasion resistance, colorfastness, flame retardancy, and dimensional stability — sold primarily to industrial customers who cared more about consistency than price, at least up to a point.

By 2022, Saluda employed 480 people at its single Spartanburg plant and generated $132 million in annual sales. It was, by the standards of the surviving Carolina textile industry, a healthy business: profitable, technically respected, and deeply embedded in its community. Many of its operators were second- or third-generation textile workers. The plant ran three shifts, and institutional knowledge — the kind that lets a veteran finisher hear a problem in a tenter frame before an instrument detects it — was one of its genuine competitive assets.

It was also, in ways the leadership understood but had not fully priced, a fragile business. Three numbers told the story. First, customer concentration: a single automotive interiors manufacturer accounted for 44 percent of revenue. Saluda supplied technical fabric that ended up in seat structures and interior trim across several vehicle programs, and the relationship was more than a decade old. Second, quality: the plant's defect rate stood at 3.8 percent — tolerable in some industries, increasingly untenable in automotive supply chains where downstream customers were pushing parts-per-million expectations up the tier structure. Third, labor economics: direct labor represented 31 percent of cost of goods sold, overtime was running $2.4 million a year, and absenteeism sat at 9 percent. The overtime and absenteeism figures fed each other in a familiar spiral — absences forced overtime, overtime drove fatigue and burnout, fatigue drove more absences and more defects.

President Dagmar Ellingson had run the company for eight years and knew the balance sheet could absorb a bad year but not a bad structural shift. Her VP of Operations, Malik Rasheed, had been arguing for two years that the finishing and inspection operations — the most labor-intensive and defect-prone stages of the process — needed capital investment. The argument had never quite won. In late 2022, the argument stopped being optional.

## The Commercial Threat

In the fourth quarter of 2022, Saluda's largest customer delivered an ultimatum that had been building for years across the automotive supply base. The customer, itself under sustained cost pressure from the OEMs it served, demanded a 12 percent price reduction phased over three years. If Saluda could not commit to it, the work would move — the customer had already qualified alternative suppliers, at least one of them offshore, and made no secret of it.

The arithmetic was stark. Forty-four percent of $132 million is roughly $58 million in annual revenue. A 12 percent price cut on that book of business was approximately $7 million a year in margin erosion at full phase-in — enough to swing the company from comfortable profitability to marginal survival if nothing else changed. But losing the business outright was worse. The plant's overhead structure was built for its full volume; strip out 44 percent of revenue and the fixed-cost absorption problem alone would likely force a downsizing that could break the company. There was no realistic path to replacing $58 million of technical fabric revenue on any timeline that mattered.

Ellingson and Rasheed spent several weeks in late 2022 working through the options with their finance lead and their sales team, and the options reduced quickly to three. They could refuse the price reduction and begin an orderly wind-down of the relationship, diversifying as fast as possible — a strategy that sounded prudent in the abstract and looked like slow-motion liquidation in the spreadsheet. They could accept the price cut and absorb it, cutting costs through conventional means — headcount reduction, wage freezes, deferred maintenance — which would hollow out exactly the capabilities that made Saluda worth keeping as a supplier. Or they could accept the price cut and earn it back through a step change in productivity and quality, which meant capital investment on a scale the company had not attempted in a generation.

Rasheed's analysis pointed at finishing and inspection. Finishing was where most of the direct labor concentrated and where throughput bottlenecked. Inspection was manual, fatiguing, inconsistent across shifts, and the source of both escaped defects (which reached the customer) and false confidence (defects logged as caught that had actually been missed). The 3.8 percent defect rate was not just a cost problem — scrap, rework, and the second-quality market — it was a commercial vulnerability. The customer's quality engineers had flagged it repeatedly, and the price demand was, in part, a message: prove you're investing in this relationship or we'll find someone who will.

## The Investment Case

In February 2023, Ellingson and Rasheed took a proposal to the board: $9.2 million to automate the finishing line and install an automated optical inspection (AOI) system across the final quality gate. The business case rested on four projected outcomes. Automated finishing would reduce direct labor content and increase line speed, attacking both the 31 percent labor ratio and the $2.4 million overtime bill. Optical inspection would catch defects consistently, around the clock, without fatigue, driving the defect rate down toward 1 percent and improving both scrap costs and the customer relationship. Together, the improvements would fund the 12 percent price concession with room to spare. And the investment itself would serve as a visible commitment to the customer — evidence that Saluda intended to be a supplier worth keeping for another decade.

The financing structure reflected the company's size. Saluda took a $6 million term loan from its regional bank, contributed cash from the balance sheet, and secured a $1.5 million grant from a South Carolina economic development program. The grant came with a condition that would later matter a great deal: a job-retention floor of 460 positions through December 2024. At the time, the condition seemed almost trivially easy to meet. The plant employed 480 people; the automation plan projected displacement of some finishing and inspection roles, but leadership assumed normal attrition and redeployment would absorb the change comfortably above the floor. Nobody modeled the scenario in which the project itself drove people out the door.

Saluda selected Brandywine Automation as its integrator after a competitive process. Brandywine had strong references in converting and web-handling industries and a credible AOI portfolio, though — a detail that received less scrutiny than it deserved — comparatively less experience with the specific optical challenges of textured, patterned, variably reflective technical fabrics. The contract set a commissioning schedule that would have the new lines fully operational by late 2023, in time to begin delivering cost savings before the first tranche of the customer price reduction took full effect.

The workforce plan, such as it was, occupied two pages of the project charter. It identified that roughly 40 operator positions in finishing and manual inspection would be affected. It stated an intention to redeploy affected employees "where feasible" and referenced future retraining "as new technical roles are defined." No budget line was attached to retraining. No timeline was attached to role definition. No one was named as the owner of the workforce transition. Human Resources Director Clarence Boatwright reviewed the charter and raised concerns in writing about the vagueness of the redeployment plan; the concerns were acknowledged and deferred. The project team's energy, understandably but consequentially, went into equipment specification, layout, and financing.

The board approved the investment. Ground was broken on the finishing line reconfiguration in the spring of 2023, and Saluda communicated the project to the customer, who responded with cautious approval and a willingness to hold the relationship steady through the transition.

## What Went Wrong: Commissioning

The first sign of trouble was the schedule. Brandywine's commissioning plan began slipping almost immediately — long-lead components arrived late, integration engineering that was supposed to happen in parallel happened in sequence, and the on-site installation window collided with Saluda's need to keep producing at full volume for a customer that had made clear it was watching. Rather than the planned late-2023 completion, commissioning stretched five months past schedule, with the systems not reaching functional operation until deep into the winter.

The delay was expensive but survivable. What nearly wasn't survivable was what happened when the equipment turned on.

The automated optical inspection system, tuned initially on Brandywine's reference materials and a limited sample of Saluda's fabrics, proved dramatically overcalibrated for the plant's actual product mix. Technical fabrics are an optically hostile environment: woven textures create regular patterns that confuse defect algorithms, coatings produce variable reflectivity, and the boundary between an acceptable natural variation and a genuine defect is subtle even for trained human eyes. The AOI system, set conservatively to avoid escaping defects to an automotive customer, began rejecting good material at extraordinary rates. At its worst, the false rejection rate reached 14 percent — one in seven yards of perfectly conforming fabric flagged as defective, pulled from the flow, and routed to review or scrap.

The operational consequences cascaded. Material flagged by the system had to be dispositioned, which meant manual review, which meant the labor the system was supposed to eliminate was now being spent adjudicating the system's mistakes. Rejected rolls clogged staging areas. Operators, watching a machine condemn fabric they knew was good, lost confidence in the system and, more corrosively, in the leadership that had bought it. Meanwhile, the automated finishing line, still in its own shakedown, ran below rated speed while engineers chased recipe and handling issues.

The combined effect was a throughput collapse. For four months, plant output ran 18 percent below the pre-project baseline — during the same period in which Saluda was contractually delivering the first stage of a price reduction to its largest customer. The company was shipping less, at lower prices, with higher chaos, from a plant that had just absorbed $9 million of capital. On-time delivery to the automotive customer came under pressure, and Rasheed found himself on weekly calls explaining recovery plans to a customer whose quality engineers were now visiting the plant and seeing rejected good material stacked in the aisles. The investment that was supposed to secure the relationship was, for one long winter, actively endangering it.

## What Went Wrong: The Workforce

The commissioning failure was visible and urgent. The workforce failure was quieter, slower, and closer to fatal.

When the automated finishing line came online, 38 operators were displaced from their roles. The project charter's language about redeployment "where feasible" now met reality: there was no retraining plan, no defined set of new technical roles, no budget, and no owner. Displaced operators were reassigned in an improvised fashion — some to material handling, some to the manual review of AOI rejections (a job that consisted of correcting the machine that had taken their old job, a fact whose bitterness was not lost on anyone), some to whatever open positions existed. Wages were nominally protected, but status, skill identity, and any sense of a future at the company were not.

The result was predictable in retrospect and shocking in the moment: 21 of the 38 displaced operators quit within months. These were not the plant's marginal performers. Several were among the most experienced finishing operators in the building — exactly the people whose process knowledge the plant needed most during a chaotic commissioning, and exactly the people with the reputations and skills to find work elsewhere quickly in a tight labor market. Each departure took irreplaceable tacit knowledge out the door and sent a message to everyone remaining: the machines are coming, and the company has no plan for you.

The departures also detonated the grant condition. The state grant's job-retention floor of 460 positions through December 2024 had seemed like a formality. Between the 21 resignations, ordinary attrition that the company froze backfilling on during the throughput crisis, and the general hemorrhage of morale, headcount sagged below the floor, exposing Saluda to potential clawback of the $1.5 million and an uncomfortable conversation with the state agency whose support it might need again.

Inside the plant, anger organized itself. Saluda was not unionized, but it had a long-standing plant committee — an employee representative body with real credibility — led by Rosario Villanueva, a veteran operator widely respected across shifts. Through the autumn and early winter, Villanueva collected grievances: the displaced operators, the broken redeployment promises, mandatory schedule disruptions from the commissioning chaos, and a pervasive sense that management had spent $9 million on machines and nothing on people.

In January 2024, matters came to a head. At a plant committee meeting attended by Ellingson, Rasheed, and Boatwright, Villanueva laid out the committee's position and made explicit what had been circulating on the floor for weeks: if the company did not produce a real plan — for the displaced workers, for retraining, for the future of every operator watching the finishing line and wondering if their department was next — the workforce was prepared to walk out. A walkout at a plant already running 18 percent below baseline, mid-commissioning, mid-price-concession, with an automotive customer scrutinizing every shipment, would have been existential. Everyone in the room understood this. So, crucially, did Villanueva, whose goal was not to break the company but to force it to keep its implicit promises.

Ellingson later described that January meeting as the moment the project's real scope became clear: Saluda had planned an equipment installation when it needed to plan an organizational transformation, and the gap between those two things had nearly cost it everything.

## The Recovery

The recovery that followed had two tracks — technical and human — and its most important feature was that leadership finally ran them as one program with named owners, budgets, and deadlines.

**The technical track** belonged to Plant Engineer Josefina Huerta. In the weeks after the January meeting, Saluda negotiated with Brandywine to place a vendor applications engineer on site full-time — not on call, not remote, but resident in the plant — for the duration of the AOI retuning effort. Huerta and the Brandywine engineer then undertook a systematic, ten-week campaign to make the inspection system work on Saluda's actual products in Saluda's actual conditions.

The work was unglamorous and decisive. They rebuilt the defect classification libraries fabric by fabric, using thousands of samples of known-good and known-defective material drawn from the plant's own production, replacing the vendor's generic reference sets. They discovered that inspection lighting — angle, intensity, and spectral characteristics — interacted with fabric texture and coating reflectivity in ways that generated a large share of the false rejections; re-engineering the lighting arrangement for each product family eliminated whole categories of phantom defects. They retuned sensitivity thresholds product by product rather than applying a single conservative global setting, accepting that an automotive-bound flame-retardant weave and a standard industrial fabric did not need identical scrutiny.

Critically, they also stopped pretending the machine could be perfect. Huerta added a manual second-look station downstream of the AOI system: material flagged by the machine went to a trained human inspector for confirmation before disposition. This hybrid design did three things at once. It converted false rejections from scrapped or delayed material into a quick secondary check, protecting throughput. It generated a continuous stream of labeled data — machine said defect, human said good — that fed directly back into threshold tuning, so the system improved week over week. And it created skilled roles for exactly the experienced inspectors the automation had displaced, turning them from the system's victims into its teachers.

Over the ten weeks, false rejections fell from 14 percent to low single digits and kept falling. Throughput recovered as the finishing line simultaneously came out of its own shakedown, and by mid-2024 the plant was running above its 2022 baseline for the first time.

**The human track** belonged to Clarence Boatwright, working closely — and this was deliberate — with Villanueva and the plant committee rather than around them. The centerpiece was a real retraining program, funded and scheduled, that converted 44 operators into equipment technicians: people trained to operate, tune, troubleshoot, and maintain the automated finishing and inspection systems. The training combined vendor-delivered technical instruction, on-the-job pairing with Huerta's engineering team, and coursework arranged through a local technical college. Graduates received a $3.10 per hour wage increase, formal technician job titles, and a defined progression path.

The wage increase mattered economically, but the signal mattered more. The program demonstrated, in the only currency the floor trusted — money, titles, and actual training hours — that automation at Saluda would mean advancement for the people who committed to it, not just displacement. Several of the retrained technicians were operators who had been displaced in the first wave; some had been among the angriest voices in January. Villanueva and the committee were given standing visibility into the program's enrollment, progress, and any future automation plans, institutionalizing the consultation that should have happened in 2023.

Boatwright also took on the grant problem directly, opening renegotiation with the state agency. The conversation was uncomfortable but ultimately constructive: Saluda could show that the headcount shortfall reflected a transition, not a retreat — that it was investing in higher-wage technical jobs, raising skill levels, and securing the plant's largest customer relationship for the long term. The retention floor was renegotiated to reflect the new reality, and the grant survived without clawback, though leadership absorbed the lesson that public-money conditions must be modeled against project scenarios, not just current headcount.

## The Results

By mid-2025 — roughly two and a half years after board approval and eighteen months after the January crisis — the numbers told the story of a project that had ultimately delivered, at a price and along a path nobody had planned.

**Quality.** The defect rate fell from 3.8 percent to 0.9 percent, a 76 percent reduction. The hybrid machine-plus-human inspection architecture proved more effective than either pure approach: the AOI system never fatigued and never varied across shifts, while the second-look station caught the machine's residual errors and continuously improved its tuning. Escaped defects reaching the automotive customer fell to levels that transformed the tone of quality reviews from adversarial to collaborative.

**Throughput.** Plant output ran 23 percent above the 2022 baseline — a 41-point swing from the depths of the commissioning trough. The automated finishing line, once fully commissioned, ran faster and more consistently than the manual process it replaced, and the elimination of AOI-driven flow disruptions let the whole plant find a rhythm it had never had.

**Cost.** Cost per yard fell 14 percent from the 2022 baseline — comfortably exceeding the 12 percent price concession, which meant the investment had fully funded the customer's demand with margin to spare. Overtime fell from $2.4 million to $700,000 annually, a $1.7 million recurring saving, as higher throughput and stabler operations broke the absence-overtime-fatigue spiral. Absenteeism improved alongside morale, though it never became a headline metric of the project.

**The commercial outcome.** The result that justified everything else: the automotive customer signed a five-year renewal worth $58 million. The relationship that had been one ultimatum away from ending in late 2022 was now contractually secured into the next decade, anchored by a supplier whose quality and cost position had visibly transformed. The customer's own engineers, who had toured the plant during the worst of the commissioning chaos, toured it again during renewal negotiations; the contrast was itself a sales argument.

**The workforce.** Headcount settled at 441 — down 39 positions from the 2022 level of 480, and below the original grant floor of 460, which was why the floor had to be renegotiated. The reduction came through attrition and the 2023–24 departures, not layoffs. Against that reduction stood 44 operators retrained and promoted into technician roles at $3.10 more per hour, a plant committee with a standing seat at the table for future automation decisions, and a workforce that had watched the company fail its people, get confronted, and respond with something real. Rasheed's assessment was blunt: the plant employed fewer people than in 2022, but more of them had futures.

## The Cost

Total spend came to $11.4 million against the approved $9.2 million — a 24 percent overrun. The overage came from the extended commissioning period and vendor support, the resident Brandywine engineer and the ten-week retuning campaign, the second-look station and its staffing, the retraining program and associated wage increases, and the accumulated cost of running a degraded plant for months. That figure does not capture the full economic damage: four months of 18 percent throughput loss during a price concession, expedited freight and recovery costs to protect customer deliveries, and the unquantifiable cost of 21 experienced operators walking out with decades of process knowledge.

Even so, the return mathematics closed decisively. A 14 percent reduction in cost per yard on roughly $130 million of revenue, $1.7 million in annual overtime savings, dramatically lower scrap and rework at a 0.9 percent defect rate, and — dominating everything — the preservation and five-year renewal of a $58 million annual customer relationship that would otherwise have been lost. On any reasonable accounting, $11.4 million to secure the company's commercial foundation and reset its cost structure was money well spent. The point of the case is not that the investment was wrong. It is that roughly $2.2 million of overrun, four months of operational crisis, 21 irreplaceable resignations, and one threatened walkout were the price of planning it as an equipment purchase rather than a transformation.

## What Saluda Would Do Differently

In internal retrospectives, Ellingson, Rasheed, Huerta, and Boatwright converged on a consistent set of conclusions.

**They would have planned the workforce transition with the same rigor as the equipment.** The two-page, unbudgeted, ownerless redeployment section of the project charter was the single most expensive document in the company's recent history. The retraining program that eventually worked — defined technician roles, vendor and college-based training, a wage premium, a career path — was entirely designable in 2023. Designed and announced before commissioning, it would likely have retained most of the 21 operators who quit, kept the grant floor intact, and prevented the January confrontation. Boatwright's rule afterward: no capital project touching more than ten jobs proceeds without a workforce plan carrying a budget line, a named owner, and dates.

**They would have brought the plant committee in at the start, not at the breaking point.** Villanueva and the committee turned out to be the recovery's most valuable partners — credible with the floor, pragmatic about automation, and invested in the plant's survival. Treating them as a constituency to be managed after the fact, rather than co-designers before it, converted potential allies into an opposition that had to threaten a walkout to be heard.

**They would have vetted the vendor's experience with their specific materials, and contracted for outcomes on their fabric.** Brandywine's AOI credentials were real but generic. The 14 percent false rejection rate traced directly to a system tuned on reference materials rather than Saluda's optically difficult product mix. Acceptance criteria defined as performance on Saluda's own fabrics under production conditions — with resident vendor engineering support written into the contract rather than negotiated mid-crisis — would have surfaced the tuning challenge in the factory acceptance phase instead of on the production floor.

**They would have designed the hybrid inspection architecture from the beginning.** The machine-plus-second-look model was adopted as a crisis workaround and became the system's permanent strength — protecting throughput, generating tuning data, and creating skilled roles. Assuming the automation would work autonomously from day one was, Huerta argued, the core technical planning error.

**They would have stress-tested external commitments against project risk.** The 460-job grant floor was accepted as a formality because nobody modeled the scenario where the project itself drove headcount down. Public funding conditions, customer commitments, and loan covenants all deserve scenario analysis against a project's downside cases, not just its base case.

**They would have protected the schedule buffer between commissioning and commercial commitments.** Phasing in a price concession on the assumption that a complex automation project would deliver on time left no margin. The five-month slip meant Saluda gave the discount before it earned the savings, funding the gap from its own margins during its worst operational stretch.

## What a Reader Should Take From This

For a leader of a mid-sized manufacturer facing the combination Saluda faced — a concentrated customer demanding a step-change in price, an aging labor-intensive process, and a capital investment as the only credible answer — the Saluda case offers a compact set of transferable lessons.

**First, the investment decision was right, and would be right again.** Faced with a 12 percent price demand from a customer representing 44 percent of revenue, the alternatives — decline and shrink, or accept and hollow out — were both paths to decline. Buying the productivity to fund the concession was the only strategy that preserved the company. Readers in similar positions should not let Saluda's painful middle chapters obscure that the fundamental call was correct: a 14 percent cost reduction, a 76 percent defect reduction, and a five-year, $58 million renewal vindicated it completely.

**Second, an automation project is a workforce project wearing an equipment budget.** The technology at Saluda ultimately worked. What nearly killed the project was everything the charter treated as an afterthought: displaced operators without a plan, retraining without a budget, and employee representatives without a seat. The cheapest insurance available to any leader running such a project is to fund and announce the people plan — new roles, training, wages, and paths — before the first machine arrives. The $3.10-per-hour technician premium and the training program cost a fraction of what the resignations and the throughput crisis cost.

**Third, automation of judgment-intensive tasks fails toward false alarms, and the plan must absorb them.** Optical inspection of textured fabric — like visual inspection in many industries — is a domain where the machine's early performance will be worse than promised on the customer's real materials. Contract for acceptance on your own products, secure resident vendor support, and build the human backstop into the design rather than bolting it on in crisis. The hybrid system that Saluda improvised under duress is the system a careful planner would specify from the start.

**Fourth, your most experienced people are both your greatest commissioning asset and your greatest flight risk — at the same moment.** The operators who best understood Saluda's processes were exactly the ones displaced first and hired away fastest. A transition plan that converts them into the automation's trainers, tuners, and technicians captures their knowledge; a vacuum sends it to competitors.

**Fifth, engage worker representatives as partners before they become adversaries.** Villanueva's committee forced the recovery Saluda's management should have initiated. In every version of this story, the workforce eventually gets a voice; the only choice leadership has is whether that voice arrives at the design table or on the picket line.

**Finally, budget and schedule for the trough, not just the peak.** Between approval and payoff, Saluda passed through five months of schedule slip, four months of 18 percent throughput loss, a 14 percent false rejection rate, a near-walkout, and a 24 percent cost overrun — all while honoring a price cut to its most important customer. The project's happy ending was earned by a company with enough balance-sheet resilience, customer credibility, and leadership humility to survive its own plan's failures. A reader planning a similar transformation should assume the trough will come, size the buffers accordingly, and — above all — write the workforce chapter of the plan first, because at Saluda, as in most such stories, the machines were never really the hard part.
