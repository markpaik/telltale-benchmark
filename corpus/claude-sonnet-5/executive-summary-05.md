# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY: TUPELO PREVENTIVE MAINTENANCE OVERHAUL

**Capital and Continuous Improvement Review Office**
**Prepared for: Ekaterina Novikova, VP of Manufacturing, and the Operations Committee**
**Date: September 19, 2025 — for the October 3, 2025 Committee meeting**
**Program Sponsor: Dwayne Lockhart, Plant Manager, Tupelo**

The Tupelo preventive maintenance overhaul is the one program in this year's review that did what it was funded to do, and did it inside a single fiscal year. On a $2.2 million investment, the plant cut unplanned downtime from 11.4 percent to 4.1 percent, raised overall equipment effectiveness from 62 to 78, and removed $3.1 million from annual scrap cost. The recommendation to the Committee is straightforward: approve replication of the Tupelo model at Dothan, at the Corinth plant, and at the Booneville plant during fiscal 2026, funded in part from the scrap savings the Tupelo line is now throwing off, and direct Mr. Lockhart to serve as internal owner of the rollout rather than treating this as a one-plant success to be admired and shelved.

The case for replication rests on numbers that hold up under scrutiny. A downtime reduction from 11.4 to 4.1 percent is a 7.3-point swing on a plant running three shifts, which by the Tupelo controller's own accounting recovered just under 2,900 hours of press and line time over the fiscal year — time that converted directly into shippable parts rather than idle capacity. The OEE gain, from 62 to 78, is not a rounding change; it moves Tupelo from a performance band typical of plants running reactive maintenance programs into the band that OEM quality auditors associate with mature, planned-maintenance operations, which matters because two of Tallapoosa's six OEM customers now ask for OEE data during supplier scorecard reviews. The scrap number is the one that should draw the Committee's attention longest. A $3.1 million annual reduction against a $2.2 million capital outlay is a payback period under nine months, which is a rare enough result in capital-intensive manufacturing that the Review Office independently re-checked Tupelo's scrap accounting against corporate cost-center reports before including the figure here. It held.

None of this happened by accident, and the mechanism is worth stating because it is exportable. Mr. Lockhart's team replaced a calendar-based maintenance schedule with a condition-based one, added vibration and thermal sensors to the eleven pieces of equipment responsible for the majority of historical downtime, and retrained the maintenance staff to act on sensor readings rather than wait for failure or for the next scheduled service window. The capital went to sensors, a maintenance management software license, and a training program; it did not go to new equipment. That matters for replication economics, because it means the Dothan, Corinth, and Booneville versions of this program should cost roughly what Tupelo's did, adjusted for line count, rather than requiring fresh capital equipment purchases.

The decision in front of the Committee is not whether the Tupelo results are real — the Review Office is satisfied they are — but whether to fund replication now, at an estimated $6.4 million across the three remaining plants, or to let each plant manager pursue a similar program independently and at whatever pace local capital budgets allow. The cost of moving now is the $6.4 million itself, plus the organizational cost of pulling Mr. Lockhart partway out of his plant-manager duties to oversee three simultaneous rollouts, which the Review Office estimates at a six-month stretch of divided attention. The cost of waiting is more diffuse but not smaller: Dothan, Corinth, and Booneville continue to run at their current downtime and scrap levels, which on Dothan's volume alone the Review Office estimates at roughly $2.4 million in avoidable annual scrap cost, and the maintenance staff who built the Tupelo playbook — condition-based scheduling, sensor calibration, the vendor relationships behind them — are a retention risk in their own right if the company signals that this expertise has no further use. A one-plant success that is not replicated within a year tends, in the Review Office's experience across other manufacturers, to become a one-plant success that is quietly reversed at the next round of maintenance-budget cuts, because it no longer has an internal champion pushing the story forward. The Committee's decision, in short, is not about the Tupelo numbers. It is about whether to spend $6.4 million to make those numbers company-wide before the momentum behind them dissipates.

# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY: DOTHAN ROBOTIC WELD CELL

**Capital and Continuous Improvement Review Office**
**Prepared for: Ekaterina Novikova, VP of Manufacturing, and the Operations Committee**
**Date: September 19, 2025 — for the October 3, 2025 Committee meeting**
**Facility: Dothan, Alabama Plant**

The Dothan robotic weld cell has absorbed $7.4 million against an original business case that does not survive contact with its own results, and the Review Office's finding is that continuing to fund the cell in its current configuration is no longer defensible without a hard remediation deadline attached. The cell runs a 61-second cycle against a 42-second design specification, holds first-pass yield at 81 percent against 96 percent on the manual line it was built to replace, and has failed customer part approval — PPAP — twice. The recommendation is to give the program a single, final remediation window of 120 days, capped at $1.1 million in additional spend, with an explicit trigger: if the cell has not reached 90 percent first-pass yield and passed PPAP by the end of that window, the Committee should authorize reversion to the manual line and classify the $7.4 million as a closed loss.

The gap between specification and reality here is wide enough that it raises a question the Review Office was not originally asked to answer but cannot avoid: how a cell built to a 42-second cycle time is running at 61 seconds, a 45 percent overage, more than two years into the program. The engineering explanation offered by the Dothan plant team centers on weld-path programming that was validated in simulation but not adequately tested against the actual part-to-part variation the cell sees on the floor, which has forced the robot to run slower search-and-correct routines than the original spec assumed. That explanation is plausible, but it is also the same explanation the Review Office was given eighteen months ago, at the last capital review, when the cell was already behind schedule. The first-pass yield figure is the more serious number of the two, because it is not an engineering curiosity — it is a cost the plant is paying today. A 15-point yield gap against the manual line's 96 percent means roughly one in five welds now requires rework or scrap that the manual process did not generate, and Dothan's own scrap accounting attributes just under $900,000 of the plant's fiscal 2025 scrap cost to weld-cell rework, a cost that did not exist before the cell replaced the manual line.

The two PPAP failures are, in the Review Office's judgment, the fact that should weigh most heavily on the Committee, because they touch customer relationships rather than internal cost accounting. Both failures were on dimensional and weld-strength criteria tied directly to the cycle-time and yield problems described above — this is not a paperwork issue or a documentation gap, it is the customer's own quality engineers finding the same problems the plant's internal data already shows. A third PPAP failure, should it occur before the cell is remediated, is the kind of event that shows up on OEM supplier scorecards and can affect sourcing decisions on future platforms; Tallapoosa's six-customer base leaves little room to absorb that kind of reputational cost, and Dothan is understood internally to be the plant most exposed to a resourcing conversation if quality performance does not stabilize.

The decision facing the Committee is a sunk-cost decision dressed up as a capital decision, and it should be treated as such. Continuing the cell costs a further $1.1 million over 120 days under the remediation plan the Dothan engineering team has proposed, which focuses on re-programming the weld paths against actual part variation data rather than simulation data, retiming the cycle to a realistic 45-to-48 second target, and running a formal yield-verification build ahead of the next PPAP submission. Stopping costs the $7.4 million already spent, in full, plus whatever it takes to reinstall or rebuild manual welding capacity at Dothan, which plant engineering estimates at $1.6 million and a ten-week line-down window if the manual stations were fully decommissioned — the Review Office recommends the Committee ask Dothan directly whether that decommissioning is in fact complete, since the answer materially changes the cost of reversal. What the Committee should not do is authorize open-ended continued investment without a yield and PPAP trigger attached to it, because the pattern of this program to date — plausible engineering explanations, incremental spend, and results that do not move — is exactly the pattern that turns a $7.4 million writedown into a $10 million one.

# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY: NORTHEAST MISSISSIPPI COMMUNITY COLLEGE APPRENTICESHIP PIPELINE

**Capital and Continuous Improvement Review Office**
**Prepared for: Ekaterina Novikova, VP of Manufacturing, and the Operations Committee**
**Date: September 19, 2025 — for the October 3, 2025 Committee meeting**
**Partner Institution: Northeast Mississippi Community College (NEMCC)**

The apprenticeship pipeline with NEMCC has produced a workforce number the Committee will want to hear — skilled-trade vacancies at the four plants fell from 17 to 12 percent over the review period — but the finding underneath that number is more mixed than the headline suggests, and the recommendation is to continue the partnership at current funding while attaching retention conditions that do not currently exist. Of 64 apprentices enrolled, 41 graduated and 15 of those 41 left the company within a year, meaning the program's net contribution to the skilled workforce is 26 retained tradespeople against 64 enrolled, a 41 percent net-yield rate that the Review Office considers the honest number rather than the graduation rate alone. The recommendation is to keep funding the pipeline, because the alternative hiring market has not produced a comparable source of skilled labor, but to redirect a portion of program spend from recruitment and enrollment toward a structured retention track for the first eighteen months after graduation, since that is where the program is currently losing its return.

The vacancy-rate improvement is real but it is not solely attributable to this program, and the Committee should hear that qualification directly rather than discover it later. The five-point drop from 17 to 12 percent coincided with a broader loosening in the regional skilled-trades hiring market — machinists and industrial electricians in the Tupelo labor shed became measurably easier to hire over the same period, driven in part by a slowdown at a competing manufacturer roughly forty miles away that released skilled workers into the market. The Review Office's plant-level interviews suggest the NEMCC apprentices account for something on the order of half of the vacancy improvement, with the remainder attributable to the general market shift, though the company does not track new-hire source with enough precision to state that split with confidence, and the Review Office recommends that gap in measurement be closed before next year's review rather than repeating an estimate built on interview impressions.

The attrition figure deserves more attention than it has received internally to date. Fifteen of 41 graduates — 37 percent — leaving within twelve months of completing the program is a high enough number that it should be treated as the program's central operating problem rather than a footnote to an otherwise positive story. Exit interview data, where it exists, points to three recurring reasons: starting pay for newly certified apprentices that graduates describe as close to what they earned as apprentices, limited visibility into advancement paths once certified, and in several cases a shift preference — apprentices trained on day shift and were then assigned to rotating or night shifts upon graduation, which a handful of exit interviews cite directly as the reason for leaving. None of these are cheap to fix, but none of them require rebuilding the program from the ground up either; they point to compensation-step and shift-assignment adjustments at the point of graduation rather than problems with the training itself, which by NEMCC's own instructional metrics and by supervisor evaluations of graduates who stayed is regarded as sound.

The decision in front of the Committee is a funding-and-conditions decision, not a continue-or-cancel one, because the case for canceling the partnership is weak — 26 net retained skilled tradespeople against a labor market that remains structurally tight in rural Mississippi is not a result Tallapoosa can easily replace through open-market hiring, and NEMCC remains, along with two competitor manufacturers, one of a small number of accessible skilled-trades pipelines in the region. The cost of continuing as currently structured is the ongoing program fee to NEMCC, budgeted at roughly $780,000 annually, plus the embedded cost of the 37 percent first-year attrition, which the Review Office estimates at approximately $340,000 in training investment walking out the door with each year's departing cohort. The cost of adding a retention track — a graduated pay step at six and twelve months post-certification, and a shift-assignment commitment made at enrollment rather than at graduation — is estimated at an incremental $210,000 annually across the four plants, a figure the Review Office considers modest against the $340,000 currently being lost to attrition it might meaningfully reduce. Canceling the program outright would return the plants to open-market hiring in a labor pool that, even with recent loosening, left 17 percent of skilled positions vacant as recently as the start of this review period, and the Review Office does not recommend that path.

# EXECUTIVE SUMMARY: MANUFACTURING EXECUTION SYSTEM INTEGRATION

**Capital and Continuous Improvement Review Office**
**Prepared for: Ekaterina Novikova, VP of Manufacturing, and the Operations Committee**
**Date: September 19, 2025 — for the October 3, 2025 Committee meeting**
**Program Owner: Fatoumata Camara, Quality Director**

The manufacturing execution system integration was approved at $5.2 million and has spent $8.9 million, a 71 percent overrun, and the plant that has not yet gone live has slipped nine months, from a January 2025 cutover to a September 2025 one that has itself now passed without confirmation of completion at the time of this writing. The finding is that the program is delivering a partial result at a cost well above what was authorized, and the recommendation is that the Committee approve a final, capped tranche of funding to complete the fourth plant and to close the inventory-accuracy gap at the three live plants, rather than either walking away from a system that is three-quarters deployed or continuing to fund it without a firm completion date and spending ceiling.

The inventory accuracy figure is the one that should concern the Committee most directly, because it is the metric the entire program was justified on. MES was approved on the premise that real-time transaction capture on the shop floor would lift inventory accuracy toward the 98 percent target the company's OEM customers increasingly expect as a condition of vendor-managed inventory arrangements; at 87 percent, the system is running eleven points below that target across the three plants where it has been live long enough to generate stable data. Ms. Camara's team attributes the gap primarily to incomplete barcode discipline at receiving and at work-in-process transfer points — operators bypassing scan steps under production pressure — rather than to a defect in the system itself, and the Review Office's spot-check of transaction logs at the Corinth plant is consistent with that explanation: the transactions that are captured are accurate, but a meaningful share of movements are not being captured at all. That is a more fixable problem than a software defect would be, but it is not a free fix, since it requires floor-level enforcement and, in some cases, station redesign to make scanning the path of least resistance rather than an extra step operators can skip.

The cost overrun is harder to explain away as a process-discipline issue and deserves a direct answer from the program team before the Committee approves further spend. A $3.7 million overage against a $5.2 million baseline is not a rounding error, and the Review Office's review of change-order documentation suggests roughly $1.4 million of it is attributable to scope genuinely added after approval — additional plant floor connectivity infrastructure that the original business case underestimated — while the remainder appears to reflect integration and configuration work that should have been scoped correctly the first time. The fourth plant's slip from January to September is connected to the same root cause: the integration complexity at the first three plants ran well ahead of the vendor's original estimates, and each plant's go-live has informed, and delayed, the next.

The Committee's decision here is genuinely a continue-or-stop decision, unlike the apprenticeship program, because a system that is 87 percent accurate and only three-quarters deployed is not yet delivering the customer-facing capability it was funded to build, and the Committee should not assume that finishing it is automatically the right call simply because $8.9 million has already been spent. The cost to continue is a further estimated $2.3 million to complete the fourth-plant cutover and fund a floor-level scanning-discipline initiative at the three live plants intended to close the accuracy gap from 87 to the 98 percent target within two quarters of completion — a plan Ms. Camara's team has proposed but which the Review Office has not yet independently validated in detail and recommends the Committee require before releasing funds. The cost to stop is the $8.9 million already spent, written off in substantial part, since a partially integrated MES system across three of four plants has limited standalone value and would likely need to be either decommissioned or left running at its current 87 percent accuracy indefinitely, a level that the Review Office notes falls short of what at least one OEM customer has begun asking about directly in supplier reviews. On balance, the Review Office's assessment is that the marginal cost of finishing — $2.3 million — is small relative to the $8.9 million already committed and the customer-facing risk of leaving the system permanently short of its accuracy target, but the Committee should condition any further release of funds on a hard completion date, a capped budget, and an independent validation of the scanning-discipline plan before the money moves.

The vendor relationship underlying the integration is also a factor the Committee should weigh before approving the next tranche, since it bears on whether $2.3 million is a reasonable estimate or another checkpoint on the way to a fourth revision. The system was implemented by a single integration vendor under a time-and-materials contract rather than a fixed-price arrangement, which the Review Office believes explains a meaningful part of the gap between the original $5.2 million estimate and the $8.9 million actually spent — there was no contractual ceiling forcing scope discipline as the work expanded. Ms. Camara's team has represented to the Review Office that the proposed $2.3 million completion tranche would be negotiated as a fixed-price change order rather than continuing on a time-and-materials basis, and the Review Office regards that as a necessary, not optional, condition of any further release of funds. Absent a fixed price on the remaining work, the Committee has no way to distinguish a genuine final tranche from another interim estimate, and the program's history over the past two years gives no reason to assume the next number offered will be the last one asked for.

There is also a customer-facing timing risk the Committee should have in view. At least one OEM customer's supplier-quality team has begun asking Tallapoosa directly about inventory accuracy as part of its vendor-managed inventory qualification process, and a live system running at 87 percent accuracy is a weaker answer to that inquiry than either a fully integrated system at 98 percent or, candidly, no system at all paired with the manual cycle-count controls the plants relied on before MES. An 87 percent figure invites a follow-up question the plants are not yet positioned to answer well: whether the shortfall is a technology gap or a discipline gap. The Review Office's own finding — that the gap is overwhelmingly a floor-level scanning-discipline problem rather than a system defect — is the right answer to give that customer, but it is only a credible answer if the scanning-discipline initiative is funded, timed, and verified rather than left as an unstaffed aspiration attached to a software rollout.

Taken together, the four programs under review this year describe a consistent pattern that the Committee may find as useful as any single program's numbers: the initiatives built around a clear operational metric, a single accountable sponsor, and a bounded scope — Tupelo's preventive maintenance overhaul chief among them — delivered results that matched or exceeded their business cases. The initiatives that drifted on scope, ran without a fixed-price ceiling, or lacked a single owner empowered to say no to expansion — the Dothan weld cell and the MES integration most visibly — are the two now asking the Committee for money beyond their original authorization. The Review Office does not offer that observation as a criticism of any individual program sponsor, several of whom inherited timelines and vendor arrangements set before their tenure, but as a design note for how fiscal 2026 capital requests are structured: a fixed-price completion condition, a fixed decision date, and a single named owner appear, on this year's evidence, to be the difference between a program the Committee approves once and a program it is asked to fund a third and fourth time.

The Review Office recommends the Committee treat the four items as four separate votes rather than a single capital-review approval, since the funding asks differ in kind and not just in size, and bundling them risks the stronger cases being slowed by the weaker ones or, conversely, the weaker cases riding through on the strength of Tupelo's results. For the October 3 agenda, the recommended actions are:

- **Tupelo preventive maintenance overhaul** — approve $6.4 million to replicate the program at Dothan, Corinth, and Booneville in fiscal 2026, with Dwayne Lockhart named as rollout owner and a quarterly reporting requirement back to this Committee on downtime, OEE, and scrap cost at each plant as it converts.
- **Dothan robotic weld cell** — approve $1.1 million in capped remediation funding under a strict 120-day window, with an automatic reversion decision — not a further review — triggered if 90 percent first-pass yield and a passed PPAP are not both achieved by the deadline.
- **NEMCC apprenticeship pipeline** — continue the $780,000 annual program fee and approve an incremental $210,000 for a graduated pay-step and shift-assignment retention track, with first-year attrition reported to the Committee at the next annual review as the program's primary performance metric going forward, alongside the graduation count.
- **Manufacturing execution system integration** — approve $2.3 million in completion funding conditioned on the vendor converting to a fixed-price change order for the remaining scope, a confirmed fourth-plant cutover date, and an independently validated plan to close the inventory-accuracy gap from 87 to 98 percent within two quarters of that cutover.

None of these four recommendations require the Committee to take the Review Office's word over the plant and program teams' own data; the underlying figures in each case were checked against corporate cost-center and quality-system reports rather than taken solely from program self-reporting, and the Review Office is prepared to walk through the supporting detail on any of the four at the meeting itself. What the Committee is being asked to decide on October 3 is not whether these numbers are accurate — the Office is satisfied they are — but how much further capital and organizational attention each program has earned based on what those numbers show. Tupelo has earned expansion. Dothan has earned a deadline. The apprenticeship program has earned continuation with a fix. The MES integration has earned one final, tightly bounded push to finish what is otherwise a $8.9 million asset sitting short of the result it was built to deliver.

**Appendix: Methodology and Data Sources**

The figures cited in each of the four summaries above were drawn from three categories of source material, and the Review Office notes the distinction here because the Committee has, in past years, asked how confident it should be in numbers that originate substantially from the programs being reviewed. The first category is corporate cost-center and general-ledger data, maintained independently of the plant and program teams, which was used to verify the capital-spend figures for all four programs and the scrap-cost figures for Tupelo and Dothan specifically. The second category is quality-system data — PPAP submission records, first-pass yield logs, and inventory-transaction audit trails — pulled directly from the systems of record rather than from summary reports prepared by the plants, which is how the Office was able to independently confirm both Dothan's yield gap and the Corinth scanning-discipline finding rather than relying on the plants' own characterizations. The third category, used more sparingly and flagged as such in the text, is structured interviews with plant management, maintenance staff, and in the case of the apprenticeship program, a sample of eleven graduates and four supervisors, conducted where the underlying system data either did not exist or could not by itself explain a result — the apprenticeship attrition reasons and the vacancy-rate attribution both rest partly on this third category, and the Office has flagged both as estimates rather than audited figures for that reason.

Two limitations are worth stating plainly rather than leaving implicit. First, the apprenticeship program's vacancy-rate attribution — the Office's estimate that roughly half of the five-point improvement is attributable to NEMCC graduates rather than general labor-market loosening — is the softest figure in this year's review, built from plant-manager interviews rather than a tracked new-hire-source field in the HR system, and the Office has recommended, and repeats here, that this tracking gap be closed before the fiscal 2026 review so that next year's figure rests on records rather than recollection. Second, the MES completion-cost estimate of $2.3 million was provided by the program team and the integration vendor jointly, and while the Office regards it as a reasonable planning figure given the fixed-price condition recommended above, it has not been independently re-estimated by a third party, and the Office would not object if the Committee asked for such an estimate before releasing the full tranche.
