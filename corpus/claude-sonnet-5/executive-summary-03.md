# Executive Summary: K-2 Structured Literacy Initiative

**Office of Research and Accountability, Piedmont Vale School District**
**Prepared for Superintendent Imani Blackshear, CFO Petra Lindqvist, and the Board Finance Committee**
**September 9, 2025**

The K-2 structured literacy rollout has produced the strongest early-grade reading gains this office has measured in the last five evaluation cycles, and the Office of Research and Accountability recommends that the district move immediately to secure continuation funding and begin planning a phased expansion beyond the twelve pilot schools. The state literacy improvement grant that funds this program expires September 30, 2025 — three weeks from this briefing — and no bridge funding has been identified if the renewal application is not submitted before that date.

The program placed structured, phonics-based literacy instruction and coaching in twelve elementary schools serving 1,900 kindergarten through second-grade students, at a cost of $2.4 million over the two-year grant term. The results justify the investment. The share of students scoring at or above the spring benchmark on the district's universal reading screener rose from 41 percent to 58 percent districtwide across the twelve pilot schools, a 17-point gain in two years against a baseline that had been essentially flat for the three years prior. First-grade gains were the most encouraging finding in the data: improvement held across all four subgroups the office tracks — students receiving special education services, English learners, students qualifying for free or reduced-price meals, and students identified as academically at risk based on kindergarten entry screening. That consistency matters. Literacy interventions frequently show gains concentrated among students closest to the benchmark already, while the students furthest behind see little movement. This cohort did not follow that pattern. The office views the uniformity of the first-grade result as the single strongest piece of evidence that the instructional model, rather than a favorable mix of students, is producing the gain.

Second-grade results, while positive, were more uneven, with two of the twelve schools showing gains below five points. Preliminary review points to coaching capacity as the likely explanation: those two schools shared a single instructional coach split across buildings, while every other pilot school had a dedicated coach. This is a staffing and scheduling matter, not a curriculum failure, and it is correctable within existing program design if the district continues the initiative long enough to fix it.

Two actions need to happen before September 30. First, the grant renewal application — already drafted by the Office of Research and Accountability in coordination with the Curriculum and Instruction division — needs the Superintendent's signature and Board Finance Committee endorsement this week to meet the state's submission window. Second, the district needs to decide now whether renewal funding will sustain the current twelve schools only or whether the Board intends to expand the model, because the state grant application requires the district to specify school count and enrollment projections at the time of submission, not after award. This office recommends renewal at current scale for one additional year, with a formal expansion plan developed during that year using general fund and future grant dollars, rather than attempting to expand and renew simultaneously without a full year of second-grade outcome data to guide school selection.

If the grant is not renewed and the program lapses, the consequences fall unevenly and quickly. Twelve literacy coaches, most hired specifically for this initiative and without guaranteed placement elsewhere in the district, would need to be reassigned or released within the current school year. The structured literacy materials and assessment protocols now embedded in twelve schools' daily schedules would need to be replaced or reverted to prior practice mid-year, disrupting instruction for the 1,900 students currently enrolled and for the incoming kindergarten cohort who would otherwise have entered the program in August. Given that the first-grade subgroup gains are the most defensible evidence this office has produced in several years that a district intervention closes gaps rather than widens them, allowing the initiative to lapse for lack of a signed renewal form would be difficult to justify to the Board, to the state, or to the twelve school communities now seeing results.

# Executive Summary: Online Credit Recovery Program

**Office of Research and Accountability, Piedmont Vale School District**
**Prepared for Superintendent Imani Blackshear, CFO Petra Lindqvist, and the Board Finance Committee**
**September 9, 2025**

The online credit recovery program is not delivering outcomes that justify its current design, and the Office of Research and Accountability recommends the district restructure the model — pairing online coursework with in-person instructional support at every high school — rather than renew it as currently configured when the state credit recovery grant comes up for its scheduled review in the 2026-27 budget cycle. This program does not expire this month, but the evaluation findings are serious enough that we are raising them now, alongside the two initiatives whose grants do lapse September 30, so that any redesign can be built into next year's grant application rather than attempted as a mid-year correction.

The program enrolled 1,240 high school students at a cost of $890,000, intended to let students who had failed a course recover the credit through self-paced online instruction rather than repeating the class in person. Of the 1,240 students enrolled, 46 percent completed a course. Of those completers, 31 percent passed the end-of-course exam required to receive credit in the tested subjects. The comparable pass rate for students who repeated the same courses in a traditional in-person format was 68 percent — more than double. This gap is the central finding of this evaluation, and it holds after the office controlled for the fact that students routed to online recovery tend to have weaker academic records than students who repeat courses in person; even after that adjustment, the in-person pass rate advantage remained above 30 points.

The completion rate is itself a warning sign independent of the exam results. More than half of enrolled students — 54 percent — did not finish the course at all, meaning they neither recovered the credit nor received meaningful instructional support during the term they were enrolled. For a program built specifically to serve students who are behind on credits and at risk of not graduating on time, a majority non-completion rate represents a missed intervention window at a moment when these students have the least room to lose additional time. The office was not able to isolate a single cause for the completion gap from the data available this cycle — course design, the absence of a live instructor, and inconsistent access to devices and connectivity outside school hours all appear to contribute — but the magnitude of the gap, more than the specific cause, is what should drive the district's response.

Before the current grant cycle closes, the district should take two steps. First, the Office of Research and Accountability should conduct a focused follow-up study this fall isolating whether the completion and pass-rate gaps are concentrated in particular subjects, schools, or student populations, since the current data supports a program-wide finding but not yet a targeted fix. Second, and more urgently, the district should not expand enrollment in the online-only format for the current school year while that follow-up is underway; principals should be notified that credit recovery placements for the fall term should favor in-person or blended sections wherever seat capacity allows, pending the redesign.

If the program is discontinued outright rather than redesigned, the immediate effect on staff is minimal — the program is contracted through a third-party online platform with no dedicated district instructional staff, so there is no reassignment or layoff question tied to this initiative specifically. The effect on students is the more serious concern. Roughly 1,240 students a year currently have access to some credit recovery pathway, however imperfect; eliminating the option without a replacement in place would leave a meaningful share of at-risk students with in-person repetition as the only route back to on-time credit accumulation, and high school master schedules in several buildings do not currently have the seat capacity to absorb that volume of repeat enrollments without displacing other course offerings. The office's recommendation is therefore not simply to end the program, but to use the year before the next grant cycle to convert it into a blended model that keeps the flexibility online recovery offers while closing the instructional gap that is currently producing a 68-versus-31 outcome disparity.

# Executive Summary: Community School Wraparound Model

**Office of Research and Accountability, Piedmont Vale School District**
**Prepared for Superintendent Imani Blackshear, CFO Petra Lindqvist, and the Board Finance Committee**
**September 9, 2025**

The community school wraparound model has produced a genuine result at one of its three sites and no measurable result at the other two, and the Office of Research and Accountability recommends the district use the three weeks before this grant expires on September 30, 2025 to submit a renewal application scoped to Redbud Elementary alone, rather than renewing all three sites at current funding levels or allowing the entire initiative to lapse.

The program placed wraparound service coordinators, family liaisons, and expanded health and counseling access at three school sites at a combined cost of $1.6 million. At Redbud Elementary, chronic absenteeism fell six percentage points over the grant period, a result consistent with what the research base on community school models predicts when implementation is stable and family engagement infrastructure is fully staffed. At the other two sites, chronic absenteeism was flat — no measurable improvement in either direction. The office examined implementation records at all three sites to understand the divergence, and one variable stands out clearly: staff turnover. Redbud maintained continuity in its wraparound coordinator and family liaison positions for the full grant period. At one of the two flat sites, staff turnover ran 44 percent over the same period, meaning coordinator and liaison roles were vacant or newly filled for substantial stretches of the year. Relationship-dependent interventions — and wraparound services are fundamentally relationship-dependent, built on trust between families and a consistent point of contact at the school — do not function the same way when the person in that role changes repeatedly. The office does not have sufficient data to fully explain the second flat site's outcome, and that site warrants its own closer look before any funding decision is made for it specifically.

This finding changes what the district should ask for in a renewal, and the timing is tight. Submitting a three-site renewal identical to the current design would ask the state to fund two sites that have not yet shown the outcome the grant is meant to produce, using data the state reviewer will also have access to. Submitting a Redbud-only renewal, with a clear narrative tied to staffing continuity, is both a more defensible application and a more honest one. For the two flat sites, the office recommends the district use general fund dollars, not grant renewal, to stabilize the coordinator and liaison positions for one additional year — specifically by reviewing compensation and workload for those roles, since exit interview data from the departing staff at the high-turnover site cited caseload size and lack of role clarity, not compensation alone, as primary reasons for leaving. That is a fixable staffing problem, and one year of stability data would tell the district whether the model works at those sites once implementation matches Redbud's.

The action required before September 30 is straightforward but has a hard deadline: the renewal application, scoped to Redbud, needs Board authorization this week to meet the state's submission window, and a parallel decision is needed on general fund support for the other two sites' staffing so that families and staff at all three schools are not left without an answer about the coming year until after the deadline has already passed.

If no renewal is submitted and the entire program lapses, the consequences are immediate and concentrated on the sites' most vulnerable families. Wraparound coordinators, family liaisons, and the expanded counseling access at all three schools would end with the grant term, regardless of whether the site showed results. At Redbud specifically, ending a program that produced a six-point drop in chronic absenteeism — a meaningful gain in a district metric the Board tracks closely — without attempting to preserve it would reverse the district's own strongest piece of evidence that the model works when staffed consistently. At the two flat sites, ending the program removes a service families have come to rely on regardless of whether the absenteeism data yet shows a district-level effect, and staff at those sites, several of whom have been in role less than a year, would be released or reassigned before the model was ever given a stable year to work as designed.

# Executive Summary: One-to-One Device Refresh

**Office of Research and Accountability, Piedmont Vale School District**
**Prepared for Superintendent Imani Blackshear, CFO Petra Lindqvist, and the Board Finance Committee**
**September 9, 2025**

The one-to-one device refresh has overrun its approved budget by $2.5 million, finished deployment seven months behind schedule, and is losing devices to breakage at a rate the district cannot sustain into a second year, and the Office of Research and Accountability recommends the Board direct Grants Manager Chandra Pillai to produce a corrective spending and procurement plan before any further disbursement is authorized, rather than treating the overage as a one-time cost to be absorbed and closed out.

The initiative was approved at $5.9 million, funded initially through a combination of federal pandemic relief dollars and district capital funds, with the expectation that the federal share would cover the bulk of device purchasing and the district would cover deployment and support staffing. Actual spending to date is $8.4 million — a $2.5 million overage, or roughly 42 percent above the approved figure. Two factors account for most of the gap. First, the federal relief funding that was expected to cover a substantial share of device costs expired before the full purchase order was executed, shifting a portion of the purchase onto the district's general fund earlier and at higher volume than budgeted. Second, device breakage in year one ran at 19 percent of the deployed fleet — nearly one in five devices requiring repair or replacement within twelve months of distribution — which the original budget did not anticipate at this scale and which has driven a second wave of purchasing to keep students equipped.

The seven-month deployment delay compounds both problems. Devices ordered later, after the funding shift, were purchased at a point when unit pricing had risen relative to the original procurement quotes the $5.9 million budget was built on, and the delay meant a portion of the fleet was still being distributed well into the school year it was meant to serve, reducing the return the district got on the year's spending. The office's review found no evidence of mismanagement in the ordering or distribution process itself; the delay traces primarily to a supplier fulfillment problem in the first two quarters of the program, not to district decision-making. The overage, however, is a budget and oversight matter regardless of its cause, and it is now large enough that the Board Finance Committee should have direct visibility into remaining spend before any additional funds are committed.

Before this reaches a point where further spending is locked in, the district should take two concrete steps. First, Grants Manager Pillai should present a itemized accounting of the $8.4 million against the original $5.9 million approval, broken out by device purchase, repair and replacement, and deployment labor, so the Committee can see precisely where the overage occurred rather than reviewing it as a single aggregate figure. Second, before any device is purchased to address further breakage, the district should evaluate whether the 19 percent breakage rate reflects a device durability problem, a student care and handling problem, or a distribution problem — such as devices issued without adequate cases or without a clear repair-reporting process — because the fix, and its cost, differs substantially depending on the cause, and the district cannot afford a second year of 19 percent losses without knowing why the first year produced them.

This initiative is a capital and procurement program rather than a service delivered by grant-funded staff, so the consequence of slowing or pausing further spending falls differently than it does for the district's other three initiatives. No teaching or coordinator positions are tied to this budget line, and pausing additional device purchases while the corrective plan is developed would not eliminate any staff role. The consequence instead falls on students directly: with 19 percent of the current fleet already needing repair or replacement, any pause in purchasing risks leaving a meaningful share of the district's one-to-one device commitment unmet for students who depend on that device for daily coursework, and the seven-month deployment delay this year means some schools are still catching up on distribution from the current cycle even as the district considers what to do next. The recommendation here is not to halt the program, but to stop treating the overage as routine before a second year of unexplained breakage produces a third consecutive year of budget overruns.

The four evaluations above point to different problems and different remedies, but two of the four grant terms close on the same date, and the Board Finance Committee's agenda for September 9 should be sequenced accordingly. The following reflects the order in which decisions need to be made, not the order in which the programs appear above.

**Immediate — requires Board action this week:**

- **K-2 Structured Literacy renewal application.** Drafted and ready. Requires Superintendent's signature and Committee endorsement to meet the state's September 30 submission window. Delay past this week risks missing the window entirely, since the state requires a minimum ten-business-day internal routing period before submission.
- **Community School Wraparound renewal, scoped to Redbud only.** Requires Board authorization to submit a single-site application rather than the original three-site design. This is a change in scope from the initial grant, not a straightforward renewal, and the office recommends the Committee explicitly vote on the narrowed scope rather than treat it as an administrative continuation.
- **General fund staffing decision for the two flat-outcome community school sites.** This is a separate line from the grant renewal and does not have the September 30 deadline, but families and staff at both sites should not go into October without an answer, and the compensation and caseload review Chandra Pillai's office would need to conduct takes several weeks to complete properly.

**Near-term — no hard deadline this month, but action should begin now:**

- **Credit Recovery program redesign.** No grant expiration until the 2026-27 cycle, but the fall semester course placement guidance — directing principals toward in-person and blended sections where seat capacity exists — should go out before schedules finalize for the new term, which in practice means before the end of September regardless of the grant calendar.
- **Device Refresh itemized accounting.** No grant deadline drives this item; the urgency is budgetary. The Committee should set a date, no later than the October meeting, for Grants Manager Pillai to present the breakout of the $8.4 million against the original $5.9 million approval, with the breakage-cause analysis attached, before authorizing any further device purchasing.

**What does not require Board action this cycle:**

The office is not recommending any changes to the K-2 literacy program's instructional design, staffing model, or school count this year; the renewal request is a straight continuation at current scale, with expansion planning deferred to next year pending a full year of second-grade outcome data. Similarly, no immediate staffing action is required for the credit recovery program, since it carries no dedicated district positions.

Two of these five action items carry the September 30 deadline; the remaining three do not, but each has its own cost to delay — a missed staffing stabilization window for community schools, a semester of continued in-person underenrollment for credit recovery, and a second wave of unexamined device spending. The Office of Research and Accountability will return to the Committee with updated findings on all four initiatives at the close of the 2025-26 fall semester, ahead of the district's spring budget development cycle.

# Appendix: Methodology and Data Sources

The findings above draw on four separate evaluation designs, each built to match the data available for that program and the questions the Board and Superintendent's office raised when each initiative was funded. This appendix documents how each figure in the preceding summaries was produced, so that the Committee and any external reviewer can trace a given number back to its source.

**K-2 Structured Literacy.** Benchmark data comes from the district's universal reading screener, administered three times annually to all K-2 students districtwide, not a sample. The 41-to-58 percent figure reflects the twelve pilot schools only, compared against their own fall baseline in the first year of the grant and their spring outcome in the second. Subgroup gains were calculated using the same screener, disaggregated by the four categories the office tracks under the district's existing equity monitoring framework — special education status, English learner status, free/reduced-price meal eligibility, and kindergarten entry risk classification. The office did not construct a matched comparison group of non-pilot schools for this cycle; that comparison is planned for next year's evaluation once second-grade coaching capacity issues are resolved and outcomes can be attributed with more confidence to the model itself rather than to coaching inconsistency at two schools.

**Online Credit Recovery.** Completion and pass-rate figures come from the online platform vendor's enrollment and grade-passback data, matched against the district's student information system to confirm enrollment eligibility and exam results. The 68 percent in-person comparison figure is drawn from the same end-of-course exams, administered to students repeating the identical courses in traditional classroom sections during the same academic terms. The office's adjustment for prior academic record used each student's GPA and course failure history at the time of referral to either pathway; the adjusted gap narrowed from roughly 37 points to approximately 31 points but did not close. This office did not have access to platform-level engagement data — login frequency, time on task, or module-level drop-off points — which limited the office's ability to isolate where in the course sequence students disengage. That data has been requested from the vendor for the follow-up study recommended above.

**Community School Wraparound.** Chronic absenteeism figures are drawn from the state's official chronic absenteeism definition — students missing 10 percent or more of enrolled school days — calculated for each site using the same enrollment period both years. Staffing turnover figures come from the district's human resources system, calculated as the percentage of grant-funded positions at each site that experienced at least one separation during the grant period, annualized. Exit interview themes cited above were drawn from HR's standard separation questionnaire, completed by five of the seven staff who departed the high-turnover site; two departing staff did not complete an exit interview, and their reasons for leaving are not reflected in the thematic summary.

**One-to-One Device Refresh.** Spending figures were reconciled against the district's finance system by Grants Manager Pillai's office and cross-checked by the Office of Research and Accountability against purchase orders and vendor invoices; the $8.4 million figure is confirmed spend as of the close of the 2024-25 fiscal year, not a projection. The 19 percent breakage figure reflects devices flagged in the district's asset management system as requiring repair, replacement, or write-off within twelve months of initial distribution to a student, and does not include devices lost or stolen, which are tracked separately and were not part of this evaluation's scope. The seven-month deployment delay is measured from the original distribution timeline submitted in the grant's implementation plan against the date each school received its full device allotment; three schools remain outside full deployment as of this writing.

Data requests, underlying files, and disaggregated tables for all four evaluations are available on request to Committee members through the Office of Research and Accountability. Director Ferrer-Guzman's office will retain the underlying datasets for the standard five-year retention period required under the district's data governance policy.

# Limitations and Disclosures

Several limitations apply across this evaluation cycle and should inform how the Committee weighs the findings above.

The office's capacity this cycle did not allow for classroom observation at any of the four program sites' full footprint. Site visits were conducted at eight of the twelve literacy schools, one of the three community school sites, and none of the credit recovery or device refresh locations, the latter two being evaluated entirely through administrative and vendor data. This means the literacy and community school findings carry a qualitative layer the other two summaries do not, and the office cautions against reading the credit recovery and device refresh findings as having received the same depth of on-site verification.

None of the four evaluations in this cycle included a formal cost-effectiveness comparison against alternative uses of the same funds — the office was asked to evaluate whether each program achieved its stated goals at its stated cost, not whether a different intervention at the same price point might have produced a larger effect. The Board has not asked for that comparison in prior cycles, but the office notes, particularly given the credit recovery findings, that this may be a useful question for the Committee to pose explicitly in next year's evaluation scope if it wants the office to address it.

The office also discloses a data completeness gap in the device refresh evaluation: three schools remain outside full deployment as noted in the appendix, meaning the 19 percent breakage figure is calculated against a fleet that was not, at the time of this report, fully distributed. That figure may move once the remaining schools receive their full allotment and those devices accumulate a comparable period of use. The office will revisit this figure at the fall semester close rather than treat it as final.

Finally, this cycle's evaluations relied on data sharing agreements with two external vendors — the online credit recovery platform and, for device asset tracking, the district's managed device services contractor. Both agreements are up for renewal within the coming year, and the office flags for the Committee that continued access to vendor-level data at the granularity used in this report is not guaranteed absent renewal terms that explicitly preserve the district's evaluation access, a provision the office recommends Chandra Pillai's office confirm is included in any renegotiated contract language.
