# Speed Management Evidence Review: Automated Enforcement, Road Diets, Speed Limit Changes, and Traffic Calming

**Prepared for the Verde Springs City Council**
**Office of Performance and Budget**

*Anand Sundaresan, Principal Management Analyst*
*Keiko Villalpando, Transportation Planner*

*July 30, 2026*

---

## 1. Introduction

In 2025, nineteen people died in traffic crashes on Verde Springs streets, up from nine in 2021. Fourteen of the nineteen were pedestrians, and their deaths concentrated on five arterial corridors that carry the bulk of the city's traffic volume and the bulk of its posted-speed violations. The council has appropriated $6.5 million for speed management and directed staff to prepare an automated enforcement request for proposals. Before that request goes out, the council asked this office to answer a narrower and harder question: what does the research evidence actually say about the tools available to reduce speed-related death and injury on urban arterials, and what does it say about a city the size of Verde Springs — 118,000 residents, a five-corridor crash concentration, and a police department that has told us directly it cannot spare officers for traffic detail?

This review does not recommend a specific intervention. It lays out what 88 studies, spanning three decades and four intervention types, show and do not show, and it is explicit about where the studies disagree and why. The council will need that disagreement in view before it acts, because the disagreement is not noise — it tracks specific, identifiable differences in study design, road context, and time horizon, and those differences matter for what Verde Springs can expect on its own arterials.

Four bodies of literature bear on the council's decision:

1. **Automated enforcement** — fixed and mobile speed and red-light cameras, and the crash, injury, and behavioral effects attributed to them (26 studies).
2. **Road diets** — lane reduction and reallocation on multilane arterials, most commonly four-to-three conversions with a center turn lane (22 studies).
3. **Speed limit changes** — reductions in posted limits absent other engineering change (17 studies).
4. **Traffic calming** — physical measures such as speed humps, chicanes, curb extensions, and raised crossings, typically on lower-volume streets (12 studies).

Eleven remaining studies are methodological or cross-cutting and are cited within the relevant thematic sections rather than as a fifth category.

The council's five arterial corridors are multilane, signal-controlled roads carrying mixed pedestrian and vehicle volumes — not the low-volume residential streets where much of the traffic-calming literature was generated, and not uniformly the six-lane suburban arterials where several road-diet studies produced disappointing results. Matching the evidence to that specific road type, rather than to traffic safety research in general, is the organizing task of this review.

## 2. How the Literature Was Searched

Sundaresan searched Scopus, PubMed, and the compiled state transportation agency report series for the period 1995 through 2026, using combinations of terms for each intervention (e.g., "automated speed enforcement," "speed camera," "road diet," "lane reduction," "posted speed limit," "traffic calming," "speed hump") crossed with outcome terms ("crash," "injury," "fatality," "pedestrian," "speed compliance"). The search returned 1,684 unique records after deduplication. Titles and abstracts were screened against three inclusion criteria: the study evaluated one of the four interventions above; it reported a crash, injury, fatality, or measured-speed outcome (citation counts and revenue figures alone were excluded); and it was conducted on a public roadway, not a test track or simulation.

Two hundred ninety-one records passed screening and were read in full. Eighty-eight met inclusion for data extraction: 26 automated enforcement evaluations, 22 road diet studies, 17 speed limit change studies, and 12 traffic calming studies, plus 11 methodological or cross-cutting papers used to interpret the rest. For each included study, the extraction sheet records the study design (before-after, before-after with comparison group, before-after with empirical Bayes correction, cross-sectional, or quasi-experimental with a discontinuity or synthetic control), the road type and posted speed context, the outcome measures and their denominators, the follow-up duration, and the reported effect size with any available confidence interval. That sheet is available to council staff on request; this review reports its findings by theme.

Two limitations in the search bear mention. First, coverage of state transportation agency reports is uneven before 2005, when agencies began archiving evaluation reports systematically; the earliest automated enforcement studies in this review are drawn primarily from published literature rather than gray literature. Second, studies published only in languages other than English were excluded, which likely undercounts the substantial European camera enforcement literature, particularly from the Netherlands and the Nordic countries. Where our synthesis draws on that literature secondhand — through systematic reviews that included it — this is noted.

## 3. Synthesis by Theme

### 3.1 Automated Enforcement

The automated enforcement literature is the largest body in this review and also the most internally contradictory, and the contradiction is not evenly distributed — it tracks study design almost exactly.

**Before-and-after studies without a comparison group** report large effects. Retting and Kyrychenko (2002), evaluating a red-light camera program, found reductions in injury crashes at treated intersections on the order of 25 to 30 percent. Chen, Meckle, and Cooper (2000) reported comparable reductions from a mobile speed camera deployment. Hess and Polak (2003) found injury crash reductions near 40 percent at fixed camera sites over a two-year window. Several studies in this design tradition — including Mountain, Hirst, and Maher (2005) and Pilkington and Kinra (2005), a systematic review synthesizing earlier before-after evaluations — report pooled effects in the 20 to 50 percent range for injury crashes at or near camera sites.

**Studies that add a comparison group and empirical Bayes correction for regression to the mean report substantially smaller effects.** The distinction matters because camera sites are rarely chosen at random — they are chosen because they had unusually high crash counts in a prior period, and crash counts at high-crash sites tend to fall somewhat on their own in the following period regardless of intervention, a statistical artifact known as regression to the mean. Studies that do not correct for this artifact will overstate camera effects by construction. Elvik (2008), in a meta-analysis restricted to studies using empirical Bayes methods, found injury crash reductions closer to 10 to 15 percent at camera sites — real, but well under half the effect reported by uncorrected before-after designs. Høye (2013) reached a similar range in a Norwegian meta-analysis using comparison-site correction. Novoa, Pérez, and Borrell (2010), reviewing camera evaluations for methodological quality, found that studies without comparison groups reported effects roughly two to three times larger than studies with them, and flagged regression to the mean as the primary mechanism.

**Several studies find that crashes do not disappear but relocate to adjacent, uncontrolled corridors.** This is the most policy-relevant point of disagreement for Verde Springs, because the city's five target corridors are not isolated — they connect to a broader arterial grid that would remain unmonitored under the current $6.5 million allocation, which the police chief has indicated will cover camera installation at a subset of high-crash locations rather than the full corridor network. Gains and Nordstrom (2008) documented a partial migration effect, in which crash reductions at camera sites were offset by increases within a half-mile radius on uncontrolled parallel routes. Chen and Warrington (2011) found a similar spillover pattern using a corridor-level rather than intersection-level unit of analysis, and argued that intersection-only evaluations systematically overstate net safety benefit because they do not measure the corridor as a whole. Not every study finds migration — De Pauw, Daniels, and Brijs (2014) found no measurable spillover in a Belgian evaluation using a wide-radius control zone — but the studies that look for migration specifically, rather than assuming it away by measuring only the treated site, are more likely to find it.

**Rear-end collisions rise in some camera studies and not others, and the split tracks camera type.** Red-light cameras are associated with increased rear-end crashes in several studies — drivers braking abruptly to avoid a violation appear to be struck from behind — including Retting and Kyrychenko (2002) and Council, Persaud, Eccles, Lyon, and Griffith (2005), both of which found rear-end increases even as right-angle crashes fell, producing an ambiguous net injury effect. Speed cameras, by contrast, generally do not show this pattern; Goldenbeld and van Schagen (2005) and Høye (2013) found no rear-end increase associated with fixed speed cameras, consistent with the different braking dynamics of speed enforcement versus red-light enforcement. This distinction is directly relevant to Verde Springs' arterial corridors, where pedestrian deaths rather than intersection right-angle crashes are the stated target: a speed camera program aimed at midblock and approach speeds carries a different rear-end risk profile than a red-light camera program aimed at intersection violations, and the RFP should specify which the department intends to deploy, because the literature does not support treating them as equivalent.

**Duration of effect is a further point of disagreement, though it is really a point of absence.** Most camera evaluations report outcomes at one or two years post-installation. Only a handful in this review tracked effects past three years, and those that did report some erosion — Høye (2013) found the injury reduction at Norwegian camera sites attenuated somewhat after the third year, plausibly as driver familiarity with camera locations changed compliance behavior at the margin (drivers slow only at the camera and resume speed immediately after). No study in this review tracked effects past five years. Verde Springs is being asked to make a capital commitment against a evidence base that speaks confidently to years one and two and speaks much less confidently to year five.

**Equity findings split on denominator choice**, discussed separately in Section 4 below because the disagreement is not specific to automated enforcement but recurs across intervention types.

Taken together, the automated enforcement literature supports a real but moderate injury crash reduction at camera sites — likely in the 10 to 20 percent range once regression to the mean is accounted for — with meaningful uncertainty about corridor-wide net effect, a rear-end tradeoff that depends on camera type, and an evidence base that thins sharply after three years.

### 3.2 Road Diets

Road diet studies — typically the conversion of a four-lane undivided road to three lanes with a center turn lane, sometimes with added bike lanes — show a pattern common in transportation research: strong, consistent results from a narrow range of contexts that do not reliably generalize to a wider one.

**The strongest and most consistent results come from smaller cities with lower baseline traffic volumes**, largely because the foundational studies in this literature were conducted there. Huang, Stewart, and Zegeer (2002), evaluating road diets across several small and mid-sized Iowa and Washington cities, found crash reductions in the range of 19 to 47 percent depending on site, with the largest reductions at sites carrying under 12,000 vehicles per day. Knapp and Giese (2001), studying a set of Iowa road diet conversions specifically, reported similarly favorable results and became something of an anchor citation for the broader field — cited in nearly every subsequent road diet evaluation in this review's set, including several that go on to complicate its conclusions. Pawlovich, Li, Carriquiry, and Welch (2006), also working with Iowa data, used a more rigorous Bayesian before-after method and confirmed a reduction, though smaller than the raw before-after figures had suggested — a methodological pattern that echoes the automated enforcement literature's regression-to-the-mean problem.

**These results do not replicate cleanly on higher-volume, multilane arterials**, which is precisely the road type carrying Verde Springs' five target corridors. Harkey, Srinivasan, Baek, Council, Eccles, Lefler, Gross, Persaud, Lyon, and Hauer (2008), in an FHWA-commissioned study drawing on a wider and higher-volume sample than the Iowa-anchored literature, found that road diet effectiveness declined as pre-conversion volume rose, with diminishing and in some cases statistically insignificant benefit above roughly 20,000 vehicles per day — a volume threshold several of Verde Springs' corridors already exceed or approach. Stamatiadis, Pigman, Sacksteder, Ruff, and Lord (2009), evaluating road diets in Kentucky, found that four-to-three conversions on arterials carrying over 15,000 vehicles per day produced measurable increases in congestion-related queuing and, in two of six sites studied, a shift of some crashes from the corridor itself onto adjacent parallel streets — a migration effect structurally similar to what several automated enforcement studies found for cameras. Rahman, Ahmed, and Anowar (2019), reviewing road diet outcomes across a larger and more geographically diverse set of arterials than earlier reviews, concluded explicitly that volume and land-use context — not lane count alone — determined outcome, and that six-lane-to-four-lane or four-lane-to-three-lane conversions on commercial arterials with heavy turning movement did not reproduce the results of the low-volume residential and small-city studies that dominate the earlier literature.

**Where studies disagree most sharply is on whether pedestrian outcomes specifically improve, as opposed to overall vehicle crash counts.** Several studies measure only total crashes or injury crashes without disaggregating pedestrian involvement, which matters directly for Verde Springs given that fourteen of nineteen 2025 deaths were pedestrians. Among the studies in this review that did disaggregate, Chen, Chen, and Ewing (2013) found a pedestrian-specific injury reduction associated with road diet conversion in a multi-city study, attributing the effect primarily to reduced crossing exposure (fewer lanes to cross) and reduced vehicle speed from the loss of a passing lane. But this finding is not universal: Pawlovich et al. (2006) found the pedestrian-specific effect smaller and less statistically stable than the vehicle-crash effect in the same Iowa dataset that produced strong overall results, and the FHWA study (Harkey et al., 2008) noted that pedestrian crash counts at many sites were too low, even after conversion, to support a statistically confident before-after comparison — a low base-rate problem that recurs throughout the pedestrian safety literature generally and that the council should expect to encounter in any post-implementation evaluation of its own.

**A design factor cutting across the road diet literature, and worth flagging directly for the RFP process, is that road diets are typically bundled with other treatments** — median refuges, bike lane buffers, signal timing changes — making it difficult in many studies to attribute effect to the lane reduction specifically rather than the package. Rahman et al. (2019) treat this as a limitation of the literature as a whole rather than of any single study; the practical implication is that a "road diet" is not one intervention but a family of them, and comparing effect sizes across studies that used different packages likely overstates the internal consistency of the literature.

For Verde Springs specifically: the road diet evidence is genuinely strong for arterials resembling the small-city, moderate-volume context of the anchor studies, and genuinely weaker — in some cases actively unfavorable on congestion grounds — for arterials resembling the higher-volume, six-lane commercial corridors that dominate several of the city's crash-concentration list. Corridor-by-corridor volume data, not a single citywide policy, should determine whether a road diet is proposed for any given segment.

### 3.3 Speed Limit Changes

The evidence on posted speed limit reduction, absent any change to the physical roadway, is the most internally consistent of the four bodies reviewed here, and the consistent finding is a discouraging one for anyone hoping posted signage alone will meaningfully slow traffic.

Parker (1997), in an early and frequently cited FHWA study, found that posted speed limit reductions without engineering changes produced measured mean speed reductions of approximately 1 to 1.5 miles per hour, with the 85th-percentile speed — the speed below which 85 percent of drivers travel, commonly used as an indicator of prevailing traffic behavior — barely moving. Subsequent studies largely confirmed this range. Gates, Qin, and Noyce (2011), studying speed limit changes on rural and urban roads across several states, found measured speed reductions clustering between 1 and 3 miles per hour, and noted that the reduction shrank further at sites where the new posted limit was more than 5 miles per hour below the previously observed 85th-percentile speed — that is, the larger the gap the city tries to legislate, the less compliance it gets. Hu and Cicchino (2020), examining speed limit reductions in several U.S. cities as part of Vision Zero-style initiatives, found measured speed effects in the same narrow band and a corresponding fatal and severe injury crash reduction that was statistically significant but modest, on the order of low single-digit percentages citywide.

There is less disagreement within this literature than within the other three, largely because the underlying behavioral mechanism is well understood and consistently observed: drivers set speed primarily by road geometry, sightlines, and traffic context, and a change in a number on a sign, without a change in what the road itself communicates to the driver, produces a small effect that erodes further the larger the requested change and the less it is reinforced by enforcement or engineering. Where studies do disagree is on whether even this small effect is worth the political and administrative cost of a citywide or corridor-wide limit change; Hu and Cicchino (2020) argue the citywide crash reduction, while small in percentage terms, is not small in absolute lives saved at scale, while Gates et al. (2011) are more skeptical, noting that limit reductions in their sample were sometimes followed by driver noncompliance rates high enough to raise questions about enforceability and about whether the new limit simply became a number that most drivers ignored.

The clear implication for Verde Springs is that a posted speed limit reduction on the five arterial corridors, treated as a stand-alone measure, should not be expected to produce a meaningful independent safety effect. It appears in this literature almost exclusively as a component of combined interventions — paired with road diets, traffic calming, or automated enforcement — rather than as an intervention that stands on its own.

### 3.4 Traffic Calming

The traffic calming literature — physical measures such as speed humps, raised crosswalks, curb extensions, chicanes, and median islands — shows large and fairly consistent local speed and crash effects, with the major caveat that nearly all of it was generated on lower-volume residential and collector streets rather than on the signal-controlled, higher-volume arterials at the center of the council's concern.

Ewing (1999), in a widely cited synthesis of early traffic calming evaluations, reported speed reductions at speed hump locations in the range of 5 to 10 miles per hour and crash reductions in the range of 30 to 50 percent at treated sites, drawn primarily from residential street applications. Bunn, Collier, Frost, Ker, Roberts, and Wentz (2003), in a Cochrane systematic review of area-wide traffic calming schemes, found consistent injury and fatality reductions across a pooled set of mostly European studies, with effects concentrated in schemes that combined multiple physical measures (a "calmed area" rather than a single device) rather than isolated installations. Elvik (2001) similarly found stronger effects for combined area-wide schemes than for single-point treatments, a finding echoed later by Grundy, Steinbach, Edwards, Green, Armstrong, and Wilkinson (2009), whose evaluation of London's 20 mph zones — which combine posted limit reduction with physical calming — found substantial reductions in casualties, including notably among children, though the authors caution that the 20 mph zones studied were disproportionately implemented in already lower-volume residential areas, raising the same generalizability question that recurs throughout this review.

**Traffic calming's application to higher-volume signalized arterials — the road type most relevant to Verde Springs' five corridors — is comparatively thin.** Most of the twelve traffic calming studies in this review's set evaluate residential or collector streets carrying under 8,000 vehicles per day; only three evaluate anything resembling a signalized arterial, and those three (Huang & Cynecki, 2001; Gan, Shen, & Rodriguez, 2005; and Hallmark, Hawkins, and Smadi, 2013) study specific point treatments — raised crosswalks and curb extensions at individual crossings — rather than area-wide calming, and all three note that speed humps and similar vertical deflection devices are generally unsuitable for arterials carrying transit or emergency response traffic at the volumes Verde Springs' corridors carry, for both operational and liability reasons. This is a genuine gap rather than a disagreement: the literature does not so much conflict on arterial-scale traffic calming as fail to address it in depth.

Where the traffic calming literature does show internal disagreement is on **durability and driver adaptation**. Ewing (1999) and later Bunn et al. (2003) both flag that some speed reduction effects at individual devices erode over one to two years as drivers habituate to the specific hump or island location, though area-wide schemes appear more durable than point treatments, plausibly because there is no single location for drivers to learn to compensate around.

### 3.5 Cross-Cutting Design Differences Behind the Conflicting Findings

Three design features explain most of the disagreement documented above, and they recur across all four intervention types rather than being specific to any one of them.

**First, before-and-after designs without a comparison group systematically overstate effect size**, because they cannot distinguish intervention effect from regression to the mean at sites selected precisely because they had unusually high crash counts. This pattern appears in automated enforcement (Section 3.1), road diets (Section 3.2), and to a lesser extent traffic calming (Section 3.4), wherever early, uncorrected evaluations are compared against later studies using comparison sites or empirical Bayes methods.

**Second, effect sizes from lower-volume, smaller-city, or residential contexts do not transfer reliably to higher-volume signalized arterials.** This is the single most consequential pattern for Verde Springs' decision, because the city's problem is concentrated specifically on five arterial corridors — the exact road type most underrepresented in the strongest parts of the road diet and traffic calming literatures, and a road type whose camera-specific migration and rear-end dynamics differ from isolated-intersection studies.

**Third, follow-up duration is short across nearly the entire evidence base.** The great majority of studies in all four categories report outcomes at one to three years. Effects that erode with driver habituation — documented for individual traffic calming devices and suggested for camera sites — are correspondingly underdocumented, because most studies end their observation window before that erosion would be expected to show up.

## 4. Gaps

Five gaps recur across this literature and bear directly on what the council can expect to know, and not know, after it acts.

**Corridor-scale evaluation is rare.** Most automated enforcement and traffic calming studies evaluate a point (an intersection, a device location) rather than a corridor. Verde Springs' problem is defined corridor by corridor, and the literature's dominant unit of analysis does not match the city's unit of decision. The migration findings in Section 3.1 and 3.2 exist specifically because a handful of studies departed from point-level analysis; most did not.

**Follow-up beyond three years is essentially absent.** Every intervention type in this review shows the same pattern: confident results at one to two years, thinner and more mixed results at three years, and almost nothing beyond that. The council is being asked to make a capital commitment whose expected lifespan is measured in years, against an evidence base that speaks with much less confidence about year four onward than about year one.

**Equity analysis is present but methodologically unsettled.** Several studies in the automated enforcement literature examine who is cited and who is struck, and they reach different conclusions depending on the denominator used — citations per resident of an area, citations per vehicle trip through an area, or citations relative to a baseline violation rate observed independently of enforcement. Studies using population-based denominators are more likely to find disparate citation impact on lower-income areas; studies using violation-rate denominators are more likely to find citation rates roughly proportionate to underlying speeding behavior. This review did not attempt to adjudicate which denominator is correct, because the studies themselves do not agree, and the choice appears to depend partly on data availability rather than a settled methodological consensus. The council should expect this same question to arise locally, and should decide in advance, before camera locations are finalized, which denominator it intends to use in any before-after equity evaluation of the Verde Springs deployment specifically — not after critics on either side of Councilmember Prosser's revenue-device concern have already framed the question.

**Bundled interventions make attribution difficult.** As noted in Section 3.2, road diets in particular are rarely deployed alone, and the same is true of some traffic calming schemes and the London 20 mph zones discussed in Section 3.4. Where the literature evaluates a package, isolating which component drove the result is often not possible from the published data, which limits how precisely Verde Springs can predict the effect of any single element of a combined program.

**Higher-volume signalized arterial contexts are underrepresented relative to residential streets and small-city grids.** This is the gap most specific to Verde Springs' actual decision. The five corridors under review are not the low-volume residential streets where traffic calming has been most thoroughly studied, nor the small-city moderate-volume arterials where road diets have shown their strongest results. They more closely resemble the higher-volume arterial contexts where Harkey et al. (2008), Stamatiadis et al. (2009), and Rahman et al. (2019) found diminished or mixed road diet effects, and where the traffic calming literature has the least to say at all.

## 5. Implications for Verde Springs

Four implications follow from the review above, offered here as evidence-based observations for council deliberation rather than as a staff recommendation, since the choice among interventions — and any combination of them — is a policy decision resting on considerations beyond what the literature alone can settle.

First, automated enforcement's evidence-supported effect is real but should be represented to the council and the public as a moderate reduction — likely in the range documented by comparison-group and empirical-Bayes-corrected studies, roughly 10 to 20 percent for injury crashes at treated sites — not the 20 to 50 percent range that appears in uncorrected before-after evaluations and that will likely surface in public comment and vendor marketing during the RFP process. Overstating the expected effect risks a credibility cost when three-year outcomes are reported back to the council, particularly given Councilmember Prosser's stated skepticism.

Second, if automated enforcement proceeds, the corridor-wide migration risk documented in Section 3.1 argues for evaluation design, camera type selection (speed rather than red-light cameras, given the differing rear-end crash profile), and possibly deployment geography that account for adjacent, uncontrolled routes — not just the treated intersections — from the start, since retrofitting that evaluation design after the fact will be far harder than building it in now.

Third, the road diet and speed limit literatures both point toward corridor-specific analysis rather than a uniform citywide policy: road diets show their strongest evidence base on lower-volume arterials and their weakest on the higher-volume, six-lane segments that likely include some of Verde Springs' five target corridors, while posted speed limit reduction alone — unaccompanied by engineering change — should not be expected to produce a meaningful independent effect on any corridor.

Fourth, given the near-total absence of evidence beyond three years across every intervention type reviewed here, any program the council funds should include a built-in multi-year evaluation commitment, not a single point-in-time before-after report, and that evaluation should be resourced and specified before the $6.5 million is obligated to enforcement infrastructure or road construction, since evaluation capacity — unlike camera hardware — is easy to omit from a budget and hard to add back in later.
