# Proposal to Establish a District-Operated Transportation Department

**Tule Bend Unified School District**
**Presented to the Board of Trustees**

**Board Meeting: April 8, 2026**
**Prepared by:** Guadalupe Serrato, Superintendent; Nathan Okonkwo, Chief Business Official
**Agenda Classification:** Action Item

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The administration recommends that the Board of Trustees authorize termination of the district's contract with First Route Student Transportation at the close of the 2025–26 school year and approve the establishment of a district-operated transportation department, to be phased in over eighteen months beginning August 2026.

The recommendation responds to a sustained and worsening failure of contracted service. In the 2024–25 school year, First Route missed 1,140 scheduled runs. In January 2026 alone, the vendor logged 22 arrivals more than 45 minutes late. These failures fall on a student population that can least absorb them: 78 percent of our 9,760 students are socioeconomically disadvantaged, 31 percent are English learners, and district chronic absenteeism now stands at 34.1 percent. Transportation unreliability is not the sole cause of that absenteeism figure, but site-level data presented in Section 3 demonstrate that it is a measurable contributor, concentrated at the schools most dependent on bus service.

The proposal requires a one-time capital investment of $14.2 million — for 46 buses, the lease and retrofit of a corporation yard, and startup equipment — and an ongoing operating budget of $6.9 million per year, compared with the current contract cost of $7.6 million per year. Capital funding would come from three sources: $9.1 million from the capital facilities fund, $3.4 million from an anticipated state clean school bus allocation, and $1.7 million from unrestricted reserves, reducing the reserve to 4.2 percent against the state minimum of 3 percent.

Time is a governing constraint. If the Board does not act by May 15, 2026, the district must exercise a one-year contract extension at a 14 percent price increase, raising annual contract cost to approximately $8.7 million while leaving the underlying service failures unaddressed. The April 8 meeting is the last regular meeting at which the Board can take this action with adequate time for orderly implementation.

The administration has evaluated the alternatives — rebidding the contract, negotiating a joint powers arrangement with a neighboring district, and extending the current contract — and concluded that none resolves the district's core problem, which is the absence of direct operational control over a service on which student attendance depends. Those analyses, together with a candid treatment of the risks of district operation and the mitigations proposed for each, appear in Sections 8 and 9.

## 2. Background: How the District Arrived at This Decision Point

Tule Bend Unified has contracted out home-to-school transportation since 2014, when the district dissolved its previous in-house operation during a period of budget contraction. First Route Student Transportation has held the contract since 2019, following a competitive procurement in which it was the lowest responsive bidder. The current contract term ends June 30, 2026, with a single one-year extension option exercisable at the district's election, at a contractually fixed 14 percent rate increase, no later than May 15, 2026.

The district serves a geographically dispersed attendance area in Tulare County. Fourteen schools — nine elementary, three middle, two high — draw students from rural routes that in some cases exceed 40 minutes of drive time. Approximately 3,900 students, 40 percent of enrollment, are transported daily, including all students served under transportation-related provisions of individualized education programs. For a substantial share of transported families, there is no practical alternative to the bus: household vehicle access in our attendance area is limited, and walking distances and road conditions make self-transport infeasible for most rural students.

Enrollment has declined 11 percent over five years, from approximately 10,970 in 2020–21 to 9,760 today. Declining enrollment reduces revenue but does not proportionally reduce route mileage in a rural district; the same roads must be driven whether a bus carries 52 students or 44. This dynamic has strained the contract relationship. First Route has responded to its own labor shortages and cost pressures by consolidating routes, lengthening ride times, and — as the data below show — simply failing to run buses.

The administration has pursued contractual remedies. The district issued formal cure notices in October 2024, March 2025, and September 2025. Liquidated damages of $168,000 have been assessed and collected over the past two fiscal years. The vendor's corrective action plans have promised driver recruitment bonuses, backup coverage pools, and improved dispatch — and service has continued to deteriorate. The district's remedies under the contract compensate for failure after the fact; they do not put a bus at a stop on a January morning.

## 3. The Service Record and Its Effect on Attendance

### 3.1 Missed and Late Runs

The service record is documented through the district's own verification logs, cross-checked against vendor GPS data and site attendance office reports.

- **1,140 missed runs in 2024–25.** A missed run is a scheduled route segment that did not operate at all — no bus, no substitute coverage. Across a 180-day year, this averages 6.3 missed runs per school day. Missed runs were not evenly distributed: they concentrated on rural elementary routes and on Monday and Friday service, consistent with vendor driver-absence patterns.
- **22 arrivals more than 45 minutes late in January 2026 alone.** These are arrivals late enough that students missed all or most of a first-period class. January's figure was the worst single month on record, and the trend line through the current year exceeds last year's pace.
- **Special education transportation failures.** Twelve of the missed runs in the current year involved routes serving students whose IEPs specify transportation as a related service. Each such failure is a potential compliance exposure for the district, not the vendor, because the district holds the FAPE obligation.

### 3.2 The Attendance Connection

Chronic absenteeism — the share of students missing 10 percent or more of enrolled days — stands at 34.1 percent districtwide. The administration does not claim transportation is the primary driver of that number, which reflects health, housing, and economic factors as well. But the connection between service failure and absence is direct and demonstrable at the site level:

- The four schools with the highest dependence on bus transportation (over 55 percent of enrollment transported) have chronic absenteeism rates averaging 6.8 percentage points higher than the four schools with the lowest bus dependence, after the two groups tracked within 2 points of each other as recently as 2021–22 — before the sharpest deterioration in vendor service.
- Attendance office logs at the three most affected elementary schools recorded 2,340 student-day absences in 2024–25 in which the family or the site attributed the absence directly to a missed or severely late bus. At the district's LCFF funding rate, those verified transportation-attributable absences alone represent approximately $175,000 in lost attendance-based revenue in a single year — a conservative figure, since many transportation-caused absences are never attributed as such.
- On days with three or more missed morning runs, districtwide daily attendance averaged 1.9 percentage points below the year's mean.

Every absent day compounds for a student population in which 31 percent are English learners for whom instructional continuity is especially consequential. The district has invested heavily in attendance recovery — outreach staff, home visits, incentive programs — while its transportation vendor has undermined those investments at the curb.

### 3.3 Why the Vendor Relationship Cannot Fix This

First Route's failures stem principally from its inability to recruit and retain drivers at the wages and conditions it offers, compounded by an aging leased fleet with high mechanical downtime. These are structural problems in the vendor's business model, not lapses a cure notice can correct. The contract's remedy structure — liquidated damages capped well below the district's actual attendance-revenue losses — gives the vendor a rational economic incentive to under-staff and absorb the penalties. The district can escalate to termination for cause, but termination without a replacement operation solves nothing; the question before the Board is what replaces First Route, not whether the status quo is acceptable. It is not.

## 4. The Proposed Transportation Department

### 4.1 Scope of Operations

The proposed department would operate all home-to-school, special education, and activity transportation currently provided under contract: 46 routes at full buildout, serving approximately 3,900 riders daily across fourteen schools, plus athletic and field trip service currently purchased at premium per-trip rates.

### 4.2 Organizational Structure and Staffing

The department would be built and led by **Wesley Frayne, Director of Operations**, whose prior experience includes standing up and managing a district transportation operation of comparable scale. Mr. Frayne would report to the Chief Business Official. The department at full staffing comprises:

| Position | FTE | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Director of Transportation | 1.0 | Department leadership; reports to Director of Operations |
| Dispatcher/Router | 2.0 | Daily dispatch, routing software, parent communication |
| Bus Drivers | 58.0 | 46 routes plus substitute/relief pool of 12 |
| Mechanics | 6.0 | Fleet maintenance, including EV-certified technicians |
| Trainer/Safety Officer | 1.0 | State-mandated driver training and certification |
| Administrative Assistant | 1.0 | Payroll, records, compliance documentation |
| **Total** | **69.0** | |

The 12-driver relief pool — roughly 26 percent above route count — is deliberate and is the single most important structural difference from the vendor's model. First Route's failures are driver-absence failures; the department is designed so that routine absence does not become a missed run.

All classified positions would be district employees within the CSEA bargaining unit, with district health benefits and CalPERS membership — a compensation package materially different from the vendor's, addressed further in Sections 6 and 9.

### 4.3 Fleet

The proposal purchases 46 buses: 30 diesel/CNG conventional buses acquired through cooperative purchasing agreements, and 16 electric buses contingent on the state clean school bus allocation discussed in Section 6. A new fleet carries warranty coverage through the startup years, when the maintenance operation is itself maturing, and eliminates the mechanical unreliability that has plagued the vendor's aged equipment.

### 4.4 Facility

The district has identified a corporation yard on the Avenue 288 industrial corridor, available on a ten-year lease with two five-year options. Retrofit requirements — fueling infrastructure, EV charging (contingent on the state award), maintenance bays, dispatch office, and secure parking for 46 buses — are included in the capital budget. The site is centrally located relative to the route network, minimizing deadhead mileage. Lease terms have been negotiated to a letter of intent; execution awaits Board authorization.

### 4.5 Technology and Accountability

The department would deploy routing and GPS tracking software with a parent-facing application showing real-time bus location — a service First Route was contractually obligated to provide and never delivered. Monthly service metrics (on-time performance, missed runs, ride times, maintenance downtime) would be reported to the Board as a standing information item, so that the same transparency the district has demanded of its vendor is applied to its own operation.

## 5. Implementation Schedule

The eighteen-month phased conversion is designed so that no route transfers to district operation before the district is demonstrably ready to run it.

**Phase 0 — Standup (April–July 2026):**
- April 8: Board action; contract termination notice issued effective June 30, 2026, with negotiated transition services from First Route through January 2027 for routes not yet converted.
- April–May: Execute facility lease; issue fleet purchase orders (cooperative purchasing timelines put first deliveries in July); post Director of Transportation and initial driver/mechanic positions; open negotiations with CSEA on the new classifications.
- May–July: Facility retrofit; hire and begin training first driver cohort (28 drivers for the 22 Phase 1 routes plus relief pool); establish maintenance operation; complete routing build in software.

**Phase 1 — August 2026: 22 routes convert.** Phase 1 routes are selected to include the routes with the worst vendor service record — predominantly the rural elementary routes where missed runs and attendance impact concentrate. First Route continues operating the remaining 24 routes under a transition amendment.

**Phase 2 — January 2027: Remaining 24 routes convert.** Second driver cohort (30 drivers) hired and trained August–December 2026, with Phase 1 operations serving as the training environment. Remaining fleet deliveries completed fall 2026. First Route relationship concludes.

**Full operation: Spring 2027,** with the first full year of complete district operation in 2027–28.

The phased structure contains risk: if Phase 1 encounters staffing or operational shortfalls, the district retains vendor coverage on more than half the network and can adjust the Phase 2 timeline before, not after, students are affected.

### 5.1 Driver Recruitment and Training

Fifty-eight drivers is the recruitment challenge at the heart of this proposal, and the administration addresses it directly rather than optimistically.

- **Known pipeline:** Approximately 20 current First Route drivers on Tule Bend routes have informally indicated interest in district employment, which would offer benefits and retirement their current employer does not. Retaining incumbent drivers also preserves route knowledge.
- **Training pipeline:** The department's Trainer/Safety Officer would run an in-house behind-the-wheel training program, allowing the district to hire candidates without existing school bus certification and train them to the state standard — widening the applicant pool well beyond currently certificated drivers. Training cohorts begin in May 2026.
- **Compensation:** The proposed starting wage is $24.10 per hour. CSEA chapter president Dolores Ybarra has correctly noted this sits below the county average of $26.75. The administration's position, developed further in Section 9.3, is that the total compensation package — district health benefits, CalPERS, guaranteed hours, and paid training — is competitive with the county average cash wage offered by private operators without benefits, but the administration also acknowledges that the wage rate is subject to negotiation with CSEA and that the operating budget carries capacity for movement on this item.
- **Recruitment incentives:** The startup budget includes signing and training-completion stipends and paid CDL training, the recruitment tools that have proven effective for districts that have recently converted to in-house operation.

Against the current contract cost of $7.6 million — and the $8.7 million the extension would cost — district operation yields:

- **$700,000 annual savings** versus the current contract;
- **$1.8 million annual savings** versus the extended contract;
- Elimination of premium per-trip charges for athletics and field trips, currently approximately $110,000 annually, which the department absorbs within the operating budget above.

Simple payback on the district's non-grant capital contribution ($10.8 million) is approximately 6 to 8 years against the extension-price baseline, within the useful life of the fleet. The financial case, however, is not primarily a savings case. It is a service case with a favorable financial profile: the district would pay less than it pays now for an operation designed not to strand its students.

### 6.4 The State Clean School Bus Allocation

The district's $3.4 million application under the state clean school bus program has passed technical review, and program staff have verbally indicated the district's rural, high-need profile scores strongly. The award letter has not arrived, and the administration will not represent contingent funds as certain. Section 9.1 sets out the contingency plan if the award does not materialize as expected. The recommended Board action is structured so that the electric bus purchase orders are not issued until the award letter is in hand.

### 6.5 Reserve Impact

The $1.7 million reserve contribution reduces the unrestricted reserve from its current level to **4.2 percent of expenditures — 1.2 points above the 3 percent state minimum**. The administration is aware that Board President Kahananui and two trustees campaigned on protecting reserves, and it takes that commitment seriously as a statement of fiscal discipline, not an obstacle to be argued around.

Three points bear on the reserve question:

1. **The draw is one-time and self-restoring.** The $700,000 annual operating savings replenishes the $1.7 million draw in under three years; against the extension baseline, in under one. The multi-year projection shows the reserve returning above its current level by fiscal year 2028–29.
2. **The alternative also spends reserves — invisibly.** The 14 percent extension increase costs $1.1 million more per year than current contract cost. Two years of extension pricing consumes more unrestricted resources than the entire proposed reserve draw, while purchasing continued failure.
3. **Attendance revenue is the hidden line.** Verified transportation-attributable absences already cost the district on the order of $175,000 per year in ADA revenue, conservatively measured. A reserve exists to protect the district's fiscal position; chronic absenteeism at 34.1 percent is itself a fiscal threat.

The administration would support, and recommends the Board adopt alongside this action, a resolution committing the first-priority use of transportation operating savings to reserve restoration until the reserve returns to its pre-draw level.

## 7. Governance, Oversight, and Labor Relations

**Board oversight.** The department would operate under Board-adopted transportation policies, with monthly public reporting of the service metrics identified in Section 4.5 and an annual independent performance and cost review for the first three years of operation, so the Board can verify — not merely be assured — that district operation outperforms the vendor record.

**Labor relations.** The new classifications fall within the CSEA unit. The administration has briefed chapter president Ybarra on the proposal and acknowledges her stated concern regarding the driver wage. The district will satisfy its negotiation obligations regarding the new classifications' wages and terms before hiring, with negotiations opening immediately upon Board approval so that agreement can be reached in time for May training cohorts. The administration regards CSEA as an ally in this proposal's success: sixty-nine stable, benefited classified jobs in this community is an outcome the district and the chapter share an interest in achieving, and the phased hiring schedule allows negotiated adjustments to be incorporated before the bulk of hiring occurs.

**Compliance.** Special education transportation would operate under direct district control, with routing decisions integrated with the special education department — eliminating the current arrangement in which the district holds FAPE liability for service quality it cannot control.

## 8. Alternatives Considered

The administration evaluated four alternatives and presents each candidly.

### 8.1 Exercise the One-Year Extension

**Cost:** approximately $8.7 million for 2026–27 (14 percent above current). **Assessment:** The extension purchases one more year of a demonstrably failing service at a premium price, from a vendor whose failures are structural. It would consume $1.1 million in additional annual cost — more than half the proposed reserve draw — while producing no improvement and deferring, not avoiding, this decision. The administration cannot recommend it, but notes it remains the default outcome if no action is taken by May 15.

### 8.2 Rebid the Contract

**Assessment:** The district tested the market informally in fall 2025. Two operators expressed conditional interest; both indicated pricing at or above the extension price, citing the same driver-market conditions that have broken First Route's model. The structural problem — private operators cannot recruit drivers in this labor market at wages their margins allow — would transfer to a new vendor, not disappear. A rebid also cannot be completed and mobilized by August 2026; it would require exercising the extension first, adding $1.1 million in bridge cost and a year's delay. Rebidding is a viable path only if the Board concludes contracted operation is acceptable in principle and the problem is vendor-specific. The five-year service record indicates otherwise.

### 8.3 Joint Arrangement with a Neighboring District

The administration held two exploratory meetings with a neighboring district that operates its own fleet, regarding either purchased service or a joint powers arrangement. **Assessment:** The neighboring operation has capacity to absorb perhaps 8 to 10 of our routes — those nearest the shared boundary — but not the network. Their leadership was clear that taking on 46 routes would require them to undertake substantially the fleet purchase and driver recruitment this proposal describes, with our district paying the cost but holding no direct control, and with our routes structurally second in priority behind their own students. A JPA governance structure could be negotiated over 12 to 18 months, but would arrive too late for this decision window and would still deliver less control at comparable cost. The administration recommends preserving this relationship for future mutual-aid agreements — shared substitute coverage, joint training, cooperative parts purchasing — which the neighboring district indicated openness to and which appear in Section 9 as mitigations. As the primary solution, it is neither available on the timeline nor superior on the merits.

### 8.4 Hybrid: Convert Only the Worst-Performing Routes

**Assessment:** Converting only the 22 worst routes and leaving 24 with a vendor was analyzed and rejected as a permanent structure. It carries most of the fixed costs of district operation (facility, management, maintenance) spread over half the fleet, worsening unit economics, while the vendor's pricing for a reduced contract would rise per-route. It also leaves half our students on a failing service indefinitely. The proposal instead adopts the hybrid structure *temporarily* — that is precisely what the Phase 1/Phase 2 design is — capturing its risk-management value without its permanent inefficiency.

## 9. Risks and Mitigations

The administration presents the risks of this proposal without minimization. A Board asked to commit $14.2 million is entitled to know what could go wrong.

### 9.1 Risk: The State Clean School Bus Award Does Not Arrive, or Arrives Reduced

**Likelihood:** Moderate-to-low based on program staff indications, but the award letter is not in hand and the administration treats it as uncertain.
**Mitigation:** The action requested is structured so no expenditure depends on the award until it is received. If the award fails entirely, the fallback is to substitute 16 additional conventional buses (approximately $2.64 million) for the electric buses ($6.32 million) and defer EV charging infrastructure ($620,000) — reducing the capital requirement to approximately $10.9 million, fundable from capital facilities and the reserve draw as proposed, with a modest additional reserve draw of up to $0.1 million or corresponding phasing of shop equipment. The program vision survives; the fleet mix changes. If the award is delayed rather than denied, conventional buses cover Phase 1 and electric buses enter at Phase 2. The Board would receive a status report and, if the fallback is triggered, a revised funding resolution before any substitution purchase.

### 9.2 Risk: Driver Recruitment Falls Short

**Likelihood:** Moderate. This is the risk that broke the vendor, and the administration does not assume immunity from the labor market.
**Mitigation:** Five layers. First, the compensation structure (benefits, CalPERS, guaranteed hours) is categorically different from what private operators offer, which is why district operations across the county staff more successfully than contractors. Second, the in-house CDL training program opens the applicant pool to uncertificated candidates. Third, the phased schedule requires only 28 drivers by August, with five months' additional runway to the second cohort. Fourth, the 12-driver relief pool means recruitment shortfalls degrade the substitute cushion before they touch route coverage. Fifth, the transition agreement with First Route retains vendor coverage on 24 routes through January 2027, and the administration will negotiate an option to extend transition coverage on a per-route basis if Phase 2 staffing lags. The trigger discipline: if fewer than 24 of the 28 Phase 1 drivers are hired and in training by June 15, the administration will return to the Board with a revised Phase 1 route count rather than launch under-staffed.

### 9.3 Risk: Wage Rate Proves Non-Competitive; CSEA Negotiations Delay Hiring

**Likelihood:** Moderate. Ms. Ybarra's comparison to the $26.75 county average is a fair marker for negotiations.
**Mitigation:** The operating budget's $700,000 annual margin against current contract cost provides negotiating capacity: moving the driver wage from $24.10 to the county average would cost approximately $290,000 annually at full staffing, absorbable within that margin while preserving savings against either baseline. The administration's negotiating objective is a package competitive on total compensation; its budget can support that objective. To prevent schedule risk, negotiations open the week following Board approval, and the administration requests the Board's concurrence that reaching agreement on these classifications is the district's top negotiating priority this spring.

### 9.4 Risk: Startup Operational Failures — the District Performs No Better Than the Vendor

**Likelihood:** Low-to-moderate in Phase 1; the first weeks of any converted route network produce routing errors and timing problems.
**Mitigation:** Experienced leadership in Mr. Frayne, who has run a comparable standup; hiring of incumbent drivers who know the routes; a full summer of dry-run route testing before students board; the relief pool; new-fleet warranty coverage minimizing mechanical downtime; and the mutual-aid substitute arrangement under discussion with the neighboring district. Critically, the phased design means early failures affect 22 routes with vendor backup still in place — the district learns on half the network, not all of it. Monthly public metrics ensure the Board sees performance immediately, not through vendor-filtered reporting.

### 9.5 Risk: Cost Overruns on Capital or Operations

**Likelihood:** Moderate for facility retrofit (construction pricing volatility); low for fleet (cooperative purchase pricing is firm-quoted).
**Mitigation:** The retrofit line carries a 12 percent contingency within the stated figure. Fleet purchases execute at quoted cooperative prices. Operating estimates for fuel, parts, and insurance are benchmarked against actuals from three comparable county district operations, adjusted upward 5 percent for conservatism. Any capital change order exceeding $100,000 returns to the Board.

### 9.6 Risk: Further Enrollment Decline Strands Capacity

**Likelihood:** Moderate over the fleet's life.
**Mitigation:** As noted, rural route mileage declines far more slowly than enrollment; the route network is geography-driven. If consolidation eventually permits, surplus buses are marketable assets with active resale demand, and the relief pool and route count flex downward through attrition without layoffs. Owning the operation gives the district precisely the flexibility to right-size that a fixed-price contract denies it.

### 9.7 Risk: Reserve Draw Coincides with an Unforeseen Fiscal Shock

**Likelihood:** Unknowable; the concern is legitimate.
**Mitigation:** The post-draw reserve of 4.2 percent remains above the 3 percent minimum by a margin exceeding $1 million. The proposed companion resolution dedicating operating savings to reserve restoration returns the reserve to its current level within three years on the conservative baseline. And the counterfactual must be weighed honestly: the extension path spends more unrestricted resources than the reserve draw within two years, with nothing owned at the end of it.

## 10. Why Now

Three clocks are running.

**The contract clock.** May 15 is the extension deadline. April 8 is the Board's last regular second-Wednesday meeting before that date. Inaction is a decision: it commits the district to $8.7 million for another year of documented failure.

**The implementation clock.** An August 2026 Phase 1 launch requires fleet orders, facility work, hiring, and training to begin in April. Each month of delay pushes conversion into mid-year or defers it a full school year — a year costing $1.1 million in extension premium and another year of attendance losses.

**The student clock.** This is the clock that matters. A third grader on a rural route who missed 14 days last year because a bus did not come does not experience this as a procurement question. Chronic absenteeism at 34.1 percent is an emergency in a district where 78 percent of students are socioeconomically disadvantaged and school attendance is the single intervention that reaches every other outcome. The district has spent five years managing a vendor. The administration is asking the Board to let it manage a bus system.

## 11. Recommended Action

The administration recommends that the Board of Trustees, at its regular meeting of April 8, 2026, adopt the following:

1. **Authorize the Superintendent** to issue notice of non-renewal and termination of the transportation services agreement with First Route Student Transportation, effective June 30, 2026, and to negotiate a transition services amendment maintaining vendor coverage on non-converted routes through January 2027, at not more than current per-route contract rates.

2. **Approve the establishment of a district-operated Transportation Department**, including the classified positions described in Section 4.2, subject to completion of negotiations with CSEA regarding new classifications.

3. **Approve the capital budget of $14,200,000** and the funding plan set forth in Section 6.2, including: (a) appropriation of $9,100,000 from the capital facilities fund; (b) appropriation of $1,700,000 from unrestricted reserves; and (c) conditional appropriation of $3,400,000 in state clean school bus funds, with electric bus purchase orders authorized only upon receipt of the award letter, and with direction to staff to return with a revised funding resolution under Section 9.1 if the award is not received by August 1, 2026.

4. **Authorize the Superintendent and Chief Business Official** to execute the corporation yard lease on the terms in the letter of intent, to issue fleet purchase orders through cooperative purchasing agreements, and to execute retrofit contracts within the approved budget, with change orders above $100,000 returning to the Board.

5. **Adopt a reserve restoration commitment** directing that transportation operating savings be applied first to restoring the unrestricted reserve to its pre-draw level, with progress reported in each interim budget report.

6. **Direct monthly public reporting** of transportation service metrics beginning September 2026, and an annual independent performance and cost review for the first three years of department operation.

The administration respectfully requests the Board's approval and stands ready to answer trustees' questions at the April 8 meeting.
