# Kinship Families Initiative: A Proposal to Strengthen Relative Caregiving Across Merced County

**Applicant:** Los Robles Family Resource Network
**Submitted to:** The Wendell Ruthers Foundation, Kinship Families Initiative
**Requested Amount:** $1,250,000 over three years
**Contact:** Chue Vang Lor, Interim Executive Director; Antoinette Boudreaux, Program Director

---

## Statement of Need

Merced County is home to approximately 3,900 children being raised by grandparents, aunts, uncles, and other relatives rather than by their parents. These kinship arrangements form when parents are unable to care for their children because of incarceration, substance use, military deployment, illness, or death, and they are, by every measure of child welfare research, the placement of first choice: children raised by relatives experience fewer school changes, fewer placement disruptions, and better long-term mental health outcomes than children placed with unrelated foster families. Yet the relatives who step forward to hold their families together receive a fraction of the support extended to licensed foster parents, and they do so while navigating guardianship courts, school enrollment offices, and benefits systems that were not designed with them in mind.

Los Robles Family Resource Network has spent nineteen years building the infrastructure Merced County lacks. Last year alone, we served 810 kinship households across the county, provided direct support to caregivers raising children whose parents cannot, and completed 210 guardianship petitions through our legal clinic — often the single most important document standing between a child and instability, since guardianship secures a child's right to remain with the relative raising them, unlocks school enrollment and medical consent authority, and in many cases opens the door to state support payments the family would otherwise never receive.

The population we serve reflects the demographic reality of Merced County's Central Valley communities. Fifty-eight percent of the families we serve are Hispanic or Latino, fourteen percent are Hmong, and nine percent are Black — communities that, in our experience and in the data collected by the county human services agency, are frequently underserved by mainstream family support systems because of language barriers, distrust born of prior negative experiences with government agencies, and a simple lack of culturally specific outreach. Sixty-four percent of the caregivers we work with are over the age of 60, many on fixed incomes, many managing their own chronic health conditions while parenting a second time. Forty-three percent of the households we serve live below the federal poverty line. These are not families with disposable resources to absorb the cost of an attorney, a support group with a childcare co-pay, or a weekend of respite care. They are families holding a county's child welfare system together through sheer will, and they are doing it largely without institutional support commensurate with the responsibility they have taken on.

The need is not hypothetical, and it is not static. Merced County's kinship care population has grown steadily over the past decade, driven by the same forces — the fentanyl crisis, rising incarceration rates in the Central Valley, and gaps in behavioral health access — that have strained kinship systems nationally. Our own placement stability data underscores both the promise of well-supported kinship care and the cost of leaving families without it: households enrolled in our full suite of services maintained kinship placements at a 88 percent rate at twelve months, compared to a county-wide average of 71 percent among kinship families with no comparable support. That seventeen-point gap represents hundreds of children each year who either remain safely with the relatives raising them or, without intervention, cycle back into the formal foster care system at far greater emotional cost to the child and far greater fiscal cost to the county and the state.

This gap exists despite — not because of — an adequately resourced service system. On September 30 of last year, an $850,000 federal earmark that had funded roughly one-third of our kinship program lapsed when it was not renewed in the federal budget cycle, a casualty of congressional turnover rather than any failure of performance on our part. We have absorbed this loss for nearly a year through reserve draws and reduced hours, but reserves are not a program model, and the caregivers we serve — many already stretched past their capacity — cannot absorb further reductions in the legal clinic, the home visiting program, or the crisis respite line without real consequences for placement stability. At the same time, our organization is in the midst of a leadership transition following the retirement of our founding executive director in January, after nineteen years building this network from a single county contract into a $6.1 million operation with 38 staff. We say this plainly because the Wendell Ruthers Foundation deserves an honest picture of where we stand: this is an organization with a proven model, an unmatched track record, and real current vulnerabilities that this grant is specifically designed to help us navigate.

The Kinship Families Initiative's stated priorities — placement stability, caregiver mental health, school stability, guardianship completion, and cost-efficient service delivery — are, in effect, a description of what Los Robles Family Resource Network already does and does well. What we are requesting is the investment to close the gap left by the lapsed earmark, to reach the Hmong caregivers our current model underserves, to relieve caregiver stress through structured respite, and to expand the legal clinic capacity that has already produced 210 completed guardianships in a single year. This is not a request to build something new. It is a request to protect and strengthen something that works, at the precise moment when it is most at risk of contracting.

## Project Description

The Kinship Families Stability Project will use a $1.25 million, three-year investment from the Wendell Ruthers Foundation to stabilize and expand three components of our existing kinship care continuum: legal permanency, caregiver mental health and respite support, and culturally specific outreach to Merced County's Hmong kinship families. Each component is designed around a gap we have identified through direct service delivery and through data shared by our partner, the Merced County Human Services Agency, which provides referrals and county-level placement data under a longstanding data-sharing agreement.

**Legal Permanency Expansion.** The legal clinic is the backbone of our permanency work and the direct driver of our guardianship completion numbers. Currently, two contract attorneys and one paralegal manage a caseload that has grown faster than our capacity to serve it; families routinely wait six to eight weeks for an initial legal consultation, and cases involving contested guardianship, interstate custody questions, or immigration-status complications for mixed-status Hmong and Latino families often require more attorney hours than our current staffing allows. This grant will fund two full-time staff attorneys, bringing dedicated legal capacity to four attorneys and reducing our average time-to-consultation from six to eight weeks down to two. These attorneys will work in direct partnership with Valley Legal Aid, our longstanding legal aid partner, which will continue to handle overflow cases and provide backup coverage during staff transitions, ensuring no family experiences a gap in legal support. We project this expansion will allow us to complete approximately 280 guardianship petitions annually by year two, up from 210, while also building capacity for the more complex cases — contested guardianships, cases involving tribal or international jurisdiction questions, and cases requiring coordination with immigration counsel — that our current staffing structure cannot adequately absorb.

**Caregiver Respite Fund.** Sixty-four percent of caregivers in our network are over 60, and our intake data consistently shows elevated caregiver stress and depression symptoms, particularly among caregivers managing their own health conditions alongside the demands of raising children a second time. Yet Merced County has almost no respite infrastructure built for kinship caregivers specifically; the licensed foster respite system that exists is not extended to informal kinship arrangements. This grant will establish a Caregiver Respite Fund providing subsidized in-home respite care, funded emergency childcare for medical appointments, and short-term overnight respite placements with vetted providers for caregivers facing acute health crises or simple exhaustion. We anticipate the fund will serve approximately 150 households in year one, scaling to 220 households by year three, with priority access for caregivers who score above clinical thresholds on our standardized depression screening at intake.

**Hmong-Language Support and Outreach.** Fourteen percent of the families we serve are Hmong, but our internal data suggests this understates actual need in the county; Hmong elders raising grandchildren face particular barriers to accessing services conducted in English or Spanish, and cultural norms around discussing family difficulty publicly can discourage engagement with mainstream support groups. In partnership with the Merced Hmong Community Center, we will launch two monthly Hmong-language kinship caregiver support groups, facilitated by a bicultural, bilingual staff member we will hire under this grant, alongside a targeted outreach campaign using Hmong-language radio, community center bulletin boards, and referrals from Hmong elders already engaged with our services. The Hmong Community Center will provide meeting space, help identify trusted community messengers, and co-host quarterly cultural events designed to build trust with families who have historically been reluctant to engage with county-adjacent services.

**Evaluation Infrastructure.** All three components will be supported by an enhanced evaluation partnership with UC Merced, described in detail in the Evaluation Plan section below, which will allow us to track outcomes with a rigor our organization has not previously had the capacity to sustain internally.

Taken together, these three components directly address the five outcome areas the Wendell Ruthers Foundation has identified as priorities. Expanded legal capacity drives guardianship completion. The respite fund and Hmong-language support groups target caregiver depression screening scores, which we will now track systematically at intake, six months, and twelve months using the PHQ-9 screening instrument administered by trained staff. Placement stability, already strong at 88 percent at twelve months against a county average of 71 percent, should improve further as caregiver burnout — one of the most common precipitants of placement disruption in our case review data — is addressed directly through respite access. School stability improves as children remain in stable kinship placements rather than cycling through additional moves. And because this project builds on existing infrastructure rather than creating a new organization or new systems from scratch, our cost per family served should remain efficient relative to comparable kinship support models, a point we address further in the Budget Narrative.

This is a three-year model, but it is designed with year four and beyond in mind from its inception, particularly with respect to the legal clinic's long-term financial footing, which we address directly in the Sustainability section.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

**Goal 1: Increase kinship placement stability across Merced County.**
- Objective 1.1: Maintain twelve-month placement stability at or above 88 percent among households enrolled in the full service model, and improve stability among households receiving only partial services (legal clinic only, or respite only) from current baseline to at least 80 percent by year three.
- Objective 1.2: Achieve 24-month placement stability of at least 80 percent among fully enrolled households, a metric we have not previously tracked systematically and will establish as a new baseline in year one through the UC Merced evaluation partnership.
- Objective 1.3: Reduce the gap between Los Robles-served households and the county-wide kinship placement stability average from 17 percentage points to at least 22 percentage points by year three, reflecting both our improvement and, we hope, a positive spillover effect on county practice as our data informs broader county policy conversations.

**Goal 2: Improve caregiver mental health and reduce caregiver burnout.**
- Objective 2.1: Screen 100 percent of newly enrolled caregivers using the PHQ-9 depression screening instrument at intake, six months, and twelve months, establishing a systematic baseline where informal observation previously stood in.
- Objective 2.2: Reduce the proportion of enrolled caregivers scoring in the moderate-to-severe depression range (PHQ-9 score of 10 or above) by 25 percent between intake and twelve-month follow-up among caregivers who access the Respite Fund or Hmong-language support groups.
- Objective 2.3: Deliver at least 1,800 hours of subsidized respite care annually by year two, serving no fewer than 150 households in year one and 220 households by year three.

**Goal 3: Expand access to legal permanency, particularly for underserved Hmong and mixed-status families.**
- Objective 3.1: Increase completed guardianship petitions from 210 to 250 in year one and 280 by year two, sustaining that level through year three.
- Objective 3.2: Reduce average wait time from initial legal intake to first attorney consultation from six to eight weeks to no more than two weeks by the end of year one.
- Objective 3.3: Increase the proportion of Hmong families accessing the legal clinic by 40 percent by year two, tracked through intake demographic data and cross-referenced with Hmong Community Center referral records.

**Goal 4: Improve school stability for children in kinship placements.**
- Objective 4.1: Track school changes among children in enrolled households through data-sharing with school districts facilitated by the county human services agency, establishing a baseline in year one.
- Objective 4.2: Reduce mid-year school changes among children in fully enrolled households to no more than 10 percent by year three, a target informed by comparable kinship program data nationally.

**Goal 5: Deliver services at a sustainable and demonstrable cost per family.**
- Objective 5.1: Maintain a blended cost per household served at or below $1,850 annually across all three program components, calculated and reported to the Foundation each year through our evaluation partnership.
- Objective 5.2: Demonstrate, by year three, that the legal clinic's cost per completed guardianship has decreased as caseload efficiency improves with the addition of dedicated staff attorneys, moving from an estimated $1,400 per completed petition to $1,100.

## Evaluation Plan

Los Robles Family Resource Network has built a strong service delivery track record, but we have historically relied on internal tracking systems that, while adequate for program management, do not meet the standard of rigor this grant's outcomes require, particularly around caregiver mental health screening and 24-month placement follow-up. This grant will fund a formal evaluation partnership with UC Merced, whose Department of Public Health and Sociology faculty have existing research relationships with Central Valley kinship and immigrant family populations and will serve as our external evaluator.

The evaluation design is a longitudinal cohort model. All households enrolled during the three-year grant period will be tracked at intake, six months, twelve months, and 24 months (for households enrolled in years one and two, allowing full 24-month follow-up within the grant period). Data collection will draw on four sources: (1) internal case management records already maintained by Los Robles staff, upgraded under this grant to a standardized intake and follow-up protocol; (2) placement stability data shared under our existing data-sharing agreement with the Merced County Human Services Agency, which will continue to provide county-wide comparison data allowing us to track our performance against the county average; (3) PHQ-9 depression screening data collected directly by trained program staff at each of the four measurement points; and (4) school stability data obtained through a new data-sharing agreement, to be finalized in year one with support from the county human services agency's existing relationships with local school districts.

UC Merced's evaluation team will lead a mixed-methods analysis combining this quantitative outcome tracking with qualitative interviews conducted annually with a stratified sample of approximately 40 caregivers, oversampled to ensure adequate representation of Hmong caregivers, whose experience of the program we consider a priority to understand in depth given the cultural and linguistic outreach component of this proposal. These interviews will explore not only outcome measures but caregiver perception of service quality, cultural responsiveness, and unmet need, informing real-time program adjustments rather than waiting for a summative report at the end of three years.

We will produce an annual evaluation report to the Wendell Ruthers Foundation each year of the grant, due 60 days after the close of each program year, reporting against every objective listed in the Goals and Measurable Objectives section above. Each report will include: placement stability rates at twelve and 24 months, disaggregated by full versus partial service enrollment; aggregate and disaggregated PHQ-9 screening results; guardianship completion counts and average time-to-consultation; school stability data; and a calculated cost per family served, broken out by program component. Where a target is not met, the report will include a narrative analysis of contributing factors and a corrective action plan, developed jointly by program staff and the UC Merced evaluation team.

We recognize that establishing new data infrastructure — particularly the school stability data-sharing agreement and the systematic PHQ-9 screening protocol — will take real work in year one, and we have built a six-month ramp-up period into our evaluation timeline to account for this. We would rather report an honest, if less polished, year-one baseline than overstate our starting capacity, and we believe the Foundation's own emphasis on measurable, specific outcomes reflects a similar preference for accuracy over polish.

## Organizational Capacity

Los Robles Family Resource Network has operated in Merced County for nineteen years, growing from a single county contract into a $6.1 million organization with 38 staff serving 810 households annually. Our core competency — legal permanency work paired with direct family support — has produced results that exceed county averages by a wide margin: an 88 percent twelve-month placement stability rate against a county average of 71 percent, and 210 completed guardianship petitions in a single year through a legal clinic that, until this grant, has operated with a fraction of the staffing this caseload actually requires. This track record is the strongest evidence we can offer that our organization has the operational discipline and community trust needed to deliver on this grant's ambitious targets.

We want to address directly two organizational realities the Foundation should weigh honestly in its review. First, our founding executive director retired in January after nineteen years leading this organization. Chue Vang Lor, formerly our Deputy Director of Programs and a fifteen-year veteran of the organization, is serving as Interim Executive Director during a national search for a permanent successor, expected to conclude by the end of this calendar year. Mr. Lor has led the organization's day-to-day operations for the past six months without disruption to service delivery — our caseload, placement outcomes, and legal clinic throughput have all remained stable or improved during this transition period — and he has the full confidence of the Board of Directors to lead the organization through this grant period. Program Director Antoinette Boudreaux, who has overseen direct service programming for eight years, will serve as the day-to-day project lead for this grant, ensuring continuity of institutional knowledge regardless of the outcome of the executive search. We view leadership succession not as a footnote but as a governance responsibility we are actively managing, and we would welcome the opportunity to update the Foundation on the search's progress as part of our regular reporting.

Second, our Board of Directors has contracted from thirteen members to eight over the past eighteen months, largely due to the natural rotation of long-serving members whose terms concluded around the same period as the executive transition. Board Chair Ravi Chandrasekaran has led a board recruitment effort now underway, prioritizing candidates with legal, healthcare, and Hmong and Latino community leadership backgrounds to both restore board capacity and strengthen the board's connection to the populations we serve. We expect to return to a board of at least eleven members within the coming year. In the interim, the current eight-member board remains active and engaged, meeting monthly rather than quarterly during this transition period specifically to maintain close oversight during a period of organizational change.

Neither of these realities has affected service delivery. Our finance department, led by a Director of Finance with twelve years at the organization, maintains clean annual audits and has managed the organization's response to the lapsed federal earmark responsibly, drawing on reserves in a measured way while avoiding layoffs or service cuts to date. Development Director Siobhan McAllister has led a diversified fundraising strategy over the past year specifically in response to the earmark's lapse, which we describe further in the Sustainability section below.

Our program staff includes bilingual Spanish-speaking case managers reflecting our largest served population, existing relationships with Hmong community leaders built over years of service delivery, and a legal team experienced in the guardianship, custody, and benefits issues our families face daily. Our four core partners — the Merced County Human Services Agency, Valley Legal Aid, the Merced Hmong Community Center, and UC Merced — each bring institutional capacity we do not need to replicate internally: the county provides referral infrastructure and placement data; Valley Legal Aid provides legal overflow capacity and specialized expertise in immigration-adjacent custody questions; the Hmong Community Center provides trusted community access; and UC Merced provides research-grade evaluation capacity. These partnerships, several of them a decade or more in duration, are themselves evidence of organizational stability that persists independent of any single staff transition.

## Sustainability

The Wendell Ruthers Foundation's investment is structured as three-year funding, and we have built our sustainability planning around a clear-eyed assessment of which components of this project can become self-sustaining and which will require continued philanthropic or public investment beyond year three — particularly the legal clinic, whose long-term financial footing is the central sustainability question for this proposal.

**Addressing the funding gap.** The $850,000 federal earmark that lapsed on September 30 funded roughly one-third of our kinship program prior to this grant request. This Foundation investment is not intended to permanently replace that federal funding but to stabilize the organization while Development Director Siobhan McAllister executes a diversification strategy already underway. Since the earmark lapsed, we have submitted three state-level applications to California's Kinship Guardianship Assistance Payment program infrastructure funds and the state Office of Child Abuse Prevention, one of which — a $400,000 two-year state grant — is pending a decision expected in the fall. We have also begun cultivating a small number of regional foundations and individual major donors, an area where our previous reliance on a single large federal award left us underdeveloped. Should the Wendell Ruthers Foundation invest, we intend to use that partnership explicitly in our case to other funders as validation of our model, a use of philanthropic capital that is at least as valuable to our long-term sustainability as the grant dollars themselves.

**The legal clinic beyond year three.** The two staff attorney positions funded by this grant represent the single largest recurring cost this proposal introduces, and we take seriously the Foundation's implicit question of what happens to that capacity after grant funding ends. Our plan rests on three pillars. First, completed guardianships in California frequently qualify families for state Kinship Guardianship Assistance Payments, and as our guardianship completion volume grows from 210 to a projected 280 annually, we intend to pursue a fee-for-service or cost-reimbursement arrangement with the county human services agency, under which the county — which currently refers cases to us at no cost to itself — would contribute a per-case reimbursement reflecting the cost savings our legal clinic generates by keeping children in stable, lower-cost kinship placements rather than the formal foster care system. We have begun preliminary conversations with the county human services agency about this model and view year two and three of this grant as the period in which we formalize it. Second, Valley Legal Aid has indicated willingness to explore a formal cost-sharing partnership in which our two staff attorneys and their attorneys operate under a shared caseload management system, potentially allowing each organization to reduce duplicate administrative overhead. Third, we will pursue Interest on Lawyers' Trust Account (IOLTA) grant funding and California State Bar access-to-justice funding streams specifically available to organizations providing free legal services to low-income families, funding sources we have not previously accessed because our legal program was, until now, too small to meet minimum application thresholds at some of these funders.

**The Respite Fund and Hmong-language programming.** These components, while smaller in dollar terms than the legal clinic, also require a sustainability path. We intend to build a dedicated respite fund endowment-style reserve through targeted individual giving, informed by the caregiver testimonials and outcome data this grant's evaluation will generate. The Hmong-language support groups, once established with paid facilitation in years one and two, are designed to build toward a peer-facilitation model by year three, in which trained caregiver volunteers from within the Hmong community — recruited through our partnership with the Hmong Community Center — co-facilitate groups alongside reduced staff time, lowering the ongoing cost of the program while deepening its community ownership.

**Board and leadership sustainability.** Board Chair Ravi Chandrasekaran's recruitment effort is directly tied to sustainability planning: incoming board members are being recruited in part for their capacity to support fundraising and community relationship-building, not solely governance oversight. The permanent executive director, once hired, will be charged explicitly with sustainability strategy as a core responsibility in the position's first-year goals, ensuring this is not a task that falls solely to Interim Executive Director Lor during a transition period.

We do not claim every component of this project will be fully self-sustaining by year four. We believe an honest sustainability plan for a program serving this population — low-income, elderly, culturally diverse caregivers with almost no capacity to pay for services themselves — must include continued public and philanthropic investment as a legitimate and expected part of the funding model, not a sign of program failure. What we can commit to is a measurable reduction in this project's dependence on any single funding source, a diversified base including county reimbursement, state grants, legal-sector-specific funding, and individual giving, and transparent annual reporting to the Foundation on our progress toward each of these goals.

## Budget Narrative

The total three-year request of $1,250,000 is allocated across personnel, respite direct services, evaluation, outreach, and administrative costs as follows, with a blended cost per household served projected at approximately $1,850 annually, a figure we consider efficient relative to comparable kinship support models nationally given the depth of legal and mental health support included.

**Personnel ($780,000 over three years, 62 percent of total request).** This category funds the two full-time staff attorney positions central to the legal clinic expansion (approximately $130,000 annually per position including benefits, or $390,000 over three years), one full-time bilingual Hmong-speaking Caregiver Support Coordinator to facilitate support groups and lead outreach in partnership with the Hmong Community Center (approximately $70,000 annually including benefits, $210,000 over three years), and one part-time Respite Fund Coordinator managing referrals, vetting respite providers, and processing subsidies (approximately $45,000 annually, $135,000 over three years). The remaining $45,000 covers a partial-time program evaluation liaison staff role coordinating data collection between Los Robles case managers and the UC Merced research team.

**Respite and Direct Assistance ($250,000 over three years, 20 percent of total request).** This funds the direct cost of subsidized respite care hours, emergency childcare vouchers, and short-term overnight respite placements described in the Project Description, scaling from approximately $70,000 in year one (serving 150 households) to $95,000 in year three (serving 220 households), reflecting both caseload growth and a modest annual cost-of-service inflation adjustment.

**Evaluation Partnership ($120,000 over three years, 9.6 percent of total request).** This funds the UC Merced evaluation partnership, including faculty and graduate research assistant time, PHQ-9 screening administration training for program staff, data infrastructure development including the new school stability data-sharing agreement, and production of three annual evaluation reports plus a final summative report at the close of the grant period.

**Outreach and Community Partnership ($60,000 over three years, 4.8 percent of total request).** This covers Hmong-language radio and print outreach, materials translation, space and event costs associated with quarterly cultural events co-hosted with the Hmong Community Center, and a modest partnership stipend to the Merced Hmong Community Center in recognition of staff time its team contributes to outreach and referral coordination.

**Administrative and Indirect Costs ($40,000 over three years, 3.2 percent of total request).** Consistent with the Foundation's guidelines, we have limited indirect cost recovery to 8 percent of direct program costs in year one, decreasing our overall administrative burden relative to our organization-wide indirect rate of 12 percent, reflecting our commitment to maximizing the proportion of this grant that reaches families directly.

This budget assumes level funding across the three grant years for planning purposes, though we anticipate modest year-over-year growth in the respite and personnel lines as caseloads scale toward year-three targets, offset by efficiencies gained as the legal clinic's caseload management improves and as the Hmong-language support groups transition toward the peer-facilitation model described in the Sustainability section. Los Robles Family Resource Network will continue to cover existing program infrastructure — office space, existing case management staff, existing legal clinic administrative support, and organization-wide finance and compliance functions — through our current operating budget, ensuring that Wendell Ruthers Foundation funds are dedicated entirely to the new and expanded capacity described in this proposal rather than to costs the organization already bears.

We are prepared to provide additional budget detail, including a full line-item breakdown and our most recent audited financial statements, upon request, and we welcome the opportunity to discuss this proposal further with Foundation staff in advance of the October 10 full proposal deadline.
