## 1. Executive Summary

The City of Ridgemark's wastewater treatment plant on Bellota Road is operating under a cease-and-desist order issued by the Central Valley Regional Water Quality Control Board following four sanitary sewer overflows in the past eighteen months. Each overflow has been traced to the same source: the plant's original 1971 headworks, both secondary clarifiers, and the influent pump station, none of which have been substantially rebuilt in fifty-five years of continuous service. These assets are past the end of their useful life, undersized for current flows, and mechanically unreliable in a way that now threatens public health, the Bellota Creek watershed, and the City's standing with its regulator.

This proposal asks the Council to authorize a $41.6 million capital project to replace the headworks, both clarifiers, and the influent pump station over a 34-month construction period, delivered through a construction manager at risk (CMAR) with a four-person City-staffed owner's team providing oversight. The project would be funded through $33 million in low-interest State Revolving Fund (SRF) borrowing, $4.5 million drawn from the sewer enterprise fund's capital reserve, and the remaining $4.1 million from a phased sewer rate increase of 9 percent annually for four years. That rate increase requires a Proposition 218 protest process, which Finance proposes to open in October 2026 so that new rates can take effect with the first SRF draw.

The order carries financial teeth: $10,000 per day in penalties beginning July 1, 2027, if construction has not started by that date. Given design, procurement, and CMAR pre-construction timelines, the City needs Council authorization now — at the March 10 meeting — to keep the project on a schedule that avoids those penalties and, more importantly, ends the pattern of overflows before a fifth event occurs.

The specific action requested of the Council on March 10 is set out in Section 8. It does not commit the City to the full $41.6 million today; it authorizes staff to proceed with final design, to execute a CMAR preconstruction services agreement, to submit the SRF loan application, and to set the October Proposition 218 hearing. The construction contract itself, and the full funding package, would return to Council for a separate action once design is at 100 percent and a guaranteed maximum price (GMP) is negotiated with the CMAR, expected in the fourth quarter of 2026.

## 2. Why Now: Condition of the Existing Works and the Regulatory Order

### 2.1 The asset

The Bellota Road plant serves all 68,400 residents of Ridgemark plus a small unincorporated fringe area under a wholesale agreement, treating an average dry-weather flow of approximately 7.8 million gallons per day (MGD) against a permitted capacity of 9.5 MGD. The plant has been expanded and partially upgraded four times since 1971 — most recently for tertiary filtration in 2009 — but the liquid-stream front end has never been replaced:

- **Headworks (built 1971):** mechanical bar screens and a single grit chamber, both original equipment, running past design capacity during wet-weather peaks. The screening mechanism has failed and been welded back into service three times since 2019. There is no redundant screening train; when the unit is down, flow bypasses screening entirely.
- **Clarifiers 1 and 2 (built 1971, recoated 1988):** steel tanks with corroded influent wells, worn drive mechanisms original to construction, and weir plates that no longer hold design elevation, causing uneven flow distribution and periodic solids carryover.
- **Influent pump station (built 1971, pumps replaced piecemeal 1994–2011):** four pumps in a wet well designed for smaller peak flows than the city now generates; only two pumps are reliably operable at a given time, leaving the plant with no true firm capacity margin during storm events.

Wastewater Superintendent Gerardo Quintanilla's staff has kept the plant in service through a combination of manual bypass operation, temporary rental pumps staged at the site since 2023, and daily monitoring during wet weather — an unsustainable operating posture that consumes significant staff time and carries real risk of a fifth overflow.

### 2.2 The four overflows and the order

| Date | Volume (gallons) | Cause | Receiving water |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jan 2025 | 42,000 | Headworks screen failure, unscreened bypass | Bellota Creek |
| Apr 2025 | 118,000 | Influent pump station overtopping in storm event | Bellota Creek |
| Sep 2025 | 26,000 | Clarifier 1 weir failure, solids washout | Bellota Creek, storm drain |
| Jan 2026 | 165,000 | Combined headworks/pump station failure in atmospheric river event | Bellota Creek |

The Regional Water Board's cease-and-desist order, issued February 2026, requires the City to submit a corrective action plan, begin construction of permanent replacement facilities by July 1, 2027, and complete construction on a schedule acceptable to the Board's Executive Officer. The order imposes stipulated penalties of $10,000 per day for any period after July 1, 2027, in which construction has not begun, separate from any penalties the Board may pursue for future overflow events. The order does not set a completion deadline beyond requiring "diligent and continuous progress," but Board staff have indicated informally that a schedule extending past 2030 would draw additional scrutiny.

### 2.3 Why the Council must act in March, not later

Working backward from the July 1, 2027 construction start required by the order:

- CMAR procurement and contract execution: 4 months
- CMAR preconstruction services (constructability review, GMP negotiation) concurrent with final design: 8 months
- Final design (currently at 30 percent, funded under a prior Council-approved planning contract): 7 months
- Permitting (Regional Board, county building, CEQA addendum to the plant's 2009 EIR): concurrent, 6 months, critical path
- Procurement of long-lead equipment (headworks screens, clarifier mechanisms, pump station VFDs): 10–14 months, must be initiated during design

These timelines overlap but the critical path from a March 2026 authorization to a July 2027 construction start leaves approximately four months of schedule float — enough to absorb normal delay, not enough to absorb a delayed Council decision. A June or July 2026 authorization would consume that float entirely and put the City at immediate risk of the $10,000-per-day penalty on day one of noncompliance, a run rate of over $3.6 million in the first year alone if the project slipped further.

## 3. Project Description, Phasing, and Continuity of Treatment

### 3.1 Scope

The project replaces, in kind and in a modern configuration:

1. **New headworks structure** with dual mechanical fine screens (duty/standby), a vortex grit removal system, and screenings/grit washing and compaction equipment, sized for the plant's permitted 9.5 MGD with peak wet-weather flow capacity to 24 MGD.
2. **Two new secondary clarifiers**, circular concrete tanks with center-feed influent wells, energy-dissipating inlets, and variable-speed drive mechanisms, replacing the 1971 steel tanks on the same footprint where feasible, with the second clarifier relocated approximately 40 feet to allow the first to remain in service during its construction.
3. **New influent pump station**, a below-grade wet well/dry well configuration with five submersible-style pumps (four duty, one standby) and a firm capacity of 24 MGD, replacing the 1971 station on an adjacent pad, connected by a new influent force main tie-in.

### 3.2 Phasing and maintaining treatment through construction

The central operational challenge is that the plant must continue treating all incoming flow — including through two wet seasons of construction — without an approved bypass discharge point. The phasing plan, developed jointly by Ontiveros's engineering staff and Quintanilla's operations staff, sequences work to keep at least one full treatment train available at all times:

**Phase 1 (Months 1–10): New influent pump station.** Built on new ground adjacent to the existing station, tied in last. The existing pump station remains in full service throughout Phase 1, backstopped by the rental pumps already on site as a contingency.

**Phase 2 (Months 8–20, overlapping): New headworks.** Built on new ground north of the existing screening building. The existing headworks continues to screen all flow until the new structure is commissioned; a temporary piping tie-in switches flow over in a planned 48-hour shutdown scheduled for a dry-weather period (targeted month 20, likely June–July of construction year two).

**Phase 3 (Months 18–34): Clarifiers, sequential.** Clarifier 2 is demolished and rebuilt first (months 18–26) while Clarifier 1 remains in service alone, at reduced but adequate capacity for average dry-weather flow; wet-weather peak flows during this window rely on the new pump station and headworks capacity margin plus the plant's existing flow equalization basin, brought back into active service for the duration. Once Clarifier 2 is commissioned (month 26), Clarifier 1 is taken out for demolition and rebuild (months 26–34), with Clarifier 2 and the equalization basin carrying full flow.

This sequencing means the plant never operates with zero clarifier capacity, and the two highest-risk single points of failure — headworks and pump station — are replaced with full duty/standby redundancy before the two wet seasons of Phase 3 clarifier work begin. Quintanilla's staff will operate the equalization basin as an active wet-weather buffer during Phase 3, a role it has not played since 2018; this requires no capital investment but does require an operations plan amendment, which will accompany the March 10 item as an informational attachment.

## 5. Delivery Method and Staffing

### 5.1 Construction manager at risk

Staff recommends CMAR delivery over traditional design-bid-build for three reasons specific to this project: the need to sequence construction around continuous plant operation (Section 3.2), the value of contractor input on constructability and phasing during design rather than after, and the ability to lock in pricing on long-lead equipment before final design is complete, which shortens the schedule relative to the July 2027 deadline. The CMAR contract structure separates preconstruction services (a fixed fee, executed now under this action) from construction (a guaranteed maximum price negotiated openly with the City once design reaches 100 percent, returning to Council for a separate authorization in November 2026 per Section 3.3). This two-step structure gives the Council a second, more precise decision point before the largest dollar commitment is made.

### 5.2 Owner's team

A four-person City-staffed owner's team will oversee the project through design and construction:

- **Project Executive** (Ontiveros, Public Works Director) — overall accountability to the City Manager and Council, change order authority up to $250,000.
- **Project Manager** (new position, to be recruited or assigned from existing capital projects staff) — day-to-day management of the CMAR relationship, schedule, and budget.
- **Resident Engineer** (contracted construction management support, budgeted within the $1.0 million construction management line) — full-time field presence during construction.
- **Operations Liaison** (Quintanilla, Wastewater Superintendent, or his designee) — responsible for the phasing plan in Section 3.2, ensuring plant operability is never compromised by construction sequencing, and serving as the operational sign-off on each phase transition (e.g., the 48-hour headworks tie-in shutdown).

This structure keeps a Public Works employee, rather than an outside consultant, as the final authority on any decision that affects the plant's ability to keep treating flow — a lesson drawn directly from the need to avoid a fifth overflow during construction itself.

## 6. Alternatives Considered

Staff evaluated three alternatives to the proposed project before recommending it.

### 6.1 Rehabilitation in place

Rehabilitating the existing headworks, clarifiers, and pump station rather than replacing them was evaluated as the lower-cost, lower-disruption option. A condition assessment performed as part of the planning phase (funded under the prior Council authorization) found that the clarifier steel tanks have lost sufficient wall thickness to require replacement rather than recoating, and that both the headworks structure and the pump station wet well have hairline cracking and rebar exposure consistent with fifty-five years of hydrogen sulfide exposure — meaning any "rehabilitation" would in practice require full tank and structure replacement, at a cost only modestly below full replacement ($36.8 million versus $41.6 million) but without the capacity increase needed to handle current wet-weather peak flows or provide the redundancy that eliminates the underlying cause of the four overflows. Staff does not recommend this alternative because it solves the age problem but not the capacity and redundancy problem that has actually caused the failures.

### 6.2 Regional connection

Staff examined a regional connection to the neighboring Cienega Valley Sanitation District's treatment plant, located approximately 6.5 miles east, which has approximately 3 MGD of unused capacity. This alternative would have required a new conveyance pipeline and lift station (estimated $28 million), an interagency agreement with Cienega Valley's board covering capacity rights and cost-sharing (with no guarantee of terms favorable to Ridgemark, given Cienega Valley's limited surplus capacity relative to Ridgemark's 7.8 MGD flow), and would not have eliminated the need to address Ridgemark's own collection system and headworks-equivalent screening needs at the connection point. Most significantly, Cienega Valley's available capacity (3 MGD) covers less than 40 percent of Ridgemark's average flow, meaning the City would still need to maintain and eventually replace substantial treatment capacity of its own — this alternative addresses only a fraction of the problem at a cost approaching the full replacement project. Staff does not recommend this alternative.

### 6.3 Interim measures only (no capital project at this time)

Staff also evaluated continuing the current interim operating posture — rental pump contingencies, manual monitoring, and reactive repair — without a capital commitment at this meeting. This alternative fails on its face: it does not bring the City into compliance with the cease-and-desist order, exposes the City to the $10,000-per-day penalty beginning July 1, 2027, and carries an unquantified but real risk of a fifth overflow, additional Regional Board enforcement, and potential referral to the Attorney General's office for a facility under a standing order that has not begun corrective construction. Staff does not recommend this alternative.

## Appendix A: Cost Control Measures and Response to Prior Capital Project Performance

Councilmember Shiraishi has asked, in finance subcommittee discussion preceding this item, why the Council should trust a $41.6 million estimate from a Public Works Department that brought forward the Civic Center parking structure at $9.2 million and closed it out at $14.8 million — a 61 percent overrun. Staff does not dispute the comparison and addresses it directly rather than in a footnote.

The parking structure overrun had three identifiable causes, documented in the 2022 post-project audit that the Finance Department prepared at Council's direction: (1) the design was authorized at 15 percent completion with an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a detailed quantity takeoff; (2) the project was delivered design-bid-build, with the low bidder submitting a price that excluded soil remediation costs later found to be required, generating $2.9 million in change orders after contract award with no owner leverage to contest them; and (3) there was no standing contingency policy, so cost growth was absorbed by sequential Council actions rather than a pre-authorized reserve, each of which arrived after the cost had already been incurred rather than before.

This project is structured specifically to close each of those three gaps:

**Design maturity at authorization.** The $41.6 million figure presented in Section 4 is built on a 30-percent design package, including a completed condition assessment of all three major structures (Section 6.1), geotechnical borings at both new clarifier locations and the new pump station pad, and a quantity-based cost estimate reviewed by an independent third-party estimator retained by Finance. This is a materially more mature basis than the parking structure's 15-percent estimate. The November 2026 return to Council will carry a Guaranteed Maximum Price built on 100-percent design — full construction drawings, not a percentage allowance — which is the appropriate point for the Council to hold staff to a number.

**Delivery method and change-order exposure.** The CMAR method was chosen in part because it eliminates the specific failure mode that produced the parking structure's largest change order: a low bidder pricing to the drawings as issued and then claiming differing site conditions after award. Under CMAR, the contractor prices the project with full knowledge of the geotechnical and condition-assessment findings before the GMP is set, and the GMP itself becomes a contractual ceiling subject to defined, bilaterally negotiated allowances — not an open-ended time-and-materials exposure. Any change order that would pierce the GMP requires either an owner-directed scope change or a differing site condition meeting a contractually defined threshold, both of which are documented and reported to Council rather than absorbed silently into project cash flow.

**Contingency governance.** Section 4.1's $3.8 million contingency and $1.6 million escalation reserve are held under a written contingency management policy, attached to this staff report as Attachment 4, that the parking structure project did not have. Under that policy, the Project Executive (Section 5.2) may authorize draws up to $250,000 per change order without further Council action, cumulative draws are tracked against the full contingency balance and reported to the finance subcommittee quarterly beginning with the first quarter after construction notice to proceed, and any single draw or cumulative total that would exceed the authorized contingency requires a Council action before the work proceeds, not after. This is the reverse of the parking structure's sequence, in which Council authorizations followed cost already committed.

Staff has offered, and recommends, that the finance subcommittee chaired by Councilmember Shiraishi receive the quarterly contingency reports directly, in addition to the standing capital project updates provided to the full Council, for the duration of construction.

## Appendix C: Anticipated Council Questions and Staff Responses

**Q: Why is the City paying $1.2 million in financing costs on top of $33 million in SRF principal? Isn't SRF supposed to be low-cost money?**

The $1.2 million figure covers SRF loan origination fees, bond counsel and disclosure costs associated with the rate covenant required by the loan agreement, and capitalized interest during the construction period — interest that accrues on drawn loan funds before the project generates revenue to service the debt, which is standard for SRF construction-period financing and is itself financed within the loan rather than paid from current rates. At 1.6 percent, the SRF loan remains the least expensive financing instrument available to the City; a comparable revenue bond issuance was estimated by the City's municipal advisor at 4.1–4.6 percent given current market conditions, which would add approximately $19 million in additional interest cost over the loan term. Staff considered and rejected bond financing for this reason.

**Q: What happens if a fifth overflow occurs before construction is complete?**

The phasing plan in Section 3.2 is designed specifically to reduce, not eliminate, this risk. Ontiveros's office cannot represent to Council that zero risk exists during a 34-month construction period on an active treatment plant; no responsible engineering judgment would make that claim. What the phasing plan does is ensure that at every point in construction, the plant retains more operational redundancy than it has today — where a single mechanical failure at the headworks or pump station has caused three of the four current overflows. The reactivated flow equalization basin (Section 3.2, Phase 3) provides an additional buffer specifically during the highest-risk wet-weather periods of clarifier replacement. Any overflow that did occur during construction would be reported to the Regional Board under the same protocol as the four prior events, and the Board has been briefed that the City is under active construction as a mitigating circumstance, which does not eliminate liability but is relevant to enforcement discretion.

**Q: Why does the owner's team need a new Project Manager position rather than using existing engineering staff?**

Public Works' current capital projects staff — two engineers — are fully committed to the City's ongoing street resurfacing program, the Corcoran Avenue storm drain project, and routine capital maintenance across water, sewer, and streets. Adding a $41.6 million, 34-month project with the phasing complexity described in Section 3.2 to that workload without dedicated staffing would either delay the existing capital program or under-resource oversight of this project at exactly the phase transitions (Section 3.2) where operational risk is highest. The position is budgeted within the $1.0 million construction management line in Section 4.1 and is proposed as a limited-term position tied to the project duration, reverting when the project closes out in January 2030.

**Q: Has the Regional Board indicated it would accept this schedule?**

Board staff have reviewed the draft schedule informally and indicated it satisfies the order's requirement for construction to begin by July 1, 2027, provided the City demonstrates continuous progress toward that date. Board staff have not issued a formal concurrence letter, and none is required by the order; the order's enforcement trigger is the construction start date itself, not an intermediate approval. Staff recommends providing the Board with the adopted schedule following this Council action as a good-faith update, which is not required but is consistent with the cooperative posture the City has maintained with the Board since the order was issued.

**Q: Could the City phase the funding request — for example, authorize CMAR preconstruction now and defer the SRF application until design is further along?**

Staff does not recommend this. SRF applications are processed on a rolling basis but the State's intake queue means later submission risks losing priority position relative to other agencies' applications, and the loan closing target of December 2026 (Section 3.3) assumes a May 2026 submission. Deferring the application would not reduce the City's cost exposure — design and CMAR preconstruction work proceeds regardless under this proposal — but would push loan closing later, compressing the schedule buffer described in Section 2.3 and increasing risk of missing the July 2027 construction start.

## Appendix D: Glossary of Terms

**CMAR (Construction Manager at Risk):** A project delivery method in which a construction firm is engaged during the design phase to provide cost and constructability input, then commits to a Guaranteed Maximum Price for construction, assuming the risk of cost overruns above that price except for defined, contractually limited circumstances.

**GMP (Guaranteed Maximum Price):** The maximum amount a CMAR contractor will be paid for construction, negotiated with the owner once design is sufficiently complete to price the work with specificity; costs above the GMP are borne by the contractor absent an approved change order.

**SRF (State Revolving Fund):** A low-interest loan program administered by the State Water Resources Control Board to finance water and wastewater infrastructure, funded jointly by state and federal appropriations and repaid by borrowing agencies over terms up to 30 years.

**Proposition 218:** A provision of the California Constitution requiring local agencies to notice affected property owners and conduct a public protest hearing before increasing certain property-related fees, including sewer service charges; if a majority of affected property owners submit written protests, the agency may not impose the increase.

**Flow equalization basin:** A holding basin that captures and temporarily stores incoming wastewater flow during peak periods, releasing it back into the treatment process at a controlled rate once peak flow subsides, used here as an operational buffer during clarifier construction.

**Firm capacity:** The maximum flow a system of pumps or treatment units can reliably handle assuming the single largest unit is out of service, used as the standard measure of true redundant capacity rather than nameplate total capacity.

**Wet-weather peak flow:** The maximum flow rate experienced during storm events, when stormwater infiltration and inflow into the sanitary sewer system substantially exceeds average dry-weather flow; the current headworks and pump station lack sufficient firm capacity to handle this peak, which has been the proximate cause of three of the four overflow events described in Section 2.2.
