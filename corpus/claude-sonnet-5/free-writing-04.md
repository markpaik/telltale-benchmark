# On the Weight of Unsent Letters

There is a drawer in every life—sometimes literal, sometimes only in the mind—where the unsent letters accumulate. Not the ones we forgot to write, but the ones we wrote and folded and addressed and then, for reasons that seemed sound at the time, never sent.

I think about the physics of this. A letter written but not sent has a strange mass. It weighs more than a letter sent and forgotten. It weighs more, even, than a letter never written at all, because the never-written letter doesn't exist to press against anything, while the unsent letter sits there, folded, taking up space in a drawer or a mind, radiating a low static hum of *almost*.

We tell ourselves we didn't send it because the timing was wrong, or because they wouldn't understand, or because saying nothing was kinder, or safer, or simpler. Sometimes this is even true. But often the real reason is smaller and more embarrassing: we were afraid of what would happen if the words left our hands and became real in someone else's.

A sent letter can be misread, dismissed, or—worst of all—answered in a way we didn't want. An unsent letter can only ever be perfect, because it never has to survive contact with another person's actual response. This is its appeal and its poison. We keep it pristine by keeping it powerless.

I wonder sometimes about all the unsent letters accumulating across all the drawers in all the world at this very moment. The apology that would have cost the writer their pride but might have given the reader something to finally rest on. The confession of love written at two in the morning with a clarity that daylight embarrassment later overwrote. The letter to a parent, now dead, that says the thing that needed saying while there was still a person alive to receive it.

Not every letter should be sent. Some are better as an exercise, a private reckoning, a way of thinking a feeling through until it's small enough to hold without letting it hold you. The page can be a place to put something down rather than a place to send something out. That's a legitimate use of paper.

But I suspect we know, in each case, which kind of letter we're holding. We know whether we didn't send it because it was finished, or because we were unfinished with our fear. The tell, usually, is whether we keep rereading it. A letter that has done its private work goes quiet after a while. A letter that still burns in the drawer is still waiting for its actual recipient—not us, rereading, but the other person, receiving.

I don't know what to do with this except notice it. Perhaps that's enough for today: to notice the specific weight of what we've written and not sent, and to ask, honestly, whether we're protecting the other person from something, or protecting ourselves from them.

The stamp is cheap. The rest is the hard part.
