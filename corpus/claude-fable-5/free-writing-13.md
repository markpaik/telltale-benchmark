# The Wolf in the Bell

The council had already raised the money. That was the first thing the churchwarden told Ilse when she stepped out of the van, before she had her boots on, before she'd even looked up at the tower. Eleven thousand euros, nine years of raffles and harvest suppers, a bequest from a woman who had died at ninety-four and never once missed a Sunday. The new bell was as good as cast. They only needed someone qualified to confirm that the old one couldn't be saved.

"You'll write a report," he said. "For the diocese."

"I'll listen first," said Ilse.

---

The bell was hung in 1742 and had not come down since. The foundry mark was worn smooth but she knew it by the shape of the crown, six loops instead of four, a family of founders who had worked out of a town on the river until a flood took the workshop and the family both. They cast perhaps two hundred bells in sixty years. Fewer than forty were still ringing.

She climbed the tower with her father's tuning forks in a canvas roll and a tablet she rarely used. The forks were older than she was. He had ground them himself against a reference bell in a museum basement, a bell so perfectly in tune with itself that the curator would not let anyone strike it with anything harder than a felt mallet. He'd told her that standing inside its sound was like standing inside a held breath.

The village bell was not like that. She'd known before she climbed, from the way the warden talked about it. *It's not a bad bell,* people always said, in the tone they used for a relative with a limp. *It's just got that thing in it.*

---

A bell is not one note. It is five notes pretending to be one, and the pretending is the whole art. The hum lies lowest, a full octave below the note you'd name if someone asked. Above it, the prime. Above that, the tierce, a minor third, the partial that gives bronze its particular grief. Then the quint, then the nominal, ringing an octave above the prime and so bright it is almost all you hear in the first half-second. The note you think you hear — the strike note — is not actually present in the metal at all. The ear manufactures it from the others. A bell's voice is a thing that happens inside the listener.

When the partials align, the bell is said to be *true.* When they don't, a founder will put it on a vertical lathe and turn away metal from inside the waist, a few grams at a time, testing after each pass, until the five notes agree. A bell can only be tuned downward. You cannot put bronze back.

Ilse set the first fork against the lip and struck.

The hum was fine. The prime was fine. The nominal was a hair sharp, well within what anyone would call acceptable. It was the tierce. The tierce sat nearly a quarter-tone flat, and because it was flat, it beat against the prime — a slow pulse inside the sound, three or four times a second, a throb like something alive under the tone. Founders called it a wolf. She'd heard worse. She had also heard it before, though she had never been in this tower.

She struck again and closed her eyes and after a moment she had it. A recording. Her father had played it for her when she was sixteen, a cassette of village bells from a radio program, the tape hissing. *Listen to this one,* he'd said. *They think it's broken.* And then, when the bell had rung out and the wolf had come up through it: *That is the sound of someone stopping.*

She'd asked what he meant. He'd said the founder had turned that bell on the lathe and taken the tierce down toward true, pass by pass, and then at some point had lifted the cutter and walked away. Maybe the light was going. Maybe the bronze had a void in it and he was afraid of breaking through. Maybe he struck it once and thought, *no, that's enough, that's the one,* and nobody has ever been able to say he was wrong, because two hundred and fifty years of people had climbed into bed on that sound and got up again on it.

---

She went down and found the warden in the vestry filling out a form.

"Well," he said.

She told him the truth, which was complicated. The bell was not cracked. The canons were sound. The clapper had worn a flat into the soundbow that should be addressed — turn the bell a quarter, let it strike fresh metal — but that was maintenance, not failure. The tierce was flat. It could, in principle, be corrected. There was enough thickness at the waist. A good foundry could bring it true in a day.

"And it would sound better?"

"It would sound correct."

He looked at her. He was a farmer, she thought, or had been; he had the hands. "That's not what I asked."

"No," she said.

---

Outside, a woman was waiting by the van. Seventies, a cardigan, a dog on a lead that had stopped wanting to go anywhere. She'd heard the bell being struck from her garden — three notes at odd intervals, not the hour — and had come up to see.

"Are you the one who's going to take it down?"

"I'm the one who's going to say whether it has to come down."

"And does it?"

"No."

The woman nodded as though that were the end of the conversation, and then did not leave. After a while she said, "My husband used to say it sounded like the wireless. Before the station came in properly. That wobble." She was looking at the tower. "He's been dead eleven years. It still sounds like that."

Ilse said nothing. She had learned that the most useful thing she could do, most days, was stand next to someone while they listened.

---

She wrote the report that night in the guesthouse. She wrote that the bell was structurally sound, that it required re-hanging and a quarter-turn, that its tierce was flat of true by approximately forty cents and that this had been so since casting. She wrote that correction was possible and that she did not recommend it, and then she sat for a long time trying to phrase the reason in language the diocese would accept.

In the end she wrote: *The bell has been in continuous use for 284 years. Its current voice is the one the parish knows.* It was not what she meant. What she meant was closer to what her father had said on the stairs to the museum basement, after the held-breath bell, when she'd asked why anyone would ring a flawed one when true ones existed.

He'd stopped on the landing to rest, because he'd already been ill by then, though she hadn't known it.

"A true bell," he said, "tells you what time it is."

He started up again, slower.

"The other kind tells you where you are."

---

She left the next morning before the hour. She was on the road out, past the last house, when it rang — eight o'clock, eight strikes — and she pulled onto the verge and cut the engine and put the window down.

The nominal came first, bright and quick. Then the body of the sound filled in under it, and then, a half-second behind, the wolf: that slow throb rising through the bronze, three beats a second, steady as a pulse. Someone had lifted a cutter from a lathe in 1742 and let it stand. Across the fields the dog that didn't want to go anywhere lifted its head.

She counted the strikes and started the engine and drove on, and for a long way after she could still hear it, though she knew — she had the forks in the back to prove it — that the sound was not in the metal, and never had been.
