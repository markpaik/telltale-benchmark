# ANNUAL PERFORMANCE REVIEW

**Employee:** Jerome Castellanos
**Title:** Teacher, Eighth-Grade Mathematics; Mathematics Department Chair
**Review Period:** August 4, 2025 – May 29, 2026
**Reviewer:** Nadia Haddad, Principal, Saguaro Bend Middle School
**Evaluation Instrument:** Arizona Four-Level Educator Effectiveness Rubric

---

## 1. Context of This Evaluation

This evaluation is written in the second year of Saguaro Bend's placement in targeted state support for mathematics, a designation that followed three consecutive years of declining math proficiency schoolwide. It is also written under two constraints that bear directly on Mr. Castellanos and that I address candidly in Section 5: the district instruction that no more than 20 percent of a building's certificated staff may be rated Highly Effective in a single cycle, and the projected 6 percent enrollment decline that will eliminate two positions at this school in the spring.

## 2. Goals Set for 2025–2026 and Performance Against Them

**Goal 1: Raise eighth-grade math median student growth percentile (SGP) to 55 or above, from a prior-year baseline of 49.**

Mr. Castellanos exceeded this goal by a margin that requires no interpretation. His students posted a median SGP of 68, the highest of any math teacher in the Canyon Vista Unified School District, not merely the highest in this building. In a school under targeted support specifically for math, this is the single most consequential instructional result produced by any member of this faculty this year. The growth was not concentrated among students who arrived proficient; his roster included two co-taught sections with a combined special education population of 31 percent, and his growth results held across subgroups. When the state support team visited in February, his classroom was the one they asked to return to.

**Goal 2: Design and operate a supplemental intervention structure for students two or more grade levels behind in mathematics.**

Mr. Castellanos proposed, staffed, and ran the Saturday Academy from October through April. He recruited three colleagues, wrote the diagnostic entry assessments himself, and served 96 students across the year — roughly one in eight students in this building. Attendance held above 70 percent per session after November, which for a voluntary weekend program at a school where 62 percent of students qualify for free or reduced-price meals is an outcome I did not expect and cannot overstate. He also negotiated donated breakfast from two local businesses without being asked to, having identified early that hungry students were leaving at the mid-morning break.

**Goal 3: As department chair, implement common formative assessments across grades 6–8 and lead data cycles every three weeks.**

Fully met. The department ran seventeen data cycles this year. Mr. Castellanos's protocols were adopted by the science department in the spring at their request. Two of the four math teachers he supervises showed their first positive growth results in three years, and both credited the data cycles by name in their own self-assessments. His leadership of adults has become as much of an asset as his own teaching.

**Goal 4: Complete leadership preparation activities in anticipation of the assistant-principal residency.**

Mr. Castellanos completed every element within his control: coursework toward his administrative certificate, the district leadership cohort, and a full application to the residency stipend program. The board froze that stipend in January. This goal was not met for reasons entirely outside his performance, and I have marked it accordingly. It would be dishonest to record it as anything other than a district failure to hold up its end.

## 3. Observation Record

Three formal observations (September 18, December 4, March 11) and six informal walkthroughs. All three formal observations were scored Highly Effective on the state rubric's instructional domains. The December observation of a lesson on linear systems, in which students critiqued three deliberately flawed worked solutions and defended revisions to peers, was among the strongest lessons I have observed in nineteen years in this profession. Cognitive demand, student discourse, and checks for understanding were exemplary in all three formal cycles. There were no incidents of concern, no parent complaints, and no attendance issues during the review period.

## 4. Self-Assessment Excerpt and Reviewer Response

> **Teacher Self-Assessment (submitted May 8, 2026):** "I'm proud of what the department did this year, and I want to be honest that I'm at a decision point. I applied for the AP residency because I believe I can do this work at a larger scale, and when the stipend was frozen, nobody at the district level talked to me about what comes next. I told Ms. Haddad in April that if there's no path here, I'll look outside the district, and I meant it. That's not a threat — it's just where I am after eleven years."

**Reviewer response:** I take this statement at face value and it is fair. Mr. Castellanos did everything the district asked of aspiring leaders and the district withdrew the mechanism midyear. I have communicated to the assistant superintendent in writing, twice, that the departure of this teacher would be the single most damaging staffing loss this school could sustain during targeted support, and I have proposed interim alternatives detailed in Section 6.

## 5. Summative Rating and Bearing of District Constraints

**Summative Rating: HIGHLY EFFECTIVE (Level 4 of 4)**

Under the district's 20 percent cap, this building may rate no more than seven of its thirty-six certificated staff Highly Effective this cycle. Mr. Castellanos occupies one of those slots without competition; if the cap permitted only one such rating in this building, it would be his. I state this in the record deliberately, because caps of this kind invite the suspicion that top ratings are rationed rather than earned, and I want no ambiguity: this rating reflects the evidence, and the cap constrained no part of my judgment in his case.

The enrollment-driven reduction of two positions does not place Mr. Castellanos at risk; his seniority, certification area, and rating protect him under the reduction-in-force order. The frozen residency stipend is the relevant constraint, and its effect is retention risk, not evaluation. I cannot pay him what the board withdrew. What I can do within my authority, I have committed to in his goals below.

## 6. Goals for 2026–2027

1. **Interim administrative internship, building-funded.** Mr. Castellanos will serve as administrative designee two days per month, lead the master-schedule build for 2027–2028, and chair the school's targeted-support steering committee — documented leadership hours creditable toward his administrative certification, funded from building discretionary money at my direction, pending the district restoring the residency stipend, which I will continue to pursue on his behalf each quarter.
2. **Sustain math growth at a median SGP of 60 or above** while transferring ownership of the data-cycle protocols to a designated successor chair-in-training, so the system survives him.
3. **Institutionalize the Saturday Academy** by producing a written program manual and training two additional staff leads, with a target of 100 students served.
4. **Complete the administrative certificate** by March 2027; I will serve as his site mentor of record.

**Reviewer's closing note:** Mr. Castellanos should read this evaluation as it is intended — as both the strongest review I will write this year and an explicit institutional argument for keeping him.

# ANNUAL PERFORMANCE REVIEW

**Employee:** Yolanda Begay
**Title:** Teacher, Seventh-Grade English Language Arts; Master Schedule Coordinator; State Testing Coordinator
**Review Period:** August 4, 2025 – May 29, 2026
**Reviewer:** Nadia Haddad, Principal, Saguaro Bend Middle School
**Evaluation Instrument:** Arizona Four-Level Educator Effectiveness Rubric

---

## 1. Context of This Evaluation

Ms. Begay is in her ninth year at Saguaro Bend and carries the heaviest non-teaching operational load of any classroom teacher in the building: she builds and maintains the master schedule for 740 students and coordinates all state assessment administration. This evaluation must do two things honestly at once. It must credit classroom instruction that has been consistently strong, and it must document two significant operational failures this year — the February materials-order lapse and chronic delays in schedule corrections — that affected hundreds of students and multiple departments. Neither honesty can be allowed to erase the other.

## 2. Goals Set for 2025–2026 and Performance Against Them

**Goal 1: Maintain Effective or higher ratings across all instructional domains in seventh-grade ELA, with a focus on writing conferencing.**

Met. All three formal observations rated Effective across the instructional domains, with the writing-conference structures she piloted in the fall rated Highly Effective in the individual domain of feedback to students in the January observation. Her students' ELA proficiency held steady at 44 percent against a district seventh-grade average of 39, in a year when the building's attention and resources were pulled heavily toward math. Her classroom remains a settled, rigorous place; her revision-portfolio system has been in use for four years and two newer ELA teachers adopted it this year on her informal mentorship.

**Goal 2: Administer the spring state assessment cycle with zero irregularities and full materials readiness by the state's deadlines.**

Not met, and this is the most serious finding in this evaluation. Ms. Begay did not submit the February materials order for the state assessment by the vendor deadline. The lapse was not discovered until testing materials failed to arrive in the first March shipment. As a result, 210 seventh- and eighth-grade students — more than a quarter of this school — were pushed into the state's make-up testing window in April. The direct consequences were substantial: eleven days of disrupted instruction for affected sections during a targeted-support year in which instructional minutes in math are under state scrutiny; a required irregularity report to the district assessment office; and staff overtime to proctor the compressed make-up schedule. I want the record to reflect the mitigation as well: once the failure surfaced, Ms. Begay's recovery work was excellent. She built the make-up matrix in a weekend, personally proctored six sessions, and we completed the window with zero test-security incidents and 99 percent participation. The recovery does not offset the failure, but it is part of the truthful account.

**Goal 3: Deliver schedule corrections to departments within five instructional days of request.**

Not met. Three department chairs — mathematics, science, and electives — reported in writing that correction requests submitted in September and again in January sat for three to five weeks. The math chair documented two co-taught sections that ran out of certification compliance for nineteen days because a correction was not processed. I raised the turnaround problem with Ms. Begay in our November check-in; the January backlog indicates the fix did not hold. In her defense, and it is a real defense, the correction backlog and the materials lapse share a root cause examined in Section 4: this role, as currently constructed, exceeds what one person teaching a full load can execute.

**Goal 4: Train a backup testing coordinator.**

Partially met. Ms. Begay identified and began training a counselor as backup, but the training was incomplete by February — which is precisely why the materials deadline had no second set of eyes on it. This goal returns, sharpened, in next year's goals.

## 3. Observation Record and Specific Incidents

Formal observations on October 2, January 22, and April 16; all rated Effective overall, with instructional domains ranging from Effective to Highly Effective and no domain below Effective. The specific incidents of record are: (a) the missed February materials order and resulting make-up window for 210 students, documented in the district irregularity report dated March 9, 2026; and (b) the schedule-correction backlogs of September–October and January–February, documented in written complaints from three department chairs. There are no classroom-conduct, attendance, or parent-complaint incidents in her file this year.

## 4. Self-Assessment Excerpt and Reviewer Response

> **Teacher Self-Assessment (submitted May 6, 2026):** "I own the materials order. There is no version of that where the mistake isn't mine — I had the deadline in my calendar and I buried it under registration corrections and I didn't ask for help. I'm not going to hide behind workload. But I do want it on the record that I have asked for a release period for coordination duties every year for three years, and I've been doing a job that other middle schools in this district split between a teacher and an office professional. I love this school. I can't keep doing all three jobs at the level all three deserve."

**Reviewer response:** I accept both halves of this statement, and I hold them together rather than letting either cancel the other. The accountability is hers: the deadline was known, calendared, and missed, and a professional in year nine coordinating high-stakes testing is expected to escalate before failure, not after. The structural claim is also accurate: her release-period requests are in my files for three consecutive years, comparably sized district schools do split these duties, and I did not secure the release. The failure occurred inside a structure I maintained. Both facts appear in this evaluation, and both are reflected in the redesign of her assignment below.

## 5. Summative Rating and Bearing of District Constraints

**Summative Rating: EFFECTIVE (Level 3 of 4)**

I considered whether the operational failures warranted a Developing rating and concluded they do not. The state rubric weights instructional practice and student outcomes as its core, and on those Ms. Begay's evidence is solidly Effective, in places stronger. The failures were in ancillary duties, were non-recurring within the year once surfaced, were met with full ownership and competent recovery, and arose partly from a staffing structure I am responsible for. A Developing rating would misdescribe her teaching and would punish her for a role design the school imposed. Conversely, the operational record forecloses any consideration of Highly Effective this cycle regardless of the 20 percent cap; the cap was not the deciding factor in her case, and she should know that.

The two-position reduction bears on Ms. Begay indirectly but materially: with fewer staff next year, coordination duties cannot simply be handed off, which makes the restructuring in Goal 1 below a necessity rather than a preference. Her position itself is not at risk in the reduction.

## 6. Goals for 2026–2027

1. **Restructured assignment.** Effective August 2026, Ms. Begay receives one release period for coordination duties, funded within the building's revised allocation; in exchange, the testing-coordinator role transfers fully to the counseling office by October 1, with Ms. Begay serving as trainer of record. She retains the master schedule only.
2. **Schedule-correction service standard:** all correction requests logged in a shared tracker visible to department chairs, with 90 percent resolved within five instructional days, reviewed with me monthly.
3. **Instructional goal:** raise seventh-grade ELA proficiency from 44 to 48 percent, and formally document the writing-conference model for buildingwide adoption.
4. **Escalation practice:** any at-risk deadline in her coordination duties is flagged to me in writing no later than ten days before due — a professional habit, not a punishment, and the specific behavior whose absence caused this year's most serious failure.

# ANNUAL PERFORMANCE REVIEW

**Employee:** Dustin Marchetti
**Title:** Teacher, Middle School Science (Grades 7–8)
**Review Period:** August 4, 2025 – May 29, 2026
**Reviewer:** Nadia Haddad, Principal, Saguaro Bend Middle School
**Evaluation Instrument:** Arizona Four-Level Educator Effectiveness Rubric

---

## 1. Context of This Evaluation

Mr. Marchetti is completing his third year of teaching. This was a consequential and uneven year: it included two Developing formal observations in the fall, a formal improvement plan opened in December and closed successfully in March, a parent complaint about grading practices that reached the district level in October, nine absences in the second semester, and — on the other side of the ledger — the largest single-year proficiency gain of any science teacher in this building. This evaluation is written with particular care because Mr. Marchetti sits at the intersection of every constraint this school faces: he is in the probationary window, the school must shed two positions in the spring, and his rating this cycle carries real employment consequence. He is owed precision, and I have tried to give it to him.

## 2. Goals Set for 2025–2026 and Performance Against Them

**Goal 1: Raise science proficiency from a baseline of 19 percent to at least 28 percent.**

Exceeded. Mr. Marchetti's proficiency rate rose from 19 to 31 percent — a twelve-point gain, the largest in the science department and among the largest in the building in any subject. The trajectory matters as much as the endpoint: the growth accelerated after the improvement plan took hold in January, with his benchmark data showing the steepest climb in the third quarter. This is the pattern of a teacher who responded to structured feedback rather than plateauing under it, and it is the single strongest piece of evidence in his file.

**Goal 2: Demonstrate Effective-level classroom management and lesson structure across formal observations.**

Not met in the fall; met by spring. The September 25 and November 13 formal observations were both rated Developing, with consistent deficiencies in the same domains: pacing that left ten to fifteen minutes of unstructured time at the end of lessons, inconsistent behavioral expectations across class periods, and objectives that were posted but not assessed. These two observations triggered the improvement plan described in Section 3. The post-plan observation on March 19 rated Effective in every previously deficient domain, and my May 7 walkthrough confirmed the gains were holding: transitions were tight, an exit ticket aligned to the objective closed the lesson, and the seventh-period class that had been his most difficult was working productively in lab groups.

**Goal 3: Communicate grading criteria to students and families through published rubrics and a current gradebook.**

Not met in the first semester. In October, a parent complaint regarding grading in Mr. Marchetti's eighth-grade sections went to the district after the parent was unable to resolve it at the school level — in part because Mr. Marchetti's gradebook had not been updated in six weeks and the weighting of lab reports had been changed midquarter without notice to families. The district's review found no grade to be inaccurate, but found the communication and gradebook-currency concerns substantiated. Corrective expectations (weekly gradebook updates, published rubrics, no midterm weighting changes) were folded into the improvement plan and have been met since January without exception. I verified gradebook currency in four spot checks between February and May.

**Goal 4: Maintain reliable attendance and full participation in department collaboration.**

Not met. Mr. Marchetti was absent nine days in the second semester. Seven of the nine were documented under FMLA-qualifying family medical circumstances that he disclosed to me appropriately, and those seven are protected and carry no evaluative weight, though their instructional impact on students is a reality we planned around together. Two absences were undocumented; both fell on Fridays before breaks, and I addressed them in a documented conversation on April 24. He has been present every duty day since. He missed three of eleven science data-cycle meetings, all corresponding to absence dates; when present, colleagues report he participates in good faith and came prepared with student work.

## 3. The Improvement Plan: Record and Outcome

Opened December 8, 2025, following the two Developing observations; targets covered lesson pacing, consistent behavioral systems, objective-aligned assessment, and the grading-communication expectations from the October complaint. Support provided: six coaching cycles with the instructional coach, three peer observations of Mr. Castellanos's classroom, and biweekly check-ins with me. The plan was closed as **successfully completed on March 27, 2026**, on the strength of the March 19 observation and coaching documentation. I note for the record that Mr. Marchetti engaged with the plan without defensiveness from its first meeting — a professional posture that is not universal among teachers on plans and that materially predicted its success.

## 4. Self-Assessment Excerpt and Reviewer Response

> **Teacher Self-Assessment (submitted May 11, 2026):** "The fall was the hardest stretch of my career and I earned some of it. My management wasn't where it needed to be and I let the gradebook slide, and a family was right to be frustrated. What I'd ask is that the second semester count too. I did everything the plan asked. My kids grew more than anyone's in the department. I know two positions are going away and I know where I sit on the seniority list. I want to stay here. I just want to be judged on the whole year, not on October."

**Reviewer response:** This request is reasonable and this evaluation grants it: the whole year is here, the fall included and the spring included. What I cannot do is let the spring retroactively erase the fall, because the rubric measures the year's demonstrated practice, and for half of this year that practice was below the Effective bar. What I can do, and have done in Sections 5 and 6, is document the trajectory explicitly, because the trajectory is what a rating alone cannot show and what any future employment decision about Mr. Marchetti ought to weigh.

## 5. Summative Rating and Bearing of District Constraints

**Summative Rating: DEVELOPING (Level 2 of 4)**

This rating reflects a year that was Developing in the fall by direct observation and Effective by spring: averaged honestly across the review period, and with a substantiated complaint and unmet attendance goal in the file, the evidence does not yet support Effective for the year as a whole. It is, however, a Developing rating with a rising slope, and I have written the following sentence for the file deliberately: **based on the March observation, the closed improvement plan, and the twelve-point proficiency gain, my professional expectation is that Mr. Marchetti performs at the Effective level in 2026–2027 if the spring-semester practice is sustained.**

The constraints bear on him more heavily than on any other teacher I am evaluating this year. The two-position reduction will be executed under the district's reduction order, which weights certification area, seniority, and rating. Mr. Marchetti is the least senior member of the science department, and a Developing rating does not strengthen his position. I owe him the truth: he is at risk. I also owe the district my professional judgment, which I will state in the staffing process as I state it here — his growth data and improvement-plan outcome make him a teacher this school should be fighting to develop, not to release, and dual science certification (see Goal 4 below) would materially change his retention standing. The 20 percent Highly Effective cap has no bearing on his rating this cycle.

## 6. Goals for 2026–2027

1. **Sustain Effective-level practice in all instructional domains** across all formal observations, with the first formal observation scheduled early (September) at Mr. Marchetti's own request, to put spring-level practice in the record as soon as possible.
2. **Raise science proficiency from 31 to 38 percent**, with quarterly benchmark checkpoints reviewed with the instructional coach.
3. **Zero undocumented absences**; continued appropriate use of protected leave as needed, with sub plans of instructional quality pre-loaded for any foreseeable absence.
4. **Add a certification endorsement** (middle grades general science plus one additional area) by February 2027, which broadens his utility and his standing in any future reduction; the building will cover the exam fee.
5. **Maintain the grading-communication practices** established under the plan: weekly gradebook currency, published rubrics, and no midterm weighting changes, verified by quarterly spot check.

**Reviewer's closing note:** Mr. Marchetti finished this year a demonstrably better teacher than he began it, by the hardest route there is. This evaluation records the whole road, and next year's should record where it leads.

## 1. Attestation of Compliance with the Rating Distribution Instruction

Saguaro Bend Middle School employs thirty-six certificated staff subject to the state rubric this cycle. Under the district instruction limiting Highly Effective ratings to no more than 20 percent of a building's certificated staff, this school may issue a maximum of seven such ratings. Of the three evaluations transmitted herewith, one (Castellanos) carries a Highly Effective rating; across the full building cycle, six Highly Effective ratings were issued in total. The school is in compliance with the instruction.

I attach one comment for the record, as the instruction permits. In the case of the three evaluations transmitted here, the cap did not alter any rating: Mr. Castellanos's evidence commands the rating regardless of the ceiling, and neither Ms. Begay's nor Mr. Marchetti's evidence supported Highly Effective this cycle independent of it. I nonetheless ask the district to note that a fixed percentage cap, applied to a building in targeted state support, creates a structural tension the district should confront: the state's improvement framework asks us to concentrate and reward exceptional practice in exactly the buildings where the cap most tightly rations recognition of it. If a year arrives in which this building produces an eighth teacher whose evidence merits the top rating, I will not manufacture a deficiency to satisfy the arithmetic, and I would rather raise that now, in a year where the conflict is hypothetical, than later, when it is not.

## 2. Statement Regarding the Spring Reduction in Force

The projected 6 percent enrollment decline will eliminate two positions at this school. The three evaluations transmitted here interact with that reduction as follows, and I ask that this statement travel with the personnel files into the staffing process:

**Castellanos.** Fully protected under the reduction order and rated Highly Effective. The employment risk in his case runs in the opposite direction: it is the risk that he leaves us. The January freeze of the assistant-principal residency stipend removed the district's only formal retention mechanism for this teacher in the same year he produced the highest math growth in the district inside a targeted-support building. I have implemented building-level interim measures, documented in his evaluation, funded from my discretionary allocation. They are a bridge, not a solution. I request, for the third time in writing, that the district restore the residency stipend or authorize an equivalent leadership pathway for Mr. Castellanos before the fall semester. If he resigns, the district will have traded a frozen stipend for the loss of its strongest mathematics educator and the collapse of the Saturday Academy serving 96 students, and I want the paper trail on that trade to be complete.

**Begay.** Not at risk in the reduction. However, the reduction constrains the remedy her evaluation requires. The operational failures documented in her file arose in substantial part from a coordination load that comparably sized schools in this district divide among multiple staff. With two fewer positions next year, I cannot solve her overload by redistribution alone; I have solved it instead by restructuring — a release period in exchange for transferring the testing-coordinator function to the counseling office. I flag for Human Resources that this restructuring is a condition of the performance expectations set in her 2026–2027 goals. If the release period is disallowed in the fall allocation, the goals in her evaluation will require formal amendment, because I will not hold a teacher accountable next year to standards premised on a structure the district declined to fund.

**Marchetti.** At material risk. He is the least senior member of the science department and carries a Developing rating, and under a mechanical application of the reduction order he is plausibly one of the two positions eliminated. I ask the district to weigh three facts before that outcome is finalized. First, his twelve-point proficiency gain was the largest in this building's science department and among the largest schoolwide, achieved in a targeted-support year. Second, he completed his improvement plan successfully and his post-plan observation rated Effective in every previously deficient domain; the Developing rating describes his year, not his current practice. Third, the district's own investment calculus should matter: it has spent three years and a full coaching cycle developing this teacher to the threshold of effectiveness, and releasing him now surrenders that investment at the moment it begins to pay. If the reduction can be absorbed through the two anticipated retirements in the elective departments — which my fall projection suggests is arithmetically possible — I recommend that path. If it cannot, I request that Mr. Marchetti be placed on the district recall list with priority standing and that this memorandum accompany his file to any district school with a science vacancy.
