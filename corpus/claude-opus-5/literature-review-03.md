# Unconditional Cash Transfers and Household Outcomes in the United States: A Review of the Evidence for Foundation Decision-Making

**Prepared for the Board of Directors, Alamo Basin Community Foundation**
**Tomás Iribarren, PhD, Senior Research Officer**
**With Chidinma Okorafor, Research Associate, and Landon Yazzie, Program Officer**
**July 2026**

---

## 1. Introduction: The Question Before the Board

The Foundation's investment committee has asked whether a $6 million, three-year unconditional cash pilot serving 500 households in three San Antonio zip codes represents a defensible use of endowment capital. That question is not primarily a question about whether cash helps poor people. It is a question about what a foundation of this size can learn, demonstrate, and durably change by spending roughly $4,000 per household per year for three years in a metropolitan area where the relevant population numbers in the tens of thousands.

The distinction matters because the evidence base has been read badly by nearly everyone who has read it publicly. Advocates have cited early pilot results as though they settled the matter. Critics have cited the 2024 three-year randomized trial as though it refuted everything prior. Both readings mistake heterogeneity for contradiction. The studies in this literature differ so substantially in transfer size, duration, benefit interaction, counterfactual income, outcome measurement, and study population that a finding of uniform effects would itself be suspicious. The analytic task is not to determine who is right but to determine which design features produce which results, and then to ask which of those features the Foundation's proposed pilot would share.

This review addresses four questions. First, what does the accumulated evidence say about the effects of unconditional cash transfers on employment, earnings, health, housing stability, and child development in high-income country settings, with emphasis on U.S. pilots? Second, where do the findings conflict, and what design differences account for those conflicts? Third, which populations relevant to Bexar County and South Texas remain unstudied or badly underrepresented? Fourth, what follows for a funder considering a pilot of this specific scale?

Two constraints shape the review. County Commissioner Odalys Fuentes-Nabors has indicated she will not advance matching county funds without evidentiary grounding, which places a premium on findings that are transportable to a South Texas context rather than findings drawn from Stockton, Chicago, or Cambridge. Two donor-advised fund holders have signaled they may relocate their accounts if the pilot proceeds, which places a premium on the review being candid about what the evidence does not show. A review that oversells will not survive contact with either audience. This document is written to be defensible to a skeptic.

A note on scope. The review covers unconditional or near-unconditional periodic cash transfers to working-age households in high-income settings. It excludes conditional transfer programs, one-time emergency grants, in-kind benefit expansions, and the large international literature on transfers in low- and middle-income countries, except where the latter is used to establish a contrast in effect magnitude. It includes the U.S. and Canadian negative income tax experiments of the 1960s and 1970s where those studies bear directly on labor supply questions, since they remain the largest randomized evidence on that outcome and their exclusion from contemporary policy debate has been an error.

## 2. How the Literature Was Searched

Searches were run in EconLit, the Social Science Citation Index, and PsycINFO, covering January 1997 through March 2026. The start date was chosen to capture the post-welfare-reform U.S. literature while permitting deliberate backward citation chasing into the negative income tax experiments, which we retrieved by hand rather than by database query.

Search strings combined three concept blocks: transfer type (*unconditional cash*, *guaranteed income*, *basic income*, *negative income tax*, *cash transfer*, *income supplement*, *child allowance*), population (*low-income*, *poverty*, *household*, *family*), and outcome (*employment*, *labor supply*, *earnings*, *health*, *mental health*, *housing*, *food security*, *child development*, *material hardship*). Terms were adapted to each database's controlled vocabulary. Gray literature was pursued separately: we obtained evaluation reports, preregistrations, and technical appendices from twelve U.S. pilot evaluations, including several where the peer-reviewed publication and the fuller technical report differ in what they report. We also searched the Open Science Framework and AEA RCT Registry for registered but unpublished trials, both to identify results still in the field and to assess publication bias.

The search returned 1,410 records after deduplication. Title and abstract screening by two reviewers (Okorafor and Yazzie, with Iribarren adjudicating disagreements) advanced 268 records to full-text review. Sixty-three studies met inclusion criteria: 14 randomized controlled trials, 19 quasi-experimental studies, 12 qualitative studies, seven cost analyses, and 11 evidence syntheses or meta-analyses. Inclusion required a defined comparison condition or, for qualitative work, a defined sample of transfer recipients with documented data collection procedures. We excluded advocacy reports without methods sections, simulation studies without empirical calibration to observed program data, and studies where the transfer was bundled inseparably with case management or employment services.

Effect estimates, transfer amounts, transfer duration, follow-up windows, outcome measurement mode, and benefit-offset handling were extracted into a workbook available to the board on request. Where studies reported multiple specifications, we extracted the preregistered primary specification. Where no preregistration existed, we extracted the specification the authors identified as primary and flagged the absence.

Three limitations of the search deserve statement. First, several of the most-discussed U.S. pilots have produced multiple overlapping reports from the same underlying sample; we treated these as single studies to avoid double-counting, but reasonable analysts might partition them differently. Second, gray literature retrieval is inevitably incomplete, and pilots that produced disappointing results are less likely to have published anything at all. Third, we did not search Spanish-language databases, which is a real omission given the review's application to a majority-Hispanic county and one we flag in the gaps section.

## 3. Synthesis

### 3.1 Labor Supply: The Central Contested Finding

No outcome has generated more disagreement, and the disagreement is more tractable than it appears.

The early wave of U.S. municipal pilots reported employment gains. The Stockton demonstration (West et al., 2021) found full-time employment among treatment recipients rose from 28 percent to 40 percent over twelve months, against a control group increase from 32 to 37 percent. The framing that followed — that cash *increases* work — traveled widely and quickly. It was, on the evidence presented, an overreading. The Stockton sample was small, the employment measure was self-reported, the transfer was $500 monthly for two years, and the study period overlapped the initial pandemic labor market disruption. The finding was directionally interesting and statistically fragile.

Against this, the three-year randomized trial reported by Vivalt et al. (2024) delivered $1,000 monthly to 1,000 participants across two states with a 2,000-person control group, and found a reduction of approximately 1.3 weekly work hours and a decline in labor market participation of about two percentage points. Individual income excluding the transfer fell by roughly $1,500 annually. Because this study was larger, longer, preregistered, and used administrative earnings records alongside survey data, it has appropriately displaced the earlier pilots as the anchor estimate for labor supply.

The two findings are not irreconcilable, and treating them as a controversy obscures what is actually informative. Four design differences do most of the explanatory work.

*Transfer magnitude.* Vivalt et al. (2024) transferred $12,000 annually; Stockton transferred $6,000. Standard labor supply theory predicts income effects scaling with transfer size, and the negative income tax experiments established this decades ago. Robins (1985), synthesizing four NIT experiments, found husbands reduced work by roughly 5 percent, wives by 21 percent, and single female heads by 13 percent, with effects increasing in guarantee level. Ashenfelter and Plant (1990), reanalyzing the Seattle-Denver experiment, found labor supply responses concentrated among secondary earners and among those with the largest guarantees. That a $1,000 monthly transfer produces measurable work reduction while a $500 monthly transfer does not is unsurprising. It is the expected pattern.

*Duration and salience.* The Stockton twelve-month results were measured while participants knew the transfer would end. Vivalt et al. (2024) measured at three years within a defined three-year program. Both are time-limited, which means both understate the labor supply response to a permanent policy — a point Ashenfelter and Plant (1990) made about the NIT experiments and which remains unaddressed by any contemporary U.S. study. Every U.S. pilot in this review is a temporary program. None estimates the parameter that matters for permanent policy.

*Measurement mode.* Self-reported employment and administrative earnings diverge systematically in this literature, and the divergence is not random. Miller et al. (2022), reporting on the Baby's First Years trial, found survey-reported maternal employment moved differently from administrative records in the same sample. Jaroszewicz et al. (2022), studying one-time transfers, found that respondents' self-assessed financial wellbeing improved while objectively measured outcomes did not, and interpreted this as a genuine psychological effect rather than measurement error. Both interpretations may be correct simultaneously, but studies relying on self-report will systematically report more favorable findings than studies relying on administrative data, and roughly half the pilots in our set rely primarily on self-report.

*Counterfactual income and benefit interaction.* This is the least discussed and possibly most important difference. Where transfers were excluded from benefit calculations by waiver, recipients received the full transfer value. Where they were not, effective transfer value was substantially lower, and marginal tax rates on earnings differed between arms. Shaefer et al. (2018), examining the interaction of cash assistance with existing benefit structures, documented effective marginal rates exceeding 50 percent for some household configurations. A study that does not report benefit-offset handling is reporting an unknown treatment. Eleven of the 33 quantitative studies in our set do not report it.

Two further observations complicate the picture in ways favorable to neither advocacy position. First, work reduction is not automatically a cost. Vivalt et al. (2024) found increased time in leisure and modest increases in reported time with children; other work has found transfer recipients using reduced hours for education or job search. Whether a 1.3-hour reduction is a problem depends on what replaces it, and the studies measuring the replacement activity are thin. Second, several studies find effects on job *quality* rather than job *quantity*. Qualitative work from multiple pilots (Baker et al., 2020; Castro Baker et al., 2020) describes recipients declining exploitative shifts, leaving unsafe workplaces, or refusing to accept the first available offer. These are real changes that show up as reduced hours in administrative data and as improved circumstances in interviews. A review that reports only the hours finding misrepresents what happened.

**Assessment.** The best current estimate is that transfers in the range of $500–$1,000 monthly produce small reductions in labor supply, concentrated among secondary earners and scaling with transfer size, with ambiguous welfare implications. Claims that cash increases employment are not supported by the strongest designs. Claims that cash produces large work disincentives are not supported either; the NIT experiments' largest estimates fall well below the responses assumed in most political argument.

### 3.2 Mental Health and Psychological Wellbeing: Effects That Fade

The mental health literature shows a characteristic pattern: strong early effects, weaker sustained effects, and a persistent gap between subjective and clinical measures.

Early pilot reports were striking. West et al. (2021) found treatment participants reported significantly lower anxiety and depression symptoms at twelve months, with effect sizes in the range typical of active clinical intervention. Qualitative accounts across pilots consistently describe reduced financial stress, improved sleep, and a sense of control (Castro Baker et al., 2020; Rodriguez and Steinbaum, 2023). The quasi-experimental literature on income shocks provides supporting evidence: Costello et al. (2003), studying casino revenue distributions to Eastern Band Cherokee households, found reductions in psychiatric symptoms among children in households moved out of poverty, with persistence documented in later waves (Akee et al., 2018).

The three-year trial found much less. Vivalt et al. (2024) reported improvements in stress and mental distress in the first year that had substantially attenuated by years two and three, with no detectable effects on several health outcomes at endline, including physical health measures, healthcare utilization apart from increased dental and hospital visits, and biomarker-adjacent indicators. Miller et al. (2022) similarly found no detectable maternal mental health effects in the Baby's First Years trial's first-year results.

Reconciling these requires attention to three features.

*Time profile.* The pattern of first-year improvement followed by attenuation appears in more than one study and is consistent with adaptation. A transfer relieves acute financial stress on arrival; recipients adjust expectations and consumption; the marginal psychological benefit declines. This is a real finding about transfers, not a methodological artifact, and it has direct implications for a three-year pilot: the wellbeing benefits are likely front-loaded and the endline measurement will understate the integrated benefit over the transfer period. A study measuring only at endline will find little. A study measuring only at twelve months will overstate.

*Counterfactual severity.* Costello et al. (2003) studied households moving across the poverty line; the effects were concentrated in those households and absent among households already above it. Where baseline deprivation is severe, transfers produce larger psychological effects. The three zip codes in the Foundation's proposal have median household incomes below $32,000, which places them closer to the Costello counterfactual than to the samples in several recent pilots that recruited across broader income bands.

*Measurement.* Nearly all mental health findings in this literature rest on brief self-report screeners — PHQ-8, GAD-7, Kessler-6 — administered to participants who know their treatment status. Response bias in unblinded trials with subjective outcomes is well documented and cannot be assumed away. The studies using administrative healthcare utilization or prescription data (fewer than five in our set) find smaller effects than the studies using screeners. This is the single most likely explanation for the divergence between the enthusiastic early pilot reports and the sober endline findings of the larger trials, and it should temper any claim about mental health benefits regardless of what a future evaluation reports.

### 3.3 Housing Stability: The Most Consistent Finding

Housing outcomes hold up better than any other domain in this literature, and the reason is instructive.

Across designs and settings, transfers reduce housing instability. Studies report fewer moves, reduced eviction filings, lower rates of doubling-up, and reduced homelessness entry. Dwyer et al. (2023), in a randomized evaluation of transfers to individuals experiencing homelessness in Vancouver, found treatment recipients moved into stable housing faster and spent fewer nights sheltered, with net social service cost savings. Palmer et al. (2019), examining emergency financial assistance in Chicago, found reduced homelessness among applicants receiving assistance relative to those turned away when funds were exhausted — a quasi-experimental design that exploits arbitrary allocation and is among the cleaner identification strategies in the set. Evans et al. (2016) found similar effects using a comparable design in another jurisdiction.

Why do housing effects replicate when earnings and mental health effects do not? Three reasons.

First, housing outcomes are measured administratively — eviction filings, shelter entry records, address changes in utility or program data — rather than by self-report. The measurement problem that plagues the mental health literature is largely absent.

Second, housing instability is proximate to cash in a way that employment is not. A $500 shortfall against rent produces eviction; $500 in hand prevents it. The causal chain is one step. Employment effects run through job search, employer decisions, childcare availability, and health, each of which introduces variance and delay.

Third, housing outcomes are binary and consequential, which means detection does not require the statistical power that continuous outcomes with small effects demand. A pilot underpowered for a 1.3-hour work change may be adequately powered for an eviction-rate change.

Two caveats. Dwyer et al. (2023) studied a one-time $7,500 transfer to a screened sample excluding participants with severe substance use or mental health conditions, and the exclusion likely inflates transportability concerns — the population most at risk of chronic homelessness was screened out. And the emergency assistance studies (Evans et al., 2016; Palmer et al., 2019) study crisis-triggered assistance, not periodic unconditional income; whether monthly transfers prevent the crises that trigger applications is a different question, less directly evidenced.

Even discounted, housing is where the literature is most defensible. For a foundation seeking a demonstrable, administratively verifiable outcome for a skeptical county commissioner, this is the relevant finding.

### 3.4 Child Development and the Infant Brain Question

The Baby's First Years trial is the most methodologically ambitious child-focused study in this literature and has been the most misreported.

Troller-Renfree et al. (2022) reported that infants in households receiving $333 monthly showed higher-frequency EEG activity in bands associated with later cognitive development, relative to infants in households receiving $20 monthly, at age one. The finding was widely reported as demonstrating that cash changes infant brain development. Later waves have not sustained it. Subsequent reports from the same trial have found the age-one differences did not persist at later measurement points, and the trial's broader developmental and cognitive outcomes have been more muted than the initial EEG result implied (Sauval et al., 2024; Gennetian et al., 2024).

Three points about how to read this.

First, non-persistence of a single-wave neural finding is not evidence of fraud or failure. Early-life neural measures are noisy, developmental trajectories converge and diverge, and the age-one difference may have been real and consequential without producing detectable differences at age three. It may also have been noise. The trial's design does not distinguish these, and the honest statement is that the infant brain finding is currently unreplicated within its own study.

Second, the trial's transfer was small — $4,000 annually — and the comparison condition was not zero but $240 annually. Any inference about the effects of a $12,000 transfer from a $4,000 transfer requires an extrapolation the data do not support.

Third, the quasi-experimental literature on larger, longer income changes finds more durable child effects. Akee et al. (2010, 2018) found improved educational attainment and reduced criminality among Cherokee children exposed to household income increases during childhood, with effects concentrated among initially poorer households. Hoynes et al. (2016), studying EITC expansions, found improved birth weight. Aizer et al. (2016), studying the Mothers' Pension program in the early twentieth century, found increased longevity and educational attainment among recipient children. These studies examine transfers lasting years, sometimes the whole of childhood, and they find effects. The tension between the durable quasi-experimental findings and the null-to-modest experimental findings is best explained by duration and dose rather than by one literature being wrong.

For a three-year pilot, the implication is uncomfortable. The child development effects that the durable literature identifies come from income changes sustained across developmental periods. A three-year transfer to a household with a two-year-old covers a meaningful window; the same transfer to a household with a fourteen-year-old does not. And no U.S. pilot has followed children long enough to observe the outcomes the quasi-experimental work identifies.

### 3.5 Material Hardship, Food Security, and Consumption

Findings here are more consistent than in employment or mental health, though smaller than advocacy summaries suggest.

Transfers reduce food insecurity. Multiple pilots report reductions in skipped meals and in affirmative responses to standard food security screeners. Studies of pandemic-era stimulus and expanded Child Tax Credit payments provide the largest-sample evidence: Parolin et al. (2021) and related work found substantial reductions in food insufficiency coinciding with monthly CTC payments and rapid reversal upon expiration. The reversal is as informative as the reduction. It indicates the effect is a direct consumption effect, not a durable capability change, and that it ends when payments end.

Consumption patterns contradict the persistent political claim that recipients spend transfers on temptation goods. Expenditure tracking across pilots consistently shows the largest categories are food, housing, transportation, and utilities. Evans and Popova (2017), meta-analyzing the international literature, found no significant effect of transfers on alcohol or tobacco expenditure across 19 studies, with several showing declines. This finding is robust and should be stated plainly to skeptical audiences: the empirical basis for the temptation-goods concern is essentially absent.

Debt findings are mixed and under-theorized. Some pilots report reduced reliance on payday lending; others report increased formal debt, which may reflect improved credit access rather than deteriorating position. Vivalt et al. (2024) found increased spending across categories with no evidence of asset accumulation. The absence of asset accumulation matters: a transfer that improves consumption without building a buffer leaves recipients as exposed to shocks at the end as at the beginning, which is consistent with the food security reversal pattern.

### 3.6 Cost Analyses

Seven cost analyses met inclusion criteria, and the honest summary is that this literature is weak.

Most compute program cost per participant and compare it to estimated savings in a narrow set of public systems — emergency shelter, emergency department use, criminal legal contact. Dwyer et al. (2023) reported net savings from reduced shelter use exceeding transfer costs for their homeless-experienced sample, which is the most favorable and most credible finding in the group, and which depends heavily on the extremely high public cost of the counterfactual for that specific population.

Analyses applied to general low-income populations find far less. When the counterfactual is a household that is housed, employed intermittently, and not in contact with the criminal legal system, the public systems offset is small. Several analyses in our set discount future benefits inconsistently, count avoided costs that accrue to different agencies than those bearing program costs, or extrapolate one-year effects across decades without justification. Two do not report a discount rate.

The relevant point for the board is that the cost-offset case for unconditional transfers to a general low-income population is not established. Where transfers are targeted to very high-cost populations — chronic homelessness, frequent hospital utilizers — the offset case is stronger and better evidenced.

## 4. Gaps in the Evidence

The gaps most relevant to this Foundation are the gaps that most directly govern whether the findings above transport to Bexar County.

**Mixed-status immigrant households.** Effectively no study in this set reports results for households with mixed immigration status, and most pilots excluded undocumented individuals or did not collect status data. This is a first-order problem for a San Antonio pilot. Mixed-status households face different benefit-offset structures, different tax filing exposure, and documented chilling effects on program participation independent of eligibility. Whether transfers reach these households, whether recipients fear participation, and whether effects differ are unanswered questions. Any Bexar County pilot will encounter them immediately.

**Rural and colonia residents.** The entire U.S. pilot literature is municipal. Stockton, Chicago, Cambridge, Los Angeles, Saint Paul — all urban, all with transit, concentrated service infrastructure, and functioning rental markets. Colonia residents in South Texas face unpaved roads, irregular water and wastewater service, informal land tenure, and housing that is often self-built and unmortgaged. The housing stability outcome — the most robust in the literature — may not even be measurable in the same way where residents own substandard structures on contract-for-deed arrangements rather than renting. Nothing in this evidence base speaks to that setting.

**Households with a disabled adult.** Disability is present in the samples but almost never analyzed as a subgroup, and households receiving SSI face the sharpest benefit-offset problem in the U.S. system, where unearned income reduces benefits nearly dollar-for-dollar and asset limits are extremely low. A transfer could reduce net household resources or trigger a loss of Medicaid categorical eligibility. Not one study in our set adequately addresses this. Given that disability rates in low-income Bexar County zip codes exceed national averages, this gap is disqualifying for any design that does not address it explicitly before enrollment.

**Spanish-language and binational literature.** We did not search Spanish-language databases, and evaluations of Mexican transfer programs relevant to comparable populations may exist outside our search. This is our limitation, not the field's, and it should be corrected before any design decision.

**Long-run outcomes.** No U.S. pilot has followed participants beyond about three years post-enrollment. The outcomes that would justify the investment — educational attainment, sustained earnings trajectories, intergenerational effects — are observed only in quasi-experimental studies of much longer income changes.

**Permanent-policy parameters.** Every study estimates the effect of a temporary transfer. Labor supply, savings, and fertility responses to permanent income differ from responses to known-temporary income by an amount no U.S. study estimates.

**Spillovers and general equilibrium.** With 500 households in three zip codes, local rent responses, effects on non-recipient neighbors, and community-level dynamics are plausible and entirely unmeasured in this literature.

**Publication and reporting bias.** Registry searching identified trials with no published results. Pilots reporting favorable findings publish faster and more visibly. The observed distribution of published effects is very likely more favorable than the true distribution.

## 5. Implications for the Foundation

**The strongest claims are the narrowest.** Transfers reduce housing instability and food insecurity while they are being paid. These findings are administratively verifiable, replicated across designs, and defensible to a skeptical audience. Employment gains are not supported. Sustained mental health improvements are not supported. Infant brain development effects are unreplicated. A board resolution asserting anything beyond the narrow claims will not survive scrutiny by Commissioner Fuentes-Nabors's staff or by a departing donor's advisors.

**The pilot as specified is underpowered for its most contested outcome.** Five hundred households, with attrition, will not detect a 1.3-hour weekly employment change or a small mental health effect. If the pilot is justified as generating evidence on employment, it will fail on its own terms and produce an ambiguous null that opponents will read as refutation. If it is justified as generating evidence on housing stability using administrative eviction and utility data, it is plausibly adequate. The board should choose which claim it is making before it commits, not after.

**Transfer size and duration should be set deliberately.** At $6 million over three years for 500 households, per-household transfers land near $4,000 annually before administrative costs — closer to Baby's First Years than to the three-year trial. That dose produced modest, largely non-persistent effects on the child outcomes it targeted. If the board wants effects comparable to the larger trials, it must transfer more to fewer households. Serving 250 households at $8,000 annually is a different study with different power properties and a different political profile. This is the central design tradeoff and it should be made explicitly.

**Benefit-offset handling is not an implementation detail.** It determines the actual treatment. Without SNAP, Medicaid, SSI, and housing-assistance disregards secured in advance, some recipients — particularly households with a disabled adult — could be made worse off. Securing waivers or hold-harmless arrangements is a precondition for a defensible pilot, not a downstream task.

**The unstudied populations are the argument for doing this.** The generic question of whether cash helps low-income urban households does not need another 500-household pilot. The question of whether transfers reach and benefit mixed-status households, colonia residents, and households with a disabled adult is genuinely open, directly relevant to South Texas, and unanswerable from existing evidence. A pilot designed to answer it would contribute something the field does not have. A pilot designed to replicate Stockton in San Antonio would not.

**Effects end when payments end.** The Child Tax Credit reversal is the clearest evidence in the literature. A three-year pilot should be planned with an exit strategy and should not be described to participants or to the public as a pathway out of poverty. It is three years of relief with measurable benefits during the payment period and limited evidence of persistence after.

**On the donor-advised fund holders.** The evidence does not support the strongest advocacy claims, and a review that pretended otherwise would be a liability. It also does not support the standard objections: the temptation-goods concern is empirically unfounded (Evans and Popova, 2017), and the labor supply response, while real, is modest and smaller than commonly assumed. A candid presentation of both is more likely to retain skeptical donors than an enthusiastic one.

**Recommendation.** The evidence supports a pilot, but not the pilot as currently specified. We recommend the board direct staff to develop a revised design that (a) names housing stability and material hardship as primary outcomes with administrative measurement, (b) concentrates the transfer among fewer households at a higher dose, (c) intentionally oversamples mixed-status households, colonia residents, and households with a disabled adult, (d) secures benefit disregards before enrollment, and (e) commits to publishing results regardless of direction. That design would be defensible to the commissioner, honest to the donors, and would generate evidence the field currently lacks.
