# Main Street Resilience Program

**Applicant:** City of Sorrel Creek, Oklahoma
**Project Title:** Sorrel Creek Confluence Resilience Initiative
**Amount Requested:** $4,000,000 over four years
**Local Match Committed:** $800,000 (20 percent)
**Primary Contact:** Renata Duchamp, Grants Coordinator
**Authorized Representative:** Ingrid Sundberg, City Manager
**Submission Date:** August 22

## Statement of Need

Sorrel Creek sits at the confluence of Sorrel Creek proper and the Tallgrass River in north-central Oklahoma, a location that made the town a viable trading point in 1893 and has made it a flood risk in every decade since. The community of 31,240 has absorbed two federally declared flood events in five years. Those two events — May 2019 and April 2023 — damaged 214 structures and generated $18.6 million in paid insurance claims. The damage was not evenly distributed. It concentrated in the eleven-block downtown core and the three residential blocks immediately north of it, where the oldest housing stock in the city sits between four and eleven feet below the base flood elevation.

The repetition matters more than the totals. Of the 214 damaged structures, 34 have been damaged in both events. These are properties FEMA classifies as repetitive loss, and several as severe repetitive loss. They are the reason the city's flood insurance claim history has not improved despite two rounds of individual repairs. Each cycle, the same buildings take on water, the same claims are filed, the same repairs are made with the same materials at the same elevation, and the community waits for the next storm. The National Flood Insurance Program has paid on some of these addresses four and five times going back to the 1990s. Repairing them again is not mitigation. It is a subscription.

The economic consequences have compounded past the point where a single bad year explains them. Downtown vacancy stands at 29 percent. Citywide sales tax receipts are down 8 percent against 2019 — not 8 percent against a peak year, but against the last full year before the first of the two floods, meaning the city has now spent five years failing to recover a baseline it once took for granted. In a municipality that draws roughly 62 percent of its general fund from sales tax, an 8 percent decline is a structural budget problem, not a soft quarter. It has already produced a hiring freeze that is directly relevant to this application and disclosed in the Organizational Capacity section below.

The vacancy figure understates the disruption. Of the storefronts still occupied, an internal survey conducted by the Sorrel Creek Main Street Association in February found that eleven operate on month-to-month leases signed after 2023 because neither landlord nor tenant would commit to a longer term in a building that has flooded twice. Three businesses that reopened after 2019 did not reopen after 2023. Two relocated to a highway commercial strip outside the corporate limits, taking their sales tax with them. The pattern is familiar to anyone who has watched a flood-prone downtown decline: each event removes a few more anchors, the remaining foot traffic thins, marginal businesses fail for reasons that look unrelated to flooding, and the district hollows out from the inside while the buildings still stand.

Who bears this is not incidental to the case for funding. Twenty-four percent of Sorrel Creek residents are Native American, most enrolled in nations with historic territory in this part of Oklahoma. Nineteen percent of residents live below the federal poverty line, roughly five points above the state figure and nearly seven above the national. Thirty-two percent of downtown businesses are minority-owned. The three residential blocks north of downtown that hold the majority of the repetitive-loss properties are also the blocks with the lowest median household income in the city and the highest share of households without a vehicle — a fact that shaped both evacuation performance in 2023 and the relocation assistance design described later in this application.

Low-income households in flood-prone housing face a particular trap. They own or rent the cheapest housing in the community precisely because it floods. They cannot sell it at a price that would buy anything else. They frequently lack flood insurance because the premium exceeds what they can carry, which means each event is an uninsured total or near-total loss absorbed by the household. After 2023, the city's code enforcement office documented nine households that returned to substantially damaged structures without permits or repairs because they had nowhere else to go. Those nine households are the sharpest expression of a problem that runs through several hundred more.

Emergency Manager Hector Villalpando's after-action review of the 2023 event identified a second-order risk that has not yet materialized but should inform the urgency here. Sorrel Creek's downtown electrical service, natural gas regulators, and telecommunications junctions are at or near grade across three blocks of Main Street and Choctaw Avenue. In 2023, floodwater reached within fourteen inches of the primary electrical vault serving the 100 and 200 blocks. Had it topped that vault, the outage would have extended past the flood zone to roughly 40 percent of the city's service area, including the hospital's secondary feed. The city has been fortunate twice. Planning around continued good fortune is not a defensible position for a public agency.

Sorrel Creek has done what it can with the tools available. The city adopted higher regulatory standards in 2020, requiring two feet of freeboard above base flood elevation for new construction and substantial improvement. It joined the Community Rating System and holds a Class 8 rating. It completed a hazard mitigation plan update in 2022 with county emergency management. It applied for and received a 2021 FEMA Hazard Mitigation Grant Program award. What the city cannot do with regulatory tools is remove existing structures from harm's way. Freeboard standards govern new construction; they do nothing for a 1924 building already sitting four feet below base flood elevation. Only acquisition, demolition, and hydraulic change address the existing risk, and those cost more than a community with an 8 percent sales tax deficit can generate.

The Main Street Resilience Program is a direct match for this need. The Trust's stated measures — structures removed from the 100-year floodplain, reduction in flood insurance claims, downtown occupancy, sales tax receipts, and jobs retained — are the same five indicators the City Council identified in its March resolution authorizing this application. That alignment is not coincidence. Sorrel Creek has been trying to move these five numbers since 2019 and has lacked the capital to do it.

## Project Description

The Sorrel Creek Confluence Resilience Initiative comprises three integrated components executed over four years: acquisition and demolition of 34 repetitive-loss properties, construction of eleven acres of stormwater detention configured as a floodable public greenway, and elevation of critical utility infrastructure across three downtown blocks. The components are engineered as one system. Acquisition creates the land area detention requires; detention lowers the flood profile that makes utility elevation effective at a feasible height; utility elevation preserves the commercial district that acquisition and detention are meant to protect.

### Component One: Acquisition and Demolition (Years 1–3)

The city will acquire 34 repetitive-loss properties through voluntary purchase at pre-flood fair market value. Twenty-six are single-family residential, five are small multi-family (two to four units), and three are commercial structures on the north edge of the downtown core. All 34 appear on the FEMA repetitive-loss inventory. All 34 sustained damage in both 2019 and 2023.

Participation is voluntary without exception. The city will not use eminent domain for any acquisition under this project, and the deed restrictions attached to acquired parcels will run with the land in perpetuity, restricting use to open space, recreation, or wetland management consistent with federal acquisition standards. Property owners will receive written notice of the voluntary nature of the program at first contact, again at offer, and again at closing.

Acquisition proceeds in three tranches. Tranche one (Year 1, twelve properties) covers parcels where owners have already submitted written interest — the city maintains a standing list from outreach conducted after the 2023 event, and 19 property owners have expressed interest, exceeding the tranche one target. Tranche two (Year 2, fourteen properties) and tranche three (Year 3, eight properties) will proceed as owners come forward. The city has identified nine alternate properties, all repetitive-loss, to substitute if any targeted owner declines. Declining carries no penalty and no change in city services.

**Environmental and cultural review.** Two of the 34 parcels lie within an area identified by the tribal environmental office as requiring formal consultation prior to any ground disturbance. Those two parcels are held in tranche three and will not proceed to demolition until written clearance is issued. This is not a scheduling convenience. It is the structural correction to the failure described in the Organizational Capacity section below, and it is embedded in the project schedule rather than appended as a condition.

The city's demolition contract will contain a clause prohibiting notice to proceed on any parcel absent a signed environmental clearance memorandum from the city's designated environmental compliance officer, countersigned by the City Manager. The contractor's payment application form requires the clearance memorandum number for each address. A contractor who mobilizes on an uncleared parcel does not get paid for that parcel, and the clause survives change orders.

**Relocation assistance.** Households displaced by acquisition receive relocation assistance consistent with Uniform Relocation Act standards, whether or not URA formally applies to a given funding stream. Assistance includes moving costs, up to 42 months of rental differential for tenants, and replacement housing payments for owner-occupants where the acquisition price is insufficient to obtain comparable decent, safe, and sanitary housing. Given the vehicle-access data cited above, the city will provide relocation counseling in person at the residence rather than requiring office visits, and will conduct sessions in Spanish on request. Two of the 34 properties are occupied by households with a member enrolled in a tribal nation; the tribal environmental office has agreed to route those households to tribal housing services for supplemental support.

**Demolition and site restoration.** Demolition follows asbestos and lead-based paint survey, which is budgeted for all 34 structures given the age of the housing stock. Salvageable material — dimensional lumber, brick, architectural millwork — will be offered to the Main Street Association's building materials reuse pilot before disposal. Sites are graded to drain, seeded with a native mix specified by the university engineering extension, and either incorporated into the greenway footprint or maintained as deed-restricted open space.

### Component Two: Detention and Floodable Greenway (Years 2–4)

Eleven acres of stormwater detention will be constructed on the contiguous land created by acquisition, supplemented by 3.2 acres of city-owned property currently used for equipment storage that will be relocated. Total project footprint is 14.2 acres running roughly northwest to southeast along the historic creek alignment.

The design is not a fenced basin. It is a floodable greenway: a linear park with a hardened multi-use trail on the upper bench, native prairie planting on the middle terrace, and a widened low-flow channel with constructed wetland at the bottom. In ordinary conditions it functions as public open space with trail connections to the existing school campus and the downtown district. In a design storm it holds water. Preliminary modeling by the university engineering extension indicates the configuration provides approximately 46 acre-feet of detention, reducing the 100-year water surface elevation in the downtown core by an estimated 1.8 to 2.4 feet depending on storm distribution.

That reduction is the hinge of the entire project. At 1.8 feet, a significant share of downtown structures move from a condition where the 100-year event floods the first floor to one where it reaches the sidewalk and stops. That is the difference between a total interior loss and a wet weekend.

Final modeling and design will be completed in Year 1 under a professional services contract; the estimates above are preliminary and will be refined. The city commits to reporting the final modeled reduction to the Trust whether or not it matches the preliminary range.

Greenway design will proceed through a public process with at least three community meetings, one of which will be held in the affected residential blocks rather than at City Hall. The Main Street Association and the tribal environmental office will each hold a seat on the design advisory committee. The city has heard clearly from residents of the north blocks that they do not want the removal of their neighborhood to produce an amenity that serves everyone but them. Trail connections, lighting, and programming will be designed with that concern as an explicit criterion.

### Component Three: Utility Elevation (Years 2–4)

Three downtown blocks — the 100 and 200 blocks of Main Street and the 400 block of Choctaw Avenue — will receive elevation and hardening of critical utility infrastructure. Work includes relocating the primary electrical vault and four secondary transformers above the post-project base flood elevation, elevating natural gas regulator stations, raising telecommunications junction cabinets, installing backflow prevention on 22 sanitary sewer service connections, and elevating or floodproofing the two lift stations serving the district.

Utility elevation is sequenced after detention construction because the post-project base flood elevation determines the required elevation height. Building to pre-project elevations would cost substantially more and protect against a condition that will no longer exist. Design begins in Year 2 using modeled post-project elevations; construction proceeds in Years 3 and 4 as detention comes online.

The electrical vault relocation requires coordination with the investor-owned utility serving Sorrel Creek. A memorandum of understanding is in negotiation and is expected to be executed in Year 1. The utility has indicated in writing that it will contribute engineering review at no cost and will consider cost-sharing on the vault itself; no commitment has been made and none is assumed in this budget.

### Partner Roles

**County Emergency Management** provides floodplain mapping support, coordinates the project with the multi-jurisdictional hazard mitigation plan, and administers the joint public notification system that will carry construction-period detour and warning information. The county emergency manager serves on the project steering committee. The county's role is in-kind and formalized through an existing interlocal agreement.

**Tribal Environmental Office** holds consultation authority over the two parcels described above and reviews the greenway design for cultural resource implications across the full footprint. Consultation is a requirement, not a courtesy, and the project schedule treats it as a gating condition. The tribal environmental office has also agreed to advise on native plant specification for the greenway and to co-sponsor two of the three public design meetings. A consultation protocol memorandum was executed in April and is included as Attachment C.

**University Engineering Extension** performs hydrologic and hydraulic modeling, reviews design submittals at 30, 60, and 90 percent, and — critically, given the vacancy discussed below — provides the licensed professional engineering review the city cannot currently perform in-house. The extension will also place two graduate research assistants on the project for construction monitoring, at a cost included in the budget. A services agreement is executed and included as Attachment D.

**Sorrel Creek Main Street Association** manages business relocation for the three commercial acquisitions, operates a temporary relocation subsidy for businesses disrupted during utility elevation construction, and conducts the quarterly downtown occupancy and employment census that supplies two of the five Trust measures. The association's executive director serves on the steering committee. A subgrant agreement is included as Attachment E.

## Goals and Measurable Objectives

The project has four goals. Each carries objectives stated in the Trust's own measurement framework with baseline, target, source, and collection interval.

### Goal 1: Remove repetitive-loss structures from the 100-year floodplain.

**Objective 1.1.** Acquire and demolish 34 repetitive-loss structures by the end of Year 3. Baseline: 0. Target: 34 (12 by end of Year 1, 26 cumulative by end of Year 2, 34 by end of Year 3). Source: recorded deeds, demolition permits, and FEMA repetitive-loss inventory reconciliation. Interval: quarterly.

**Objective 1.2.** Record perpetual open-space deed restrictions on 100 percent of acquired parcels within 90 days of closing. Baseline: not applicable. Target: 100 percent. Source: county recorder. Interval: quarterly.

**Objective 1.3.** Reduce the number of structures in the 100-year floodplain by not fewer than 34 through acquisition and by an additional count through floodplain remapping following detention construction. Baseline: 341 structures in the effective 100-year floodplain. Target: 34 removed by demolition; a further reduction, quantity to be established by post-construction modeling in Year 4, through map revision. Source: FEMA Letter of Map Revision application and effective FIRM. Interval: annually, with LOMR submission in Year 4.

Objective 1.3's second element is stated without a numeric target because the city will not project a remapping outcome before final modeling. The city commits to submitting the LOMR and reporting the result.

### Goal 2: Reduce flood insurance claims and flood damage.

**Objective 2.1.** Reduce annual NFIP claim counts in the project area by 60 percent from baseline by the end of Year 4. Baseline: 34 repetitive-loss properties generating an average of 21 claims per flood event across the 2019 and 2023 events. Target: not more than 8 claims from the project area in any single event occurring after Year 3. Source: NFIP claims data obtained through the state NFIP coordinator. Interval: annually, event-triggered.

**Objective 2.2.** Reduce paid claim dollars in the project area by 65 percent from the 2019/2023 average. Baseline: $18.6 million across two events, of which $7.4 million was attributable to the 34 acquisition properties and the three utility-elevation blocks. Target: not more than $2.6 million from the project area in a comparable event. Source: NFIP claims data. Interval: annually, event-triggered.

Both objectives depend on a flood occurring for verification. Absent an event, the city will report the modeled reduction in expected annual damages using the FEMA benefit-cost methodology, updated with as-built conditions, as a proxy measure. The city will state clearly in each report which measure is being provided and why.

**Objective 2.3.** Improve Community Rating System classification from Class 8 to Class 6 by the end of Year 4, reducing NFIP premiums for all policyholders citywide by an additional 10 percent. Baseline: Class 8. Target: Class 6. Source: ISO/CRS verification cycle. Interval: annually.

### Goal 3: Restore downtown economic function.

**Objective 3.1.** Increase downtown occupancy from 71 percent to 85 percent by the end of Year 4. Baseline: 71 percent (29 percent vacancy), February census. Target: 76 percent Year 2, 80 percent Year 3, 85 percent Year 4. Source: Main Street Association quarterly storefront census, ground-truthed. Interval: quarterly.

**Objective 3.2.** Recover citywide sales tax receipts to 2019 levels in constant dollars by the end of Year 4. Baseline: 8 percent below 2019. Target: within 2 percent of 2019 by Year 3; at or above 2019 by Year 4. Source: Oklahoma Tax Commission municipal distribution reports, deflated by CPI-U. Interval: monthly, reported quarterly.

The city notes that sales tax receipts respond to regional and macroeconomic conditions the project does not control. Reporting will include a comparison against a peer set of four similarly sized north-central Oklahoma municipalities so that the Trust can distinguish project effect from regional trend.

**Objective 3.3.** Retain not fewer than 340 downtown jobs and add 60 net new jobs by the end of Year 4. Baseline: 347 jobs in the downtown district, February census. Target: 340 retained minimum; 400 total by Year 4. Source: Main Street Association employer census supplemented by state quarterly census of employment and wages at the ZIP level. Interval: semiannual.

**Objective 3.4.** Retain 100 percent of the three acquired downtown businesses within the downtown district through relocation assistance. Baseline: 3 businesses. Target: 3 relocated within district, 0 closures attributable to acquisition. Source: Main Street Association relocation case files. Interval: quarterly through Year 3.

### Goal 4: Protect critical downtown infrastructure.

**Objective 4.1.** Elevate or floodproof 100 percent of identified critical utility assets across three downtown blocks by the end of Year 4. Baseline: 0 of 31 identified assets. Target: 31 of 31. Source: construction close-out documentation and utility acceptance letters. Interval: quarterly beginning Year 3.

**Objective 4.2.** Eliminate flood-driven outage risk to the hospital secondary feed. Baseline: primary vault at 14 inches of freeboard in the 2023 event. Target: not less than 3 feet of freeboard above post-project base flood elevation. Source: as-built survey. Interval: one-time at completion.

**Objective 4.3.** Construct 14.2 acres of floodable greenway providing not less than 40 acre-feet of detention. Baseline: 0. Target: 14.2 acres, 40 acre-feet minimum. Source: as-built survey and final hydraulic model. Interval: at substantial completion, Year 4.

## Evaluation Plan

Evaluation is structured in three layers: implementation monitoring, outcome measurement, and independent verification. The design assumes the Trust will want to know not only whether numbers moved but whether the project caused them to move.

**Implementation monitoring** is continuous and internal. The Grants Coordinator maintains a project tracking register with a row for each of the 34 acquisitions, each construction contract, and each utility asset, carrying status, date, cost, and compliance documentation reference. The register generates the quarterly report to the Trust. Any variance greater than 60 days or 10 percent of a line item triggers written notice to the Trust within 15 days rather than waiting for the quarterly cycle. The city would rather report a problem early than explain a surprise late.

Environmental compliance receives separate monitoring. Each parcel carries a clearance file that must contain the environmental review determination, the tribal consultation record where applicable, the asbestos and lead survey, and the signed clearance memorandum before the file is marked cleared. The Grants Coordinator audits five files per quarter at random against the register and reports the audit result in the quarterly report, including any discrepancy found. This is a direct response to the 2021 deobligation and is described further below.

**Outcome measurement** follows the schedule in the objectives above. Data responsibilities are assigned: the Main Street Association collects occupancy, employment, and business relocation data; the Finance Director pulls sales tax data; the Emergency Manager obtains NFIP claims data through the state coordinator; the university engineering extension performs modeling and as-built verification; the Grants Coordinator compiles.

The city will collect two years of baseline data retrospectively where available — 2024 and 2025 occupancy, employment, and sales tax figures — so that trend is visible rather than a single pre-point. The Main Street Association has conducted its storefront census since 2018, which gives seven years of consistent methodology.

**Independent verification.** In Year 2 and Year 4, the city will contract an external evaluator with municipal resilience program experience to verify reported data and assess attribution. The Year 2 review is formative: it examines whether implementation is on schedule, whether relocation assistance is functioning as designed for the households actually served, and whether the environmental compliance controls are working under real conditions. Its purpose is to find problems while they are still fixable, and its findings go to the Trust unedited.

The Year 4 review is summative. It addresses the five Trust measures, the attribution question against the peer municipality comparison, and a qualitative assessment drawn from interviews with acquired-property households, downtown business owners, and partner staff. Both reports are budgeted and both are delivered to the Trust.

**Attribution.** The city will not claim that every downtown occupancy gain is a project effect. The evaluation design addresses attribution three ways: the peer municipality comparison for sales tax and occupancy; a within-district comparison between the three utility-elevation blocks and the remaining downtown blocks, which functions as a rough internal control; and interview data on decision-making from business owners who lease or renew in the district during the project period. Where attribution cannot be established, the evaluation will say so.

**Learning use.** Quarterly data goes to the steering committee, which meets monthly during construction seasons. Two decision points are pre-specified. At the end of Year 1, if acquisition is behind by more than four properties, the city will convert to a modified approach using the alternate property list and, if necessary, request Trust concurrence on a revised target. At the end of Year 2, if modeled detention performance falls below 40 acre-feet, the city will value-engineer the utility elevation scope upward to compensate rather than proceed with an under-performing system. Both contingencies are budgeted at a level that permits the adjustment.

## Organizational Capacity

The City of Sorrel Creek is a home-rule municipality operating under a council-manager form of government, with 148 full-time employees and an FY26 general fund budget of $19.4 million. The city has administered federal and state grant awards continuously since 1998, including CDBG, FEMA Public Assistance following both flood events, ODOT transportation enhancement funds, and Oklahoma Water Resources Board loan programs. The city's most recent single audit, for the fiscal year ended June 30, 2025, contained no findings and no questioned costs.

**Project leadership.** City Manager Ingrid Sundberg, in her sixth year at Sorrel Creek and nineteenth in Oklahoma municipal management, serves as executive sponsor with signature authority and chairs the steering committee. Emergency Manager Hector Villalpando, a certified emergency manager with eleven years in the position, serves as project director; he authored both flood after-action reviews and led the 2022 hazard mitigation plan update. Grants Coordinator Renata Duchamp administers compliance, reporting, and financial management. Public Works Superintendent Otis Brumley directs construction management. Mayor Franklin Standing Bear leads the local match effort and intergovernmental coordination.

This section discloses three matters that bear on the Trust's capacity assessment. The city has chosen to state them plainly rather than allow the Trust to discover them during due diligence.

### Disclosure One: 2021 FEMA Deobligation

In 2021 the city received a Hazard Mitigation Grant Program award for acquisition and demolition of flood-damaged residential structures. FEMA subsequently deobligated $1.1 million of that award because the city began demolition on a portion of the properties before environmental review had cleared.

The facts are not in dispute and the city does not contest the deobligation. Following the 2019 event, the city was under substantial pressure — from property owners, from neighborhood residents, and from the council — to remove damaged and hazardous structures. Demolition was initiated on several parcels in the belief that categorical exclusion applied and that documentation would follow. It did not apply to all of them, documentation had not been completed, and the work proceeded anyway. The failure was procedural, and it was the city's.

The city has taken five corrective actions.

First, environmental compliance authority was separated from project delivery. Environmental review determinations are now made by a designated environmental compliance officer in the City Manager's office who does not report to Public Works and has no schedule accountability. The person who benefits from moving faster no longer decides whether it is permissible to move.

Second, the clearance memorandum system described in the Project Description was adopted by council resolution in 2022 and applies to all city projects regardless of funding source. No notice to proceed issues on any ground-disturbing activity without a signed and numbered clearance memorandum. The requirement is written into the city's standard construction contract.

Third, all four staff named above completed federal environmental review training in 2023, and the Grants Coordinator holds a current certification in federal grant management.

Fourth, the tribal consultation protocol memorandum executed in April establishes a defined process, named points of contact on both sides, and a written clearance product — replacing what had been informal and inconsistent contact. The two parcels requiring consultation under this project are scheduled in tranche three specifically so that consultation has three years to complete without schedule pressure, and the city has committed in writing that those parcels will be dropped from the project rather than advanced on an incomplete consultation.

Fifth, the quarterly file audit described in the Evaluation Plan provides ongoing internal verification, with results reported to the Trust including any discrepancy.

The city has completed two subsequent projects with ground-disturbing activity under the new controls — a 2023 sanitary sewer rehabilitation and a 2024 park improvement — with no compliance findings. The 2021 project's remaining scope was completed under the corrected process, and the city's relationship with the state hazard mitigation office remains in good standing; a reference from the state hazard mitigation officer is included as Attachment F.

The Trust should weigh this history. The city's position is that an organization that has failed a specific control, been penalized $1.1 million for it, rebuilt the control, and operated under it for four years without recurrence is a more reliable steward of environmental compliance than one that has never been tested. The city offers the Trust the option of a special condition requiring pre-demolition clearance certification to the Trust on each parcel. It would be redundant with existing practice, and the city would accept it without objection.

### Disclosure Two: Vacant City Engineer Position

The city engineer position in Public Works has been vacant fourteen months. Two recruitments have failed to produce a hire. The competitive difficulty is regional — small Oklahoma municipalities are consistently outbid for licensed civil engineers — and it is compounded by the hiring constraints the sales tax decline has imposed.

Superintendent Brumley has covered the operational functions of the position, which is sustainable for maintenance and small capital work and is not sustainable for a $4.8 million construction program. The city is not asking the Trust to assume otherwise.

Three measures address the gap.

The university engineering extension agreement provides licensed professional engineering review at 30, 60, and 90 percent design and during construction, with two graduate research assistants for construction monitoring. This is a formalized services agreement with defined deliverables, not a collegial arrangement.

The project budget includes a full-time owner's representative — a licensed professional engineer under contract to the city — for the duration of construction, Years 2 through 4. This position is dedicated to this project and reports to the City Manager. Recruitment for a contract position is materially easier than for a permanent municipal position, and the city has received three qualified expressions of interest from firms in the Tulsa and Oklahoma City markets.

The city continues to recruit for the permanent position. Council approved a compensation study in June, and the FY27 budget includes a salary adjustment for the classification. The city will notify the Trust if the position is filled, and the owner's representative contract will continue regardless, because a newly hired city engineer would need to run the entire capital program and not this project alone.

The city considered whether the honest answer was to reduce scope to match current staffing. It concluded that contracted engineering capacity is a legitimate and common delivery model for municipalities of this size, and that reducing scope would produce a project that does not achieve the hydraulic performance the downtown requires. Half a detention system is not half a benefit.

### Disclosure Three: Local Match Contingency

The 20 percent local match is $800,000. Mayor Standing Bear's match commitment depends on a general obligation bond issue going to voters in September — one month after this application is due.

The city cannot certify a secured match on the application date, and it will not represent otherwise.

What the city can represent: the council approved the bond resolution unanimously in May. The issue is $2.4 million total, of which $800,000 is designated for this match and the balance for street and water line work. Sorrel Creek voters have approved four of the last five bond issues, including a 2020 water system issue at 68 percent during the pandemic. The city's bond counsel has reviewed the issue and the city retains substantial debt capacity, with current general obligation debt at 34 percent of its constitutional limit. A community coalition including the Main Street Association, the chamber, and the ministerial alliance has organized in support.

The city offers three contingency arrangements and will accept whichever the Trust prefers.

**Option A — Delayed obligation.** The Trust conditions award on bond passage, with funds obligated upon certification of election results in October. Year 1 activities shift approximately six weeks. Acquisition tranche one closings move from December to February, which is defensible because the city would not close on properties before the match is secure in any case.

**Option B — Phased match certification.** The Trust obligates on schedule against a partial match the city can certify now: $180,000 in the FY27 capital fund committed by council resolution in March, plus $95,000 in the flood mitigation reserve, plus a documented in-kind valuation of $140,000 for county emergency management and university extension contributions. That is $415,000 of the $800,000 certifiable today, with the balance certified upon bond passage in October. Year 1 activity is scaled to the certified portion.

**Option C — Alternate match path.** If the bond fails, the city will pursue the balance through three routes: an Oklahoma Water Resources Board REAP grant, for which the project is eligible and the cycle opens in November; a dedicated allocation from the city's capital improvement sales tax, which requires council action and reduces street maintenance for two years; and a Main Street Association capital campaign with an initial commitment of $50,000 already pledged by two downtown property owners. The city would provide the Trust a written match plan within 30 days of a failed election and would accept a reduced award scaled to the match actually secured rather than proceed on an unfunded commitment.

The city's judgment is that Option A is cleanest and Option B best preserves schedule. The city has no preference the Trust needs to accommodate.

The bond issue will be decided six weeks after the Trust's anticipated award date, and the city recognizes that this asks the Trust to carry uncertainty it did not create. The alternative would have been to skip this cycle and apply in four years. Two floods in five years does not leave four years of patience available.

## Sustainability

Sustainability for this project means three distinct things: that the physical assets are maintained, that the organizational capacity persists, and that the economic recovery continues without external subsidy.

**Asset maintenance — the greenway.** Fourteen acres of floodable greenway generates recurring maintenance obligation: mowing on the upper bench, native prairie management including prescribed burn on a three-year rotation, trail surface repair, sediment removal from the detention area and forebay, invasive species control, and post-event debris removal. The city estimates annual maintenance at $68,000 at full build-out.

Funding comes from four sources, layered deliberately rather than concentrated in one.

The primary source is a stormwater utility fee. The council adopted the enabling ordinance in 2024 at $3.50 per equivalent residential unit monthly, generating approximately $412,000 annually. The fee is in place and collecting. Council resolution in March dedicated $68,000 annually to greenway maintenance beginning in the year of substantial completion, with escalation tied to the municipal cost index. This is the load-bearing element: the fee exists, the revenue is flowing, and the dedication is adopted.

Second, the Parks Department absorbs mowing and trail maintenance into its existing route structure. The greenway is contiguous with two existing parks; the marginal crew cost is materially lower than a standalone facility. This is reflected as $19,000 of the $68,000 as in-kind rather than new cash.

Third, the university engineering extension has agreed to include the greenway in its prairie restoration research program, providing prescribed burn planning and student labor for vegetation management for at least five years post-construction. This is documented in the services agreement.

Fourth, the Main Street Association will operate an adopt-a-segment program for the trail corridor's downtown-adjacent portion, modeled on the one it runs for downtown planters. This is the least reliable of the four and is not counted in the $68,000.

The city has also established a stormwater capital reserve, funded at 12 percent of stormwater utility revenue, to cover major sediment removal and structural repair on a longer cycle. The reserve balance at project completion is projected at approximately $290,000.

**Deed-restricted parcels.** The 34 acquired parcels carry perpetual open-space restrictions and become a permanent city obligation. Parcels within the greenway footprint are maintained as described above. Parcels outside it — approximately nine, scattered on the north edge — will be maintained as mowed open space at an estimated $11,000 annually, absorbed into the Parks Department route structure. The city has begun conversation with the tribal environmental office about native planting on three of these parcels, which would reduce mowing cost and is culturally appropriate to the location; no commitment exists yet.

**Utility infrastructure.** Elevated utility assets are owned by the city or by the investor-owned utility and enter existing maintenance programs. The elevated lift stations and backflow prevention devices become part of the city's wastewater collection system maintenance schedule, funded through sewer rates. The transferred electrical assets become the utility's obligation under the memorandum of understanding in negotiation. No new maintenance category is created; existing programs absorb the assets.

**Organizational sustainability.** The staffing arrangements described above are transitional by design. The owner's representative contract ends with construction. The city's intent is that the permanent city engineer position be filled during the project period and that the compensation adjustment in the FY27 budget makes that possible. If it is not filled, the city will contract for engineering services on an ongoing basis, as it did before 2010 and as many Oklahoma municipalities of this size do.

The environmental compliance controls are permanent. They were adopted by council resolution, apply citywide, and are embedded in standard contract language. They do not depend on this project's funding.

**Economic sustainability.** The theory of change is that flood risk suppresses downtown investment and that reducing the risk releases it. Under this theory, sustainability arrives through private capital: property owners who invest in buildings they now believe will not flood, businesses that sign five-year leases instead of month-to-month, and lenders who underwrite downtown property at rates reflecting reduced risk.

The city is taking three steps to accelerate that.

The Main Street Association will pursue designation of the downtown district as a Certified Oklahoma Main Street community in Year 2, which brings technical assistance and access to state facade and rehabilitation programs. The application is drafted.

The city adopted a downtown reinvestment incentive district in 2024 that abates the municipal portion of ad valorem increase on qualified improvements for five years. Utilization has been minimal — two applications — which the city attributes directly to flood uncertainty. Post-project, the city expects the tool to activate.

The Community Rating System improvement from Class 8 to Class 6 reduces flood insurance premiums 20 percent citywide, an ongoing annual savings to policyholders of an estimated $184,000 that requires no further external funding once the credit is earned.

**What happens if the Trust does not renew.** The project is designed to reach a completed, self-sustaining state at the end of Year 4. There is no year-five program the Trust would be asked to continue. The greenway is built and its maintenance funded from an adopted utility fee. The structures are demolished and the deed restrictions recorded. The utilities are elevated. The city is not building a program that requires perpetual grant support; it is building infrastructure that requires ordinary municipal maintenance, and it has identified the revenue for it.

## Budget Narrative

Total project cost is $4,800,000: $4,000,000 requested from the Trust and $800,000 in local match, a 20 percent match ratio consistent with program requirements.

### Personnel — $412,000 (Trust: $412,000)

**Project Director (0.35 FTE, four years) — $148,000.** Emergency Manager Villalpando at 35 percent effort. Salary and benefits prorated from the city's classification schedule at $101,600 fully loaded. Covers steering committee leadership, partner coordination, tribal consultation liaison, and technical oversight of acquisition prioritization.

**Grants Coordinator (0.45 FTE, four years) — $132,000.** Duchamp at 45 percent effort, fully loaded at $73,300. Covers financial management, compliance monitoring, environmental clearance file audits, quarterly and annual reporting, subgrant administration.

**Acquisition and Relocation Specialist (1.0 FTE, three years) — $132,000.** New contract position at $44,000 annually fully loaded, Years 1 through 3. Manages 34 acquisitions from first contact through closing, prepares relocation assistance determinations, conducts in-home relocation counseling. Terminates at the end of Year 3. The city considered assigning this to existing staff and concluded that 34 voluntary acquisitions with URA-standard relocation is more than existing capacity can absorb, and that under-resourcing acquisition is how acquisition programs fail.

Superintendent Brumley's construction management time, City Manager Sundberg's executive sponsorship, and Finance Director time on drawdowns are contributed as in-kind and appear under match.

### Contractual Services — $694,000 (Trust: $554,000 / Match: $140,000)

**Owner's representative, licensed PE (Years 2–4) — $288,000.** Full-time contract engineer at $96,000 annually. Directly addresses the vacant engineer position. Scope covers construction administration, submittal review, pay application certification, change order review, and as-built verification.

**Hydrologic and hydraulic modeling and design review — $140,000 (match, in-kind and cash to university extension).** Preliminary and final modeling, 30/60/90 percent design review, post-construction model update, LOMR technical support, two graduate research assistants. Valued at the extension's published rate schedule; $85,000 is contributed in-kind by the extension and $55,000 is cash from the city's capital fund. Both count toward match.

**Design engineering, greenway and detention — $186,000 (Trust).** Professional services for survey, geotechnical, civil design, landscape architecture, and permitting. Approximately 7 percent of the $2.6 million detention construction cost, consistent with regional norms for public infrastructure design.

**Design engineering, utility elevation — $80,000 (Trust).** Approximately 8 percent of the $980,000 utility construction cost. The higher percentage reflects coordination complexity across four utility owners.

### Acquisition and Demolition — $1,946,000 (Trust: $1,796,000 / Match: $150,000)

**Property acquisition, 34 parcels — $1,428,000.** Twenty-six single-family at an average of $38,500, five multi-family at an average of $62,000, three commercial at an average of $76,000. Averages derived from a certified appraiser's mass valuation completed in January using pre-flood fair market value. Individual appraisals will be performed at acquisition; the budget carries the mass valuation as an estimate and the city will report variance.

**Title, closing, appraisal, and survey — $102,000.** $3,000 per parcel for individual appraisal, title work, closing, and boundary survey.

**Asbestos and lead survey and abatement — $187,000.** Survey for all 34 structures at $1,400 each ($47,600) plus abatement at an estimated $4,100 per structure across an estimated 34 structures. Housing stock predating 1978 makes abatement likely on most parcels. This line is a source of estimating risk and the city has budgeted at the high end deliberately.

**Demolition and site restoration — $229,000.** Approximately $6,700 per structure including disposal, foundation removal, grading, and native seeding. Reflects two competitive bids received on the city's 2023 demolition work escalated for market conditions.

Match within this category is $150,000 in city capital funds committed by council resolution in March, applied to acquisition costs.

### Construction — $3,580,000 (Trust: $2,890,000 / Match: $690,000)

**Detention and greenway construction — $2,600,000.** Earthwork and excavation for 14.2 acres ($980,000); outlet structure, forebay, and hydraulic controls ($420,000); low-flow channel and constructed wetland ($310,000); multi-use trail, 1.4 miles hardened surface ($386,000); native prairie establishment ($164,000); site amenities including lighting, benches, interpretive signage, and trail connections ($188,000); erosion control and construction-period stormwater management ($152,000).

Unit costs derive from ODOT and Oklahoma Water Resources Board bid tabulations for comparable work in the region within the past 24 months, escalated 6 percent annually to midpoint of construction.

**Utility elevation — $980,000.** Primary electrical vault relocation ($312,000); four secondary transformers ($148,000); natural gas regulator elevation, two stations ($94,000); telecommunications junction elevation, six cabinets ($68,000); sanitary sewer backflow prevention, 22 connections ($121,000); lift station elevation and floodproofing, two stations ($237,000).

The construction total of $3,580,000 against component subtotals of $3,580,000 reflects that contingency is carried as a separate line below rather than embedded.

Match within construction is $690,000, which is the portion of the bond proceeds designated for this project after the $110,000 allocated to acquisition and other match categories.

### Business Relocation and Downtown Support — $148,000 (Trust: $148,000)

**Commercial relocation assistance — $96,000.** Three acquired downtown businesses at up to $32,000 each, covering moving costs, fixture reinstallation, signage, and up to six months of rent differential. Administered by the Main Street Association under subgrant.

**Construction-period business disruption subsidy — $52,000.** Utility elevation will disrupt access to approximately eighteen downtown businesses for periods ranging from four days to three weeks. The subsidy provides up to $3,000 per affected business for extended-disruption cases. The city learned from the 2019 recovery that businesses fail during construction as readily as during floods, and that a modest, quickly disbursed subsidy prevents closures that would otherwise be counted against Goal 3.

### Evaluation — $84,000 (Trust: $84,000)

**External evaluator, Year 2 formative review — $32,000.** Includes data verification, implementation assessment, relocation household interviews, and compliance control testing.

**External evaluator, Year 4 summative review — $44,000.** Includes full outcome measurement, attribution analysis against peer municipalities, qualitative interviews, and final report.

**Data collection support — $8,000.** Main Street Association census costs above baseline operations, at $2,000 annually.

### Other Direct Costs — $86,000 (Trust: $66,000 / Match: $20,000)

**Public engagement — $24,000.** Three design meetings, printed and mailed notification to all affected property owners and downtown businesses, translation and interpretation, meeting facilities, childcare at evening meetings.

**Legal and recording — $28,000.** Deed restriction preparation and recording, subgrant agreement drafting, utility memorandum of understanding, bond counsel coordination on match.

**Permitting and regulatory fees — $22,000.** Section 404 permitting, state stormwater permits, LOMR application fee, county recording fees.

**Travel and training — $12,000.** Staff travel to state hazard mitigation and Main Street conferences, environmental review refresher training on a two-year cycle.

Match within this category is $20,000 in city general fund.

### Contingency — $150,000 (Trust: $150,000)

Contingency is 4.2 percent of construction cost, which is below the 8 to 10 percent typical for public infrastructure. The city has constrained it deliberately in order to maximize direct project scope, accepting the tradeoff that unit cost overruns will require scope adjustment rather than contingency draw. The Year 2 decision point described in the Evaluation Plan is the mechanism for that adjustment. The city will request Trust concurrence before any contingency draw exceeding $25,000.
