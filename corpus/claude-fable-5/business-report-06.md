## 1. Purpose and Scope

This report presents the operating results of Susquehanna Rising Charter Schools for the 2025–26 fiscal year. It is written for two audiences at once. The first is this board, which must adopt it in August with a full and unvarnished understanding of where the network stands. The second is the authorizing district's board, which will read it as part of our charter renewal packet in October and will judge, in part, whether we describe ourselves honestly.

Chief Executive Antoine Lemieux, Chief Financial Officer Holly Steinmetz, and Board Chair Cordell Bishop each asked that this report resist the temptation to average our four campuses into a single network story. That instruction shapes the structure of what follows. Susquehanna Rising had a strong year at Riverfront Elementary, a difficult year at Cameron Street Middle, a mixed year at Allison Hill Academy, and a financially serious year at Steelton Prep. A blended narrative would misrepresent all four. Each campus therefore receives its own section with its own enrollment, staffing, attendance, and academic results, followed by network-level sections on finances, staffing and turnover, the Steelton Prep shortfall, and the considerations most relevant to the renewal hearing.

All figures are drawn from the network's student information system, the finance office's year-end close, and campus-level reporting verified by the central office in July 2026. Where numbers remain preliminary pending state confirmation, they are marked as such.

---

## 2. Executive Summary

Susquehanna Rising operated four campuses in Harrisburg during 2025–26, serving 3,120 students with 380 staff and a budget of $47 million. The network ends the year solvent, in compliance with its charter obligations, and with demonstrable academic results at two campuses that exceed city benchmarks. It also ends the year with three problems the board should hold clearly in view: an enrollment and deficit problem at Steelton Prep, an attendance and staffing problem at Cameron Street Middle, and a systemwide teacher turnover rate of 31 percent that touches every building.

The headline results by campus:

- **Riverfront Elementary** enrolled 780 students, 96 percent of target, held attendance at 92 percent, and posted 41 percent proficiency in third-grade reading against a 29 percent city average. It is the network's strongest campus and the clearest evidence of the model working.
- **Cameron Street Middle** declined from 720 to 640 students, ran chronic absenteeism of 22 percent, and operated with 6 of 44 teaching positions vacant from January through June, covered by long-term substitutes. Its trajectory is the reverse of Riverfront's and demands intervention, not monitoring.
- **Allison Hill Academy** graduated 84 percent of its senior class, documented postsecondary plans for 61 percent of graduates, and enrolled 210 students in dual credit — genuinely strong outcomes for a high school serving this population. It lost its only counselor to a district position in February and finished the year without one, which shows in the postsecondary planning figure.
- **Steelton Prep**, our newest campus, enrolled 690 students against a 970-student facility model. The gap produces a $2.3 million operating deficit on a building leased at $1.4 million per year. This is the network's largest financial exposure and the subject of Section 8.

Systemwide, the network holds 38 days of cash on hand, eliminated 14 positions previously funded by expiring federal relief dollars, and experienced teacher turnover of 31 percent.

Going into the renewal hearing, the board should understand that our case rests on Riverfront and Allison Hill, that our credibility rests on how candidly we address Cameron Street and Steelton, and that our financial position — while stable today — has less cushion than it did a year ago and depends on decisions this board will make in the coming twelve months.

## 4. Riverfront Elementary

### 4.1 Overview

Riverfront Elementary is the network's flagship and its oldest campus. In 2025–26 it delivered the strongest results in the network across every category the authorizer measures, and it did so with the most stable staff in the system.

| Riverfront Elementary | 2025–26 |
|---|---|
| Enrollment | 780 (96% of 810 target) |
| Average daily attendance | 92% |
| Chronic absenteeism | 11% |
| Third-grade reading proficiency | 41% (city average: 29%) |
| Teaching positions | 52, all filled by October 1 |
| Teacher retention into 2026–27 | 81% |
| Waitlist at year end | 140 students |

### 4.2 Enrollment and Attendance

Riverfront opened the year at 96 percent of target and held that enrollment with minimal attrition; net mid-year withdrawals totaled 22 students, most attributable to families moving out of the city. The campus maintained a waitlist of roughly 140 students throughout the year, concentrated in kindergarten and first grade, which suggests demand well in excess of capacity at the entry grades.

Average daily attendance of 92 percent compares favorably with the city elementary average of approximately 87 percent. Chronic absenteeism — the share of students missing ten percent or more of enrolled days — was 11 percent, roughly half the rate at Cameron Street Middle. Principal leadership attributes this to a family engagement model that assigns every classroom a designated attendance contact and triggers a home visit at the fifth absence rather than the fifteenth. The model is inexpensive, well documented, and, in the leadership team's judgment, exportable. Section 10 recommends piloting it at Cameron Street.

### 4.3 Academic Results

Third-grade reading proficiency reached 41 percent against a city average of 29 percent — a twelve-point advantage on the single metric most predictive of long-term academic success and, not incidentally, the metric the authorizer has weighted most heavily in prior renewal reviews. This is the third consecutive year Riverfront has exceeded the city average in third-grade reading, and the margin has widened each year (six points in 2023–24, nine in 2024–25, twelve this year).

The result reflects a deliberate multi-year investment: the campus adopted a structured literacy curriculum in 2022, trained all K–3 teachers in it over two summers, and protected a daily 90-minute reading block against all scheduling pressures. The trustees should understand this as evidence that the network can execute an instructional strategy over multiple years when staffing is stable — and should read the Cameron Street section with that conditional clause in mind.

### 4.4 Staffing

Riverfront filled all 52 teaching positions before October 1 and retained 81 percent of its teachers into the coming year, well above the network average. The campus has had the same principal for six years. The correlation between leadership stability, staff stability, and academic results at Riverfront is the clearest internal evidence we have for what the rest of the network should be built toward.

### 4.5 Assessment

Riverfront is the campus the renewal case should lead with. Its results are externally benchmarked, sustained over multiple years, and produced by practices the network can name and describe. Its principal risk is complacency in the reporting rather than anything in the building.

## 5. Cameron Street Middle

### 5.1 Overview

Cameron Street Middle had the most difficult year of any campus in the network, and this report will not soften that. Enrollment fell, attendance deteriorated, and the campus operated for the entire second semester with substitutes in six classrooms.

| Cameron Street Middle | 2025–26 |
|---|---|
| Enrollment | 640, down from 720 (89% of target) |
| Chronic absenteeism | 22% |
| Average daily attendance | 86% |
| Teaching positions | 44; 6 vacant January–June |
| Long-term substitutes in core subjects | 4 of 6 vacancies |
| Teacher retention into 2026–27 | 62% |
| State assessment proficiency (math) | 18% (preliminary) |
| State assessment proficiency (ELA) | 24% (preliminary) |

### 5.2 Enrollment

Cameron Street lost 80 students over the course of the year, falling from 720 to 640 — an 11 percent decline and the sharpest single-year drop at any established campus in network history. Exit interviews, which the central office conducted with 54 of the withdrawing families, identify three drivers in descending order: dissatisfaction with substitute-covered classrooms (cited by 31 families), school climate and discipline concerns (cited by 19), and relocation (cited by 12, with some overlap among categories).

The enrollment decline has a direct financial consequence — roughly $1.1 million in per-pupil revenue against a cost structure built for 720 — and a compounding operational one: the families most likely to leave are frequently those most engaged, and their departure makes the climate problems they cite harder to solve.

### 5.3 Staffing Vacancies

Six of the campus's 44 teaching positions were vacant from January through June. Two vacancies resulted from mid-year resignations in December; four resulted from positions that were never successfully filled in the fall and were held by a rotation of short-term substitutes until long-term substitutes were secured in January. Four of the six vacancies were in core subjects: two in mathematics, one in English language arts, one in science.

The long-term substitutes assigned to these classrooms were, by campus leadership's own account, uneven. Two performed capably. The mathematics classrooms in particular saw significant instructional loss, and the preliminary math proficiency figure of 18 percent — down from 23 percent the prior year — almost certainly reflects it. The trustees should not accept an explanation of the academic results at Cameron Street that omits the staffing failure, and they should not accept an explanation of the staffing failure that treats it as bad luck. The network's recruiting pipeline for middle school math and science is thin, our salary position against the district for those subjects is weak, and Cameron Street's climate reputation makes it the hardest of our four campuses to recruit into. All three of those are addressable, and Section 10 addresses them.

### 5.4 Attendance

Chronic absenteeism of 22 percent means that more than one in five Cameron Street students missed at least ten percent of the school year. This is roughly double Riverfront's rate and above the city middle school average of approximately 19 percent. Attendance and staffing interact: students skip classes taught by substitutes at measurably higher rates, and second-semester attendance in the substitute-covered sections ran four to six points below the campus average.

### 5.5 Assessment and Turnaround Status

Cameron Street requires intervention, not monitoring. Mr. Lemieux has already taken three actions the board should be aware of: the campus principal and the network's chief academic officer have jointly developed a staffing plan that had 41 of 44 positions filled with permanent hires as of July 25, with signing incentives funded for the remaining three; the Riverfront attendance model will be implemented at Cameron Street this fall with a dedicated coordinator; and the network has retained a school climate consultant to work with campus leadership through December.

For renewal purposes, the honest framing is this: Cameron Street is the campus where the network's model did not hold in 2025–26, the causes are identified and specific, and the corrective actions are funded and underway. The authorizer will find the problems whether or not we name them. We should name them.

## 6. Allison Hill Academy

### 6.1 Overview

Allison Hill Academy, the network's high school, produced outcomes that stand up well against any comparison group in the city, alongside one significant service gap that the trustees should understand and that the postsecondary numbers reflect.

| Allison Hill Academy | 2025–26 |
|---|---|
| Enrollment | 810 (103% of 790 target) |
| Four-year graduation rate | 84% |
| Graduates with documented postsecondary plans | 61% |
| Students enrolled in dual credit | 210 (26% of enrollment) |
| Dual credit hours earned | 1,340 |
| Average daily attendance | 89% |
| Counseling positions filled at year end | 0 of 1 |

### 6.2 Graduation and Postsecondary Outcomes

Allison Hill graduated 84 percent of its senior class of 187 students. The city's comprehensive high school graduation rate for the same period is approximately 71 percent, and the state average is 87 percent; Allison Hill sits much closer to the state than to the city, serving a student population that mirrors the city's demographics. This is the second-strongest external benchmark in the renewal case after Riverfront's reading results.

Dual credit enrollment reached 210 students — roughly one in four — who collectively earned 1,340 college credit hours through the partnership with Harrisburg Area Community College, at no cost to families. Twenty-two graduates left with fifteen or more college credits. The dual credit program is the campus's signature offering and a meaningful recruitment driver; it is also, at approximately $190,000 in annual cost, one of the network's highest-return investments.

The soft spot is postsecondary planning. Sixty-one percent of graduates had documented postsecondary plans — enrollment in a two- or four-year institution, an apprenticeship or trade program, military enlistment, or verified employment — at graduation. That figure was 74 percent the prior year. The decline maps directly onto the counseling vacancy described below.

### 6.3 The Counseling Vacancy

Allison Hill's sole school counselor resigned in February to accept a position with the district at a salary approximately $9,000 above ours. The position remained unfilled through year end despite an active search; two candidates declined offers, both citing compensation, and the network was not in a position mid-year to break its salary schedule for a single role.

The practical consequence: for the final four months of the year — the months in which financial aid forms are completed, acceptances are weighed, and plans are documented — 810 students, including 187 seniors, had no counselor. An assistant principal and two teachers absorbed pieces of the work. They did it conscientiously and it was not enough, and the thirteen-point drop in documented postsecondary plans is the measurable result.

A single counselor for a high school of 810 was inadequate before the resignation; the American School Counselor Association recommends a ratio of one to 250. The 2026–27 budget adopted in June funds two counseling positions at Allison Hill at a revised salary band. One has been filled as of July; the search for the second continues. The trustees should treat the counseling gap as a lesson in single-point-of-failure staffing rather than an isolated HR event, because the same structural fragility exists elsewhere in the network — several campuses have exactly one nurse, one social worker, or one special education coordinator.

### 6.4 Assessment

Allison Hill is over enrollment target, above city benchmarks on graduation, and running the network's most distinctive academic program. Its 2025–26 story is strong outcomes with one self-inflicted gap that has been diagnosed, budgeted for, and partially remedied. That is a presentable story at a renewal hearing, provided we present the counseling gap ourselves rather than waiting to be asked.

## 7. Steelton Prep

### 7.1 Overview

Steelton Prep, opened in 2023 under principal Yesenia Duarte, is the network's newest campus and its largest financial exposure. This section covers its operations; Section 8 addresses the enrollment shortfall and deficit in detail, because that issue is significant enough to warrant its own treatment.

| Steelton Prep | 2025–26 |
|---|---|
| Enrollment | 690 (71% of 970 target) |
| Year-over-year enrollment growth | +85 students |
| Operating deficit | $2.3M |
| Annual facility lease | $1.4M |
| Average daily attendance | 90% |
| Chronic absenteeism | 14% |
| Teaching positions | 46, all filled |
| Teacher retention into 2026–27 | 76% |

### 7.2 Operations and Academics

It is important that the financial situation not obscure what is going well inside the building. Steelton Prep filled all 46 teaching positions on time, retained 76 percent of its teachers — second only to Riverfront — and held attendance at 90 percent with chronic absenteeism of 14 percent, both better than the city average and far better than Cameron Street. Ms. Duarte has built a stable, functional school culture in three years, and family satisfaction surveys returned the highest scores in the network (4.4 of 5.0 on the composite measure, on a 68 percent response rate).

Academic results are appropriate to a young campus: preliminary state assessment proficiency of 27 percent in ELA and 21 percent in math, both slightly above the city average and trending upward from the prior year. Steelton students are not yet posting Riverfront-caliber results, but the trajectory is positive and the school-level fundamentals — attendance, retention, culture — that precede academic gains are in place.

### 7.3 Enrollment

Steelton enrolled 690 students against a facility model built for 970. That is a serious shortfall, and it is also 85 students more than the prior year; the campus is growing, just more slowly than the model assumed. The waitlist dynamics are instructive: entry grades (K–2) are at or near capacity with modest waitlists, while grades 6–8 are substantially under-enrolled. Families in the Steelton catchment have proven willing to start their children with us but reluctant to transfer them in at the middle grades — a pattern consistent with enrollment behavior across the sector, and one the original facility model did not adequately account for.

### 7.4 Assessment

Steelton Prep is a well-run school inside an oversized financial commitment. The distinction matters for the renewal hearing and for the board's own decision-making: the problem to be solved is not school quality or leadership, it is the gap between a 970-seat lease obligation and a realistic enrollment trajectory. Section 8 lays out that problem and the options.

## 8. The Steelton Prep Shortfall

### 8.1 The Structure of the Problem

When the network signed the Steelton facility lease in 2022, the enrollment model projected 970 students by year three. The lease — $1.4 million annually, ten-year term, through June 2033 — was priced to that projection, as was the campus staffing plan. Actual year-three enrollment is 690. The result is a $2.3 million operating deficit at the campus for 2025–26, absorbed by the network's consolidated budget.

The deficit decomposes roughly as follows:

| Component | Approximate Amount |
|---|---|
| Per-pupil revenue shortfall (280 students below model) | $1.6M |
| Facility cost carried against unrealized enrollment | $0.4M |
| Fixed campus costs (administration, operations) not scalable to actual enrollment | $0.3M |
| **Total operating deficit** | **$2.3M** |

Put plainly: the network is paying for roughly 280 seats nobody sits in, and a $1.4 million lease consumes about 15 percent of the campus's revenue at current enrollment, against a network norm of 8 to 10 percent.

### 8.2 How the Projection Missed

The board is entitled to an honest accounting of the original error, because the authorizer will ask and because our own future facility decisions depend on learning from it. The 2022 model assumed (a) that a K–8 campus would fill across all grades at comparable rates, when in practice families enroll heavily at entry grades and sparsely at grades 6–8; (b) a catchment demand estimate built on pre-2020 population data that overstated school-age population in the Steelton corridor; and (c) a three-year ramp that left no margin for slower-than-modeled growth. None of these was reckless at the time, and all three were wrong. Enrollment has grown every year — 490, then 605, then 690 — but on a slope that reaches perhaps 800 to 830 at maturity, not 970.

### 8.3 Options Before the Board

CFO Steinmetz and Mr. Lemieux have developed four options, which will come to the board as a formal decision item in the fall. They are summarized here so the trustees enter the renewal season with the full picture.

**Option A — Grow into the building.** Continue current trajectory, invest approximately $250,000 in targeted recruitment for grades 6–8, and accept deficits of a projected $1.6 million in 2026–27 and $0.9 million in 2027–28 before approaching breakeven near 830 students in 2028–29. This option preserves the campus intact but consumes cash the network no longer has in abundance (see Section 9) and assumes the growth curve holds.

**Option B — Renegotiate or partially sublease.** The landlord has signaled preliminary willingness to discuss subleasing one wing (approximately 200-student capacity) to a compatible tenant. Realistic recovery is $250,000 to $350,000 annually. This narrows the deficit without solving it and constrains future growth if enrollment exceeds current projections.

**Option C — Reconfigure the grade span.** Convert Steelton to K–5 at roughly 700 students — essentially its current demonstrated demand — and route rising sixth graders to Cameron Street Middle, which has 80 empty seats and a per-pupil revenue problem of its own. This addresses two campus shortfalls with one structural change, but it asks families to transfer into the network's most troubled campus, and it should not be executed until Cameron Street's turnaround shows results.

**Option D — Exit the facility.** Early termination of the lease carries a contractual penalty of approximately $2.1 million plus relocation costs, and closing or relocating the network's newest campus during a renewal cycle would raise more questions with the authorizer than it answers. Management does not recommend this option and includes it only for completeness.

Management's preliminary recommendation is a combination of A and B — pursue the sublease conversation while funding middle-grades recruitment — with Option C held as the structural solution once Cameron Street is stabilized. The board is not asked to decide in August; it is asked to understand the shape of the decision it will make in the fall, and to be prepared to describe that decision process credibly at the renewal hearing.

## 9. Network Financial Position

### 9.1 Operating Results

The network operated on a $47.0 million budget in 2025–26 and closed the year with a consolidated operating deficit of approximately $1.4 million: the $2.3 million Steelton shortfall and the Cameron Street revenue loss, partially offset by surpluses at Riverfront and Allison Hill and by mid-year expenditure controls imposed in February.

| Financial Summary | 2025–26 |
|---|---|
| Total revenue | $45.6M |
| Total expenditures | $47.0M |
| Consolidated operating result | ($1.4M) |
| Days cash on hand, June 30 | 38 |
| Days cash on hand, prior year | 51 |
| Debt service coverage | Compliant with all covenants |
| Audit status | Fieldwork scheduled September; no findings in prior three audits |

### 9.2 Cash Position

Days cash on hand fell from 51 to 38 over the fiscal year. Thirty-eight days is above the 30-day floor set by board policy and above typical lender covenant thresholds, but the direction matters more than the level: the network consumed roughly a quarter of its liquidity cushion in a single year, and a second year of comparable deficit would bring the network within range of its policy floor by late 2027. This is the single most important financial fact in this report. The network is not in financial distress. It is, however, spending down the margin for error at the same time that Cameron Street's turnaround and Steelton's growth plan both require investment. The 2026–27 budget adopted in June projects a deficit of $0.9 million, which management regards as the maximum the cash position can responsibly absorb.

### 9.3 The End of Federal Relief Funding

Federal pandemic relief funds, which had supported network operations since 2021, fully expired during this fiscal year. The network eliminated 14 positions that had been funded with those dollars: six interventionists, four family engagement coordinators, two technology support roles, and two central office positions. The reductions were achieved through a combination of attrition and non-renewal, with no involuntary layoffs of tenured-equivalent staff.

Management chose reductions over absorbing the positions into the operating budget, a decision the finance committee reviewed in March. It was the fiscally correct call and it was not cost-free: the loss of the interventionists in particular removes academic support capacity at exactly the moment Cameron Street needs it, and the family engagement coordinators had been part of the attendance infrastructure at two campuses. The trustees should understand the relief-funding cliff as a permanent reduction in the network's service model, not a one-time budget event, and should expect its effects to appear in next year's results.

### 9.4 Revenue Outlook

Per-pupil funding rates for 2026–27 are confirmed at a 2.1 percent increase. The network's revenue question is therefore almost entirely an enrollment question, which is to say it is the Cameron Street and Steelton question. Every 100 students of enrollment recovery is worth approximately $1.15 million in annual revenue at blended rates.

## 10. Staffing and Turnover

### 10.1 The Turnover Picture

Network teacher turnover reached 31 percent in 2025–26, up from 26 percent the prior year and well above the healthy range. The network figure again conceals sharp campus variation:

| Campus | Teacher Turnover | Teacher Retention |
|---|---|---|
| Riverfront Elementary | 19% | 81% |
| Steelton Prep | 24% | 76% |
| Allison Hill Academy | 29% | 71% |
| Cameron Street Middle | 38% | 62% |
| **Network** | **31%** | **69%** |

Exit survey data (collected from 71 percent of departing teachers) identifies three dominant reasons for leaving: compensation relative to the district (cited by 44 percent of respondents), workload (31 percent), and campus climate — concentrated overwhelmingly at Cameron Street (18 percent).

### 10.2 The Compensation Gap

The district's most recent contract settlement widened its salary advantage over the network to an average of $4,200 at comparable experience levels, and to more than $8,000 in shortage subjects (mathematics, science, special education, counseling). The Allison Hill counselor's departure and the Cameron Street math vacancies are both direct expressions of this gap. The network cannot match the district dollar for dollar within a balanced budget. The 2026–27 compensation plan instead targets the gap where it does the most damage: revised salary bands for shortage subjects and counseling, retention stipends at Cameron Street, and signing incentives for hard-to-fill positions. Total cost is $610,000, funded within the adopted budget.

### 10.3 What Stability Buys

The internal evidence in this report is unambiguous: the two campuses with the highest teacher retention (Riverfront, Steelton) posted the best attendance and the strongest climate and family satisfaction results; the campus with the lowest retention (Cameron Street) posted the worst results on every measure. Turnover is not one operational metric among many. On the evidence of our own four buildings, it is the leading indicator of everything else, and the board should evaluate management's staffing investments in that light.

## 11. Considerations for the Renewal Hearing

The charter comes before the authorizing district's board in October. Management offers the trustees the following framing.

**The affirmative case is real and should be led with.** Riverfront's third consecutive year beating the city average in third-grade reading, with a widening margin, is the kind of sustained, externally benchmarked result renewal decisions are made on. Allison Hill's 84 percent graduation rate — thirteen points above the city's comprehensive high schools — and its 210 dual-credit students at no cost to families are the second pillar. Steelton's culture and retention results demonstrate the network can open and stabilize a new school. These are not selectively favorable statistics; they are the network's genuine strengths.

**The problems should be presented by us, in our own words, with corrective actions attached.** The authorizer's staff will have the enrollment data, the vacancy data, and the financials. Cameron Street's decline, the six-month substitute coverage, the Steelton deficit, the counseling vacancy, and the falling cash position will all be visible to them. Our credibility at the hearing depends on whether the authorizer concludes we see our own problems as clearly as they do. This report is written to demonstrate that we do, and management recommends the renewal presentation follow the same approach: name the problem, name the cause, name the funded corrective action, name the timeline.

**Three questions should be anticipated and rehearsed.** First: *Why should the authorizer believe Cameron Street will improve?* The answer is the staffing plan (41 of 44 positions filled as of late July, against 38 at the same point last year), the funded climate intervention, and the attendance model with a three-year track record at Riverfront. Second: *Is Steelton Prep financially viable?* The answer is that the school is operationally sound, the deficit is a facility-sizing problem with identified options, and the board will act on those options in the fall — the hearing should not be the first time the authorizer hears that a decision process exists. Third: *Is the network financially stable without federal relief funds?* The answer is 38 days of cash, covenant compliance, three clean audits, a balanced-trajectory budget, and a candid acknowledgment that the cushion has narrowed and is being actively managed.

**One risk should be managed before October.** The Steelton decision (Section 8.3) will not be final before the hearing. The board should agree in advance on how to characterize its status — a defined set of options, a defined timeline, a defined decision standard — so that trustees and management describe it consistently. Divergent answers on Steelton would do more damage at the hearing than the deficit itself.

## 12. Recommendations and Requested Actions

Management requests that the board take the following actions at the August meeting:

1. **Adopt this report** for inclusion in the October renewal packet.

Susquehanna Rising's 2025–26 year does not reduce to a single sentence, and this report has deliberately not tried to reduce it to one. It holds 38 days of cash, has absorbed the end of federal relief funding without layoffs of permanent staff, and faces a turnover problem it has begun to price and address.

That is the whole picture, and it is the picture the trustees should carry into October: a network with proven capacity to run excellent schools, honest about the two places it fell short, and specific about what it is doing in both.
