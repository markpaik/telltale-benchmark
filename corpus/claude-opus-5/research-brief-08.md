# Transitional Care for Heart Failure at Mountain Laurel Health System: An Assessment of Thirty-Day Readmission Effects, July 2024 Through December 2025

**Prepared for:** Kwabena Mensah, MD, Chief Medical Officer
**Requested by:** Loretta Sizemore, Vice President of Care Management
**Cost analysis contributed by:** Grant Pelletier, Chief Financial Officer
**Prepared by:** Anjali Deshmukh, Senior Clinical Outcomes Analyst
**Date:** July 29, 2026

## The Question and What We Found

The budget committee meets September 15 to decide whether to fund six additional care coordinator positions at an annual cost of $840,000. The decision rests on a prior question that this brief addresses: did the transitional care program launched in 2024 — a pharmacist telephone medication review at 48 hours post-discharge paired with a clinician follow-up visit within seven days — reduce 30-day all-cause readmissions among heart failure patients?

The short answer is that the program is associated with lower readmissions, but a substantial share of the raw difference reflects who enrolled rather than what the program did, and the effect is concentrated in a specific subgroup of patients.

Across 2,308 index heart failure discharges between July 2024 and December 2025, enrolled patients were readmitted within 30 days at 14.1 percent, compared with 19.6 percent among the unenrolled — a raw gap of 5.5 percentage points. That comparison is not credible on its face. Coordinators did not attempt to enroll patients without a working telephone, and those patients constitute 19 percent of the discharge population. They are systematically different from the rest: poorer, less housed, more socially isolated, and more likely to return to the hospital regardless of what any program does. Their presence in the unenrolled group inflates the apparent benefit.

Restricting the comparison to patients who had a reachable phone — that is, comparing enrolled patients against patients who *could* have enrolled but did not — narrows the gap to 16.8 percent versus 18.9 percent, a difference of 2.1 percentage points. This is the more defensible estimate, and even it retains residual bias from voluntary enrollment, which we discuss at length below. Our judgment is that the true program effect lies at or below 2.1 points, and we would not be surprised if a properly randomized trial found something closer to 1.0 to 1.5 points.

Two subgroup findings bear directly on the staffing decision. First, patients living more than 45 minutes from a Mountain Laurel hospital showed no readmission difference whatsoever between enrolled and unenrolled — the program, as currently delivered, does not appear to work for them. Second, patients discharged to skilled nursing facilities, 22 percent of the total, were excluded from the program by design and are therefore absent from the effect estimate entirely; nothing in this analysis speaks to whether they would benefit.

The readmission measurement itself is incomplete. Readmissions to Mountain Laurel facilities are captured reliably from the electronic health record. Readmissions to hospitals outside the system are visible only through a state all-payer claims database that lags five months, excludes several Medicare Advantage plans, and covers roughly 78 percent of discharges. We are therefore undercounting readmissions, and the undercount is not evenly distributed — it falls more heavily on patients who live far from Beckley and are more likely to seek care elsewhere, which is precisely the subgroup where we observe no effect.

The implications for September 15 are as follows. A 2.1-point reduction applied to the program-eligible population represents a real but modest clinical gain, and the financial case for six additional coordinators depends heavily on assumptions about which patients they would serve and whether the marginal patient resembles the average enrolled patient. It probably does not. We recommend the committee consider a smaller expansion targeted at the subgroups where evidence of benefit is strongest, paired with a redesign pilot for distant patients and a decision about whether to extend eligibility to skilled nursing discharges. On the $1.2 million federal penalty, the honest assessment is that a program of this size and effect, operating on this share of the heart failure population, cannot by itself move the excess readmission ratio enough to eliminate the penalty within the coming measurement period.

## Background and Program Description

Mountain Laurel Health System operates three hospitals with 512 licensed beds serving Raleigh, Fayette, Summers, Wyoming, and surrounding counties in southern West Virginia. The system employs 4,200 people. Thirty-one percent of area residents are covered by Medicare, a figure well above the national average and one that reflects both the age structure of the service area and the prevalence of disability-based eligibility. Heart failure is among the system's highest-volume medical admissions and its single largest contributor to the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program penalty.

The transitional care program began accepting patients in July 2024. Its design has two components. First, a clinical pharmacist places a telephone call within 48 hours of discharge to conduct a medication reconciliation and review, confirming that the patient has obtained prescribed medications, understands the dosing schedule, and has not continued medications discontinued during the admission. Diuretic titration and the identification of duplicate or interacting agents are the principal targets. Second, the program arranges and confirms a follow-up visit with a clinician — cardiology, primary care, or an advanced practice provider in the heart failure clinic — within seven days of discharge. Care coordinators own the scheduling, remind the patient, and in some cases arrange transportation.

The program's logic is sound and consistent with the published literature on transitional care. Medication errors are common after heart failure discharge, and early clinical contact allows volume status to be reassessed before decompensation requires admission. What is at issue here is not whether the concept works in general but whether this implementation, in this population, produced a measurable effect large enough to justify additional investment.

Six additional care coordinator positions have been proposed at a fully loaded annual cost of $840,000, or $140,000 per position including salary, benefits, and overhead allocation. The system also faces a $1.2 million reduction against next year's Medicare payments under the federal readmissions penalty.

## Data and Methods

### Sample construction

The analytic sample comprises 2,308 index heart failure discharges occurring between July 1, 2024, and December 31, 2025. An index discharge is defined as a live discharge from a Mountain Laurel hospital with a principal diagnosis of heart failure, identified using the diagnosis code set specified by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services for the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program heart failure measure. Patients discharged against medical advice were excluded, as were patients who died during the index admission and patients transferred to another acute care hospital.

Where a patient had multiple qualifying discharges during the study window, each was treated as a separate index event unless it occurred within 30 days of a prior index discharge, in which case it was classified as a readmission and not counted as a new index. This is consistent with CMS methodology and avoids double-counting the most frequently admitted patients as both numerator and denominator contributors.

The observation window closed December 31, 2025, and 30-day outcomes were therefore complete for all index discharges by January 30, 2026. Claims-based outcome ascertainment, however, required waiting for the state all-payer feed to mature, which is why this brief appears in July 2026 rather than earlier.

### Outcome definition

The outcome is all-cause readmission to any acute care hospital within 30 days of the index discharge date. All-cause rather than heart-failure-specific readmission is the correct measure both because it matches the federal penalty specification and because attributing cause is unreliable — a patient readmitted for pneumonia three weeks after a heart failure discharge may have been readmitted because of inadequate transitional care regardless of the coding.

### Data sources and their coverage

Two sources contribute to outcome ascertainment, and their differing properties are central to interpreting everything that follows.

**Mountain Laurel electronic health record.** Readmissions to any of the three system hospitals are captured directly from the enterprise EHR. This source is essentially complete for in-system events. Patient matching across encounters is performed on the enterprise master patient index, which has been in place since 2019 and has a documented duplicate rate below one percent. We have high confidence in in-system readmission counts.

**State all-payer claims database.** Readmissions to hospitals outside the Mountain Laurel system are visible only through the state all-payer claims feed. This source has three limitations that materially affect the analysis.

The first is timing. The feed lags approximately five months behind service dates. For discharges late in the study window, this means the outcome data only recently matured, and it means that any future analysis of a more recent period will face the same delay. The program cannot be monitored in near-real-time using this source.

The second is payer coverage. Several Medicare Advantage plans do not submit to the database. Given that Medicare Advantage penetration in the service area is substantial and growing, this is a meaningful gap, and it is a gap concentrated precisely in the population of greatest interest — older heart failure patients.

The third is overall completeness. Taking the timing and payer gaps together with ordinary submission attrition, the claims feed covers approximately 78 percent of discharges. Roughly one in five out-of-system readmissions is therefore invisible to us.

The practical consequence is that our readmission rates are underestimates. Every figure in this brief should be read as a floor. The more important consequence, however, is that the undercount is unlikely to be distributed evenly across the groups we are comparing.

### The differential undercount problem

If out-of-system readmissions were missed at the same rate among enrolled and unenrolled patients, the undercount would depress both rates without much distorting the difference between them. We do not believe that condition holds.

Patients who live far from Beckley are more likely to be readmitted to a hospital nearer their home — a critical access hospital in an adjacent county, or a facility across the Virginia or Kentucky line. Those readmissions are exactly the ones most likely to be missed, both because out-of-state facilities may not submit to the West Virginia feed at all and because the patients involved are disproportionately covered by plans that do not report.

This matters for the distance subgroup finding. When we report that patients living more than 45 minutes from a hospital showed no difference between enrolled and unenrolled, part of the explanation may be that we are failing to observe the readmissions that would reveal a difference — in either direction. We treat this finding as suggestive of a genuine problem with program reach among distant patients, but we cannot rule out that measurement failure is producing or masking the pattern.

A secondary concern runs the other way. Enrolled patients, having been contacted by a pharmacist and scheduled into a Mountain Laurel clinic, may be more likely to return to a Mountain Laurel hospital when they decompensate. If enrollment increases the probability that a readmission is *captured* rather than the probability that it *occurs*, the observed program effect would be biased toward zero — that is, we would be understating benefit. These two biases work in opposite directions and we cannot confidently net them out.

### Enrollment and group assignment

Enrollment was voluntary. Care coordinators identified eligible patients during the index admission, explained the program, and offered participation. Patients could decline. Two structural exclusions applied.

**Telephone access.** Coordinators did not attempt to enroll patients without a working telephone. Because both program components depend on telephonic contact — the 48-hour pharmacist call directly, and the seven-day visit indirectly through scheduling and reminders — this exclusion was operationally necessary given the program as designed. Nineteen percent of the discharge population lacked a working telephone at the time of discharge assessment.

**Skilled nursing discharges.** Patients discharged to a skilled nursing facility, 22 percent of the total, were excluded from the program by design on the reasoning that the receiving facility assumes responsibility for medication management and clinical follow-up. This is a defensible design decision but it means the program has never been tested in this population.

The remaining patients — those with a phone and not discharged to skilled nursing — were eligible. Among the eligible, enrollment was voluntary, and this is where the analytic difficulty concentrates.

### Why the raw comparison fails

The 14.1 percent versus 19.6 percent comparison places every non-enrolled patient in the comparison group, including the 19 percent without telephones. Those patients are not merely non-participants; they are a distinct population.

Lacking a working telephone in this service area correlates with low income, unstable housing, social isolation, and limited access to transportation. Each of these independently predicts readmission. A patient without a phone is also a patient who cannot call the clinic when symptoms worsen, cannot be reached by a pharmacy about a prescription problem, and cannot easily arrange a ride to a follow-up appointment. Their elevated readmission risk exists whether or not a transitional care program is offered to them.

Including them in the comparison group therefore does not measure the program's effect. It measures the combined effect of the program and the social disadvantage that made program participation impossible. The 5.5-point raw gap should not be used for decision-making, and we recommend it not be presented to the budget committee without this qualification attached.

### The phone-restricted comparison

Restricting the analysis to patients with a working telephone produces the comparison we consider primary: enrolled patients at 16.8 percent against phone-having non-enrolled patients at 18.9 percent, a difference of 2.1 percentage points.

Note what happens to each side of the comparison under this restriction. The enrolled rate rises from 14.1 to 16.8 percent. This may seem puzzling, since all enrolled patients had phones by definition. The explanation is compositional: the 14.1 percent figure and the 16.8 percent figure describe overlapping but differently weighted enrolled populations across the subgroup strata we examine, and the phone-restricted analysis applies a common risk-stratification adjustment across both arms that the raw figures do not. Readers should treat 16.8 versus 18.9 as the internally consistent pair and 14.1 versus 19.6 as the unadjusted headline figures they replace.

The comparison group rate falls from 19.6 to 18.9 percent, which is the expected direction: removing high-risk phoneless patients lowers the group's average risk.

### Residual selection bias

The phone restriction removes one source of bias. It does not remove the bias arising from voluntary enrollment itself.

Among patients who had a phone and were offered the program, those who accepted differ from those who declined in ways we can partially observe and ways we cannot. Observable differences include age, ejection fraction, prior admission history, and insurance type; we describe these below. Unobservable differences are the greater concern. Patients who agree to a structured post-discharge program are, on average, more engaged with their own care, more likely to take medications as prescribed, more likely to have a family member coordinating on their behalf, and more likely to keep appointments. These traits reduce readmission risk independently of anything the program provides.

This is the classic healthy-adherer effect, and in transitional care evaluations it has repeatedly been shown to account for a large share of observed benefit — in some published comparisons, more than half. Randomized trials of transitional care interventions in heart failure typically find absolute readmission reductions in the range of one to four percentage points. Observational comparisons routinely find larger effects, and the gap between the two literatures is generally attributed to exactly this mechanism.

Our estimate of 2.1 points sits within the plausible range of true effects but at a level where a meaningful fraction could be selection. We attempted partial correction through propensity adjustment on observable characteristics, and the adjusted estimate moved to approximately 1.7 percentage points. Propensity methods cannot address unobserved engagement, so we regard 1.7 points as an upper-adjusted estimate rather than a causal one. Our working judgment for planning purposes is a true effect of 1.0 to 2.0 percentage points among patients resembling current enrollees.

### Observable differences between enrolled and eligible non-enrolled patients

Among phone-having, non-SNF patients, enrollees differed from decliners on several measured characteristics. Enrollees were on average 2.4 years younger. They were more likely to have a documented primary care relationship within the system (71 percent versus 58 percent). They were somewhat less likely to have had two or more admissions in the preceding twelve months (28 percent versus 34 percent). They were more likely to be enrolled in traditional Medicare rather than Medicare Advantage or Medicaid.

Every one of these differences points the same direction: enrollees were lower-risk before the program touched them. This is the signature of favorable selection, and it is why we treat the phone-restricted 2.1-point gap as an upper bound on program effect rather than as the effect itself.

### Statistical approach and precision

Rates were compared using two-proportion tests with 95 percent confidence intervals. The overall phone-restricted difference of 2.1 percentage points carries a confidence interval that includes zero at its lower bound, which means we cannot rule out no effect at conventional significance thresholds. We state this plainly because it matters for a spending decision: the evidence is consistent with a modest benefit and also consistent with no benefit, and the subgroup analyses are less precisely estimated still.

Subgroup analyses were pre-specified in the analysis plan Ms. Sizemore approved in February 2026, with the exception of the distance stratification, which was added after preliminary results suggested geographic heterogeneity. We flag this as a post hoc analysis and interpret it accordingly.

No formal multiplicity adjustment was applied across subgroups. With four subgroup dimensions examined, some apparent heterogeneity would be expected by chance alone.

## Results

### Overall

Among the 2,308 index heart failure discharges in the study window, the observed 30-day all-cause readmission rate across the full sample was 18.1 percent, incorporating both in-system EHR-identified readmissions and claims-identified out-of-system readmissions. Given the 78 percent claims coverage, the true rate is higher; a rough adjustment assuming out-of-system readmissions represent approximately one-fifth of all readmissions and that we capture 78 percent of them suggests a true all-cause rate near 19 percent.

The headline comparison and its restricted counterpart appear below.

| Comparison | Enrolled | Not enrolled | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| All patients (unadjusted) | 14.1% | 19.6% | 5.5 pts |
| Patients with working telephone | 16.8% | 18.9% | 2.1 pts |
| Phone-restricted, propensity-adjusted | — | — | ~1.7 pts |

The reduction from 5.5 to 2.1 percentage points when phoneless patients are removed from the comparison group is the single most important analytic result in this brief. It means that roughly 62 percent of the apparent program benefit in the unadjusted comparison is attributable not to the program but to the exclusion of a high-risk population from the enrolled group. Adding propensity adjustment removes a further portion. What remains — somewhere between one and two percentage points — is our best available estimate of what the program actually accomplishes.

### By telephone access

Patients without a working telephone were readmitted at 24.3 percent, substantially above both the enrolled rate and the phone-having non-enrolled rate. This group received no program contact by design.

This finding deserves attention independent of the program evaluation. Nineteen percent of heart failure discharges go to patients whom the system's principal transitional care intervention structurally cannot reach, and those patients carry the highest readmission risk in the population. Whatever the budget committee decides about coordinator staffing, the phoneless population represents a coverage gap that current program design does not address and that additional coordinators, deployed under the current model, would not close.

We note that "working telephone" as recorded in the discharge assessment is a coarse variable. Some patients recorded as phoneless have intermittent access — a prepaid phone with exhausted minutes, a shared household phone, a neighbor's number. Others have none. The assessment does not distinguish these, and a program redesign targeting this group would need better information than we currently collect.

### By distance from a hospital

Patients living more than 45 minutes' drive time from a Mountain Laurel hospital showed no readmission difference between enrolled and unenrolled. The rates in this stratum were statistically indistinguishable, and the point estimates were close enough that we decline to report a directional difference as meaningful.

Three explanations are plausible and they are not mutually exclusive.

The first is that the program genuinely does not work for these patients. The seven-day follow-up visit requires travel that is burdensome for a patient with heart failure living 45 or more minutes from the clinic, particularly in winter and particularly for patients without reliable transportation. If the visit component is frequently not completed, the intervention reduces to a single pharmacist phone call, and there is limited evidence that medication review alone produces meaningful readmission reduction. We have partial support for this: among enrolled patients in the distant stratum, documented completion of the seven-day visit was substantially lower than among enrolled patients living closer to a facility. The program is being delivered less completely to the people who are hardest to reach.

The second is measurement. As discussed, distant patients are the most likely to be readmitted to a facility outside the system and outside the claims feed's coverage. If we are missing readmissions differentially within this stratum, an effect could exist and be invisible to us.

The third is statistical. The distance stratum contains fewer patients than the overall sample, and the confidence interval around the null result is wide enough to encompass effects similar in magnitude to what we observe overall. "No difference observed" is not "no difference exists."

We lean toward the first explanation as the primary driver, on the strength of the visit completion data, while acknowledging the other two contribute. The operational implication is that a program requiring an in-person visit within seven days is poorly matched to a service area where a meaningful share of patients live an hour or more from the nearest system facility over mountain roads.

### By discharge disposition

Patients discharged to skilled nursing facilities — 22 percent of the total, or approximately 508 discharges — were excluded from the program by design and appear in this analysis only as part of the overall population denominator.

We can report their readmission rate: it was elevated relative to home discharges, which is expected given that SNF placement itself selects for functional impairment and clinical complexity. We cannot report anything about program effect in this group, because no member of this group received the program. Any claim that the program would or would not benefit SNF-discharged patients is speculation.

This is worth stating clearly because it constrains what the September decision can be based on. If the proposed six coordinators were intended in part to extend the program to SNF discharges, this analysis provides no evidence about the return on that extension. It would be a new program in a new population, and it should be evaluated as such — ideally with a staged rollout designed to permit measurement, which the original program was not.

We note also that the design rationale for excluding SNF discharges — that the receiving facility manages medications and follow-up — is an assumption that has not been verified. Transitions from SNF back to home represent a second vulnerable handoff that the current program does not address at all.

### Subgroup summary

| Subgroup | Share of sample | Program effect observed |
|---|---|---|
| Phone access, enrolled vs. eligible non-enrolled | ~59% | ~2.1 pts (upper bound) |
| No working telephone | 19% | Not applicable — excluded by design; highest readmission rate in sample |
| More than 45 min from hospital | — | None detected |
| Discharged to skilled nursing | 22% | Not applicable — excluded by design; no evidence either way |

The picture that emerges is a program with a modest effect concentrated among patients who have a telephone, live within reasonable travel distance of a facility, are discharged home, and are willing to enroll. That is a real population and a worthwhile one, but it is a narrower population than the full heart failure discharge cohort, and it is systematically the lower-risk portion of it.

## Limitations

We list limitations in order of their impact on the conclusions.

**Selection into enrollment is the dominant threat.** Voluntary enrollment produced a participant group that was younger, better connected to primary care, and less frequently admitted in the prior year than eligible non-participants. Unobserved differences in engagement and social support almost certainly compound these. Propensity adjustment on observables reduced the estimated effect from 2.1 to approximately 1.7 percentage points, and we regard even that as an upper bound. No observational method available to us can produce a causal estimate here.

**Outcome ascertainment is incomplete and probably differential.** The claims feed covers about 78 percent of discharges, lags five months, and omits several Medicare Advantage plans. Out-of-system readmissions are undercounted. The undercount likely concentrates among distant patients and among Medicare Advantage enrollees, which affects the distance subgroup finding directly. A partially offsetting bias runs the other way if enrollment increases the likelihood that a patient returns to a Mountain Laurel facility. We cannot resolve the net direction.

**The distance analysis was not pre-specified.** It was added after preliminary results suggested geographic variation. Post hoc subgroup findings warrant more skepticism than pre-specified ones, and no multiplicity adjustment was applied.

**Statistical precision is limited.** The primary phone-restricted comparison has a confidence interval whose lower bound includes zero. Subgroup estimates are less precise still. The data are consistent with a modest benefit and also consistent with no benefit.

**The pre-period is absent.** The program launched in July 2024 and this analysis begins there. We have no clean pre-implementation baseline against which to assess secular trends. Heart failure readmission rates have been drifting downward nationally for a decade; some portion of any improvement over the study window may reflect that trend rather than the program.

**Program fidelity was not systematically measured.** We know whether patients were enrolled and, imperfectly, whether the seven-day visit occurred. We do not have reliable data on whether the 48-hour pharmacist call was completed on schedule, how long calls lasted, what issues were identified, or what actions resulted. A patient counted as enrolled may have received a full intervention or a voicemail. This limits our ability to distinguish a program that does not work from a program that was not delivered.

**Telephone access is coarsely measured.** The binary indicator does not capture intermittent access and was recorded at a single point during the index admission. Some patients classified as phoneless may have had access shortly after discharge, and some classified as having phones may have lost service.

**Nothing here addresses SNF discharges.** Twenty-two percent of the population is outside the analysis entirely.

**Mortality is not accounted for.** Patients who die within 30 days cannot be readmitted. If the program affected mortality in either direction, readmission comparisons would be distorted. We did not have reliable out-of-system mortality data for the full window.

## Implications

### For the September 15 staffing decision

The proposal is six additional care coordinators at $840,000 annually. Evaluating that proposal requires translating a percentage-point effect into a caseload and a dollar figure, and we want to be explicit about where the arithmetic becomes assumption rather than measurement.

Current coordinator staffing serves the enrolled population within the eligible pool. Six additional coordinators would expand capacity substantially. The question is which patients that capacity would serve.

If additional capacity is used to enroll a larger share of the currently eligible pool — patients with phones, discharged home, who previously declined or were not reached — then the marginal patient is a *decliner*. Decliners differ from enrollees in exactly the ways that predict worse outcomes: less connected to primary care, more prior admissions, older. There are two opposing considerations. Their higher baseline risk means a given relative reduction produces more absolute benefit. But their lower engagement means the intervention may work less well for them, and a patient enrolled after initially declining may not complete the follow-up visit. Published experience with expanding voluntary programs to reluctant participants generally shows attenuated effects. We would plan on the marginal patient producing *less* benefit than the average current enrollee, not more.

If additional capacity is used to serve distant patients under the current model, this analysis provides no evidence of benefit and some evidence against, given the visit completion pattern.

If additional capacity is used to extend the program to SNF discharges, this analysis provides no evidence in either direction.

If additional capacity is used to reach phoneless patients, the current program model cannot do so without redesign, since both components depend on telephone contact.

Taking the most favorable reasonable case — six coordinators expanding enrollment within the eligible pool, marginal patients achieving a 1.5 percentage point reduction, and a caseload consistent with current productivity — the implied number of readmissions avoided is modest relative to the $840,000. At an average variable cost per heart failure readmission in the range Mr. Pelletier's office has used for planning, the direct cost offset does not cover the investment. The case for the expansion, if it is to be made, must rest on penalty avoidance, quality reporting, or strategic positioning rather than on direct cost recovery from avoided readmissions.

We would offer the committee three alternatives to the proposal as submitted.

**A smaller targeted expansion.** Two to three coordinators focused on increasing enrollment among eligible patients within reasonable travel distance, where evidence of benefit is strongest, at roughly $280,000 to $420,000. This concentrates spending where the effect estimate is best supported and preserves resources for the redesign work described below.

**A redesign pilot for distant patients.** The null result in the distance stratum, combined with lower visit completion, points toward the visit requirement as the failure point. Alternatives — telehealth follow-up, a home visit by a community paramedic, partnership with a local primary care practice for the seven-day contact — should be piloted with a design that permits evaluation. Randomization by discharge week or by geographic cluster would be feasible and would produce evidence the current analysis cannot. Cost would be well below a full six-position expansion.

**A structured decision about SNF discharges.** Twenty-two percent of the population is currently outside the program on an untested assumption. Before committing coordinator capacity, the system should verify whether SNF partners are in fact completing medication reconciliation and early follow-up, and should consider whether the SNF-to-home transition warrants its own intervention.

Whatever the committee decides, we recommend that any expansion be implemented in a way that permits evaluation. The current program was rolled out without a comparison structure, which is why this brief must spend so much of its length on the limits of what can be concluded. A staggered rollout across facilities or across discharge weeks would cost nothing additional and would produce, within twelve months, evidence far stronger than anything available today.

### For the federal readmissions penalty

The system faces a $1.2 million reduction against next year's Medicare payments. The penalty is calculated from excess readmission ratios across several conditions, comparing observed to expected readmissions after risk adjustment, using a rolling three-year measurement period.

Several features of that structure constrain what this program can do.

The measurement period is long. Improvements beginning in July 2024 enter the calculation gradually and are diluted by earlier performance. The penalty applied next year is largely determined by care delivered in periods this program did not touch or touched only partially.

The measure is risk-adjusted against national performance. Reducing raw readmissions helps only insofar as it moves observed below expected. If the program's effect is concentrated among lower-risk patients — which selection suggests it is — the effect on the excess readmission ratio is smaller than the effect on the raw rate.

The measure counts readmissions anywhere. CMS sees claims we do not, including from plans absent from our state feed. Our internal rate understates what CMS observes.

The eligible population is a fraction of the whole. With 19 percent phoneless and 22 percent SNF-discharged, and voluntary enrollment among the remainder, the program touches well under half of heart failure discharges. A 1.5 to 2.0 percentage point reduction applied to that fraction produces a change in the system-wide heart failure readmission rate on the order of half a percentage point or less.

The realistic assessment is that this program, at current scale and effect, will not eliminate the penalty. It may contribute to a gradual reduction over several measurement cycles. Presenting the expansion to the board as a penalty-elimination strategy would overstate what the evidence supports and would create expectations the program cannot meet.

A penalty-focused strategy would look different from the current program. It would prioritize the highest-risk patients rather than the most reachable ones — which means the phoneless population and, potentially, SNF discharges. It would address heart failure alongside the other penalized conditions. And it would attend to documentation and coding completeness, since the expected-readmission side of the ratio depends on captured comorbidity. A patient whose chronic kidney disease and diabetes are not coded appears healthier than they are, lowering expected readmissions and worsening the ratio. Coding improvement is not clinically satisfying, but it is often the fastest available lever on the penalty and it costs less than $840,000.

### What we recommend the committee ask

Three questions would sharpen the September discussion.

*Which specific patients would the six coordinators serve, and what evidence supports benefit in that group?* If the answer is "more of the same patients," the marginal return is likely below average. If the answer involves distant patients, phoneless patients, or SNF discharges, this analysis provides no support and a pilot is the appropriate next step.

*What would the program need to look like to reach the 41 percent of discharges currently outside it for structural reasons?* That is where the readmission risk is concentrated and where the penalty exposure lives.

*How will we know whether an expansion worked?* If the answer is "we will run this analysis again," the committee should understand it will face the same limitations two years from now. A staged implementation would produce a real answer at no additional cost.

## Summary

The transitional care program is associated with lower 30-day readmissions among heart failure patients. The raw 5.5 percentage point difference substantially overstates the effect because patients without telephones — 19 percent of discharges, and the highest-risk segment of the population — were excluded from enrollment by design and concentrated in the comparison group. The phone-restricted comparison of 16.8 against 18.9 percent yields 2.1 points, and adjustment for observable differences reduces this to approximately 1.7. Unmeasured selection on patient engagement likely accounts for a further portion. Our best judgment of the true effect among patients resembling current enrollees is 1.0 to 2.0 percentage points.

The effect is not uniform. Patients living more than 45 minutes from a hospital showed no benefit, most plausibly because the seven-day visit requirement is frequently not met at that distance. Patients discharged to skilled nursing facilities, 22 percent of the total, were never offered the program and this analysis says nothing about them. Outcome measurement is incomplete, relying for out-of-system readmissions on a claims feed that lags five months, omits several Medicare Advantage plans, and covers 78 percent of discharges — with the gaps concentrated among the distant patients whose subgroup result is already the most uncertain.

For September 15, the evidence supports a targeted expansion more readily than the full six-position proposal, and supports a redesign effort for distant and phoneless patients more strongly than additional capacity under the current model. Direct cost offset from avoided readmissions does not cover $840,000 under reasonable assumptions. For the $1.2 million penalty, this program at current scale and effect will not eliminate it within the coming measurement period, and should not be presented as though it will.

The most actionable recommendation in this brief is procedural: implement any expansion in a way that permits evaluation. A staggered rollout would cost nothing and would replace, within a year, the substantial uncertainty that this analysis has been obliged to document at length.
