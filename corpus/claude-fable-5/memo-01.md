**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** All Members of the Medical Staff, Haverhill and Lowell Campuses
**FROM:** Anjali Ramaswamy, MD, Chief Medical Officer
**DATE:** January 26, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Consolidation of Inpatient Obstetric Services onto the Lowell Campus

I am writing to inform the medical staff, ahead of any public announcement, that the Board of Trustees voted on January 22 to consolidate all inpatient obstetric services onto the Lowell campus, with a target completion date of October 1, 2027. The Haverhill obstetrics unit will continue to accept and deliver patients until the transition date. Prenatal and postpartum ambulatory care will remain at Haverhill without interruption. I want you to hear the reasoning, the timeline, and the process directly from me, and I want to be candid about both the case for this decision and the legitimate concerns that surround it.

The clinical and financial trajectory at Haverhill has been unmistakable for several years. Births at the Haverhill campus have declined from 1,180 in 2019 to 690 in the fiscal year just closed — a 42 percent drop in seven years, driven by demographic shifts in the service area, outmigration to Boston-area academic centers for higher-risk pregnancies, and the closure of two large obstetric practices that historically admitted there. At 690 deliveries a year, the unit averages fewer than two births a day. That volume is below the threshold at which most published literature suggests a unit can reliably maintain team proficiency in obstetric emergencies, and it is well below the volume needed to sustain 24/7 coverage economically. The unit lost $4.3 million last fiscal year. That loss is not an accounting artifact; it is a real subsidy drawn from the rest of the system at a time when our overall operating margin does not permit it indefinitely.

I want to be equally candid that low volume cuts both ways as a safety argument. A unit delivering fewer than two babies a day sees hemorrhage, shoulder dystocia, and emergent cesarean sections infrequently, and skills atrophy. But the alternative — asking laboring patients to travel an average of 14 additional miles — carries its own risks, particularly for precipitous deliveries and for patients with limited transportation. Neither option is risk-free. The Board's judgment, which I supported, is that a single well-staffed, well-resourced unit at Lowell will deliver better outcomes across the whole population than two thinly staffed units, and that the transition risks can be managed through protocol, training, and transport arrangements. I do not expect everyone to agree with that judgment, and I am aware that the Medical Staff Executive Committee voted 11 to 4 against the proposed timeline at its January meeting. The committee's written objection regarding transfer protocols for precipitous deliveries has been received and will get a substantive written response, not a form letter. Latasha Greer, our Director of Community Benefits, is coordinating that response together with my office because the objection raises community access questions as well as clinical ones.

The regulatory path is prescribed by Massachusetts law. Discontinuation of an essential service requires 120 days' advance notice to the Department of Public Health, submission of an essential services plan describing how the affected population will continue to be served, and a public hearing. Our notice was filed January 23. The public hearing has been scheduled for April 9 at Haverhill City Hall. DPH may attach conditions to the closure, and we should all expect that it will — most likely around emergency transport, ED readiness at Haverhill for unplanned deliveries, and continued access to prenatal care. We intend to build those elements into our plan before DPH asks for them.

On the practical side: the Lowell unit will expand from 12 to 20 licensed beds. Capital planning and construction sequencing are being finalized now and Aaron Lindqvist will communicate the budget separately. Sixty-two full-time positions at Haverhill are affected — nursing, surgical technologists, unit clerks, and support staff. Marisol Vega-Ruiz will lead redeployment planning with a stated goal, which I share, of offering a position within the system to every affected employee in good standing. She will communicate the process, including a bidding process for Lowell positions, in the coming weeks. For the medical staff specifically: obstetric hospitalist coverage, community obstetrician privileges, and call arrangements at the consolidated unit are being worked through with department leadership, and I will hold open forums at both campuses in February — dates to follow within the week.

Between now and October 1, nothing changes for patients. Haverhill remains a fully operational obstetric unit, and I expect every one of us to keep it that way. A unit approaching closure is at elevated risk for exactly the lapses in vigilance we cannot afford. If staffing on any shift falls below safe levels, the escalation path runs through the house supervisor to Marisol's office and, if needed, to me directly at any hour.

I have spent enough of my career in community hospitals to know what an obstetrics unit means to a hospital and to a city. This decision was not made lightly, and it will not be executed carelessly. I ask for your engagement — including your disagreement, stated plainly and in the right forums — over the next eight months.

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Executive Leadership Team; Finance Committee of the Board of Trustees
**FROM:** Aaron Lindqvist, Chief Financial Officer
**DATE:** February 20, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Revised Capital and Transition Budget — Obstetrics Consolidation Project

This memo revises the capital and transition budgets for the obstetrics consolidation project following receipt of the general contractor's bid on February 11 and six weeks of actual transition spending. Both revisions are unfavorable, and I want the executive team and the Finance Committee to see the numbers plainly, together with my recommendations, before we are asked about them at the April 9 public hearing — where I expect the project's cost to be raised as evidence that the consolidation is financially driven and financially shaky at once.

**Capital budget.** The approved capital line for expanding the Lowell obstetrics unit from 12 to 20 beds was $5.5 million, based on a schematic-level estimate prepared last October. The lowest responsive bid, from Castellane Construction of Woburn, came in at $8.4 million — $2.9 million, or 53 percent, over the approved line. I have reviewed the bid in detail with Devon Okonkwo and our owner's representative, and the overage decomposes roughly as follows: approximately $1.1 million reflects mechanical, electrical, and medical-gas scope that the schematic estimate simply missed, principally the need to upsize the air handling serving the second floor rather than tie into existing capacity as originally assumed; approximately $0.9 million reflects escalation in structural steel, casework, and skilled-trade labor between the October estimate and the January bid documents; approximately $0.5 million reflects phasing costs — temporary partitions, off-hours work, and infection-control measures — required to keep the existing 12-bed unit operational throughout construction, which the original estimate treated too optimistically; and approximately $0.4 million is contractor contingency and general conditions priced to the compressed schedule. The second and third bids were $8.9 million and $9.3 million, so I do not believe rebidding buys us anything except lost time, and lost time is the one thing the October 1 date cannot absorb.

We have three realistic options. First, fund the full $8.4 million and seek Board approval for a $2.9 million capital supplement. Second, value-engineer the project to a target of roughly $7.4 million; the design team believes about $1.0 million can be removed by deferring two of the eight new beds to a shelled second phase, substituting finishes, and simplifying the family waiting area — meaning Lowell would open at 18 beds on October 1 with two shelled rooms completable within twelve months for approximately $650,000 in then-year dollars. Third, delay the project to redesign and rebid, which I mention only to dismiss: the 120-day DPH clock, the hearing date, and the hospitalist staffing situation make a delayed timeline more expensive and riskier than either funding option. My recommendation is the second option. Combined delivery volume projects to roughly 2,400 births annually, which 18 beds can accommodate at reasonable occupancy, and the shelled rooms preserve the ability to reach 20 beds without a second construction disruption. I am asking the Finance Committee to approve a revised capital authorization of $7.4 million plus a 5 percent owner's contingency, for a total of $7.77 million, at its March 5 meeting.

**Transition budget.** Separately, transition operating costs are running ahead of plan. Against the approved $2.6 million transition contingency, we have committed or spent $1.9 million as of February 15, with seven and a half months remaining before the transition date. The major drivers: locum tenens obstetric hospitalist coverage following the February resignations of Dr. Feld and Dr. Marchetti, which alone is running approximately $118,000 per month and which I project through at least August; retention stipends for Haverhill obstetrics staff, which Marisol Vega-Ruiz and I structured to pay out at transition and which are accruing on schedule at $640,000 total; travel-nurse premiums attributable to the Lowell unit's 23 percent vacancy rate, of which I have allocated the incremental portion above baseline to the project; legal and consulting costs associated with the DPH essential services plan; and community transport planning work under Latasha Greer's office. On current run rate, transition costs will reach approximately $3.4 million by October 1, exceeding the contingency by roughly $800,000. I am not asking for a supplemental transition authorization today — several of these costs, particularly locums, are sensitive to recruitment outcomes that could break favorably — but I am flagging the exposure now so that a supplemental request in May or June, if needed, surprises no one.

**The larger frame.** It is worth restating why we are spending this money. The Haverhill obstetrics unit lost $4.3 million last fiscal year, and that loss was widening, not narrowing, as volume fell. Even at the revised capital figure and the projected transition overrun, the consolidation reaches financial breakeven against the status quo in under three years, and the recurring annual improvement thereafter — conservatively $3.6 million after accounting for incremental Lowell staffing — is money this system needs for commitments we have made elsewhere, including the behavioral health expansion the Board approved in principle last spring. The project's costs have grown; its rationale has not shrunk. But I would rather the Board approve realistic numbers now than watch us return quarterly with unfavorable variances against a budget that was never achievable.

I will present the revised capital request at the March 5 Finance Committee meeting and am available before then to walk any member of the executive team or the committee through the detail.

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Nursing Directors, Managers, and Clinical Coordinators — Haverhill and Lowell Campuses
**FROM:** Marisol Vega-Ruiz, RN, MSN, Chief Nursing Officer
**DATE:** February 27, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Redeployment of Affected Haverhill Obstetrics Staff and the March 15 Position Bidding Process

This memo describes how we will handle the redeployment of the 62 full-time employees whose positions are affected by the consolidation of inpatient obstetrics onto the Lowell campus, and it sets out the mechanics and timeline of the bidding process that opens March 15. I am sending it to nursing leadership first because you will be answering questions about it every day between now and October, and I need every one of you giving the same answers. A staff-facing version, reviewed with the union for our represented employees, will be distributed the week of March 2, and I will hold in-person sessions on the Haverhill unit on March 3 and 4 covering all shifts, including nights.

Let me start with the commitment, because everything else is machinery in service of it: every affected employee in good standing will be offered a position within Merrimack Valley Health Partners. I said this in the town hall on January 28 and I am putting it in writing. That commitment does not mean every employee will be offered the exact role, shift, or campus they hold today — I will not pretend otherwise — but no one loses employment with this system because of this consolidation unless they choose to leave. Given that the Lowell obstetrics unit is carrying a 23 percent vacancy rate and the expanded unit adds positions on top of that, the arithmetic is actually in our favor: we have more obstetric openings at Lowell than we have obstetric nurses at Haverhill. The harder cases will be surgical technologists, unit clerks, and support staff, where the match between Haverhill roles and Lowell openings is less clean, and I address those below.

**The affected population.** Of the 62 affected full-time positions, 38 are registered nurses, 6 are surgical technologists, 5 are certified nursing assistants, 7 are unit clerks and administrative support, and 6 are environmental services and other support roles assigned to the unit. Two of the 38 RN positions are currently vacant, so we are planning for 60 people.

**The bidding process.** All posted positions on the expanded Lowell obstetrics unit — and this is the important part — will be posted internally to affected Haverhill staff before they are posted to anyone else. The exclusive internal window opens Monday, March 15, and closes Friday, March 26 at 5:00 p.m. During that window, affected employees may bid on any posted Lowell obstetrics position for which they meet minimum qualifications. Awards will be made by seniority within qualification, consistent with the collective bargaining agreement for represented staff and with parallel treatment for non-represented staff, and employees will be notified of awards by April 9. An employee awarded a position who declines it remains eligible for the general redeployment process described below but forfeits the transition stipend tied to remaining through October 1; an employee awarded a position who accepts it will remain assigned to Haverhill until the transition date and will begin Lowell orientation on a staggered schedule starting in June. Managers: do not promise anyone a particular shift or line ahead of the bid results. I know the temptation. Resist it.

**Employees who do not bid or are not matched.** Any affected employee not placed through the March bid will work with a redeployment coordinator — Karen Osei from Human Resources is assigned full-time to this project as of March 1 — to identify openings elsewhere in the system. We currently carry more than 140 open positions across both campuses. Employees redeployed into a lower-paid role will receive rate protection at their current base rate for twelve months. The system will reimburse required certifications or course work for employees retraining into a new clinical area, and I have set aside dedicated education funds for Haverhill obstetrics nurses who wish to cross-train into the emergency department, medical-surgical, or perioperative services rather than commute to Lowell. For the surgical technologists, perioperative services at both campuses has confirmed openings sufficient to absorb all six if they choose that path. Unit clerks and support staff will be matched against openings at both campuses; I expect all can be placed at Haverhill for those who cannot or will not travel, though possibly not all on their current shifts.

**Retention through the transition.** We cannot safely run the Haverhill unit through September if staff leave in large numbers this spring, and I will be blunt with you: that is my single biggest operational worry. The retention stipend — details in the staff-facing document, but in summary a lump sum payable at transition for employees who remain in their Haverhill roles through October 1 — is designed to hold the unit together. Managers should reinforce it in every conversation. If any shift on the Haverhill unit falls below the staffing grid, escalate to the house supervisor and to my office immediately; Dr. Ramaswamy and I have agreed that a unit heading toward closure gets more scrutiny on staffing, not less.

**What I need from nursing leadership.** Three things. First, accuracy: answer questions from this memo and the staff-facing document, and when you don't know, say so and route the question to Karen Osei rather than improvising. Second, humanity: some of these employees have delivered babies in Haverhill for twenty-five years, and for them this is a loss no stipend cures; give people room to be angry without treating anger as disloyalty. Third, vigilance: patient care at Haverhill does not get one degree less safe because the unit has an end date. I will be on the Haverhill unit weekly through the transition, and my door — at either campus — is open.

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Executive Leadership Team
**FROM:** Devon Okonkwo, Vice President of Operations
**DATE:** April 16, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Project Status — Obstetrics Consolidation, Week Following the April 9 Public Hearing

This is the consolidated status memo I committed to circulating the week after the DPH public hearing. It covers the hearing itself and what we should expect from the Department, followed by construction, staffing, clinical protocols, and budget. The summary judgment up front: October 1 remains achievable, but the margin has narrowed on two fronts — hospitalist coverage and the likelihood of DPH conditions requiring transport commitments we have not yet contracted — and I want the executive team aligned on both before our April 23 Board update.

**The public hearing.** The hearing was held April 9 at Haverhill City Hall before a DPH designee, running just over four hours with 47 registered speakers. Attendance was approximately 320. Testimony ran heavily against the consolidation, as expected: the mayor of Haverhill, two city councilors, a state representative, the Haverhill firefighters' union president, and roughly 30 residents spoke in opposition, with recurring themes of travel distance, winter road conditions on Route 110 and I-495, ambulance availability, and the equity impact on lower-income patients without reliable transportation. Several speakers cited the Medical Staff Executive Committee's 11-to-4 vote against the timeline, which had been reported in the Eagle-Tribune in March; Dr. Ramaswamy addressed it directly in her testimony, which I thought was the right call, acknowledging the vote and describing the transfer-protocol work underway rather than minimizing the disagreement. Our panel — Dr. Ramaswamy, Latasha Greer, and myself — presented the volume and safety data, the essential services plan, and the community transport commitments. Latasha's testimony on the transportation program and the continuation of prenatal services at Haverhill drew the only neutral-to-positive questions of the evening. Written comments remain open until April 24. DPH's response, including any conditions, is expected in mid-to-late May. Based on the hearing officer's questions, I anticipate conditions in three areas: a binding commitment on emergency transport arrangements, likely including a memorandum of agreement with Trinity EMS and mutual aid provisions; a requirement that the Haverhill emergency department maintain and drill precipitous-delivery capability, with documentation; and a multi-year commitment to maintain prenatal and postpartum ambulatory services at Haverhill. None of these is objectionable — all three are already in our plan in some form — but the EMS memorandum of agreement is not yet signed, and I have moved it to the top of my list. I do not expect DPH to reject the closure or extend the timeline, but that is a judgment, not a guarantee.

**Construction.** The Finance Committee approved the revised $7.77 million capital authorization on March 5, and Castellane Construction mobilized March 23 on the value-engineered 18-bed scope with two shelled rooms. As of this week the project is on schedule: demolition of the second-floor administrative suite is complete, and structural work begins April 20. The critical path runs through the air-handling unit, which has a confirmed delivery of July 6 with a two-week float. Phasing has so far kept the existing 12-bed Lowell unit fully operational; one infection-control breach on April 2 (a compromised temporary barrier) was caught on rounds, corrected within hours, and reported through the quality system with no patient impact. Substantial completion is projected for September 8, leaving three weeks for equipment installation, licensure inspection, and staff orientation before October 1. That is tight but workable; it has no room for a second slip.

**Staffing.** The March 15–26 bidding process concluded on schedule, and award notifications went out April 8, one day ahead of the hearing — deliberately, so employees heard their own outcomes from us before hearing the hospital discussed at a podium. Of the 60 affected Haverhill employees, 41 bid on Lowell obstetrics positions and 36 were awarded them, including 27 of the 36 active RNs. Nine employees were not matched in the bid and have moved into the general redeployment process with Karen Osei; 19 did not bid, of whom 11 have indicated interest in retraining or in non-obstetric roles at Haverhill and 8 are undecided or expected to separate. Combined with the bid awards, the projected Lowell RN vacancy on the expanded unit drops from 23 percent to approximately 9 percent by October, before external recruitment — a genuinely good result, and Marisol and her team deserve the credit. The unresolved problem is hospitalist coverage. With Drs. Feld and Marchetti departing in June, we are down to two employed obstetric hospitalists against a need for five at the consolidated volume. Locum coverage is holding the line at significant cost. Recruitment has produced two signed offers, one starting August 3 and one September 14, and a third candidate is in final interviews. If the third offer closes by mid-May, we open October 1 with adequate employed coverage supplemented by one locum line; if not, we open dependent on two locum lines, which is workable but fragile and expensive. Dr. Ramaswamy is personally leading the third recruitment.

**Clinical protocols.** The transfer protocol for precipitous deliveries — the subject of the MSEC's written objection, answered in Latasha Greer's March 20 memo — has been revised through three joint sessions between the obstetrics department, both emergency departments, and Trinity EMS. The revised protocol was presented to the MSEC on April 7; the committee has not withdrawn its objection to the timeline, but it endorsed the protocol itself by a vote of 12 to 3, which I regard as meaningful progress. Emergency department precipitous-delivery simulation drills begin at Haverhill on May 6 on a monthly cadence through the transition and quarterly thereafter.

**Budget.** Capital spending is $1.4 million against the $7.77 million authorization, tracking to plan. Transition spending stands at $2.35 million against the $2.6 million contingency, and Aaron's February projection of a roughly $800,000 overrun by October remains the working estimate, driven almost entirely by locum costs; a favorable outcome on the third hospitalist recruitment would cut the overrun materially. Aaron intends to bring a supplemental transition authorization to the Finance Committee in June once the DPH conditions and recruitment picture are known.

**Asks of this group.** Three items for the April 23 Board update: alignment on the expected DPH conditions and authority for me to finalize the Trinity EMS memorandum of agreement; visible executive support for the hospitalist recruitment, including schedule flexibility for candidate visits; and agreement that we hold October 1 publicly and privately unless DPH or the air-handling delivery forces the question. A wavering date is the fastest way to lose the staff we just successfully rebid.

**MEMORANDUM**

**TO:** Medical Staff Executive Committee, via Dr. Howard Chen, President of the Medical Staff
**FROM:** Latasha Greer, Director of Community Benefits
**CC:** Anjali Ramaswamy, MD, Chief Medical Officer
**DATE:** March 20, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Response to the Medical Staff Executive Committee's Written Objection Regarding Transfer Protocols for Precipitous Deliveries

Dr. Ramaswamy asked my office to coordinate the administration's response to the Executive Committee's written objection of January 20, and I am answering it jointly on behalf of her office and mine because the committee's concerns sit exactly at the intersection of clinical protocol and community access. I want to begin by saying plainly that the objection was well founded in substance even where we disagree with its conclusion about the timeline. The committee identified real gaps in the transfer plan as it stood in January. Several of those gaps have now been closed because you identified them, and this memo documents what has changed, what remains open, and why the administration continues to believe the October 1 date is the right one.

The committee's objection made four specific points. First, that the draft transfer protocol did not adequately define which patients presenting to the Haverhill emergency department in labor should be delivered on site rather than transferred, leaving individual emergency physicians without obstetric backup to make that call alone. Second, that the plan assumed EMS transport availability that had not been validated against Trinity EMS's actual unit deployment in the northern service area, particularly overnight. Third, that the 14-mile average travel figure understated the risk for the specific subpopulation most likely to present precipitously — patients with limited prenatal care, who are disproportionately low-income and disproportionately concentrated in the Haverhill neighborhoods farthest from Lowell. Fourth, that the timeline did not allow adequate training and drilling of Haverhill emergency department staff before the unit closed. I will take these in order.

On the first point, the transfer protocol has been substantially rewritten through three joint working sessions in February and March involving the obstetrics department, emergency medicine leadership from both campuses, and Trinity EMS medical direction, with Dr. Okafor and Dr. Braganza representing the objecting members of this committee at all three sessions at Dr. Ramaswamy's request. The revised protocol replaces the original's general language with explicit deliver-in-place criteria: cervical dilation of 8 centimeters or greater, active second stage, or any imminent-delivery finding on examination triggers on-site delivery at Haverhill with simultaneous activation of the neonatal transport pathway, rather than maternal transfer. A 24/7 obstetric consultation line staffed from the Lowell unit gives the Haverhill emergency physician immediate specialist backup for the transfer-versus-deliver decision, with a documented response standard of five minutes. The revised protocol was distributed to the committee on April 1 and is scheduled for presentation at your April 7 meeting.

On the second point, the committee was right and the original plan was wrong. Trinity EMS's deployment data, which we obtained in February, showed that overnight ALS coverage in the northern Haverhill response zones could produce transfer response times exceeding twenty minutes under mutual-aid conditions. As a direct result, the essential services plan filed with DPH has been amended to include a contractual commitment — a memorandum of agreement now in final negotiation with Trinity EMS — for a dedicated ALS-capable unit posted within the Haverhill primary service area during overnight hours for the first twelve months following the transition, with performance reporting to a joint oversight group on which the medical staff will hold two seats. We expect DPH to make some version of this a formal condition of the closure, and we will not resist that.

On the third point, my office conducted the access analysis the committee asked for. The committee is correct that averages obscure the burden. For residents of the census tracts comprising Haverhill's Acre and Mount Washington neighborhoods, drive time to Lowell exceeds 35 minutes in ordinary traffic, and approximately 22 percent of households in those tracts lack access to a vehicle. In response, the essential services plan commits to three things beyond the EMS arrangement: continuation of full prenatal, postpartum, and lactation services at the Haverhill campus, so that routine care requires no additional travel at all; a funded transportation program providing scheduled rides to Lowell for prenatal visits, scheduled inductions, and cesarean deliveries, and reimbursement for emergency transportation costs not covered by insurance, budgeted at $210,000 annually for three years through my office; and expansion of the community health worker program to provide delivery planning — including transportation planning — for every Haverhill patient identified as high-risk or care-limited during prenatal care. I would welcome the committee's help in making sure obstetric practices actually refer into that program; a plan on paper serves no one.

On the fourth point, the training timeline has been accelerated and formalized. Monthly precipitous-delivery simulation drills for Haverhill emergency department staff begin May 6 — five months before the transition, not after it — continuing monthly through October and quarterly thereafter, with obstetric and neonatal faculty from Lowell participating. Precipitous-delivery equipment carts and neonatal resuscitation equipment for the Haverhill emergency department have been ordered and will be in place before the first drill.

What this memo does not do is concede the committee's conclusion that the timeline should be extended, and I owe you an honest account of why. The safety case for delay assumes the status quo is safe, and the administration's judgment — Dr. Ramaswamy's clinical judgment in particular, which she has stated to the committee directly — is that it is not. A unit delivering fewer than two babies a day, now covered substantially by rotating locum hospitalists following the February resignations, carries its own accruing risk with every month of extension, and an extended timeline would prolong exactly the fragile-coverage period the committee is worried about. The disagreement between the committee's majority and the administration is, at bottom, a disagreement about which set of risks is larger, and reasonable clinicians have landed on both sides of it. What the administration can commit to is this: the mitigation measures described above will be in place and demonstrated — not merely planned — before October 1, and if the drills, the EMS agreement, or the consultation line are not performing to standard by September 1, Dr. Ramaswamy will bring that finding to this committee and to the Board before the transition proceeds. That commitment is made in writing here and she has authorized me to make it.

The committee's objection made this plan better. I hope the committee will stay engaged in the same spirit — through the two oversight seats, through the April 7 protocol review, and through the drills — whether or not the objection to the timeline is ever withdrawn. My office's obligation runs to the patients on both ends of those 14 miles, and I take the committee's letter as evidence that yours does too.
