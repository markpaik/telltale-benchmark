# Incident Postmortem: Undeclared Milk Allergen in Line 3 Plant-Based Entrees

**Kettle Point Foods — Fall River, Massachusetts**
**Incident ID:** FR-2026-0402
**Classification:** Class I Recall — Undeclared Allergen (Milk)
**Postmortem Prepared:** June 12, 2026
**Distribution:** Executive Leadership Team, Plant Leadership, Corporate Food Safety Council, Board Audit Committee

---

## 1. Purpose and Scope

This postmortem examines the sequence of decisions, system conditions, and organizational conditions that permitted cheese powder residue to enter a dairy-free certified production line, remain undetected for nineteen days across nine production days, and continue after a formal investigation had been opened and a corrective action taken.

The document is written to the standard of a blameless postmortem. Individuals are named where naming is necessary to establish sequence and accountability for follow-up actions. The analysis does not treat any individual decision as the cause of the incident. Every decision described below was made by a competent person acting on the information available to them within the systems that existed at the time. Where a decision was wrong, the relevant question this document asks is why the system made that decision reasonable, available, or unavoidable — not why the person made it.

Scope covers the period from March 2, 2026 (submission of the revised food safety plan for approval) through May 29, 2026 (closure of the FDA inspection response). Certain contributing conditions predate this window, notably the 2025 headcount reduction that reduced allergen environmental monitoring coverage, and these are examined as background conditions rather than as events in the incident timeline.

This document is not a legal filing, does not constitute a regulatory submission, and does not supersede the Corrective Action Preventive Action (CAPA) records filed under FR-2026-0402-CAPA-01 through -09.

## 2. Summary

Over the shutdown weekend of April 4–5, 2026, a maintenance technician reconfigured a diverter valve in the dry ingredient transfer system serving Line 3. The reconfiguration routed Line 3's dry ingredient conveyance through a pneumatic header dedicated to Line 2. Line 2 runs a cheese powder product. Line 3 is certified dairy-free and produces a plant-based entree sold in retail nationally, with dairy-free status as a primary consumer purchase driver and label claim.

The work order for the valve reconfiguration was closed the same day without the verification step the procedure required. It was never routed to allergen review, because the change control system did not classify pneumatic conveyance modifications as allergen-relevant changes and therefore did not generate a review trigger.

Line 3 ran nine production days between April 6 and April 24, producing 218,000 cases distributed to fourteen states. The first consumer complaint arrived at the plant on April 17 and was coded as a flavor complaint under the existing complaint taxonomy. Two additional complaints arrived during the week of April 20; one described a reaction requiring hospitalization in Rhode Island.

An investigation opened April 24. It attributed the contamination to a newly qualified dry blend supplier, placed the implicated raw material on hold, and cleared Line 3 to restart. Line 3 produced and shipped an additional 26,000 cases before analytical testing on April 29 detected casein in a lot that did not contain any material from the suspect supplier. Line 3 was shut down that day. The valve configuration was traced on April 30. A Class I recall was filed with FDA on May 2.

The recall cost $4.7 million in direct expense, recovered 71 percent of distributed cases, and drew an FDA inspection that closed with six Form 483 observations. Three allergic reactions were reported, one requiring hospitalization.

The incident is best understood not as a single failure but as the sequential failure of five independent controls that were each individually designed to catch precisely this class of event: change control, verification of maintenance work, environmental allergen monitoring, consumer complaint escalation, and investigation methodology. Each of these controls had been weakened, deferred, narrowed, or under-specified in the eighteen months preceding the incident, largely through decisions that were individually defensible and collectively fatal.

**March 2, 2026** — Revised food safety plan, including an expanded allergen hazard analysis and revised preventive controls for cross-contact, submitted for approval. The revision specifically identified shared pneumatic conveyance as a cross-contact hazard requiring dedicated controls. Approval was pending at the time of the incident and remained pending on April 24.

## 5. Root Cause

**The dry ingredient conveyance system was designed so that a single valve reconfiguration could connect a dairy-free line to a dairy-bearing line, and the plant's change control system did not recognize that reconfiguration as a change requiring allergen review.**

This is a design and control-classification failure, not a maintenance execution failure. The technician who repositioned DV-317 resolved a real and repeatedly reported flow restriction using an available and physically permitted configuration. The system did not prevent the connection, did not flag the connection, and did not require anyone with allergen expertise to evaluate it.

Three properties of the system made this outcome available:

**The physical system permitted the connection.** The pneumatic headers for Lines 2 and 3 shared a common manifold with a diverter valve that could be positioned to cross-connect them. There was no physical interlock, no keyed lock, and no engineering barrier preventing the cross-connection. A dairy-free line and a cheese-powder line were separated by the position of a manually repositionable valve.

**The change control system classified by work type rather than by hazard exposure.** Change control triggers were keyed to categories such as "formulation change," "equipment replacement," and "process parameter change." Corrective maintenance was excluded from change control by design, on the reasoning that corrective maintenance restores a system to its specified state rather than altering it. In this case corrective maintenance altered the system's allergen segregation architecture. The classification scheme had no way to see that.

**The verification requirement was not enforced by the system that recorded it.** The procedure required second-person verification of the work. The CMMS permitted the same credential to open, perform, and verify. The control existed on paper and did not exist in the software.

The root cause statement above is deliberately narrow. It identifies the condition that, if absent, would have prevented the incident entirely. Everything in the following section describes conditions that determined how long the incident lasted and how much product was affected — but not whether it occurred.

## 6. Contributing Factors

### 6.1 Detection Lag — Nineteen Days

Nineteen days elapsed between the first contaminated production day and the shutdown. Four independent detection opportunities existed and none functioned.

**Environmental monitoring.** The allergen swab program had been reduced from 20 sites to 6 in 2025. The six retained sites were selected on historical positive rate — that is, on where positives had been found before — which systematically favored sites where contamination was chronic and disfavored sites where segregation was believed robust. The dry ingredient conveyance system had never produced a positive, precisely because segregation was believed to be reliable, and so it was among the first sites cut. The selection methodology inverted the logic of environmental monitoring: monitoring exists to detect the failure of controls believed to be working, and the program was reconfigured to monitor only where controls were already known to be unreliable. Had any of the fourteen eliminated sites remained, the probability of detection within the first production week was high.

**Finished product testing.** Line 3's dairy-free verification relied on quarterly milk protein testing supplemented by segregation controls. The testing frequency was set on the assumption that segregation was the primary control and testing was verification of that control. When segregation failed silently, verification was too infrequent to catch it. The most recent quarterly test predated the valve reconfiguration.

**Pre-operational inspection.** The April 6 pre-op inspection passed. Its scope covered product contact surfaces on the line. Upstream conveyance routing was outside scope, on the reasoning that conveyance routing does not change. This is true under normal conditions and was false on April 6.

**Consumer complaints.** Discussed in 6.3.

The common structure across all four: each control's scope or frequency had been set on an assumption about system stability that the April 4 change violated. None of the controls was designed to detect a change in the system's configuration; they were designed to detect variation within a stable configuration.

### 6.2 The Supplier Misdiagnosis and the April 24 Restart

The April 24 investigation reached a wrong conclusion and acted on it in a way that extended the incident by two production days and 26,000 cases. The reasoning that produced this outcome is worth reconstructing carefully, because it was not careless.

The investigation asked: what changed? It queried the change control log, the authoritative record of process changes. The log showed a new dry blend supplier qualified in March, first used April 8. The supplier operated a facility handling dairy. This is a textbook cross-contact scenario, it was the only change visible in the authoritative record, and it fit the timeline approximately.

The investigation did not find the valve change because corrective maintenance did not generate change control entries. The change control log was, by design, an incomplete record of changes. The investigation trusted it as complete. This is the central failure of the investigation: not that it reached a plausible hypothesis, but that it treated a single record system as an exhaustive account of what had changed, and did not cross-check against the maintenance record.

Three further conditions shaped the restart decision:

**Hypothesis confirmation preceded hypothesis testing.** The supplier was placed on hold and the line restarted on April 24. Confirmatory testing was submitted the same day with a five-day turnaround. The corrective action was implemented and production resumed before the hypothesis supporting it was tested. The sequence was containment, then restart, then verification — when the risk profile required containment, then verification, then restart.

**The timeline mismatch was not treated as disqualifying.** The first complaint arrived April 17, describing product consumed some days earlier. The suspect material entered production April 8. This is compatible but tight. More significantly, no one tested whether complaint lot codes predated April 8. Retained sample testing would have falsified the supplier hypothesis in days rather than weeks; instead, testing was scoped to the supplier's material rather than to the affected lots. The investigation tested its hypothesis rather than testing the product.

**Restart authority did not require independent concurrence.** The decision to clear Line 3 rested with the Director of Food Safety and Quality alone. No procedure required a second signature, a plant manager concurrence, or a hold on distribution pending confirmatory results. For a line under active investigation for an undeclared allergen with a reported medical event, restart authority resting with a single individual on an unconfirmed hypothesis represents a governance gap rather than an individual judgment error. Anyone in that role, holding that hypothesis, facing production pressure on a nationally distributed SKU, would likely have made the same call.

It also bears noting that the investigation was opened on April 24 with no dedicated investigator, no defined methodology, and no allocated time. It was conducted in a single day alongside normal duties. The plant had no procedure specifying the depth of investigation warranted by a suspected allergen event, no requirement for a structured technique, and no requirement that a suspected undeclared allergen trigger an automatic hold on distributed product pending confirmation.

### 6.3 Complaint Handling

Three complaints over six days on a single SKU, one of them describing a hospitalization, did not reach Quality for seven days after the first arrived. The reason is structural rather than behavioral.

**The taxonomy had no allergen category.** Complaints were coded by the descriptive language consumers used. A consumer who says the product tasted cheesy generates a flavor complaint. A consumer with a dairy allergy who says the product tasted cheesy generates a flavor complaint. The taxonomy could not represent "consumer describes a sensory attribute characteristic of an allergen not declared on this label," which is exactly what the April 17 complaint was.

**The medical complaint route bypassed Quality.** MED-01 routed to Legal and Consumer Affairs management. This routing was designed for liability management and functioned as designed. It did not route to Food Safety, meaning the plant's most severe complaint category — a consumer reporting hospitalization — reached the function responsible for identifying food safety hazards only through informal channels.

**No trend rule existed.** Three complaints on one SKU in six days is a signal. Nothing in the system computed that. Detection depended on a manager noticing an anomaly during a weekly review — which is what happened, and which is why the investigation opened at all. The system's only functioning detection mechanism in this incident was an individual's pattern recognition operating outside any defined process.

**Escalation had no clock.** No rule specified that any complaint class must reach Quality within a defined window. The seven-day lag violated no requirement.

### 6.4 The Change Control Failure

Beyond the classification gap described in Section 5, three enabling conditions:

**Verification was recordable by the performer.** The CMMS accepted the same credential for execution and verification. The procedure's requirement was not enforced by the system holding the record. This affected an unknown number of work orders beyond this one; a subsequent audit found 31 percent of corrective maintenance work orders closed in the preceding twelve months carried identical execution and verification credentials.

**Allergen review was triggered by change type, not by location.** The system asked what kind of change was being made. It did not ask where. A location-based trigger — any work on systems serving Line 3, or any work on shared conveyance — would have caught this. Any change-type-based trigger would have had to anticipate that a diverter valve reconfiguration was allergen-relevant.

**The revised food safety plan had identified this hazard and was pending.** The March 2 revision specifically identified shared pneumatic conveyance as a cross-contact hazard requiring dedicated controls. The plan was in approval queue on April 4. The organization had identified the hazard, documented it, and not yet implemented the control. The delay was not the result of any decision to defer; the plan was in normal queue with no expedite mechanism and no risk-based prioritization for revisions addressing newly identified hazards. There is no mechanism by which the identification of a hazard accelerates the approval of its control.

### 6.5 The Swab Program Reduction

The 2025 reduction from 20 sites to 6 warrants specific examination as a decision-making failure rather than a resourcing failure.

The reduction was executed as a headcount decision with a downstream sampling consequence. It was documented as a resource allocation. It was not documented as a change to a preventive control, was not risk-assessed, and was not routed through food safety plan reanalysis — despite Preventive Controls requirements that a change to a verification activity for an identified hazard requires reanalysis.

Two properties of the reduction compounded the risk:

The **selection methodology was inverted**, as described in 6.1. Sites were retained on historical positive rate. Environmental monitoring exists to detect failures of controls believed reliable. Selecting on historical positives systematically eliminates monitoring from exactly the locations where a control failure would be most surprising and most consequential.

The **reduction was invisible in risk terms.** No document existed after Q3 2025 stating that Line 3's dry conveyance system was no longer monitored for milk allergen. The change was recorded as a laboratory staffing adjustment. Anyone assessing Line 3's allergen control posture in early 2026 — including the authors of the March food safety plan revision — would have had to reconstruct the monitoring gap from sampling schedules rather than read it from a risk document.

### 6.6 Organizational Conditions

Three background conditions appear across multiple contributing factors:

**Controls were assessed individually rather than as a system.** The swab reduction, the quarterly testing frequency, the pre-op inspection scope, and the change control classification were each defensible in isolation. No process existed that would have shown that their combination left dry ingredient conveyance with no allergen detection coverage at all. Nothing in the plant's governance asked what the aggregate detection coverage was for any given hazard.

**Food safety decisions were distributed across functions with no integrating role.** Complaint taxonomy sat with Consumer Affairs. Swab site selection sat with the Quality laboratory. Change control classification sat with Maintenance and Engineering. Verification enforcement sat with the CMMS administrator. Each function made locally rational decisions. No role held the integrated view.

**Investigation quality was not governed.** The plant had no standard for what a suspected allergen investigation must include, no requirement for structured methodology, no requirement to test the product rather than the hypothesis, and no requirement for independent concurrence on restart. Investigation quality depended entirely on the individual conducting it.

## 7. What Worked

**The April 29 shutdown decision.** When the laboratory result falsified the supplier hypothesis, the plant shut Line 3 within thirty minutes, without waiting for confirmation of an alternative cause and without waiting for a root cause to be identified. Thibodeau's decision to hold Line 2 as well — before any evidence implicated it — was correct and consequential. This is the incident's clearest example of good judgment under uncertainty.

**Retained sample program.** Retained samples existed for every production day in the window and were adequate for expedited testing. Without them, the exposure window could not have been bounded and the recall scope would have been substantially wider.

**The April 30 physical trace.** Mbeki's decision to conduct a physical walkdown from receiving forward, rather than relying on documentation, located the cause in just over three hours. The documentation available on April 30 was the same documentation available on April 24; what changed was the method. This is the strongest available evidence that the April 24 investigation failed on methodology rather than on information.

**Cross-functional team formation.** The April 29 team convened within twenty minutes of the shutdown decision with clear functional ownership. From April 29 forward, response execution was fast and well-coordinated.

**Traceability.** Lot-level traceability from production through distribution was accurate and complete. The 14-state distribution list was reconstructed within hours. The 71 percent recovery rate is above industry median for a retail-distributed frozen product with this consumption profile.

**Regulatory execution.** FDA notification occurred within six hours of root cause confirmation. The recall was filed within 48 hours of shutdown. The health hazard evaluation and classification were completed without regulatory rework. FDA accepted the Form 483 response without further observation — an outcome that reflects response quality, not incident severity.

**Consumer Affairs pattern recognition.** The manager who noticed three complaints on one SKU in six days and forwarded them to Quality initiated the only investigation that occurred before laboratory confirmation. No process required this. It was the system's only functioning detection mechanism.

## 8. What Did Not Work

**Detection.** Nineteen days, nine production days, 218,000 cases. Every engineered detection control was out of scope, out of frequency, or eliminated. The plant detected this incident through consumer harm.

**The April 24 investigation.** Reached a wrong conclusion by treating an incomplete record as complete, tested the hypothesis rather than the product, implemented corrective action before verification, and did not cross-check maintenance records. Conducted in one day with no dedicated resource and no methodology requirement.

**The April 24 restart decision.** Extended the incident by two production days and 26,000 cases — 10.7 percent of total recall volume. Made on a single unconfirmed hypothesis, by a single authority, without concurrence, without a hold on distributed product, and without finished product testing of the affected window.

**Complaint escalation.** Seven days from first complaint to Quality. A reported hospitalization did not route to Food Safety. The taxonomy could not represent an allergen signal. No trend rule, no escalation clock.

**Change control classification.** Did not classify a change that connected a dairy-free line to a dairy line as allergen-relevant. Corrective maintenance was categorically excluded from change control.

**Verification enforcement.** Second-person verification was procedurally required and technically unenforced. Subsequent audit found the same pattern in 31 percent of corrective maintenance closures over twelve months.

**Environmental monitoring.** Reduced 70 percent without risk assessment, without food safety plan reanalysis, and using a selection methodology that inverted the purpose of the program.

**Food safety plan approval queue.** The control for this exact hazard had been documented for 33 days and was awaiting approval. No expedite path existed for revisions addressing newly identified hazards.

**Aggregate control assessment.** No process existed to detect that a hazard had lost all detection coverage through the accumulation of individually reasonable decisions.

## 10. Closing Observations

Three points bear emphasis.

**First, the decisive failure was silent, not loud.** The valve was reconfigured to solve a real problem, by a competent person, using an available configuration. Nothing alerted. Nothing failed visibly. The plant operated for nineteen days with a dairy-free line connected to a cheese powder line, and every indicator read normal, because every indicator was measuring something else. Systems that depend on people noticing what has changed will fail whenever a change is not visible from where people are looking.

**Second, the extension of the incident past April 24 is the more instructive failure.** The initial contamination was a design gap. The five additional days and 26,000 additional cases resulted from an investigation that trusted an incomplete record, tested a hypothesis instead of a product, and restarted before verifying. Nothing about the information available on April 24 made the correct answer inaccessible. Mbeki found it on April 30 using the same records, in three hours, by walking the line. The difference was method, and method is governable.

**Third, the controls that failed had been deliberately weakened, and each weakening was reasonable at the time.** The swab reduction was a defensible response to a headcount constraint. The complaint taxonomy was adequate for the complaints it was designed for. The change control classification correctly excluded routine maintenance from a process built for engineered changes. The quarterly testing frequency was appropriate for verifying a segregation control believed sound. Each decision was locally correct. Their combination removed every detection control from a hazard that the organization had itself identified in writing five weeks before the incident. No mechanism existed to see the combination. Action items 23, 24 and 27 exist for this reason and are, in the assessment of this review, the most important items on the list.

The organization did not lack the knowledge to prevent this. The March 2 food safety plan revision identified shared pneumatic conveyance as a cross-contact hazard requiring dedicated controls. That document was correct, was written by people who understood the risk, and was awaiting a signature on April 4.
