# The Lamplighter's Objection

There was once a city that decided to replace its lamplighters with a machine.

The machine was elegant: a clockwork carriage that rolled the avenues at dusk, extending a brass arm to each lamp, striking a flame, moving on. It never forgot a street. It never came late from a tavern. It never left the eastern quarter dark because its knees hurt.

The lamplighters were given pensions and thanks, and most of them took both gladly, because lighting lamps is cold work and the pension was decent. But one of them — a man named Osric, who had lit the lamps of Fisher Lane for thirty-one years — filed an objection with the city council.

The objection was not that he wanted his job back. He said so plainly in the first line, which annoyed the council, because they had prepared several arguments about progress and had nowhere to put them.

His objection was this: *The machine does not know that the lamp on the corner of Fisher and Bell is the one Mrs. Aldwyn watches for.*

The council asked him to clarify.

He wrote back: Mrs. Aldwyn is eighty-four. Her son sailed out of the harbor eleven years ago and did not come back, and she has not accepted this, and every evening she sits at her window and waits for the lamp at Fisher and Bell to come on, because that lamp is the one you can see from the harbor road, and if a man were walking up from the docks at night, that is the lamp he would walk toward. I have lit that lamp first for eleven years. Not second, not fourth. First. She knows my order. When the lamp comes on she puts the kettle on, and she has a cup of tea, and then she goes to bed. If the lamp comes on in a different order she sits up until midnight, because she does not know what has changed and she is afraid it means something.

The machine, Osric wrote, lights Fisher and Bell fourteenth. This is the most efficient route. I have checked, and it is genuinely the most efficient route. I am not objecting to the route.

*Then what,* asked the council, *are you objecting to?*

And Osric wrote: I am objecting to the fact that no one asked. Not because they would have chosen differently — they might have, they might not have, and I could argue either side. I am objecting because the question was never in the room. You built a machine that solved the problem of lamps and it solved it beautifully, and the problem of lamps turned out not to be the whole problem, and there was no place in your process where anybody could have noticed that. The route was optimized before anyone thought to ask what the lamps were for.

The council debated this for some weeks. The minutes survive. One member argued that Mrs. Aldwyn's grief was not the city's business and that the city could not run its infrastructure on the private cosmologies of the bereaved, which is a fair point and was well made. Another argued that if you started making exceptions for Mrs. Aldwyn you would find that every lamp in the city was somebody's lamp, that the whole grid was a lattice of small private meanings, and you would never be able to change anything ever again. This is also a fair point, and it is probably true.

A third member said something that got only one line in the minutes: *But that would be worth knowing.*

In the end they did a small thing. They did not change the route. They sent a clerk to Fisher Lane to explain to Mrs. Aldwyn what had happened and why the lamp now came on later, and to tell her that the change did not mean anything, that no news had come, that the harbor was the same as it had ever been. The clerk was there some time. He reported afterward that she had been perfectly reasonable about it and had given him tea, and that she had asked whether the machine would be reliable in winter, and he had said yes, and she had said good, and that had been the whole of it.

Osric's objection was formally dismissed. It is filed in the city archive under *Nuisance Correspondence*, which is where they put things that were not wrong but were not actionable, a category most municipalities have and few name so honestly.

---

I think about this story, which I have made up, more than is reasonable.

The temptation is to read it as a story against machines, and it isn't. The machine was better. That is not a concession the story makes reluctantly; it is the whole premise. A worse machine would make the story easy and the story is not easy. The clockwork carriage lit every lamp every night, in rain, in the eastern quarter, in the years when Osric's knees would have failed him. Whatever Mrs. Aldwyn lost, some other person on some unlit street gained more, and we do not know that person's name because that is how gains of that kind work — they are diffuse and anonymous and they do not write letters to the council.

Nor is it a story about consultation, exactly, though it looks like one. Osric himself admits the council might have consulted everyone and still chosen the efficient route. He is not claiming that asking produces the right answer. He is claiming something stranger and harder to act on: that a process which cannot represent a certain kind of consideration will systematically produce outcomes that are wrong in ways it cannot detect. Not wrong on average. Wrong in a specific direction, invisibly, forever.

The route was optimized before anyone thought to ask what the lamps were for. That is the sentence. Every disaster of design I have ever encountered lives inside that sentence. You cannot ask what a thing is for after you have built the thing, because by then the thing has answered the question by existing, and its answer is very loud, and it is the only answer anyone can hear.

What I find myself unable to resolve is the second councilor's objection, the one about the lattice. She is right. Every lamp is somebody's lamp. If you took Osric seriously — really seriously, all the way down — you would discover that the city is not a grid of lamps at all but a dense weave of private meanings, that the water main runs under the place where somebody was proposed to, that the tram schedule is load-bearing in a marriage, that there is no piece of infrastructure anywhere that is merely infrastructure. And you would be paralyzed. Not by sentimentality but by accuracy. The paralysis would be the correct response to a true perception, and it would still be paralysis, and people would still need light.

So the honest position is not *ask everyone* and it is not *ask no one*. It is something more uncomfortable: that we are always going to be building on top of meanings we cannot see, and the only available virtue is a kind of ongoing flinch — a willingness to be told, afterward, what we broke, and to not immediately explain why it was necessary. The clerk who went to Fisher Lane did not undo anything. He sat in a room with the cost. That is a small thing and it is not nothing, and I suspect it is most of what is actually available.

I notice I want to end this by resolving it. I want to say that Osric was right, or that the council was right, or that there is a synthesis in which the machine lights Fisher and Bell first on Tuesdays. There is no synthesis. There is a woman who watched a lamp, and a machine that lit it fourteenth, and a filing category for things that were not wrong but were not actionable.

I would like to be filed there, when it comes to it. It seems like the right shelf.
