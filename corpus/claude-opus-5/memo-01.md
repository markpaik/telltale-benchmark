# Merrimack Valley Health Partners
## Internal Memorandum Packet — Obstetric Services Consolidation

---

**TO:** Medical Staff, Haverhill and Lowell Campuses
**FROM:** Anjali Ramaswamy, MD, Chief Medical Officer
**DATE:** January 26, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Consolidation of Inpatient Obstetric Services to the Lowell Campus

---

I am writing to inform you that on January 21 the Board of Trustees approved the consolidation of inpatient obstetric services onto the Lowell campus, with a target effective date of October 1, 2027. The Haverhill obstetric unit, including labor and delivery, the postpartum unit, and the Level I nursery, will cease inpatient operations on that date. Prenatal care, ultrasound, non-stress testing, lactation consultation, and the midwifery practice will remain at Haverhill. What is moving is the delivery of babies and the inpatient care that immediately surrounds it.

I want to give you the reasoning directly, because you will hear versions of it secondhand within the week and I would rather you hear it from me first.

Deliveries at Haverhill have declined from 1,180 in fiscal 2019 to 690 in fiscal 2026, a drop of forty-two percent over seven years. This is not a local anomaly. Birth rates across Essex County have fallen, the population served by the Haverhill campus has aged, and a meaningful share of the patients who would once have delivered with us now deliver at Holy Family in Methuen or at Anna Jaques in Newburyport. In fiscal 2026 the Haverhill obstetric service recorded a direct operating loss of $4.3 million. That figure excludes allocated overhead. It is the cost of running the unit against what the unit collected.

The volume figure concerns me more than the financial one. At 690 annual deliveries the Haverhill unit averages just under two births per day. On a meaningful number of days there are none. A labor and delivery unit is a team performance, and teams that perform infrequently perform worse. Our own data shows this. Over the past three fiscal years the Haverhill unit's rate of unanticipated transfers to a higher level of care, our time-to-incision on unscheduled cesarean sections, and our postpartum hemorrhage response times have each drifted in the wrong direction relative to Lowell, and relative to our own performance five years ago. None of these figures has crossed a threshold that would require reporting. All of them are moving in a direction that I do not want to keep watching.

The consolidated Lowell unit is projected to handle approximately 2,100 annual deliveries, which places it in a volume band where continuous in-house obstetric hospitalist coverage, twenty-four-hour anesthesia dedicated to the labor floor, and a full-time neonatal nurse practitioner become financially and operationally sustainable. None of those three things is sustainable at Haverhill's current volume, and the absence of the first two is what worries me most about the status quo.

Regulatory Process

Massachusetts requires 120 days of advance written notice to the Department of Public Health before the discontinuation of an essential health service. We filed that notice on January 22. The regulation further requires the submission of an essential services plan describing how the affected population will continue to receive care, and a public hearing conducted by the Department. That hearing has been scheduled for April 9 at the Haverhill Citizens Center. Attendance is open. I expect it to be well attended and I expect much of the testimony to be against us. Members of the medical staff are free to testify in any capacity they choose, and I would ask only that anyone speaking as a representative of the medical staff make clear whether they are doing so or speaking personally.

The essential services plan is being drafted by Latasha Greer's office with clinical input from me and from Marisol Vega-Ruiz. A draft will circulate to the medical staff executive committee before submission. I want your comments on it, particularly on the transfer and stabilization sections, and I want them in writing so that they can be incorporated or answered rather than absorbed into a hallway conversation.

Workforce

Sixty-two full-time equivalent positions are affected. Marisol Vega-Ruiz will communicate directly with nursing leadership regarding redeployment, the bidding process, and the applicable collective bargaining provisions. The system's intention is that every affected employee who wishes to remain with Merrimack Valley Health Partners will be offered a position. I want to be careful about how strongly I state that. The intention is real. The mechanics of matching sixty-two people to open positions across two campuses, with seniority rules and shift preferences and commuting distances in play, will not be clean, and I would rather not promise a clean outcome and then deliver a messy one.

What I Expect to Hear From You

The distance question is real. Patients in the Haverhill service area will travel an average of fourteen additional miles to reach the Lowell campus. For a patient in Bradford or Groveland that is a meaningful change. For a patient without a car it is a larger change than the mileage suggests. I do not intend to minimize this and the essential services plan will address transportation directly.

The precipitous delivery question is also real, and it is the one I take most seriously. A patient who would have arrived at Haverhill in advanced labor will now arrive at a Haverhill emergency department that has no obstetric unit behind it. That patient will be stabilized and delivered where she is if delivery is imminent, and transferred if it is not. Both pathways require protocols, equipment, and drilled staff that we do not currently have in place at the Haverhill ED. Building them is a condition of the consolidation, not a follow-on to it, and I will say the same thing to the Board that I am saying to you.

I will hold open sessions on February 3 at 7:00 a.m. and February 5 at 5:30 p.m., both in the Haverhill medical staff conference room, and I will attend the February medical staff executive committee meeting in full. My office line and pager are unchanged. I would rather field this directly than have it accumulate.

**TO:** Executive Team; Finance Committee of the Board
**FROM:** Aaron Lindqvist, Chief Financial Officer
**DATE:** February 24, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Revised Capital and Transition Budget — Obstetric Consolidation

---

The construction bid for the Lowell obstetric expansion came in on February 17 at $8,412,000. The approved capital line is $5,500,000. The variance is $2,912,000, or fifty-three percent above authorization. This memo describes what happened, what I propose to do about it, and what I am not able to solve.

The Bid

We received three responsive bids. The low bid was $8,412,000 from Casella Brothers Construction; the other two came in at $8,790,000 and $9,140,000. The clustering tells me the number is the market, not an outlier. Our original $5,500,000 estimate was developed in August 2025 by our owner's representative using a cost-per-square-foot factor drawn from a 2023 project at a comparable facility, escalated at four percent annually. Actual escalation in Massachusetts healthcare construction over that period was closer to eleven percent annually, and the escalation factor alone accounts for roughly $980,000 of the gap.

The remainder is scope. Three items drove it. First, the expansion from twelve to twenty beds triggers a full sprinkler and fire alarm upgrade for the entire third floor east wing rather than the affected rooms only, because the addition crosses a threshold in the state building code that we had not identified. That is approximately $740,000. Second, the existing medical gas infrastructure on the third floor cannot support eight additional patient rooms plus a second operating room, and the riser replacement runs from the basement. That is approximately $610,000. Third, the two new operating rooms require air handling capacity that the current rooftop unit cannot deliver, and the structural analysis indicates the roof requires reinforcement to carry a larger unit. That is approximately $455,000, with the balance of the variance spread across general conditions, contractor contingency, and a longer schedule.

I want to be plain that the first two of these were discoverable in August 2025 and were not discovered. The estimate was prepared without a mechanical, electrical, and plumbing walkthrough of the existing space. That was a process failure in my department, and I have changed our threshold so that any capital project above two million dollars requires a full MEP assessment before an estimate goes to the Board.

Transition Costs

Separately from capital, we budgeted $2,600,000 in transition costs, funded as a contingency reserve. Through February 20 we have committed or expended $1,918,000. The composition is not what we projected. We budgeted $900,000 for retention bonuses and have committed $1,340,000, because the retention offers required to hold Haverhill obstetric nursing staff through October have been substantially richer than modeled. We budgeted $600,000 for temporary agency staffing at Lowell and have committed $410,000, which is favorable, though I expect that to reverse in the third and fourth quarters as Lowell volume builds. We budgeted $500,000 for the essential services plan, the public hearing process, outside regulatory counsel, and community engagement, and have committed $168,000, with the majority of that spend still ahead of us. The $600,000 balance covered signage, information technology, medical records migration, and equipment relocation, against which we have committed zero because none of that work has started.

The $1,918,000 committed against $2,600,000 authorized leaves $682,000 of headroom against approximately seven months of remaining transition activity. That is not adequate. My working projection is that transition costs will finish at $3,100,000 to $3,400,000, which implies an overrun of $500,000 to $800,000 on top of the capital variance.

The obstetric hospitalist resignations in February compound this. Two of four hospitalists gave notice. Replacement recruitment in this market carries a placement fee of roughly twenty-eight percent of first-year compensation, and interim locum coverage runs between $2,400 and $2,900 per twenty-four-hour shift. If we bridge two positions with locums from May through December, that is between $580,000 and $700,000 that is not in any budget I have presented, because it sits in operating expense rather than transition and will hit the medical group's bottom line.

Options

I have developed four approaches and I do not consider any of them clean.

Value engineering the construction package. Casella has identified approximately $640,000 in reductions, principally by deferring the second operating room shell-out, substituting finishes, and reducing the scope of the family waiting area renovation. Deferring the second operating room is the largest single item at $390,000 and I would want Dr. Ramaswamy's judgment on whether a twenty-bed unit at 2,100 deliveries can operate on one dedicated obstetric operating room. My non-clinical read is that it cannot, and that we would be spending $390,000 to create a problem we pay for later. The remaining $250,000 in finish and waiting area reductions I would take without hesitation.

Phasing. We could build sixteen beds by October and the remaining four by the following spring, deferring roughly $1,100,000 into fiscal 2029. This preserves the October 1 date and the full mechanical scope. The risk is that sixteen beds at 2,100 annual deliveries produces an occupancy rate around eighty-four percent at average census, which means we will be diverting or holding patients in triage during peaks. Marisol's staff have modeled this and their view is that sixteen is workable but uncomfortable and that the discomfort falls on the nursing staff we are simultaneously asking to absorb a merger.

Drawing on the capital reserve. The system's unrestricted capital reserve stands at $14,200,000. Drawing $2,900,000 leaves $11,300,000, which remains above the covenant floor of $9,000,000 in our master trust indenture but reduces our cushion at a moment when days cash on hand has slipped from 118 to 104 over four quarters. Our bank covenant compliance certificate is due June 30 and I do not want to file it having just spent three million dollars of cushion.

Deferring other capital. The fiscal 2027 capital plan includes $3,400,000 in projects that have not been committed: the Lowell endoscopy suite refresh at $1,600,000, the Haverhill parking structure repairs at $1,100,000, and a laboratory information system replacement at $700,000. The parking repairs are a deferred maintenance item that our engineering firm has already extended once and I am reluctant to extend again. The endoscopy refresh has a revenue argument behind it. The laboratory system is at end of vendor support in March 2029.

Recommendation

I recommend a combination: accept $250,000 in value engineering excluding the second operating room, phase four beds into fiscal 2029 for $1,100,000 of deferral, defer the endoscopy refresh for $1,600,000, and draw the residual from the capital reserve. This closes the capital gap without touching the parking structure and without a reserve draw large enough to trouble the covenant certificate.

I recommend separately that the Board authorize an increase in the transition contingency from $2,600,000 to $3,400,000, funded from operating cash, and that the medical group's locum exposure be recognized explicitly in the fiscal 2027 forecast rather than absorbed. My experience with projects of this kind is that costs which are not named migrate into the operating budget and then get attributed to the operating budget's own performance, and I would rather we see this one clearly.

One further observation, offered as a finance matter rather than a clinical one. The consolidation's business case rests on eliminating $4,300,000 in annual operating loss. Against that we are now looking at roughly $2,900,000 in incremental capital, $800,000 in incremental transition cost, and $650,000 in unbudgeted locum coverage, for approximately $4,350,000 in one-time and near-term costs above plan. The project still returns, but the payback period moves from under twelve months to approximately twenty-four months on a cash basis. I do not think that changes the decision. I do think it changes how much additional cost the project can absorb before it stops making sense, and I would want the Board to hear that stated rather than inferred.

**TO:** Nursing Leadership — Directors, Managers, Assistant Nurse Managers, Both Campuses
**FROM:** Marisol Vega-Ruiz, RN, MSN, Chief Nursing Officer
**DATE:** March 2, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Redeployment of Affected Staff and the March 15 Bidding Process

---

This memo covers how we will handle the sixty-two full-time equivalent positions affected by the obstetric consolidation, and specifically how the March 15 bidding process will run. I need every manager receiving this to read it completely, because you will be asked questions you cannot answer from the announcement alone, and I would rather you have the detail than improvise.

Who Is Affected

The sixty-two FTEs break down as follows. Forty-one are registered nurses: twenty-six in labor and delivery, eleven in postpartum and the newborn nursery, and four in the Haverhill obstetric triage area. Nine are certified nursing assistants and obstetric technicians. Six are unit secretaries and patient access staff assigned to the unit. Four are lactation consultants and childbirth educators, of whom two hold RN licensure. Two are the unit's clinical educator and a quality coordinator.

Because these are FTEs, the headcount is higher. Seventy-eight individuals hold positions within the affected cost centers, including part-time and per diem staff. Per diem staff do not hold bid rights under Article 14 of the collective bargaining agreement but they are people who have worked here for years and several of them for decades, and I expect them to be treated as such in every conversation you have. I will address per diem placement separately below.

Governing Provisions

The Massachusetts Nurses Association agreement, Article 14, Sections 3 through 9, governs unit closure and reduction in force. The provisions that matter most are these.

Affected nurses have bid rights to any posted vacancy in the bargaining unit for which they meet the stated qualifications, exercised in order of system seniority. Seniority is system-wide, not campus-specific, which means a Haverhill nurse with nineteen years may displace a Lowell posting ahead of a Lowell nurse with six who is bidding for a transfer. This is the provision most likely to generate friction and I want you to understand it before someone challenges you on it in a hallway.

Nurses who accept a position in a different specialty are entitled to a full orientation period appropriate to that specialty, not an abbreviated one, and to wage protection at their current rate for twelve months. Wage protection applies even where the new position carries a lower differential structure. It does not preserve shift differentials that are not worked.

Nurses who accept no position and are laid off retain recall rights for eighteen months and must be offered any vacancy for which they qualify during that period before it is posted externally.

The employer must provide the union with a list of affected positions and all anticipated vacancies at least fourteen days before the bidding process opens. That list went to MNA leadership on February 27, which sets March 13 as the earliest permissible opening. We have set March 15 to build in margin.

Available Positions

We have identified ninety-four posted or anticipated vacancies across the system that affected staff may bid into. This number is larger than sixty-two and I want to be careful about the reassurance that invites, because the vacancies are not distributed the way the affected staff are.

The consolidated Lowell obstetric unit will carry thirty-eight budgeted RN FTEs, of which twenty-nine are currently filled. That leaves nine RN openings on the receiving unit, plus four CNA and two unit secretary positions. These are the natural landing spots and they will be the most heavily bid, and there are far fewer of them than there are Haverhill obstetric nurses.

The Lowell campus overall carries a twenty-three percent RN vacancy rate. In absolute terms that is fifty-one open RN FTEs across medical-surgical, telemetry, the emergency department, critical care, and perioperative services. That is where the volume of available work sits. It is also work that most of the affected staff have not done in years or, in some cases, ever. A labor and delivery nurse with fifteen years of experience is a highly skilled clinician who is not, on day one, a competent telemetry nurse, and she knows it better than anyone. I want managers receiving these transfers to understand that they are receiving experienced professionals who will feel like novices, and that the gap between those two things is where people decide whether to stay.

Haverhill retains eleven RN vacancies across medical-surgical, the emergency department, and same-day surgery. For staff who live in Haverhill, Bradford, Groveland, or Methuen, these are the positions that preserve their commute, and I expect them to be bid heavily by senior staff.

The remaining vacancies are ambulatory, in the physician practices, and in care management. Several affected staff have already asked about these and they are legitimate destinations, though the compensation structure differs and I want that disclosed before someone bids, not after.

The March 15 Process

The bidding window opens March 15 at 8:00 a.m. and closes March 29 at 5:00 p.m. Bids are submitted through the system's electronic posting platform. Paper bids will be accepted at the human resources office at either campus for any employee who requests that accommodation and I do not want anyone turned away from a paper form.

Each affected employee may submit up to five bids, ranked. Awards will be made in system seniority order, working down the seniority list and assigning each employee the highest-ranked bid still available when their name is reached. Employees will be notified of awards by April 10 and must accept or decline in writing within five business days.

An employee who declines an awarded bid returns to the pool for a second round, which opens April 20, but forfeits seniority priority in that round. This provision is in the agreement and I raise it because it is easy to misunderstand. An employee who bids casually on something they do not want and then declines it has damaged their own position, and it is the responsibility of every manager reading this to make sure the people you supervise understand that before March 29.

Before the window opens I am requiring three things of you.

Every affected employee will have a one-on-one conversation with their direct manager between March 4 and March 13. Not a group meeting, not an email, a conversation. The purpose is to review their seniority date, their qualifications, the positions realistically available to them, and their own preferences. I have prepared a discussion guide that will reach you March 3 and I am asking you to document the date of each conversation, not its content.

Every affected employee will be offered a session with human resources on benefits, wage protection, and the mechanics of the platform. Sessions are scheduled March 5, 9, and 11 at Haverhill and March 6 and 10 at Lowell, with two evening sessions and one at 6:30 a.m. for night staff. Night staff have historically been underserved by these processes and I have asked HR to track attendance by shift and report to me weekly.

Every receiving manager with a posted vacancy will complete a written statement of the orientation plan for that position by March 12. I want to know how long, who precedes, what competencies, and what the checkpoints are. These will be attached to the postings so that a nurse bidding into telemetry can see what the first ninety days look like rather than guessing.

Per Diem and Non-Bargaining Staff

The twenty-two individuals in per diem or non-bargaining status do not have contractual bid rights. We will nonetheless run a parallel process for them, opening March 15 on the same timeline, and I have secured agreement that per diem staff will be considered for open positions before external candidates during the March 15 to April 30 window. This is a commitment I made and intend to hold. Please communicate it as a commitment and not as a guarantee of placement, because those are different things and the difference will matter in June.

What I Expect From You

There is a version of this process that is technically correct and humanly terrible. It runs on time, it complies with every provision of Article 14, and eighteen months from now we have lost thirty experienced nurses to Lawrence General and Holy Family and we cannot understand why. I have seen that version. I do not want it here.

The Haverhill obstetric unit has an average tenure of 11.4 years. Nine nurses on that unit have more than twenty years of service. These are people who have delivered the children of people they delivered. What we are asking them to do is not a job change; it is the end of something they built, and the fact that it is financially necessary does not make it feel like anything other than a loss. You do not need to argue them out of that feeling. You need to be accurate, available, and unhurried, and you need to say "I don't know, I will find out" when that is the true answer instead of filling the silence.

I will be at Haverhill every Tuesday and Thursday through the end of April. My door is open and so is my calendar. If a member of your staff wants to talk to me directly, put them on it and do not route them back through you first.

**TO:** Executive Team
**FROM:** Devon Okonkwo, Vice President of Operations
**DATE:** April 16, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Post-Hearing Status — Obstetric Consolidation

---

This is the weekly status memo, expanded to cover the April 9 public hearing and its consequences. I have organized it around what changed, what is at risk, and what I need decided.

The Hearing

The Department of Public Health hearing convened at 6:00 p.m. at the Haverhill Citizens Center and adjourned at 9:40 p.m. Attendance was approximately 310, which exceeded both our estimate and the room's comfortable capacity; roughly forty people stood along the back wall and the fire marshal's office asked us to stop admitting at 9:15. Fifty-one people signed up to testify and forty-four spoke. The hearing officer kept a firm three-minute limit.

Of the forty-four speakers, thirty-eight opposed the consolidation, three spoke in support, and three testified without taking a position. The opposition testimony clustered around four themes and I want to describe them accurately because the accuracy matters more than the tone.

First, distance and transportation. Eleven speakers addressed this, including four who described not owning a vehicle and two who described relying on the MVRTA, which does not provide a direct route from central Haverhill to the Lowell campus. One speaker had timed the trip by public transit at two hours and ten minutes each way with two transfers. Our essential services plan proposes a scheduled shuttle, and several speakers noted that a scheduled shuttle is of limited use to someone in labor. That is a fair criticism and our plan does not currently answer it.

Second, precipitous delivery. Nine speakers, including three physicians and two Haverhill firefighters. The firefighters' testimony was the most substantive of the evening. They described current transport times from the Bradford and Ayers Village areas to Haverhill of eight to eleven minutes and to Lowell of twenty-six to thirty-four minutes depending on Route 495 conditions, and stated that Haverhill Fire has had four field deliveries in the past three years, all of which were transported to the Haverhill campus. They did not oppose the consolidation as such. They asked what the plan is when a delivery happens in the back of an ambulance on 495 and requested that the system fund additional obstetric emergency training and equipment for the department. That request was made publicly, on the record, and I do not think we can decline it.

Third, equity. Seven speakers, including representatives from two community organizations, characterized the consolidation as a withdrawal of services from a lower-income and more heavily immigrant community in favor of a campus in a different city. The Haverhill obstetric patient population is fifty-eight percent Medicaid against thirty-four percent at Lowell, and that figure was cited from the podium. It is our figure and it is accurate.

Fourth, process. Six speakers, including two members of the medical staff executive committee, objected that the decision was made before consultation rather than through it. State Representative Ferreira's staff read a letter making the same point and requesting that the Department require a delay.

I would note for the record that our own communications were cited against us twice. Dr. Ramaswamy's January 26 memo stated that building Haverhill emergency department obstetric protocols is "a condition of the consolidation, not a follow-on to it." A speaker read that sentence aloud and asked what the protocols currently say. The honest answer is that they are in draft. That exchange was reported in the Eagle-Tribune the following morning and it is the single most damaging thirty seconds of the evening.

The hearing officer's summary will issue within thirty days. Outside counsel's read is that the Department is unlikely to block the consolidation, which it lacks clear authority to do, but is likely to issue findings requiring specific amendments to the essential services plan, and that our timeline should assume amendment rather than approval as filed.

Timeline Status

The October 1 date is at risk. I am not yet recommending that we move it, but I want the exposure stated.

Construction. The revised package, following the Finance Committee's March 12 approval of Aaron's phasing recommendation, went to Casella on March 19. Notice to proceed issued April 1. The schedule shows substantial completion September 12 for sixteen beds, which provides nineteen days of float before October 1. Nineteen days is thin for a project of this type. The medical gas riser work is the critical path item and it requires four separate shutdown windows on the third floor east wing, each coordinated against active inpatient units. We have secured two windows. The remaining two are contested with the surgical schedule and I need a decision on priority.

Staffing. The March 15 bidding process closed March 29 as scheduled. Fifty-eight of sixty-two affected FTEs submitted bids. Awards issued April 10. Of the fifty-eight, forty-one accepted an awarded position, nine declined and entered the second round which opens April 20, and eight received no award because their bids were exhausted by more senior claims. Four employees did not bid at all, and three of those four have since resigned. Marisol's assessment is that the outcome is roughly at the midpoint of what was modeled, which is to say worse than hoped and better than feared.

The more serious staffing exposure remains the obstetric hospitalists. Recruitment has produced two candidates, one of whom withdrew April 6 after a site visit and one of whom is in negotiation with an expected start no earlier than August. Locum coverage is contracted through July 31 and we do not yet have coverage for August or September. Aaron's projection of $580,000 to $700,000 in locum expense now looks low; my working number is $760,000.

Lowell nursing vacancy has moved from twenty-three percent to twenty-one percent, driven by the bid awards. This is real improvement and it is the clearest positive item in this memo.

Budget. Capital is tracking to the revised $7,772,000 authorization with $312,000 of contingency consumed against $580,000 available, at eleven percent schedule completion. That consumption rate concerns me. Transition costs stand at $2,340,000 against the increased $3,400,000 authorization.

Emergency department protocols. This is the item I am most concerned about and it is the item the hearing exposed. The Haverhill ED obstetric stabilization protocol has been in draft since February 12. It has been through three revisions. The current draft is held on the question of whether a delivering patient at Haverhill is managed by the ED attending with telephone support from Lowell obstetrics, or whether an obstetric provider is dispatched from Lowell, or whether we contract standby coverage locally. Each option has a cost and a clinical objection and the decision has been deferred at two consecutive project meetings. It cannot be deferred again. The protocol requires approval, equipment procurement, simulation training for approximately ninety ED staff across three shifts, and competency validation. Working backward from October 1, the decision must be made by May 1 or the training will not complete.

Decisions Required

I am asking the executive team to resolve five items by April 30.

One. The Haverhill ED obstetric coverage model. My recommendation is contracted local standby coverage supplemented by telephone support, which is the most expensive of the three options at approximately $340,000 annually and the only one I can defend at a public hearing.

Two. Medical gas shutdown window priority. My recommendation is that obstetric construction takes precedence over elective surgical volume for the two contested windows, with the understanding that this displaces approximately fourteen elective cases.

Three. The Haverhill Fire Department training and equipment request. My recommendation is that we fund it, in the range of $60,000 to $85,000, and announce it before the hearing officer's summary issues rather than after.

Four. Transportation. The scheduled shuttle does not answer the labor transport question. My recommendation is a contracted non-emergency medical transportation arrangement with on-demand availability for obstetric patients in the Haverhill service area, which Latasha's office estimates at $190,000 annually.

Five. A formal go/no-go review on the October 1 date, scheduled for June 15, using medical gas completion, ED protocol training completion, and hospitalist coverage for September as the three gates. I would rather schedule the decision now than make it in September under pressure.

**TO:** Medical Staff Executive Committee
**FROM:** Latasha Greer, Director of Community Benefits
**DATE:** April 24, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Response to the Committee's Written Objection of February 18

---

The committee's objection, filed following the 11-4 vote of February 17, raised four concerns: the adequacy of transfer protocols for precipitous deliveries, the sufficiency of the October 1 timeline, the impact on the Haverhill service area's Medicaid population, and the adequacy of consultation preceding the decision. Dr. Ramaswamy has asked that I respond on the second, third, and fourth, and that the first be answered jointly by her office and mine. This memo does that. I have deliberately not softened the places where the committee was right.

On Transfer Protocols

The committee wrote that the consolidation proceeds "without an approved, drilled, and equipped protocol for the patient who presents to the Haverhill emergency department in advanced labor, and that this omission is not a detail but the central clinical risk of the plan."

That statement was accurate when written and it remained accurate on April 9, when it was effectively repeated from the podium at the public hearing by a Haverhill firefighter. The protocol has been in draft since February 12 and has not been approved because the executive team has not resolved the coverage model underlying it.

As of April 22 that decision has been made. The Haverhill emergency department will be supported by contracted obstetric standby coverage: a credentialed obstetric provider available on-site within thirty minutes at all hours, contracted through the Merrimack Valley OB/GYN group, supplemented by continuous telephone consultation with the Lowell labor floor and a dedicated video link in the Haverhill ED resuscitation bays. Funding of approximately $340,000 annually has been approved. This was the most expensive of the three options considered and it was selected because it is the only one that answers the question the committee asked.

The implementation sequence is as follows. Protocol approval through the medical staff quality committee and the emergency department is targeted for May 8. Equipment procurement, which includes two obstetric emergency carts, a warmer and resuscitation station in each of two resuscitation bays, and point-of-care hemoglobin testing, runs May through June. Simulation training for approximately ninety emergency department staff across three shifts runs July and August, delivered in eight cohorts, with competency validation for every participant. A full multidisciplinary drill involving Haverhill Fire and Trinity EMS is scheduled for September 8.

The committee asked that consolidation be contingent on protocol readiness rather than concurrent with it. The executive team has adopted that principle. A formal go/no-go review is scheduled for June 15, and emergency department protocol training completion is one of three explicit gates. If that gate is not met, the October 1 date moves. I am authorized to state that without qualification.

I would ask the committee to note that this outcome followed from the committee's objection. The written filing, and the hearing testimony that echoed it, are the reasons a $340,000 recurring commitment was approved over two cheaper alternatives. The process the committee criticized worked in this instance because the committee used it.

On the Timeline

The committee objected that October 1 was selected before the operational requirements of the transition were understood, and that the date drove the plan rather than the plan producing the date.

This objection is substantially correct and I am not going to argue it. October 1 was selected in November 2026 because it aligned with the start of fiscal 2028 and because 120 days of regulatory notice from a January filing produced a May eligibility date with margin. It was a calendar decision. It was not derived from a construction schedule, which did not exist, or from a training plan, which did not exist, or from an assessment of obstetric hospitalist stability, which was not conducted.

Several consequences have followed. The construction estimate that supported the date was $2.9 million low. Two of four obstetric hospitalists resigned in February, and replacement recruitment will not produce coverage before August at the earliest. The emergency department protocol has consumed ten weeks in draft. The current construction schedule shows nineteen days of float before October 1, which is thin.

What I can tell the committee is that the date is now under formal review rather than being defended. The June 15 go/no-go uses three gates: medical gas infrastructure completion, emergency department protocol training completion, and confirmed obstetric hospitalist coverage for September. Failure of any gate moves the date. I would also tell the committee, candidly, that a portion of the executive team believes the date will hold and a portion does not, and that the review exists because that disagreement was not resolvable by argument in April.

On the Medicaid Population

The committee raised the disproportionate impact on Medicaid-insured and immigrant patients in the Haverhill service area. This is the area of my direct responsibility and I will give the committee the full picture, including the parts that are unfavorable to the institution.

The figures are these. Fifty-eight percent of Haverhill obstetric patients are Medicaid-insured, against thirty-four percent at Lowell. Twenty-two percent of Haverhill obstetric patients received care in a language other than English in fiscal 2026, predominantly Spanish, with Portuguese, Khmer, and Arabic following. In our 2025 community health needs assessment, transportation was identified as a barrier to care by thirty-one percent of respondents in the Haverhill service area, the highest of any barrier measured. These populations will bear the largest share of the burden of a fourteen-mile increase in travel distance. That is a factual statement about who is affected and it is not answered by pointing out that the Haverhill unit lost $4.3 million.

The mitigations now committed are the following.

Transportation. The scheduled shuttle in the original essential services plan was correctly criticized at the hearing as useless to a patient in labor. It is being replaced with a contracted on-demand non-emergency medical transportation arrangement available around the clock to obstetric patients in the Haverhill service area, at an estimated $190,000 annually, covering prenatal visits, scheduled deliveries, postpartum visits, and non-emergent presentations in early labor. Emergent transport remains 911. This has been approved.

Prenatal capacity at Haverhill. All outpatient obstetric services remain at Haverhill and we are expanding rather than holding them. The midwifery practice adds one full-time midwife and eight additional hours of clinic time weekly beginning July 1. Ultrasound capacity increases by twelve hours weekly. The intent is that the number of trips to Lowell over the course of a pregnancy falls even as the distance for the delivery trip rises, and for an uncomplicated pregnancy the net change should be one to two additional Lowell trips rather than a wholesale relocation of care.

Language access. Two additional Spanish-language medical interpreters and one Portuguese interpreter will be added to the Lowell campus by September 1, and video remote interpretation will be installed in every Lowell obstetric room rather than on shared mobile units. The committee should know that our current Lowell interpreter coverage is thinner than Haverhill's on a per-patient basis and that this addition corrects an existing gap rather than creating a new capability.

Postpartum follow-up. Home visiting for Medicaid-insured postpartum patients in the Haverhill service area expands from the current thirty percent of eligible patients to a target of eighty percent by the end of fiscal 2028, adding two full-time community health nurses. This is the mitigation I would defend most strongly on clinical grounds, because it addresses the period where distance most plausibly translates into worse outcomes.

Measurement. Beginning October 1 we will report quarterly to this committee on prenatal visit adequacy by insurance status and language, postpartum follow-up completion, unplanned out-of-hospital deliveries in the Haverhill service area, emergency department obstetric presentations at Haverhill, and transportation utilization and denials. The baseline is being established now, in the two quarters before the change, so that the comparison is honest.

On Consultation

The committee objected that it was informed of the decision rather than consulted before it. The chronology supports the objection. The Board approved the consolidation on January 21. Dr. Ramaswamy's announcement issued January 26. The committee had received a financial briefing on the Haverhill obstetric service in October 2026 that did not present consolidation as a decision under active consideration, and no formal request for the committee's recommendation was made at any point.

I have no counterargument. What I can describe is what has changed since. The committee's February objection produced the $340,000 standby coverage commitment, the go/no-go review structure, and the replacement of the shuttle with on-demand transport. The essential services plan amendments now in preparation will be circulated to this committee in draft with a fourteen-day comment period before filing, and comments will be answered individually in writing rather than summarized. Two committee members have been added to the transition steering group with full access to the project record.

Beyond this project, Dr. Ramaswamy has proposed to the Board an amendment to the medical staff bylaws requiring formal medical staff executive committee consultation, with a written recommendation, before any Board vote on the discontinuation or relocation of a clinical service. That proposal goes to the Board's governance committee in June. It is a direct response to this objection and I would encourage the committee to comment on the draft language, because the value of the amendment will depend on what triggers it.

Closing

The committee voted 11 to 4 against the timeline. That vote was, in my reading, a judgment that the institution was moving faster than its own preparation, and the record of February through April supports that judgment more than it refutes it. A construction estimate was off by fifty-three percent. Two hospitalists resigned. A protocol identified in January as a precondition sat in draft for ten weeks and was read back to us at a public hearing. These are not the marks of a plan that was ready.

What I would ask the committee to weigh is whether the corrective structure now in place is adequate: a dated gate review with defined criteria, a funded coverage model, a measurement commitment with a pre-change baseline, and a bylaws amendment that changes how the next decision gets made. If the committee's view is that it is not adequate, I would rather receive that in writing before June 15 than hear it afterward, and I will ensure that any such filing reaches the Board with the executive team's response attached rather than in place of it.
