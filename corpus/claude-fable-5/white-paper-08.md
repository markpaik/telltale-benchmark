## Abstract

Between 2021 and 2025, the fourteen emergency departments of Cedarbrook Health Partners recorded 11,900 pediatric behavioral health emergency visits across a catchment of 1.4 million people in southern Minnesota and western Wisconsin. Among children who boarded — defined here as remaining in an emergency department more than twelve hours after the disposition decision — the median boarding stay was 31 hours, the ninetieth percentile was 106 hours, and 18 percent waited more than three days. Restraint or seclusion occurred in 9.4 percent of boarded stays. Boarding consumed an average of 4.2 treatment bays per day systemwide and $1.1 million in sitter labor in 2025 alone. The region's inpatient capacity — 38 child psychiatric beds at two facilities — is structurally insufficient, and those facilities refused 44 percent of transfer requests in 2025.

This paper presents the system's five-year dataset, evaluates five intervention models — a dedicated pediatric psychiatric emergency unit, telepsychiatry consultation into rural sites, mobile crisis teams with short-stay stabilization beds, an in-department pediatric observation protocol, and bridge clinics with 72-hour follow-up — and builds a financial model for a blended investment anchored by a twelve-bay psychiatric emergency unit ($6.8 million capital, $4.2 million annual operating cost). We conclude that no single intervention resolves the problem, but a sequenced portfolio, financed in part through a state grant round closing in September 2026, can reduce boarding hours by an estimated 55 to 65 percent within three years while lowering total system cost per crisis episode. We address the principal objections — rural travel distance, psychiatrist recruitment, Medicaid reimbursement adequacy, and skepticism that upstream investment moves emergency volume — directly and with the system's own data.

## 1. The Problem: Children in Crisis, Held in Place

A child who arrives at an emergency department in psychiatric crisis receives, in most American hospitals, a medical screening, a safety assessment, and a disposition decision — and then, too often, nothing. The emergency department is built to stabilize and move patients. When the destination does not exist, the child stays. The clinical term is boarding. The lived experience is a child in a windowless bay, under continuous observation by a sitter, without therapy, without schooling, without ordinary daylight, for days.

Pediatric behavioral health boarding is a national phenomenon, documented in joint statements by the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American College of Emergency Physicians, and the Emergency Nurses Association, and worsened sharply after 2020. But national framing can obscure how the problem actually presents inside a regional system: as an operational, clinical, and financial burden that falls simultaneously on rural critical-access hospitals, on a hub emergency department, on families driving long distances, and on county human services agencies with nowhere to place a child.

Cedarbrook Health Partners operates nine hospitals and fourteen emergency departments across southern Minnesota and western Wisconsin. Our catchment includes small cities, farm communities, and tribal lands; several of our emergency departments are more than ninety minutes from the nearest inpatient child psychiatric bed. The region holds **38 inpatient child psychiatric beds for 1.4 million people** — roughly 2.7 beds per 100,000 population, well below commonly cited adequacy benchmarks. Those beds sit in two facilities, and in 2025 those facilities **refused 44 percent of our transfer requests**, most often citing acuity mismatch, staffing, or the child's behavioral profile (aggression, autism, or complex medical comorbidity).

The consequence is predictable. The child stays with us. This paper is about what that costs — to children, to staff, to the system, and to public payers — and what a regional system can do about it with realistic capital, realistic staffing, and a state grant window that closes in September 2026.

Three points frame everything that follows:

1. **Boarding is not a treatment.** A boarded child's condition typically does not improve in the emergency department; agitation, restraint events, and repeat crises are more likely the longer the stay.
2. **Boarding is not free.** Sitter labor, occupied bays, deferred medical volume, and staff turnover carry a quantifiable cost that rivals the operating cost of the interventions that would prevent it.
3. **Boarding is not inevitable.** Systems that have built dedicated psychiatric emergency capacity, crisis stabilization beds, and rapid-access follow-up have demonstrably reduced boarding hours and inpatient admission rates. The question for our region is not whether the models work, but which combination fits our geography and payer mix.

## 2. Five Years of System Data, 2021–2025

Cedarbrook Health Institute maintains a registry of every pediatric (age 5–17) emergency visit carrying a primary behavioral health diagnosis or chief complaint across all fourteen emergency departments. The registry captures arrival and disposition timestamps, boarding duration, restraint and seclusion events, sitter hours, transfer requests and outcomes, and payer.

### 2.1 Volume and trajectory

From 2021 through 2025 the system recorded **11,900 pediatric behavioral health emergency visits**. Volume rose through the period, with the steepest growth in suicidality-related presentations among adolescents aged 12–15 and in presentations by children with autism spectrum disorder in behavioral crisis — the two groups most likely to be refused transfer.

**Table 1. Pediatric behavioral health ED visits and boarding, Cedarbrook system, 2021–2025**

| Measure | 2021 | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 | 5-yr total/avg |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pediatric BH ED visits | 2,050 | 2,210 | 2,390 | 2,560 | 2,690 | 11,900 |
| Visits resulting in boarding (>12 hrs post-disposition) | 490 | 560 | 640 | 720 | 780 | 3,190 |
| Boarding rate | 23.9% | 25.3% | 26.8% | 28.1% | 29.0% | 26.8% |
| Median boarding duration (hrs) | 26 | 28 | 30 | 33 | 35 | 31 |
| 90th percentile duration (hrs) | 88 | 94 | 102 | 112 | 121 | 106 |
| Boarded stays >72 hrs | 14% | 16% | 18% | 19% | 21% | 18% |
| Transfer requests refused | 34% | 38% | 41% | 43% | 44% | 40% |

*Annual figures are registry-derived; five-year summary statistics (median 31 hours, 90th percentile 106 hours, 18 percent exceeding 72 hours) are computed across the pooled cohort.*

Every trend line moves in the wrong direction. Visits are up roughly 31 percent over five years; the boarding rate has climbed from about one in four visits to nearly three in ten; median duration has lengthened; and the tail has lengthened faster than the median — the ninetieth percentile grew from 88 to 121 hours, meaning the hardest-to-place children are waiting far longer than they did in 2021.

### 2.2 What happens during boarding

Boarding is clinically consequential, not merely inconvenient.

- **Restraint and seclusion occurred in 9.4 percent of boarded stays** over the five-year period. These events cluster in stays exceeding 48 hours and among children with autism or aggression histories — precisely the children most likely to be refused a bed. Each restraint event carries injury risk to child and staff, trauma to both, regulatory reporting burden, and, for many families, a durable rupture of trust in the health system.
- **Therapeutic activity is minimal.** A boarded child in our system typically receives daily safety checks, medication continuation, and social work contact attempting placement. Structured psychotherapy, family therapy, and psychiatric treatment adjustment occur inconsistently, dependent on consult availability at each site.
- **Deterioration is common in long stays.** Chart review of stays exceeding 72 hours shows escalating agitation orders, increased PRN medication use, and rising sitter acuity classifications as stays lengthen.

### 2.3 Operational and financial burden

- **Sitter labor cost $1.1 million in 2025.** Nearly every boarded child requires continuous 1:1 or line-of-sight observation. This labor is largely unreimbursed and pulled from nursing assistant and float pools, degrading staffing elsewhere.
- **Boarding occupies an average of 4.2 treatment bays per day systemwide.** At our hub emergency department, boarded psychiatric patients regularly hold monitored bays during peak medical demand, extending waiting-room times for all patients and contributing to ambulance diversion hours.
- **Opportunity cost of occupied bays.** At conservative assumptions (4.2 bays × 365 days, average net revenue per bay-day for medical throughput), the system forgoes an estimated $3.4–4.6 million annually in medical emergency capacity — detailed in Section 5.
- **Staff attrition.** Exit interviews from our emergency nursing workforce cite prolonged psychiatric boarding, and the moral distress and assault risk that accompany it, among the top three reasons for departure.

### 2.4 Geography and payer

Roughly 60 percent of boarded stays originate at our hub and two regional hospitals; the remaining 40 percent begin at rural sites, where the absence of on-site psychiatry means every disposition decision waits on a consult and every admission requires transfer. Medicaid is the payer for approximately 55 percent of boarded stays — a figure that matters both because Medicaid crisis-service rates shape the financial viability of every intervention in Section 3, and because the state bears much of the cost of the status quo through repeat visits, county placements, and juvenile justice involvement.

### 2.5 The transfer refusal problem

The 44 percent refusal rate in 2025 deserves emphasis because it reframes the problem. Even if regional inpatient beds were perfectly efficient, 38 beds cannot serve 1.4 million people. But refusals mean the effective capacity is smaller still, and it is smallest for exactly the children who board longest. Any strategy that consists solely of "find more inpatient beds elsewhere" fails on arithmetic. The system must build capacity to **treat crisis without admission** wherever clinically appropriate, and to **hold and treat** — not merely hold — those awaiting admission.

## 3. Five Intervention Models: Reach, Cost, and Fit

We evaluated five models against four criteria: expected reduction in boarding hours, geographic reach across a dispersed fourteen-site system, cost (capital and operating), and workforce feasibility.

### 3.1 Dedicated pediatric psychiatric emergency unit (PEU)

A twelve-bay unit adjacent to the hub emergency department, purpose-built for pediatric behavioral crisis: calming environment, milieu staffing, on-site child psychiatry and psychiatric nursing, capacity to observe and treat up to 72 hours. Modeled on the EmPATH (Emergency Psychiatric Assessment, Treatment and Healing) design, adapted for pediatrics.

- **Evidence base:** EmPATH-style units in published evaluations have reduced psychiatric boarding times by 50 percent or more and reduced inpatient admissions by 25–40 percent, because active treatment during observation resolves many crises that would otherwise default to admission requests.
- **Cost:** $6.8 million capital; $4.2 million annual operating.
- **Reach:** Direct reach is the hub and, via transfer, the entire system — but only if rural transport is solved (Section 6.1).
- **Fit:** This is the anchor intervention. It converts our largest concentration of boarding hours into treatment hours and relieves the transfer-refusal bottleneck by resolving crises that never needed an inpatient bed.

### 3.2 Telepsychiatry consultation into rural sites

Contracted or employed child psychiatrists and psychiatric APRNs providing on-demand video consultation to all fourteen emergency departments, with standardized assessment protocols.

- **Evidence base:** Tele-consultation reliably shortens time-to-disposition and reduces unnecessary transfers; studies in rural networks show 20–35 percent of planned transfers averted when a specialist assessment is available at the originating site.
- **Cost:** Approximately $850,000 annually (coverage contracts, coordination staff, equipment refresh); minimal capital (~$300,000).
- **Reach:** The broadest of any model — every site, immediately.
- **Fit:** Highest value per dollar for the rural 40 percent of boarding. It does not create capacity, but it prevents boarding that begins as a diagnostic delay and prevents transfers that were never necessary.

### 3.3 Mobile crisis teams with short-stay stabilization beds

Regional mobile teams (clinician plus peer/family specialist) responding to homes, schools, and rural emergency departments, paired with 8–12 short-stay crisis stabilization beds (up to 5 days) in two sub-regional locations.

- **Evidence base:** Mobile crisis response resolves a majority of encounters in place; stabilization beds serve children who need more than an ED visit and less than hospitalization. Minnesota's children's mobile crisis framework and federal Medicaid mobile crisis options (American Rescue Plan Act, Section 9813) provide an enhanced-match financing pathway.
- **Cost:** Approximately $2.6 million annually for two teams plus stabilization beds; capital ~$1.9 million for bed conversion in existing space.
- **Reach:** Sub-regional; strongest upstream effect — it prevents ED arrivals, not just boarding.
- **Fit:** The only model that reduces demand at the front door. Slower to stand up; dependent on county partnerships and Medicaid crisis rates.

### 3.4 Pediatric observation protocol inside the emergency department

A standardized in-ED pathway: dedicated behavioral health observation status, scheduled re-assessment, structured therapeutic contact, environmental modifications to existing bays, and criteria-driven discharge planning — implemented at all sites now, without new construction.

- **Cost:** Approximately $600,000 annually (protocol staffing, training, environmental modifications ~$400,000 one-time).
- **Reach:** All fourteen sites.
- **Fit:** This is the immediate mitigation. It does not eliminate boarding, but it converts custodial holding into rudimentary treatment, reduces restraint triggers, and shortens stays at the margin. It should be treated as a bridge measure and a floor of care, not a destination.

### 3.5 Bridge clinics with follow-up within 72 hours

Rapid-access outpatient clinics — psychiatry, therapy, care coordination — guaranteeing an appointment within 72 hours of ED or PEU discharge, holding the child until community services engage.

- **Evidence base:** Rapid post-crisis follow-up reduces 30-day ED return rates substantially; it is also the intervention that makes ED and PEU clinicians comfortable discharging rather than admitting, because "discharge to nothing" is a principal driver of defensive admission requests.
- **Cost:** Approximately $1.4 million annually (two clinic sites plus telehealth follow-up for rural families); capital minimal.
- **Fit:** The multiplier. Bridge clinics do not reduce today's boarding directly, but they raise the safe-discharge rate of every other intervention and cut repeat volume.

### 3.6 Comparison

**Table 2. Intervention comparison**

| Model | Capital | Annual operating | Est. boarding-hour reduction (system) | Reach | Time to impact |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Psychiatric emergency unit (12 bays) | $6.8M | $4.2M | 35–45% | Hub + transfers | 18–24 months |
| Telepsychiatry to rural sites | $0.3M | $0.85M | 8–12% | All 14 sites | 6–9 months |
| Mobile crisis + stabilization beds | $1.9M | $2.6M | 10–15% (via demand reduction) | Sub-regional | 12–18 months |
| In-ED observation protocol | $0.4M | $0.6M | 5–8% (duration/harm reduction) | All 14 sites | 3–6 months |
| Bridge clinics (72-hr follow-up) | $0.2M | $1.4M | 5–10% (via reduced returns and admissions) | Hub regions + telehealth | 9–12 months |

*Reduction estimates are Institute projections applying published effect sizes to Cedarbrook registry volumes; ranges are deliberately conservative. Effects are partially additive; the portfolio estimate in Section 4 accounts for overlap.*

The comparison yields a clear conclusion: **no single model suffices.** The PEU delivers the largest single reduction but cannot reach rural sites alone. Telepsychiatry reaches everywhere but creates no treatment capacity. Mobile crisis reduces demand but slowly. The observation protocol is cheap and fast but shallow. Bridge clinics amplify everything else. The strategic question is sequencing and financing a portfolio — which we turn to now.

## 4. The Proposed Portfolio and Sequencing

We propose a three-phase build, anchored by the PEU and paced to the September 2026 state grant round.

**Phase 1 (months 0–9): Immediate mitigation and grant submission.**
Deploy the in-ED observation protocol at all fourteen sites; launch telepsychiatry coverage; submit the PEU capital application to the state behavioral health infrastructure grant round closing September 2026, requesting $4.0 million toward the $6.8 million capital cost.

**Phase 2 (months 9–24): Build the anchor.**
Construct and open the twelve-bay PEU; open the first bridge clinic co-located with the PEU; begin Medicaid crisis-service enrollment for mobile teams.

**Phase 3 (months 18–36): Extend upstream and outward.**
Stand up two mobile crisis teams and sub-regional stabilization beds; open the second bridge clinic; integrate rural telepsychiatry with direct PEU transfer protocols and contracted secure transport.

**Projected portfolio effect:** applying conservative, overlap-adjusted estimates, boarding hours fall **55–65 percent by end of year three**, the systemwide boarding rate falls from 29 percent toward 12–14 percent of behavioral health visits, restraint events in boarded stays fall proportionally faster (because duration is the dominant predictor), and inpatient transfer requests fall roughly 30 percent — easing pressure on the region's 38 beds and, plausibly, the refusal rate itself.

## 5. The Financial Model

### 5.1 The cost of the status quo

The status quo is not a zero-cost baseline. Conservatively:

**Table 3. Estimated annual cost of pediatric behavioral health boarding, Cedarbrook system (2025 basis)**

| Cost element | Annual estimate | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Sitter labor | $1.10M | Actual 2025 expense |
| Forgone bay capacity | $3.40M–$4.60M | 4.2 bays/day × 365 × net contribution per medical bay-day ($2,200–$3,000) |
| Unreimbursed boarding care (nursing, security, ancillary beyond facility payment) | $0.90M | Cost-accounting allocation to boarded hours |
| Restraint/seclusion events (staff injury, workers' comp, regulatory, remediation) | $0.35M | ~300 events/yr × loaded event cost |
| Avoidable repeat visits (30-day BH returns attributable to no follow-up) | $0.70M | Registry return rates × per-visit cost |
| Staff turnover attributable share (ED nursing) | $0.60M | Exit-interview attribution × replacement cost |
| **Total** | **$7.05M–$8.25M** | |

This estimate excludes costs borne outside the system — family lost wages and travel, county placement costs, school disruption, and law enforcement time — which fall heavily on the same public budgets this paper addresses.

### 5.2 Portfolio cost

Full portfolio at steady state (year three): capital of **$9.6 million** ($6.8M PEU, $1.9M stabilization conversion, $0.9M protocol/telehealth/clinic fit-out) and gross annual operating of **$9.65 million** ($4.2M PEU, $0.85M telepsychiatry, $2.6M mobile/stabilization, $0.6M observation protocol, $1.4M bridge clinics).

### 5.3 Revenue and offsets

Gross operating cost overstates net cost substantially:

- **PEU revenue.** Twelve bays at projected occupancy generate facility and professional revenue under emergency, observation, and crisis stabilization codes. At our payer mix (55 percent Medicaid), we project **$2.9–3.4 million** annual net revenue at current Minnesota and Wisconsin rates — rising materially if Medicaid crisis rates are adjusted (Section 6.3).
- **Mobile crisis enhanced match.** Federal law provides an 85 percent federal match for qualifying community mobile crisis services for three years; with state participation, net system cost of the mobile component falls to an estimated **$0.9–1.2 million**.
- **Bridge clinic revenue.** Outpatient psychiatry and therapy billing recovers an estimated **$0.8–1.0 million** of the $1.4 million cost.
- **Recaptured bay capacity.** Reducing boarding by 55–65 percent returns roughly 2.4–2.7 bays per day to medical throughput, worth **$1.9–2.9 million** in contribution margin.
- **Avoided costs.** Sitter labor, turnover, restraint, and repeat-visit costs decline with boarding hours: an estimated **$1.6–2.0 million** avoided annually at steady state.

**Table 4. Steady-state annual financial position (year 3)**

| | Low | High |
|---|---|---|
| Gross operating cost | $9.65M | $9.65M |
| Less: direct service revenue (PEU, mobile match, clinics) | ($4.60M) | ($5.60M) |
| Less: recaptured bay contribution | ($1.90M) | ($2.90M) |
| Less: avoided costs (sitter, turnover, restraint, returns) | ($1.60M) | ($2.00M) |
| **Net annual system cost** | **$1.55M** | **($0.85M)** |

Under conservative assumptions the portfolio costs the system a net $1.55 million a year; under favorable but defensible assumptions it is better than break-even. Against a status quo costing $7–8 million annually in identified expense and forgone capacity — and rising on every trend line — **the portfolio is cheaper than inaction under every scenario we modeled.**

### 5.4 Capital financing

Proposed capital stack for $9.6 million: state infrastructure grant $4.0 million (September 2026 round), philanthropic capital campaign $2.5 million (foundation commitments in progress), system capital allocation $3.1 million. The grant deadline is the binding constraint: a board capital commitment and site designation are required before submission.

### 5.5 The payer's ledger

For Medicaid agencies and managed care organizations, the arithmetic is separate and equally favorable. A boarded episode ending in out-of-region inpatient admission routinely exceeds $25,000–40,000 in combined ED, transport, and inpatient payments. A PEU episode resolving without admission, followed by bridge-clinic care, costs a payer a fraction of that. If the portfolio averts even 200 admissions annually at our Medicaid mix, state savings plausibly exceed the state's entire grant contribution within three to four years — before counting county placement and juvenile justice offsets.

## 6. Answering the Objections

Four objections have been raised consistently in our discussions with boards, county partners, and legislators. Each deserves a direct answer.

### 6.1 "A hub unit doesn't help a family two hours away."

Partially true, and it is why the PEU is proposed as an anchor, not a solution. Three responses:

First, **the rural strategy is telepsychiatry plus mobile crisis, not travel.** Telepsychiatry reaches every site in months and averts 20–35 percent of transfers outright. Mobile teams respond toward the family, and sub-regional stabilization beds keep children within their communities.

Second, **for children who do need the PEU, the alternative is worse travel.** Today, rural children who secure an inpatient bed are frequently transferred out of region entirely — sometimes out of state. A hub PEU two hours away that resolves a crisis in 36 hours is closer, faster, and more family-accessible than a bed 300 miles away after four days of boarding.

Third, the portfolio includes **contracted, non-law-enforcement secure transport and family lodging support** at the PEU. Distance is a design constraint we have budgeted for, not an unanswerable objection.

### 6.2 "You cannot recruit child psychiatrists to southern Minnesota."

The shortage is real; the staffing model accounts for it. The PEU's clinical model is deliberately psychiatrist-efficient: it is built on psychiatric nursing, licensed therapists, and psychiatric APRNs, with child psychiatrist coverage concentrated where it changes decisions — daily rounding, complex assessments, and tele-supervision. Our full portfolio requires the equivalent of **3.5 child psychiatrist FTEs**, of which 1.5 can be remote. Cedarbrook currently employs 2.1 FTEs in relevant roles and has two APRN fellows completing training in 2027. The recruitment case itself improves with the investment: a purpose-built unit, a defined crisis continuum, and academic affiliation for the PEU are precisely the conditions under which child psychiatrists accept regional positions — whereas the status quo, in which psychiatric consultation means managing boarded children in ED hallways, is a recruitment liability. Finally, telepsychiatry contracts provide a coverage floor that does not depend on local recruitment succeeding on schedule.

### 6.3 "Medicaid rates won't sustain the operating cost."

At current rates, the model in Section 5 already reaches near-break-even on conservative assumptions — because the offsets (recaptured capacity, avoided sitter labor, mobile crisis enhanced match) do not depend on rate changes. But the objection identifies a genuine policy problem, and this paper makes the ask explicit: **Minnesota and Wisconsin should establish adequate bundled rates for crisis stabilization and psychiatric emergency observation**, as a growing number of states have done. Every dollar of rate adequacy converts directly into reduced inpatient and boarding expenditure on the state's own ledger; Section 5.5 shows the state recoups its position through averted admissions alone. We are not asking payers to subsidize an unproven model; we are asking them to pay for a cheaper setting instead of continuing to pay for the most expensive one.

### 6.4 "Upstream investment never actually moves ED volume."

Skepticism about upstream promises is warranted — many community investments have failed to bend ED curves. Our answer is threefold.

First, **most of the projected benefit does not depend on demand reduction.** The PEU, telepsychiatry, and observation protocol reduce boarding *conditional on arrival*; they work even if not one visit is prevented. Only the mobile crisis component (10–15 percent of projected effect) is a demand-side bet, and it is the component with an 85 percent federal match limiting the system's downside.

Second, **the specific upstream mechanisms here have measured effects**: mobile crisis programs resolve most encounters without ED transport, and 72-hour follow-up measurably reduces 30-day returns — our own registry shows elevated return rates precisely among discharges with no follow-up scheduled.

Third, **we commit to measurement.** The Institute will publish boarding hours, restraint rates, transfer refusals, return rates, and per-episode costs annually, against the 2021–2025 baseline in this paper. If a component does not perform, the phased structure allows reallocation. We ask partners to fund a portfolio with a published scoreboard, not a promise.

## 8. Conclusion

For five years, the emergency departments of this system have functioned as the region's de facto pediatric psychiatric institution — 11,900 crisis visits, thousands of boarded stays, a median wait of 31 hours and a lengthening tail beyond four days, restraint or seclusion in nearly one in ten boarded stays, and $1.1 million a year in sitter labor to watch children we are not treating. Every trend line worsens. The two facilities holding the region's 38 child psychiatric beds now refuse nearly half of our transfer requests, and the children they refuse are the children who wait longest.

The status quo costs this system $7 to 8 million a year in identified expense and forgone capacity, and it costs children days of their lives in treatment bays that treat nothing. The portfolio proposed here — a twelve-bay psychiatric emergency unit, telepsychiatry to every rural site, mobile crisis teams with stabilization beds, an immediate observation protocol, and bridge clinics guaranteeing follow-up within 72 hours — is buildable with realistic staffing, financeable through a capital stack anchored by the September 2026 state grant round, and, on conservative assumptions, cheaper than continuing to board.

No element of this proposal is experimental. Each model has been built elsewhere and measured. What has not been done is assembling them into a continuum fitted to a dispersed rural-and-regional system like ours — and committing, in public, to report the results. That is what Cedarbrook proposes to do. The grant window closes in September 2026. The children in our bays are waiting now.

## Appendix A. Registry Methodology and Definitions

**A.1 Inclusion criteria.** The Cedarbrook pediatric behavioral health emergency registry includes all emergency department visits by patients aged 5 through 17 at any of the system's fourteen emergency departments between January 1, 2021 and December 31, 2025, in which (a) the chief complaint at triage was behavioral or psychiatric in nature, or (b) the primary encounter diagnosis fell within ICD-10-CM chapters F01–F99, or (c) the visit involved a self-harm diagnosis code (X71–X83, T14.91, or relevant T36–T65 codes with intentional self-harm designation). Visits in which a behavioral health diagnosis was secondary to a primary medical or traumatic presentation were excluded unless a psychiatric disposition (admission request, hold, or crisis referral) was documented.

**A.2 Boarding definition.** Boarding is measured from the timestamp of the documented disposition decision — the order for psychiatric admission, transfer request, or crisis placement — to physical departure from the emergency department. Stays exceeding twelve hours from disposition decision to departure are classified as boarded. This threshold is more conservative than definitions used in some published literature (which begin the clock at four hours or at arrival); readers comparing Cedarbrook figures to other systems should note that our reported durations *understate* total time in department. Median total length of stay for boarded children, arrival to departure, was 39 hours over the study period.

**A.3 Restraint and seclusion.** Events are drawn from the system's regulatory event reporting, cross-validated against nursing documentation and pharmacy records for chemical restraint orders. The 9.4 percent figure represents the proportion of boarded stays with at least one physical restraint, seclusion, or emergency involuntary medication event; it is not an event count. Stays exceeding 48 hours accounted for 71 percent of all events.

**A.4 Transfer refusal.** A refusal is recorded when a completed transfer request to either regional inpatient child psychiatric facility received a declination for any stated reason. Requests withdrawn by Cedarbrook before facility response, and requests resolved by the child's clinical improvement before response, are excluded from both numerator and denominator. Multiple refusals for the same stay are counted once per facility per stay.

**A.5 Limitations.** The registry is an operational dataset, not a research protocol; timestamps depend on documentation practice, which improved over the study period and may bias early-period durations downward. Payer attribution reflects payer at registration and does not capture retroactive Medicaid enrollment. Out-of-system emergency visits by catchment residents are not captured, meaning true regional crisis volume exceeds the figures reported here.

## Appendix B. Financial Model Assumptions

**B.1 Forgone bay capacity (Table 3).** Net contribution per medical bay-day is estimated from the hub and two regional emergency departments' 2025 cost accounting: average visits per functioning bay-day (5.1–5.8 by site), average net revenue per medical visit less direct variable cost, yielding a contribution range of $2,200–$3,000 per bay-day. The estimate assumes displaced capacity would have been used at 85 percent of average productivity, reflecting the reality that recaptured bays at rural sites recover less volume than at the hub. Boarding bay-days are weighted by site accordingly.

**B.2 PEU revenue projection.** The projection assumes 12 bays, ramp to 78 percent average occupancy by month 18 of operation, average length of stay 28 hours, and approximately 2,700 episodes annually at steady state — of which an estimated 60 percent arrive directly through the hub emergency department, 25 percent by transfer from system rural sites, and 15 percent by direct referral from mobile crisis teams and county partners in later years. Revenue is modeled at 2025 Minnesota and Wisconsin Medicaid rates for emergency, observation, and crisis stabilization services, commercial rates at current contracted multiples, and the system's observed pediatric behavioral health payer mix (55 percent Medicaid, 36 percent commercial, 9 percent other/self-pay). No rate increases are assumed in the base case; Section 6.3 sensitivity is presented separately.

**B.3 Mobile crisis enhanced match.** The 85 percent federal match under ARPA Section 9813 applies to qualifying community-based mobile crisis services for twelve fiscal quarters following state election of the option. The model assumes Minnesota participation for the full period and conservatively assumes Wisconsin-side services are financed at standard match. After match expiration, the model reverts mobile crisis to standard Medicaid crisis rates plus county contribution, raising net system cost of that component by approximately $0.7 million annually in year four and beyond — a step-up disclosed to the board and incorporated in the long-range plan.

**B.4 Avoided-cost attribution.** Sitter labor avoidance is scaled linearly to projected boarding-hour reduction and discounted 15 percent for fixed scheduling inefficiency. Turnover attribution uses the conservative bound of exit-interview data (boarding cited as a primary factor) rather than the broader "contributing factor" figure. Repeat-visit avoidance applies published bridge-clinic effect sizes to only the registry subpopulation discharged without documented follow-up.

**B.5 Sensitivity.** The break-even case in Table 4 is most sensitive, in order, to: PEU occupancy ramp (each 5-point occupancy shortfall widens net cost by ~$0.35M), Medicaid crisis rate action (a bundled stabilization rate at cost would improve the position by $0.8–1.1M), and recaptured-capacity realization. The model does not reach net cost above $2.4 million annually under any single-variable stress; combined worst-case (low occupancy, no rate action, 60 percent capacity realization) yields net cost of $3.1 million — still less than half the identified cost of the status quo.

## Glossary

**Boarding.** Continued occupancy of an emergency department treatment space after the clinical decision to admit, transfer, or place a patient, pending availability of the destination. Defined in this paper as exceeding twelve hours post-disposition.

**Bridge clinic.** An outpatient clinic providing rapid post-crisis psychiatric and therapeutic care — here, within 72 hours of discharge — until longitudinal community services engage.

**Crisis stabilization bed.** A short-stay (typically 1–5 day) residential-style bed providing sub-acute psychiatric treatment as an alternative to hospitalization.

**EmPATH unit.** Emergency Psychiatric Assessment, Treatment and Healing unit; a calming, open-milieu observation unit model in which patients receive active psychiatric treatment during emergency evaluation rather than boarding in medical bays.

**Mobile crisis team.** A dispatched clinical team (typically a licensed mental health professional paired with a peer or family specialist) responding to behavioral health crises in homes, schools, and community settings.

**Sitter.** A staff member assigned to continuous 1:1 or line-of-sight observation of a patient at safety risk.
