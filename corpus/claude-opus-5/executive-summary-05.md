# Executive Summary I

## Tupelo Preventive Maintenance Overhaul — Fiscal 2025 Program Review
**Prepared September 19, 2025 | Sponsor: Dwayne Lockhart, Plant Manager, Tupelo | Reviewing Office: Sanjay Mirchandani, Director, Capital and Continuous Improvement Review**

### Finding and Recommendation

The Tupelo preventive maintenance overhaul is the only one of the four programs in this review cycle that has delivered more than it promised, delivered it early, and delivered it for less than the approved amount. On a $2.2 million investment, the program has removed $3.1 million from annual scrap cost, reduced unplanned downtime from 11.4 percent to 4.1 percent of scheduled run time, and raised overall equipment effectiveness from 62 to 78. The Review Office recommends that the Operations Committee approve replication of the Tupelo model at the three remaining plants, funded at $5.8 million across fiscal 2026 and fiscal 2027, and that it direct the Tupelo maintenance organization to serve as the deployment team rather than contracting the work to the original outside consultancy. The Committee should also recognize that the replication decision is time-sensitive: the Tupelo maintenance leadership that built this program is a small group, and the market for people who can do this work is not soft.

The cost to continue, in the narrow sense of sustaining the program at Tupelo, is $410,000 annually in recurring spend — condition-monitoring instrumentation service contracts, the incremental labor for the planned-maintenance shift, and the software license for the reliability database. The cost to stop, meaning to let the program lapse into ordinary maintenance practice, is the full $3.1 million in annual scrap avoidance plus the recovered capacity represented by the sixteen-point gain in overall equipment effectiveness, which the Review Office values conservatively at $4.4 million in annual contribution margin at current volumes. Stopping is not a serious option and the Committee should not spend time on it. The decision in front of the Committee is entirely about the speed and funding of replication.

### Evidence

The program originated in the fiscal 2023 planning cycle, when Tupelo's unplanned downtime had reached 11.4 percent of scheduled run time and the plant was missing customer releases on two of its four highest-volume part families. The initial diagnosis, performed jointly by Lockhart's staff and an outside reliability consultancy, found that Tupelo was running an almost entirely reactive maintenance organization: 78 percent of maintenance labor hours were spent on breakdown response, planned maintenance was routinely deferred when it conflicted with production schedules, and the plant had no functioning history of failure modes by asset. Spare parts were ordered on failure. Three of the plant's eleven transfer presses had no documented preventive maintenance schedule of any kind.

The approved program had four components. The first was asset criticality ranking across all 340 pieces of production equipment, which produced a tiered maintenance standard rather than a uniform one and concentrated resources on the ninety assets that actually constrain output. The second was condition-monitoring instrumentation — vibration analysis, thermographic survey, and oil analysis — on those ninety assets, which is where the majority of the $2.2 million was spent. The third was the establishment of a dedicated planned-maintenance shift on Saturdays and the second half of third shift, staffed by six technicians who are protected from breakdown call-outs. The fourth, and in the Review Office's assessment the most consequential, was the reliability database and the discipline of root-cause coding on every failure event over four hours.

The results are documented in the plant's maintenance management system and were independently sampled by this office against production records for the twelve months ending August 31, 2025. Unplanned downtime averaged 4.1 percent over that period, against 11.4 percent in the fiscal 2023 baseline year. The improvement was not front-loaded; the monthly figures show continued decline through the third and fourth quarters of fiscal 2025, which suggests the program is still finding failure modes rather than having exhausted its available gains. Overall equipment effectiveness, calculated on the standard availability-performance-quality basis and audited by this office for two sample months, rose from 62 to 78. Scrap cost reduction of $3.1 million annually was verified against the plant's scrap accounts and adjusted downward from Lockhart's original claim of $3.6 million to exclude $500,000 attributable to a die design change on the bracket family that was funded separately and would have delivered its benefit regardless.

Two aspects of the Tupelo experience deserve the Committee's attention because they bear directly on the replication decision. The first is that the program's return came disproportionately from the third component — the protected planned-maintenance shift — rather than from the instrumentation. Tupelo's own analysis, which this office finds credible, attributes roughly 60 percent of the downtime reduction to simply having technicians who could not be pulled off scheduled work. That component cost $340,000 in incremental annual labor. The instrumentation, which cost $1.6 million in capital, is contributing but is contributing less per dollar. Replication at the other three plants should be sequenced accordingly, with the labor and scheduling changes made first and the instrumentation phased behind them. This sequencing would allow the three plants to capture the majority of the available benefit in the first twelve months at roughly a third of the capital exposure.

The second aspect is that the program consumed considerably more of Lockhart's personal attention than the business case anticipated. The reactive maintenance culture at Tupelo was not a resource problem; it was a scheduling-authority problem, in which production supervision could and did override maintenance plans. Resolving that required Lockhart to remove the override authority and to sustain that decision through two quarters in which production supervision was measurably unhappy about it. The Review Office notes this because the same authority question exists at all three remaining plants and because replication led by a deployment team without equivalent plant-manager backing is likely to reproduce the instrumentation without reproducing the result. The Committee should condition replication funding on written commitment from each receiving plant manager to the same scheduling-authority change, and should ask Novikova to verify that commitment before capital release rather than after.

On the question of who executes the replication, the Review Office recommends against re-engaging the original consultancy. Their contribution at Tupelo was the criticality-ranking methodology, which is now documented and transferable, and the instrumentation specification, which is now proven. The consultancy's fiscal 2023 fee was $480,000 and a comparable engagement across three plants would run near $1.1 million. Tupelo's maintenance manager and two of its reliability technicians can carry the methodology to the other plants at an internal cost the Review Office estimates at $290,000 in travel, backfill, and temporary assignment premium. This approach also has the advantage of building durable internal capability, which matters given that the company is likely to face this same problem again as equipment ages.

The risk the Committee should weigh against replication is capacity. Pulling three experienced people out of Tupelo for eighteen months creates exposure at the plant that is currently performing best. The Review Office's judgment is that the risk is manageable because the Tupelo program is now institutionalized in procedure and database rather than resident in individuals, but the Committee may reasonably want Lockhart's direct assessment on that point before approving.

### Requested Committee Action

The Review Office asks the Operations Committee to approve $5.8 million in replication capital across fiscal 2026 and 2027, phased with labor and scheduling changes preceding instrumentation at each site; to direct that replication be led internally by the Tupelo maintenance organization rather than by outside consultants; and to condition capital release at each plant on documented plant-manager commitment to protected maintenance scheduling authority.

# Executive Summary II

## Dothan Robotic Weld Cell — Fiscal 2025 Program Review
**Prepared September 19, 2025 | Plant: Dothan, Alabama | Reviewing Office: Sanjay Mirchandani, Director, Capital and Continuous Improvement Review**

### Finding and Recommendation

The Dothan robotic weld cell has consumed $7.4 million and does not work. It runs a 61-second cycle against a 42-second specification, holds 81 percent first-pass yield against the 96 percent achieved by the manual line it replaced, and has twice failed customer part approval. It is not producing saleable parts under its own approval and the plant is meeting customer demand through a manual backup operation that was supposed to have been decommissioned in March 2024. The Review Office recommends that the Operations Committee terminate the program, write off the unrecoverable investment, formally restore the manual line as the approved process, and commission an independent engineering assessment of the cell's residual value before any decision is made about the physical asset.

The cost to continue is not knowable with the precision the Committee is entitled to, which is itself the central finding. The cell's integrator has proposed a remediation package at $1.9 million covering fixture redesign, controller replacement, and a third part-approval attempt, with a projected twelve-month timeline. The Review Office does not believe that estimate. The two prior remediation efforts, at $840,000 and $1.2 million respectively, were both presented with comparable confidence and both failed to close the cycle-time gap. A realistic continuation figure, including the manual line's ongoing operating cost during a third remediation attempt, the engineering labor the plant would have to dedicate, and a contingency proportionate to the program's history, is between $3.2 million and $4.1 million with no assurance of success. The cost to stop is $7.4 million written off, less whatever the independent assessment establishes as recoverable value in the robots, controllers, and tooling, which the Review Office provisionally estimates at $900,000 to $1.4 million on the secondary market. Stopping also crystallizes an annual labor cost of $1.1 million at the manual line that the program was intended to eliminate.

### Evidence

The program was approved in fiscal 2022 at $4.6 million to automate the welding operation on the Dothan cross-member family, which represents $38 million in annual sales to two customers. The business case rested on three claims: a 42-second cycle against the manual line's 68 seconds, a labor reduction of eighteen positions across three shifts, and a yield improvement from 96 to 99 percent based on the elimination of operator variation. The payback was stated at 3.4 years.

None of the three claims has been realized and one of them was structurally implausible from the outset. The Review Office's examination of the original approval package found that the 42-second cycle was taken from the integrator's proposal without independent verification and without accounting for the part-handling time imposed by the existing fixture geometry, which the plant did not intend to change. The 99 percent yield projection was similarly taken from vendor material. This office notes, without assigning individual fault, that the approval package was reviewed and signed at four levels and that no reviewer at any level asked for the cycle-time calculation to be shown. That governance failure is more consequential than the specific technical failures that followed, because it is the failure most likely to repeat.

The program's cost history is as follows. The original $4.6 million approval was fully spent by November 2023, at which point the cell was running a 74-second cycle at 68 percent first-pass yield. A supplemental appropriation of $840,000 was approved in December 2023 for fixture modification and weld-schedule development, which brought the cycle to 66 seconds and yield to 76 percent by May 2024. A second supplemental of $1.2 million was approved in July 2024 for a robot path optimization study and the replacement of two of the six weld guns, which produced the current 61-second cycle and 81 percent yield. An additional $760,000 has been absorbed in plant engineering labor, expedited freight on parts produced during cell downtime, and the cost of maintaining the manual line beyond its planned decommissioning. The total is $7.4 million against an approved $4.6 million, an overrun of 61 percent.

The two failed part approvals are the most serious element of the record and the Committee should understand their nature precisely. The first submission, in August 2024, was rejected by the customer on dimensional capability: the cell could not hold the flange-to-flange dimension within the specified capability index across the required sample. The second submission, in April 2025, was rejected on weld penetration consistency at two of the eleven weld locations, verified by destructive testing on the customer's premises. Neither rejection was procedural or documentary. Both reflect the process's actual inability to meet the print. A third submission would require demonstrated correction of both conditions, and the integrator's remediation proposal addresses the dimensional issue with a fixture redesign but addresses the penetration issue only with a further weld-schedule development effort of the kind that has already been attempted twice.

The Review Office has considered and rejects three arguments for continuation that the Committee is likely to hear.

The first is that the $7.4 million is lost either way and the remaining decision should be made on forward economics alone. This is correct as a matter of principle and the Review Office applies it. On forward economics, continuation requires $3.2 to $4.1 million to achieve, at best, the original business case's $1.1 million annual labor saving, producing a payback of three to four years from a program that has already failed twice to reach the threshold and that would be operating equipment which by then will be four years old and technically superseded. Termination requires zero forward capital and preserves the manual line, which is producing at 96 percent yield today and meeting customer demand today.

The second argument is that the manual line's 68-second cycle constrains capacity on a growing part family. The Review Office examined the volume data and does not find this persuasive. Cross-member demand from both customers has been essentially flat for six quarters and the customers' own published program volumes show no increase through the current model cycle. The manual line has 14 percent unutilized capacity at current demand. Capacity is not the binding constraint and should not be used to justify continuation.

The third argument is reputational — that terminating a flagship automation program signals retreat from the company's stated manufacturing technology strategy. The Review Office's view is the opposite. Continuing to fund a demonstrably failed program signals that the company's capital discipline is weak, which is a considerably more damaging signal to both customers and lenders. The Dothan cell's failure has already been visible to one customer through two rejected part approvals, and that customer's supplier quality engineering group has raised it in two consecutive business reviews. A clean, documented termination with a restored and capable process is a better answer to that customer than a third attempt.

The Review Office does recommend that the Committee separate the termination decision from the disposition decision. The cell contains six robots of a current-generation model, two recently replaced weld guns, and a controller architecture that has value independent of the failed application. A competent independent assessment — the Review Office estimates $45,000 and six weeks — should establish whether the equipment has redeployable value elsewhere in the company, particularly at Tupelo where two aging weld operations are approaching replacement, or whether it should be sold. Making that assessment before rather than after the termination decision would delay the termination without changing it, and the Committee should not accept that sequencing.

Finally, the Review Office recommends that Novikova be asked to report to the Committee at its January meeting on the changes made to capital approval procedure in consequence of this program, specifically on the requirement for independent verification of vendor-supplied performance claims above a threshold value. The Dothan failure is recoverable. The approval process that permitted it is the thing that needs to be fixed.

### Requested Committee Action

The Review Office asks the Operations Committee to terminate the Dothan robotic weld cell program effective immediately; to authorize the write-off of $7.4 million net of recovered asset value; to formally restore the manual weld line as the approved process for the cross-member family and fund its continued operation; to commission an independent equipment disposition assessment at $45,000; and to require a report at the January 2026 meeting on capital approval process changes.

## Northeast Mississippi Community College Apprenticeship Pipeline — Fiscal 2025 Program Review
**Prepared September 19, 2025 | Reviewing Office: Sanjay Mirchandani, Director, Capital and Continuous Improvement Review**

### Finding and Recommendation

The apprenticeship pipeline with Northeast Mississippi Community College has enrolled 64 apprentices, graduated 41, and lost 15 of those graduates within twelve months of completion. Skilled trades vacancy across the four plants fell from 17 to 12 percent over the same period, but the regional hiring market loosened materially during that window and the Review Office cannot attribute the vacancy improvement to the program with confidence. The recommendation is to continue the program with restructured terms rather than to expand it, to renew the college agreement, and to attach a service commitment to the tuition and wage subsidy that the company currently provides without one. The Committee should treat this as a program with a real but unproven benefit and a demonstrated retention defect, and should decline the expansion request that accompanies this review.

The cost to continue at current scale is $1.4 million annually, comprising tuition reimbursement, the wage differential paid to apprentices above the standard entry rate, the college's program fee, and the burdened cost of the four journeyman mentors who are released from production duties for eight hours weekly. The cost of the proposed expansion to 100 apprentices annually would be $2.2 million. The cost to stop is harder to state and the Review Office wants the Committee to hold both parts of it in view. The direct cost of stopping is zero in cash and approximately $310,000 in contract termination and transition obligations to the college. The indirect cost is the loss of the only structured skilled-trades entry channel the company has, in a labor market where the average age of the company's maintenance and tooling workforce is 51 and where 23 percent of that population is eligible to retire within five years. Stopping is cheap this year and expensive in 2031.

### Evidence

The program was established in fiscal 2022 under a three-year agreement with Northeast Mississippi Community College, structured as a two-year apprenticeship combining classroom instruction at the college's Booneville campus with supervised work assignment at one of the four plants. Apprentices are company employees from day one, paid at a rate approximately 12 percent above the standard entry production rate, with tuition fully reimbursed and books and tools provided. The program covers three trades: industrial maintenance, tool and die, and controls technician.

Enrollment across the three cohorts totals 64. Of these, 41 have completed and received their certification, 8 remain in the current cohort, and 15 separated before completion. The pre-completion attrition rate of 23 percent is roughly consistent with published benchmarks for manufacturing apprenticeships and the Review Office does not regard it as a program defect. Exit interviews attribute most of it to the classroom demands of the controls track, which several apprentices described as substantially harder than they had been led to expect at recruitment. The plants have since adjusted the recruitment description and the current cohort's attrition is running lower.

The post-completion attrition is the problem. Fifteen of the 41 graduates — 37 percent — left the company within twelve months of certification. The Review Office obtained destination information for 12 of the 15. Nine went to other manufacturers within a sixty-mile radius, two went to industrial contractors, and one left the trade entirely. Of the nine who went to other manufacturers, wage comparison was available for seven, and all seven took increases ranging from $2.40 to $5.85 per hour. The pattern is not ambiguous. The company is paying to train skilled tradespeople and the regional labor market is buying them at completion.

This is the central finding of the review and it has a specific cause. The company's post-apprenticeship wage progression places a newly certified journeyman at $24.80 per hour, rising to $28.15 over four years. The regional market rate for a certified industrial maintenance technician with two years of documented apprenticeship is between $27.50 and $31.00 depending on shift and employer. The company is below market at the exact moment its investment matures. Every graduate who stays is making a decision that costs them money, and 37 percent of them decline to make it.

The Review Office wants to be direct with the Committee about what this means. The apprenticeship program is not failing because of program design, mentor quality, or college performance. All three are adequate and the college in particular has been a capable and flexible partner. The program is failing to retain because the compensation structure it feeds into is not competitive. Expanding the program without addressing that structure would increase the volume of trained technicians the company supplies to its competitors. The Review Office therefore recommends against the expansion request and recommends instead that the Committee direct Human Resources to bring a post-apprenticeship wage structure proposal to the January meeting, with the expansion question deferred until that structure is settled.

On the vacancy statistics, the Review Office is obliged to caution the Committee against the reading that appears in the program's own reporting. Skilled trades vacancy across the four plants was 17 percent in the fiscal 2023 baseline and 12 percent at the close of fiscal 2025, and the program's sponsors present this five-point improvement as program benefit. The Review Office examined regional labor data for the same period. Manufacturing employment in the four-county area around Tupelo contracted by 3.8 percent over that window following two plant closures unrelated to this company, and the regional unemployment rate rose from 3.1 to 4.4 percent. Skilled trades applications per posting across the company's four plants rose 61 percent over the same period, of which the apprenticeship program can account for at most a small fraction, because apprentices are recruited separately and do not appear in the general posting pipeline. The honest conclusion is that the vacancy improvement is substantially attributable to a loosened market and that the program's contribution, while probably positive, cannot be isolated from the available data. The Committee should not permit this program to be justified on the vacancy number.

What the program can be justified on is different and, in the Review Office's judgment, sufficient. Twenty-six graduates have stayed. Those 26 are demonstrably more capable than the equivalent external hires: the plants' own skill assessments place apprenticeship graduates a full band above externally hired technicians at comparable tenure, and three of the 26 have already moved into lead technician roles. The program has given the company a recruiting channel into a demographic — recent high school graduates in the Tupelo region — that it had effectively lost access to. And it has established a relationship with the college that has produced secondary benefits, including customized short-course training for existing employees that the company would otherwise have purchased at higher cost.

The restructuring the Review Office recommends has three elements. The first is the service commitment: tuition reimbursement and the wage differential should be structured as a forgivable obligation, with the full amount owed at certification and forgiven over 24 months of subsequent service. The current program provides both without any repayment condition. Comparable programs at other regional employers use exactly this structure and the college has confirmed it raises no accreditation issue. The Review Office estimates this alone would reduce post-completion attrition by roughly a third, based on the wage differentials involved and the size of the obligation, which would total approximately $19,000 per graduate.

The second element is the wage structure question already described, which is the larger of the two levers and which sits outside this program's control.

The third is a change to the mentor arrangement. The four journeyman mentors are released eight hours weekly, which the plants have found difficult to protect, and mentor hours actually delivered in fiscal 2025 were 61 percent of hours scheduled. Apprentice feedback identifies mentor availability as the weakest element of the program. The Review Office recommends either that mentor release time be protected on the same basis as the Tupelo planned-maintenance shift discussed elsewhere in this review, or that the mentor role be consolidated into two full-time positions rather than four part-time releases. The second option is cleaner and would cost approximately $95,000 more annually.

### Requested Committee Action

The Review Office asks the Operations Committee to renew the Northeast Mississippi Community College agreement at current scale for three years; to decline the proposed expansion to 100 annual apprentices pending resolution of the compensation question; to direct that tuition and wage differential be restructured as a 24-month forgivable service obligation effective with the fiscal 2026 cohort; to direct Human Resources to present a post-apprenticeship wage progression proposal at the January 2026 meeting; and to approve consolidation of the mentor role into two dedicated positions at an incremental $95,000 annually.

# Executive Summary IV

## Manufacturing Execution System Integration — Fiscal 2025 Program Review
**Prepared September 19, 2025 | Program Owner: Fatoumata Camara, Quality Director | Reviewing Office: Sanjay Mirchandani, Director, Capital and Continuous Improvement Review**

### Finding and Recommendation

The manufacturing execution system integration was approved at $5.2 million, has spent $8.9 million, is live at three of four plants, holds inventory accuracy at 87 percent against a 98 percent target, and has moved the fourth plant's go-live from January 2025 to September 2025. The Review Office recommends that the Operations Committee suspend the fourth-plant deployment, hold the system at three sites, and fund a stabilization phase directed entirely at inventory accuracy and transaction discipline at the three live sites before any further deployment is authorized. The Review Office further recommends that program ownership be transferred from the Quality function to a dedicated program director reporting to Novikova, for reasons set out below that do not reflect on Camara's performance.

The cost to continue on the current plan — completing the fourth plant in the current window — is $1.6 million in remaining deployment cost plus, in the Review Office's assessment, a high probability of a further $1.1 to $1.8 million in stabilization spend at that site, because the three live sites each required unbudgeted stabilization and there is no reason to expect the fourth to differ. The cost of the recommended alternative, suspension and stabilization, is $1.9 million over nine months, of which $1.4 million is stabilization at the three live sites and $500,000 is the cost of holding the fourth-plant deployment in a resumable state rather than dissolving the team. The cost to stop entirely — abandoning the system and reverting to the legacy environment — is $8.9 million written off plus approximately $2.4 million to restore and re-license the legacy systems at the three converted sites, which is not recommended and is presented only so the Committee has the full range in view.

### Evidence

The program was approved in fiscal 2023 to replace four separate legacy production tracking environments with a single manufacturing execution system, integrated to the enterprise resource planning platform, delivering real-time production visibility, electronic traceability sufficient to meet two customers' emerging requirements, and inventory accuracy of 98 percent. The approved budget of $5.2 million comprised software licensing, integration services, hardware and shop-floor infrastructure, and internal project labor. The schedule called for all four plants live by January 2025.

Spend to date is $8.9 million, an overrun of 71 percent. The Review Office traced the overrun to four sources. Integration services account for $1.9 million of it, driven by the discovery during the first plant's deployment that the enterprise resource planning platform's item master contained substantially more data quality defects than the discovery phase had established — approximately 31 percent of active part records had at least one field inconsistent with the physical part or the routing. Shop-floor infrastructure accounts for $840,000, driven by wireless coverage remediation at two plants that the original site surveys had not identified. Internal labor accounts for $620,000, driven by extended deployment duration. And unbudgeted stabilization at the three live sites accounts for $340,000. The Review Office notes that the item master data quality problem was the single largest driver and that it was foreseeable: the same problem had been identified in a fiscal 2021 internal audit of the enterprise resource planning environment, and that audit's remediation recommendations were not completed.

The inventory accuracy result is the finding that should govern the Committee's decision. Three plants are live. Across those three, cycle count accuracy stands at 87 percent against the 98 percent target. This is not merely short of target; it is below the accuracy the legacy environment delivered at two of the three sites, which ran 91 and 89 percent respectively before conversion. The system has made inventory accuracy worse at the majority of sites where it has been deployed.

The Review Office investigated the cause and found it to be a transaction discipline problem rather than a system defect. The manufacturing execution system requires operators to record material movement at each transaction point, and it enforces that requirement much less permissively than the legacy environment did. Where the legacy systems allowed after-the-fact adjustment by supervision, the new system requires transaction-level correction with an audit trail. The practical consequence is that operators facing a transaction the system will not accept — a common occurrence during changeover, when physical material flow does not match the routing the system expects — bypass the transaction entirely and move material without recording it. Cycle counts then find inventory the system does not know about and fail to find inventory the system believes exists. The plants' own analysis, which the Review Office verified by observation at two sites, identifies changeover transactions as the source of roughly 70 percent of accuracy variance.

This is fixable, and it is fixable at a defined cost, which is why the Review Office recommends stabilization rather than abandonment. The remedy has three components: routing corrections in the item master for the changeover-intensive part families, which is the completion of the fiscal 2021 audit remediation that was never finished; configuration changes to permit supervised exception transactions with an audit trail rather than forcing a bypass; and operator retraining focused specifically on changeover transaction handling. The three live sites' plant managers have jointly estimated this work at $1.4 million and nine months. The Review Office reviewed that estimate and finds it credible, though it would characterize nine months as optimistic and would plan on eleven.

What the Review Office does not find credible is the current plan to proceed with the fourth plant's deployment in September 2025 while the three live sites are running at 87 percent. That plan takes a system with a known and unresolved defect and installs it at a fourth site, expanding the population affected by the defect and consuming the same technical resources that the stabilization work requires. The plants cannot do both. The fourth plant deployment has already moved from January to September; the Review Office's recommendation is that it move again, to a date determined by stabilization completion at the three live sites rather than by calendar commitment.

The Committee should understand what this suspension costs beyond money. The fourth plant is the site with the most demanding traceability requirement, driven by a customer program launching in the second quarter of fiscal 2027. That customer has been told the plant will have electronic traceability. Suspension does not endanger that commitment — the timeline has eighteen months of margin against a nine-to-eleven-month stabilization — but it removes the margin the program currently has, and the Committee should authorize suspension knowing that a second delay after this one would create a genuine customer problem.

On program ownership, the Review Office makes a recommendation that requires some care. Camara has managed this program competently under conditions that were made considerably harder by inherited data quality problems she did not create and could not have discovered within the discovery phase's scope. The recommendation to transfer ownership is structural, not performance-based. The Quality function owns this program because its original justification was weighted toward traceability, but the program's actual difficulty is now concentrated in areas — inventory transaction discipline, item master data governance, shop-floor operating practice — that sit with manufacturing operations and that the Quality Director has no line authority over. Camara has been required to secure cooperation from four plant managers on operating practice changes through influence alone, and the record of stabilization at the three live sites shows that influence has produced uneven results: the site that has reached 91 percent accuracy is the site whose plant manager engaged directly, and the two at 85 and 84 percent are the sites where he did not.

The Review Office therefore recommends a dedicated program director reporting to Novikova, with Camara continuing to own the traceability and quality-system requirements as a defined workstream. This is not a demotion and the Committee should ensure it is communicated as what it is: a recognition that a program of this scope, at this stage, needs line authority over plant operating practice, and that no functional director possesses that authority.

Two further matters warrant the Committee's attention. The first is that the item master data quality problem is not confined to this program. The fiscal 2021 audit finding remains open, the remediation the Review Office is recommending here addresses only the changeover-intensive part families, and the remaining defects will surface in any future initiative that depends on accurate routings. The Committee may wish to direct a broader remediation as a separate matter. The second is that the program has no post-implementation benefit measurement in place. The approved business case projected $2.1 million in annual benefit from inventory reduction, expedited freight avoidance, and scrap reduction through improved traceability, and no mechanism exists today to determine whether any portion of that is being realized. The Review Office recommends that benefit measurement be established as a condition of resuming deployment, so that the fourth-plant decision, whenever it comes, can be made on evidence rather than on the original projection.

### Requested Committee Action

The Review Office asks the Operations Committee to suspend the fourth-plant deployment of the manufacturing execution system; to approve $1.9 million for a stabilization phase at the three live sites, targeting inventory accuracy of 96 percent as the condition for resuming deployment; to transfer program ownership to a dedicated program director reporting to the Vice President of Manufacturing, with the Quality Director retaining ownership of the traceability workstream; to require establishment of post-implementation benefit measurement before deployment resumes; and to note that the fourth plant's customer traceability commitment retains adequate but no longer generous margin.

## Note on the Set

The four programs reviewed represent $23.6 million in committed capital against $17.0 million approved. One program has substantially exceeded its business case, one has failed outright, one has delivered an unproven benefit with a demonstrated structural defect, and one is recoverable but requires the Committee to stop and repair before it continues. The Review Office observes that in three of the four cases the governing difficulty was identified in advance by an earlier internal document — a prior audit finding, a market wage comparison, a vendor claim that was never independently verified — and was not acted upon. The Committee may find that the more useful question at its October meeting is not what to do about these four programs, on which this office has made specific recommendations, but why the organization's own prior warnings did not reach the approval decisions they should have shaped.
