## 1.1 Summary of the Year

Emmanuel supervised city dispatch through the hardest twelve months this terminal has had since I've been in the chair. He carried a 34-driver city fleet through 68 percent turnover, a conditional safety rating, a 9 percent tonnage decline that scrambled the load plan every four to six weeks, and a customer escalation from our second-largest account. He finished the year at 96.4 percent on-time service against a 95.0 percent standard and reduced empty city miles by 11 percent. Those two numbers, produced simultaneously, are not a common result. Reducing empty miles usually costs service, because tighter route pairing removes the slack that absorbs a late inbound. He got both because he rebuilt the city route plan from the ground up rather than trimming the old one.

I want to be direct about the context of this review. Emmanuel applied for the Operations Manager position posted in April. That requisition was frozen by corporate on July 8, 2026, along with all supervisor and manager promotions company-wide. He has told me he holds a written offer from a competitor in New Braunfels. Nothing in this document is written to persuade him or to manage the situation. It is written as the accurate record of what he did this year, which is what he is entitled to whether he stays or leaves.

## 1.2 Performance Against FY26 Goals

**Goal 1 — Maintain city on-time service at or above 95.0 percent. Result: 96.4 percent. Exceeded.**

He held this through a year in which the average tenure of a city driver on his board fell to 14 months. Monthly service never dropped below 94.1 percent (January, the freeze week) and hit 97.8 percent in May. The January result deserves specific credit: during the February 4–6 ice event he pre-staged nine drivers at the terminal the night of the 3rd, cut the delivery plan to hospital, grocery, and pharmaceutical consignees, and communicated the reduced plan to 61 accounts before 7:00 a.m. We took eleven service failures that week. Terminal 09 in Austin, with a comparable footprint, took forty-three.

**Goal 2 — Reduce empty city miles by 8 percent. Result: 11 percent. Exceeded.**

The route rebuild he completed between August and November 2025 consolidated 34 routes into 29 base routes with five flex assignments, re-cut the Northeast and Southwest zones along the 410/1604 corridors instead of by ZIP block, and paired 71 percent of afternoon pickups to a delivery route ending within six miles. Annualized fuel and hours savings validated by Finance on May 12: $214,000. This is the single largest cost-side improvement produced by any supervisor at this terminal this year.

**Goal 3 — Reduce dispatch-attributable driver turnover. Result: not met, partially outside his control.**

Terminal turnover was 68 percent. Exit interview coding attributes 31 percent of separations to pay, 24 percent to home time and schedule, 19 percent to equipment condition, and 11 percent to supervision or dispatch treatment. That 11 percent figure is down from 18 percent in FY25, which is the part he owns and improved. The rest is a wage and equipment problem that sits with me and with corporate, not with him. I am not rating him down for it, but the goal as written was not achieved.

**Goal 4 — Support closure of hours-of-service findings from the September 2025 compliance review. Result: met.**

The compliance review cited HOS records among the deficiencies underlying the conditional rating, specifically unassigned drive time in the ELD system and form-and-manner errors on driver-submitted corrections. Dispatch is not the record owner, but dispatch assignment practice is where unassigned segments originate. Emmanuel built the daily unassigned-time reconciliation on October 6, 2025 and has run it every operating day since. Unassigned drive time fell from 4.3 percent of city segments in September to 0.6 percent in June. He also stopped the practice — which predated him and which I had not caught — of dispatchers verbally authorizing a driver to "finish the last two stops" against remaining hours. Three dispatchers were coached on this in November; one, Aaron Vestal, received a documented verbal warning in December after a repeat.

## 1.3 Customer Escalation — Vaquero Home & Ranch

Vaquero Home & Ranch, 90 stores, $3.8 million in annual revenue, placed this terminal on 90-day notice on March 19, 2026, citing 12 percent late deliveries against their measurement. The gap between their 12 percent and our 96.4 percent is real and Emmanuel identified it before I did. Our number measures service-standard days. Theirs measures arrival inside a 90-minute store receiving appointment scanned at the dock door. A shipment can deliver on the standard day at 3:40 p.m. against a 1:00–2:30 p.m. window and count as on-time for us and late for them.

Emmanuel's root-cause work, delivered April 2, found that 64 percent of Vaquero's window failures came from three causes: dock load sequence putting Vaquero freight behind general commodity on the same trailer, Rio Grande Valley store deliveries running off a linehaul arrival that averaged 47 minutes late, and appointment times in our system that had not been updated after Vaquero changed receiving hours at 31 stores in August 2025. He fixed the third himself in eleven days, negotiated the second with Linehaul, and coordinated the first with Jamal Whitfield on the dock. Vaquero's measured late rate for June was 4.1 percent. Their transportation manager, Denise Karcher, wrote on July 6: *"The last two months look like a different carrier. Whoever rebuilt the appointment table should be told it mattered."* The notice has not yet been lifted; the decision is expected in late August.

## 1.4 Feedback from Drivers, Peers, and Customers

Driver feedback was collected in the May engagement survey (28 of 34 city drivers responding) and in exit interviews. Dispatch fairness scored 4.1 of 5, the highest supervisory score at this terminal. Two recurring criticisms: drivers on the flex assignments report they learn their route later than they'd like, and three drivers named the weekend Saturday rotation as opaque. Ruben Sifuentes raised the Saturday issue with me directly in February; Emmanuel published a posted 8-week rotation on March 2 and the complaint has not recurred.

Marisela Quintanilla wrote in peer feedback: *"Emmanuel is the only supervisor who calls me before he does something that touches a driver's file, not after."* Jamal Whitfield wrote that the joint dock-dispatch load sequence meeting Emmanuel started in April is the reason his outbound cut times improved. Two dispatchers described his coaching as direct and occasionally blunt; one used the word "impatient." I have observed this. Under pressure he answers the question and moves on, and newer employees sometimes need the reasoning.

## 2.1 Summary of the Year

This is the most difficult review I have written. Marisela has cut this terminal's preventable accident rate from 3.1 to 1.9 per million miles over four years, a 39 percent reduction that has kept drivers and members of the public from being hurt, and I do not want anything that follows to obscure it. She also owns the driver qualification files, and 22 of 31 files sampled in the September 2025 federal compliance review were incomplete. That finding, together with the hours-of-service records finding, produced a conditional safety rating for a company with 640 employees and eleven terminals. It is the most serious operational event at this terminal in a decade, and the record custodianship failure is in her area of responsibility.

Both things are true. The review has to hold both.

## 2.2 The Compliance Review Findings

The onsite review ran September 15–19, 2025. Investigator sampled 31 driver qualification files. Findings by category:

- 14 files missing or expired annual motor vehicle record reviews
- 9 files with no documented inquiry to previous employers within 30 days of hire
- 7 files missing the annual list of violations certification
- 4 files with medical examiner certificates expired between 11 and 94 days
- 2 files with no road test certificate or equivalent on record

Twenty-two of the thirty-one files carried at least one deficiency. Four carried three or more. The two files with expired medical certificates beyond 60 days represent drivers who operated in that condition, which is the most serious item in the set.

Marisela self-reported eleven of these deficiencies to me on September 8, a week before the investigator arrived, in a memo requesting temporary clerical help to work the backlog. I did not act on that memo before the review. That is my failure and it belongs in this document. It does not eliminate her accountability for a filing system she had owned for eleven years and had not raised as at-risk before September, but it materially changes how I weigh it.

The corrective action plan was submitted to the agency October 24, 2025 and accepted November 12. As of the June 30 audit, 100 percent of active driver qualification files are complete and a monthly self-audit of 15 randomly selected files has been run without a miss since January. She did that work, largely alone, largely on her own time. The upgrade request to restore a satisfactory rating is filed and pending.

## 2.3 Performance Against FY26 Goals

**Goal 1 — Reduce preventable accident rate to 2.2 per million miles or below. Result: 1.9. Exceeded.**

Twelve preventable accidents on 6.3 million miles. Down from 15 the prior year on higher mileage. Her behind-the-wheel remedial program for drivers with a first preventable — four hours with a certified trainer within ten days — has a repeat rate of 6 percent against a company average of 21 percent. Corporate Safety Director Roy Teichmann cited the program in the April network safety call as the model he wants replicated at the other ten terminals.

**Goal 2 — Zero deficiencies on driver qualification and hours-of-service records. Result: failed.**

Addressed above.

**Goal 3 — Reduce new-hire road test wait to 3 days or less. Result: failed. Current wait: 9 days.**

This is the operational cost of the single-point-of-failure problem. She is the only certified road test examiner at this terminal and the only person trained on the compliance recordkeeping system. In a year of 68 percent turnover, nine days between offer acceptance and road test is a hiring leak. Recruiting reports six candidates lost to competitors during that window in FY26, four of them to carriers that road-test same-week. At a conservative $4,200 cost per city driver hire, that is roughly $25,000 of waste, and it made Emmanuel's staffing problem worse than it needed to be.

**Goal 4 — Cross-train a backup on the compliance system. Result: failed. Not started.**

She remains the only trained person. She took four days of paid time off in the entire fiscal year and carried 137 hours into FY27, above the 120-hour cap, requiring an exception I approved on June 30. When she was out with influenza February 9–11, driver onboarding stopped for three days.

## 3.1 Summary of the Year

Jamal took the dock supervisor role nine months ago under circumstances no one would choose. His predecessor was terminated on October 2, 2025 for falsifying a manifest. The shift he inherited had been running under a supervisor whose numbers were partly fiction, a crew that knew it and had adjusted to it, and an outside audit of six months of shipping documents running in the background. He was promoted from within the same crew, which meant supervising people who had been his peers the previous Friday.

Nine months later the shift moves 19 bills per hour against 14 at handover, a 36 percent productivity gain on a dock handling its share of 2,400 shipments a day. That is the headline and it is real. The rest of the review is about the cost at which he got it and whether that cost is a first-year artifact or a pattern.

## 3.2 Performance Against Goals

Goals were set November 14, 2025, six weeks into the role, and cover the partial year.

**Goal 1 — Raise outbound dock productivity from 14 to 16 bills per hour. Result: 19. Substantially exceeded.**

Monthly progression: October 14.2, December 16.1, March 18.4, June 19.0. He got there through three changes — a fixed door assignment by destination terminal instead of first-available, a pre-shift ten-minute stand-up with the load plan posted, and moving the two fastest forklift operators off general freight and onto the two highest-volume outbound doors. None of these are exotic. What is notable is that he made all three inside four months while learning the job.

**Goal 2 — Hold cargo damage claims at or below the FY25 baseline. Result: failed in Q1, recovered by Q4.**

Damage claims on his shift rose 40 percent in his first quarter, October through December, against the prior-year quarter. Dollar value $61,400 against $43,900. The largest single claim was $18,700 on a shipment of glass shower enclosures on November 21, crushed by a stack-height violation on door 14.

He asked me not to soften this in the review and I won't: he pushed the crew for speed before he had put the handling standards in place, and freight got broken. He identified the cause himself in the January damage review, implemented posted stack-height and freight-class placards at every door, restarted the load-securement refresher with Marisela's help in December, and added a two-minute door walk before every trailer close. Claims by quarter: Q1 $61,400, Q2 $47,200, Q3 $39,800, Q4 $36,100. The June figure is below the FY25 monthly average. The recovery is more informative than the spike, but the spike happened and it cost the company approximately $17,500 net of the baseline.

**Goal 3 — Complete the manifest documentation reset following the predecessor's termination. Result: met.**

Every outbound manifest on his shift since October 6 has been signed by a second checker. The internal audit closed on February 20 with no findings after the handover date. Corporate Audit's memo noted the double-check practice as a control worth adopting network-wide.

**Goal 4 — Support Vaquero Home & Ranch load-sequence correction. Result: met.**

Jamal and Emmanuel established a joint dock-dispatch load sequence review in April. Vaquero freight now loads last-on for first-off at store level, which was the single largest dock contributor to the 12 percent late-delivery rate. Vaquero window compliance improved from 88 percent in March to 95.9 percent in June. Jamal's share of that is direct.

## 3.3 The Overtime Complaints

On February 11, 2026, two dock workers — Curtis Bonham, 19 years of service, and Ernesto Villareal, 14 years — filed a joint complaint with Priya Raghunathan, Director of Human Resources, alleging that Jamal assigned overtime to junior employees ahead of senior employees.

HR's investigation, closed March 6, found no violation of policy or of the seniority provisions in the employee handbook, because overtime assignment on the dock is discretionary and always has been. It did find that Jamal had no documented method, assigned overtime verbally at the end of shift, and had in fact given a disproportionate share to four employees — all of them, on the data, the four fastest on the productivity report. He was optimizing the shift and had not considered how it looked, or that two men with a combined 33 years on that dock would read it as a message about their standing.

Jamal's response was the strongest thing he did this year. He did not dispute the finding. He met with Bonham and Villareal individually on March 9, posted a rotating overtime list effective March 16, and now publishes the coming week's overtime by Thursday. Bonham told me unprompted on June 3: *"He fixed it. I'll give him that. He listened, which the last one never did."* Villareal remains cooler and told me he considers the matter closed but has not changed his view that the promotion should have gone to a longer-tenured employee. I consider the complaint resolved and it carries no disciplinary record.
