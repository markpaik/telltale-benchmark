## Abstract

Across the Institute's 140 member foundries and approximately 21,400 hourly workers in eight states, 38 percent of new hires on the melt and molding lines separate within twelve months. The average fully loaded cost of a separation is $11,800, and the average time to fill an entry-level production vacancy is 14 weeks. Thirty-four percent of the member hourly workforce is over 55. These are no longer human resources statistics; they are operating statistics. Plants in the top turnover quartile of the Institute's census run scrap at 9.4 percent against 5.1 percent in the bottom quartile and carry a recordable injury rate 60 percent higher. Two large agricultural equipment customers have begun conditioning long-term supply agreements on documented staffing commitments, converting a labor problem into a revenue problem.

This paper presents the Institute's 2019–2025 turnover census, compares the five pipeline approaches members have deployed, and builds a three-year cost model that a single plant can run against its own numbers. The central finding is that no single approach solves the problem and that members who have adopted only one have generally chosen the weakest one. The recommended architecture is layered: a structured ninety-day onboarding as the universal floor, registered apprenticeship as the spine for melt, molding, and maintenance progression, youth apprenticeship and community college cohorts as the top of the funnel, and temp-to-hire narrowed sharply from a hiring channel to a surge-capacity tool. The paper closes with a twenty-four-month implementation sequence, a metrics dashboard, and direct responses to the four objections members raise most consistently: poaching, the burden on plants under 75 employees, seniority language in collective bargaining agreements, and supervisors who will not release trainees from production.

The federal apprenticeship expansion grant window closes November 14, 2026. A plant that has not begun standards development by the second quarter of 2026 will not be positioned to apply.

## 1. The Problem Is No Longer a Labor Problem

For most of the last two decades, member plants treated hourly turnover as an irritant with a known workaround: hire more, hire faster, accept that some fraction will not stay. That workaround depended on two conditions. The first was a reserve of experienced tradespeople who could absorb the instability — melt deck leads and molding line operators with fifteen or twenty years who kept the process inside specification while the crew churned around them. The second was slack in the regional labor market that made the next applicant cheap and quick to find.

Both conditions have failed at once. Thirty-four percent of the member hourly workforce is over 55, which means the absorbers are leaving. Average time to fill has moved from 6 weeks in the Institute's 2019 baseline to 14 weeks in the 2025 collection, which means the next applicant is neither cheap nor quick. The consequence is that turnover is no longer buffered. It transmits directly into scrap, into injury rates, into missed ship dates, and now into customer contracts.

This is the shift members should internalize. A 38 percent first-year separation rate is survivable when a plant has depth. It is not survivable when the depth is retiring. The plants in our census that are performing worst on quality and safety are not the plants with the oldest equipment or the tightest margins. They are the plants where the ratio of tenured to untenured operators on the molding line has crossed a threshold, and the crew no longer contains enough people who know what a bad mold looks like before it gets poured.

Two agricultural equipment OEMs have now made this explicit. Both have added workforce continuity language to their supplier qualification packages, requiring documented staffing plans, retention data, and in one case a named training program with a defined completion rate as a condition of multi-year supply agreements. Members who have historically viewed workforce development as a cost center and a discretionary one are being told by their largest customers that it is a qualification requirement. That is a change in kind, not degree.

## 2. What the Census Shows

The Institute has collected standardized turnover, cost, and quality data from member foundries annually since 2019. Participation in the 2025 collection was 118 of 140 members, covering approximately 18,600 of the 21,400 hourly workers in the membership. Cost-per-separation figures are computed on a common definition that includes requisition and recruiting cost, pre-employment screening, orientation and initial training hours, supervisor and mentor time, productivity ramp loss, overtime backfill, and separation processing. It does not include scrap or rework attributable to inexperienced operators, which is treated separately below.

**Table 1. Great Lakes Metalcasting Institute turnover census, selected indicators, 2019 and 2025**

| Indicator | 2019 | 2025 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| First-year separation, melt and molding lines | 29% | 38% | +9 pts |
| First-year separation, all hourly | 24% | 31% | +7 pts |
| Average cost per separation (fully loaded) | $8,200 | $11,800 | +44% |
| Average time to fill, entry-level production | 6 wks | 14 wks | +8 wks |
| Hourly workforce over 55 | 27% | 34% | +7 pts |
| Hourly workforce under 30 | 19% | 15% | −4 pts |
| Members reporting refused or deferred orders due to staffing | 11% | 38% | +27 pts |
| Median overtime hours per hourly FTE per year | 214 | 341 | +59% |

*Source: GLMI Turnover Census, 2019 and 2025 collections. 2025 n=118 members.*

Two features of Table 1 deserve emphasis. First, the deterioration is concentrated on the melt and molding lines rather than distributed evenly across the plant. Core room, finishing, and shipping have degraded, but not at the same rate. The lines with the hottest, dirtiest, most physically demanding work and the steepest learning curve are the lines losing people. Second, the 59 percent increase in overtime per FTE indicates that plants are covering the gap with the incumbent workforce. This is a mechanism by which turnover becomes self-reinforcing: understaffing drives overtime, sustained overtime drives fatigue and attrition among the tenured operators the plant can least afford to lose.

### 2.1 The quartile spread

The census permits ranking members by first-year separation rate on the melt and molding lines. The spread between the top and bottom quartiles is the most operationally useful finding the Institute has produced.

| Measure | Q1 (lowest turnover) | Q2 | Q3 | Q4 (highest turnover) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First-year separation rate | 19% | 31% | 42% | 58% |
| Scrap rate | 5.1% | 6.4% | 7.9% | 9.4% |
| Recordable injury rate (index, Q1=100) | 100 | 118 | 141 | 160 |

*Source: GLMI Turnover Census, 2025 collection. Quartiles assigned on first-year separation rate for melt and molding classifications only. Injury rate indexed to Q1 to permit comparison across differing exposure bases.*

The scrap differential is worth quantifying. For a plant shipping 11,500 tons of good castings annually, a 4.3-percentage-point scrap gap between Q4 and Q1 performance represents, at typical member conversion economics, roughly $118,000 per scrap point in melt energy, labor, binder, sand, and yield loss — approximately $507,000 per year. The injury differential compounds it through experience modification factors on workers' compensation premiums, and through the direct and indirect costs of each recordable.

We are not claiming that turnover causes all of this. Plants with high turnover often have other problems — older equipment, tighter capital, weaker supervision — and causality runs in both directions, since a bad plant to work in is both a cause and a consequence of churn. But the association is strong, it has been stable across six annual collections, and it survives controls for plant size, casting class, and metal type. The operational literature on collective turnover reports the same pattern in comparable process environments (Shaw, Gupta and Delery 2005; Hausknecht and Trevor 2011). The practical reading for a plant manager is straightforward: if you are in Q4, your quality and safety numbers are not independent of your staffing numbers, and quality initiatives that do not address staffing will underperform.

Thirty-four percent over 55 means that on a five-year horizon, member plants face the loss of roughly 7,300 hourly workers to retirement alone, before any voluntary or involuntary separation. Against a current under-30 cohort of 15 percent — approximately 3,200 workers, of whom the census indicates 38 percent will leave within their first year — the arithmetic does not close. The retirement cohort is also disproportionately concentrated in exactly the roles that require the longest development: melt deck lead, pouring, mold line setup, pattern and tooling, and maintenance. The Institute's classification-level data show 41 percent of melt deck leads and 47 percent of journey-level maintenance workers over 55. These are not roles a plant fills from an application. They are roles a plant grows, typically over three to five years, from operators who were themselves developed over two.

This is the single most actionable finding in the census. Fifty-eight percent of first-year separations occur within the first ninety days. The hazard is front-loaded, steep, and concentrated in weeks 2 through 10 — the period when a new hire has exhausted the novelty, absorbed the physical reality of the environment, has not yet built social attachment to a crew, and is producing at a rate that draws negative attention from a supervisor under schedule pressure.

Interventions targeted at this window have leverage that interventions targeted anywhere else do not. A retention program aimed at year three is aimed at people who have already survived the filter. The corresponding safety implication is well established in the occupational health literature: injury risk is sharply elevated in the first month of employment across manual occupations (Breslin and Smith 2006), and the Institute's data show member new hires carrying roughly 2.9 times the recordable rate of workers with more than one year of tenure in the same classification.

## 3. Five Approaches, Compared

Members have deployed five distinct pipeline approaches. All five are represented in the census in sufficient numbers to permit outcome comparison. The comparison below is descriptive, not experimental; plants that adopt registered apprenticeship differ systematically from plants that do not, and some portion of the retention advantage reflects selection rather than treatment. We flag where we believe this matters most.

### 3.1 Registered apprenticeship

Formal apprenticeship under 29 CFR Part 29, typically 6,000 hours of on-the-job learning over three years plus 432 to 576 hours of related technical instruction, ending in a nationally portable journeyworker credential. Member programs exist in molding technician, melt technician, industrial maintenance, and pattern/tooling.

Cost is approximately $27,000 per apprentice over three years, against roughly $9,400 in available offsets — state apprenticeship tax credits, federal grant support, employer tuition reimbursement of related technical instruction through partner colleges, and in some states a wage subsidy in year one. Net employer cost is therefore about $17,600 per apprentice, or $5,900 per year.

Twelve-month retention among member apprentices is 91 percent; thirty-six-month retention is 78 percent. The comparable national evidence is favorable: the Mathematica assessment for the Department of Labor found substantial earnings and retention gains associated with registered apprenticeship participation (Reed et al. 2012), and the Case Western business-perspective study found positive net returns to employers in most sponsoring firms studied, with returns strongest where the apprentice replaced an external hire in a high-vacancy-cost role (Helper et al. 2016). Both caveats apply here: apprentices are selected, and selection accounts for some of the gap.

### 3.2 High school youth apprenticeship and co-op

Junior- and senior-year students working 10 to 16 hours weekly during the school year and full time in summer, with articulated coursework. Wisconsin, Michigan, and Indiana members have the deepest programs, generally through state youth apprenticeship frameworks.

Cost runs approximately $6,800 per student-year against roughly $2,200 in offsets, net $4,600. The conversion rate to full-time hire at graduation is 61 percent across member programs. Of those who convert, twelve-month retention post-hire is 84 percent — the highest of any channel, because these hires have already self-selected through eighteen months of exposure to the actual work environment.

The limitation is volume and lag. A youth program producing four converts a year does not solve a forty-hire annual requirement, and it does not produce anything for two years after launch. It is a long-cycle investment that pays in the second half of the decade.

### 3.3 Community college cohort partnerships

Contracted cohorts through regional technical colleges — typically an 8 to 16 week certificate in foundry fundamentals, industrial safety, blueprint reading, and basic maintenance, with the employer committing to interview or hire graduates. Some members co-fund; some rely on state incumbent-worker or job-training grants.

Cost is approximately $4,900 per participant to the employer where the plant co-funds, less where state grants cover instruction. Twelve-month retention among hires sourced this way is 79 percent. The channel's strength is volume — a cohort of 15 to 20 can be run twice a year — and its weakness is that graduates are not obligated to any single plant, and in tight markets colleges will place them wherever wages are highest.

### 3.4 Structured ninety-day in-house onboarding

A defined, documented ninety-day progression: assigned mentor with a wage premium, written skill checklist with weekly sign-off, protected training hours, reduced production expectation on a published ramp, scheduled check-ins at days 3, 14, 30, 60, and 90, and a completion milestone tied to a wage step.

Cost is approximately $3,100 per hire — mentor premium, mentor training amortized, reduced output during ramp, supervisor and HR time. Offsets are minimal. Twelve-month retention among members with a fully implemented program is 74 percent, against 62 percent baseline. That is a 12-point improvement for $3,100, and it is available to every member regardless of size, union status, or state.

This is the highest-return intervention in the census on a cost-per-retention-point basis, and it is the one fewest members have implemented well. Forty-four percent of members report "having" a structured onboarding program; when the Institute audited against the six elements above, 17 percent had all six.

### 3.5 Temp-to-hire conversion through staffing agencies

Contract labor through a regional staffing agency with conversion to direct hire at 90 or 120 days. Agency markup runs 42 to 55 percent of base wage; conversion fees where charged run $2,500 to $6,000.

This is the most widely used channel — 71 percent of members use it for at least some entry-level production hiring — and the worst performing. Twelve-month retention among converted hires is 49 percent. Effective cost per successful conversion, accounting for markup during the temp period, conversion fees, and the cost of temps who never convert, is approximately $14,200. It is also the channel most associated with elevated new-worker injury rates, since agency workers frequently receive abbreviated orientation and are placed on shifts with the least supervisory coverage.

Temp-to-hire is not without a legitimate role. It is genuinely useful for absorbing volume surges, for covering seasonal peaks, and for plants that cannot forecast beyond a quarter. It is a disaster as a primary hiring channel, and the census shows that the plants most dependent on it are concentrated in Q4.

### 3.7 The reading

Table 3 does not identify a winner, and members looking for one are asking the wrong question. The approaches differ on three independent dimensions — speed, volume, and durability — and no approach is strong on all three. Temp-to-hire is fast and unlimited and does not last. Registered apprenticeship lasts and does not scale or arrive quickly. Community college cohorts scale and arrive reasonably quickly and produce middling durability. Youth apprenticeship produces the best-fitting people on the longest lag. Structured onboarding does not source anyone at all; it improves the yield of whatever channel is feeding it.

That last point is the architectural key. Onboarding is not a fifth alternative to the other four. It is a multiplier applied to all of them. A plant that sources through a community college cohort and puts graduates through a structured ninety-day program does better than a plant that does either alone. A plant that runs registered apprenticeship without structured onboarding loses apprentices in the first quarter of year one, before any of the $27,000 has produced a return.

The recommended architecture follows:

- **Floor:** structured ninety-day onboarding, applied to every hourly hire without exception, including agency conversions.
- **Spine:** registered apprenticeship for melt technician, molding technician, and industrial maintenance, sized to replace the retiring skilled cohort — typically 4 to 8 apprentices in process at any time for a 150–200 hourly plant.
- **Funnel:** community college cohorts for entry-level volume; youth apprenticeship for long-horizon supply and for building a local reputation as a place young people can start a career.
- **Valve:** temp-to-hire restricted to defined surge conditions, with a written policy specifying what triggers its use and a cap on the percentage of the production workforce it may represent.

## 5. Implementation Sequence

The most common failure mode among members who have attempted this is launching the registered apprenticeship first. It is the most visible component, it attracts grant money, and it is the one the board will approve. It is also the slowest to produce anything, the most administratively demanding, and the most likely to fail if the underlying onboarding and supervisory infrastructure is not in place. Apprentices lost in month four are the most expensive turnover a plant can generate.

The sequence below front-loads the cheap, fast, high-leverage work and gates each subsequent phase on demonstrated performance in the prior one.

**Phase 0 — Baseline (weeks 1–6).** Compute cost per separation on the Institute's standard definition. Pull the tenure-week distribution of the last three years of separations. Identify which supervisors and which shifts account for disproportionate first-90-day loss; in the median member plant, two supervisors account for more than 40 percent of it. Establish the P&L line where turnover cost will be reported going forward. This phase costs almost nothing and is skipped by nearly everyone.

**Phase 1 — Structured onboarding (months 2–6).** Build the six elements: mentor assignment with premium, written weekly skill checklist, protected training hours, published production ramp, check-ins at days 3/14/30/60/90, wage step at completion. Select mentors on demonstrated instruction ability, not seniority; train them for eight hours. Apply the program to every hire including agency conversions from day one of their assignment. *Gate at month 6: 90-day retention improved by at least 6 points against baseline.*

**Phase 2 — Supervisory infrastructure (months 4–10).** This phase exists to solve the release problem discussed in Section 6.4 and is the phase most often omitted. Establish a floating relief position or relief pool sufficient to cover trainee absence from the line. Add training-hours-delivered to the supervisor scorecard with equal weight to schedule attainment. Report unreleased training hours weekly to the plant manager. *Gate at month 10: scheduled training hours delivered ≥ 85 percent.*

**Phase 3 — Registered apprenticeship (months 8–16).** Develop standards with the state apprenticeship agency or through the Institute's group sponsorship (Section 6.2). Negotiate selection and progression language with the bargaining unit where applicable. Identify the related technical instruction provider. Register standards. Select the first cohort of 4 to 6 from incumbent workers who have completed the ninety-day program and demonstrated aptitude — do not select from external hires in the first cohort. **Members intending to apply for the federal apprenticeship expansion grant must have standards in development by Q2 2026; the window closes November 14, 2026.** *Gate at month 16: first cohort registered, RTI provider under contract, 100 percent of cohort past month 6.*

**Phase 4 — External pipeline (months 12–24).** Contract the community college cohort partnership. Sign youth apprenticeship agreements with two to three feeder high schools. Both require lead time measured in school-year cycles; starting the conversation in month 12 produces a first cohort in month 20 to 24. *Gate at month 22: first CC cohort placed, ≥ 70 percent at 90 days.*

**Phase 5 — Agency discipline (months 16–24).** With the internal pipeline producing, write and enforce the surge-only policy: defined triggers, a cap on agency headcount as a percentage of production workforce, mandatory application of the ninety-day program to all agency workers regardless of conversion intent, and a standing conversion review at 60 days rather than 90.

## 6. The Objections

Members raise four objections with consistency across states, plant sizes, and union status. All four are legitimate. None is disqualifying.

### 6.1 "We'll train them and the plant across town will take them."

This is the most frequently voiced objection and the one with the most evidence behind it. In the Institute's 2025 collection, 26 percent of separations at the 12-to-30-month tenure mark were to another metalcasting or heavy manufacturing employer within 40 miles. Poaching is real.

Three responses.

First, the asymmetry runs the other way from how members perceive it. A plant that does not train is not neutral in the poaching game; it is the prey. Q4 plants in our census lose more workers to nearby employers in absolute and percentage terms than Q1 plants do, because their workers are less attached, less well paid relative to skill, and more actively looking. The choice is not between training-and-losing and not-training-and-keeping. It is between training and losing some, or not training and losing more.

Second, the mechanism that reduces poaching loss is wage progression transparency, not secrecy about training. Members who publish a skill-linked wage ladder — where completing the ninety-day program, each apprenticeship year, and journeyworker certification carries a defined and known increase — report 30-month retention 14 points higher than members with discretionary progression. Workers leave for a known offer when their own future is unknown.

Third, and this needs stating plainly: **the Institute will not facilitate, and members should not enter, any agreement among employers not to recruit or hire one another's workers.** No-poach and wage-fixing agreements among competing employers are treated as criminal antitrust violations under the joint DOJ/FTC guidance for human resources professionals (DOJ/FTC 2016), and metalcasting is not exempt. What members may lawfully do, and what the Institute will support, is cost-sharing on training infrastructure that no single plant can afford, shared related technical instruction, common credential standards, and reasonable, individually negotiated training repayment agreements where a plant funds a specific, portable credential — provided such agreements are proportionate, time-limited, and reviewed by counsel in the applicable state, several of which restrict them.

### 6.2 "This is built for plants with 200 employees. We have 62."

Fifty-one of the Institute's 140 members have fewer than 75 hourly employees. For those plants, the objection is not about willingness; it is about fixed cost. A registered apprenticeship program carries roughly $22,000 in one-time standards development and $46,000 per year in coordination, against a plant that may hire six people annually. The per-apprentice administrative load is prohibitive.

The answer is group sponsorship. Under 29 CFR Part 29, an intermediary may serve as the registered sponsor holding the standards, with participating employers signing on as training sites. The Institute is prepared to act as group sponsor for member foundries in all eight states, which changes the economics substantially:

**Table 8. Registered apprenticeship cost per apprentice, standalone vs. group sponsorship, plant with 62 hourly employees**

| Cost element | Standalone | Group sponsored |
|---|---|---|
| Standards development, amortized | $3,700 | $310 |
| Program administration, per apprentice-year | $4,100 | $900 |
| RTI cost per apprentice-year | $3,200 | $2,100 |
| Mentor training | $700 | $250 |
| OJT wage cost differential | $5,900 | $5,900 |
| **Total per apprentice-year** | **$17,600** | **$9,460** |
| Less offsets | ($3,133) | ($3,133) |
| **Net per apprentice-year** | **$14,467** | **$6,327** |

*Source: GLMI Program Cost Survey, 2025; group figures modeled on comparable intermediary-sponsored programs in Wisconsin and Ohio.*

Group sponsorship also solves the related technical instruction volume problem. A plant with two apprentices cannot fill a college section; twelve plants with two apprentices each can. The Institute's proposed structure would run regional RTI cohorts at four community college partners with rotating start dates.

The same logic applies to the community college cohort, which small plants should approach as a consortium — three to five plants co-funding a single cohort with proportional hiring commitments. Small plants can and should implement the ninety-day onboarding program unilaterally; nothing about it requires scale.

### 6.3 "Seniority language in our contract won't allow it."

This objection is usually more tractable than members assume, in part because it is often raised without a specific reading of the contract. The Institute reviewed 31 member collective bargaining agreements in 2025. Twenty-four contained no language that would prevent an apprenticeship program. Five contained language requiring that any new classification be bargained. Two contained job-bidding language that would place apprentice openings into the normal seniority bid sequence.

Where the issue is real, the building trades model resolves it and has for seventy years. The elements:

- **A joint apprenticeship and training committee** with equal employer and union appointment, holding authority over selection, progression, and discipline within the program. This is the mechanism that converts apprenticeship from a management program the union tolerates into a joint program the union owns.
- **Negotiated apprentice-to-journeyworker ratios**, which address the union's legitimate concern that apprentices will be used as cheap labor to displace full-rate positions.
- **A transparent, weighted selection matrix** — attendance, safety record, demonstrated aptitude, seniority as one factor among several — rather than either pure seniority or pure management discretion.
- **Apprentice seniority accrual during the program**, with placement into the regular seniority roster on completion. Apprentices should not be asked to forfeit seniority, and asking them to is the fastest way to lose the union.
- **Apprentice classification outside the normal bid sequence** for entry, with an agreed cap on the number of apprentice positions.

Bargaining units in this sector generally do not oppose apprenticeship. They oppose programs that create a second tier, that displace bid opportunities for incumbents, or that they had no part in designing. Members who have brought the local into program design at the standards-development stage rather than presenting a finished program report ratification without significant friction. Members who have not have generally deserved the resistance they got.

### 6.4 "My supervisors won't release people from the line."

This is the objection that kills programs, and it is the only one of the four that is entirely within the plant's control. It is also the one most often treated as a personnel problem when it is a design problem.

A production supervisor is measured on schedule attainment, scrap, and safety, in that order in most member plants. Releasing a trainee for four hours of structured instruction costs that supervisor measured performance today in exchange for benefits that accrue to the plant in eighteen months and to a different supervisor if the trainee moves. The supervisor is responding correctly to the incentives in place. Exhorting them to care more about training will not work, has not worked, and should be abandoned as a strategy.

Four design changes:

**Measure release.** Track scheduled training hours versus delivered training hours by supervisor, weekly, and report it to the plant manager alongside schedule attainment. What is unmeasured is unreleased. Members who instituted this measurement alone report delivered-hours rates moving from a median of 51 percent to 79 percent within two quarters, with no other change.

**Weight it.** Put training hours delivered on the supervisor scorecard at no less than 15 percent, and tie a component of supervisory incentive compensation to it. If a plant is unwilling to do this, it should not start the program; it will spend the money and lose the trainees anyway.

**Staff the relief.** The honest core of this objection is that a plant running at 94 percent staffing cannot release anyone without missing the schedule. That is true, and it is why Phase 2 of the sequence funds a floating relief position or relief pool before the apprenticeship launches. One relief operator per shift on the molding line at roughly $62,000 loaded is the enabling cost of the entire program, and it is cheaper than the four apprentices who will otherwise wash out.

**Move instruction off the constraint.** Schedule related technical instruction and classroom components off-shift with pay, or on the shift with the most slack. A plant with a bottleneck on second-shift molding should not be scheduling training against second-shift molding.

## 7. What the Institute Will Do

The recommendations above ask a great deal of individual plants. Several components are more efficiently delivered collectively, and the Institute commits to the following:

1. **Group sponsorship of registered apprenticeship** in all eight member states, with standards for melt technician, molding technician, and industrial maintenance available to members at the cost structure modeled in Table 8. Standards development begins Q1 2026.
2. **Grant application support** for the federal apprenticeship expansion program, including a shared application framework and technical assistance for members applying individually or through the group sponsorship. **Members should notify the Institute of intent to participate no later than June 30, 2026** to permit a coordinated filing before the November 14, 2026 close.
3. **Regional related technical instruction cohorts** at four community college partners, permitting small-plant participation.
4. **A published, standard ninety-day onboarding toolkit** — mentor curriculum, skill checklists by classification, ramp schedules, and check-in protocols — available to all members at no charge in Q1 2026.
5. **Model CBA language** for apprenticeship selection, ratios, and seniority treatment, developed with input from member bargaining units.
6. **Annual benchmarking** of member pipeline performance against the census, permitting each plant to locate itself in the quartile structure of Table 2.

The Institute will not, under any circumstances, facilitate agreements among members regarding recruitment, hiring, or compensation of workers.

## 8. Conclusion

The metalcasting workforce problem in the Great Lakes region has passed from a nuisance to a constraint on production and, with the entry of customer staffing requirements into supply agreements, to a constraint on revenue. Thirty-eight percent first-year separation on the melt and molding lines, $11,800 per separation, 14 weeks to fill, and 34 percent of the hourly workforce over 55 do not describe a hiring difficulty. They describe a plant population that is consuming its remaining experience faster than it is replacing it, with visible consequences in a 4.3-point scrap spread and a 60 percent injury rate differential between the best and worst quartiles.

The evidence in this paper supports four conclusions.

First, the highest-return intervention available to any member, of any size, in any state, union or not, is a properly implemented structured ninety-day onboarding program. It costs approximately $3,100 per hire, it addresses the window in which 58 percent of first-year separations occur, and it multiplies the yield of every other channel. Seventeen percent of members have implemented it fully. That number should be near one hundred by the end of 2026.

Second, registered apprenticeship is the correct answer to the retirement of the skilled cohort and the wrong answer to next quarter's headcount. It should be built second, not first, on an onboarding and supervisory foundation that does not yet exist in most member plants. At $17,600 net per apprentice and 78 percent three-year retention, it is not expensive relative to the alternative of hiring journey-level maintenance from a market that does not have any.

Third, temp-to-hire at 49 percent twelve-month retention and $14,200 per effective conversion is the most expensive channel most members use and the one they use most. Reducing dependence on it is the fastest available cost reduction in the entire model — $301,900 over three years in the Plant M case, more than the entire cost of the apprenticeship component.

Fourth, the objections members raise are real and are answerable. Poaching is addressed by wage progression transparency and shared training infrastructure, not by agreements among employers, which are illegal. Small-plant burden is addressed by group sponsorship, which cuts net cost per apprentice-year from $14,467 to $6,327. Seniority language is addressed by joint apprenticeship committees and negotiated ratios, a model with seventy years of precedent. Supervisor release is addressed by measuring it, weighting it on the scorecard, and funding the relief position that makes it physically possible — and if a plant will not do those three things, it should not start.

The modeled plant reaches payback in month 26 and generates approximately $260,000 annually thereafter, before counting the value of contracts that now depend on demonstrating a staffing plan. The federal apprenticeship expansion grant window closes November 14, 2026, and members not in standards development by the second quarter of that year will not be positioned to apply. The window on the retirement cohort does not have a published closing date, but it is shorter than the three years it takes to produce a journeyworker.
