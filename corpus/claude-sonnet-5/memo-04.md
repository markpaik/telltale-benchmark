# City of Ashmont Falls
## Interoffice Memorandum Packet — Lead and Unknown Service Line Replacement Program

---

## Memorandum 1

**TO:** Faisal Chaudhry, Public Works Director; Nadia Petrosyan, Finance Director; Grace Muriuki, Water Superintendent; Members of the City Council
**FROM:** Yolanda Priestley, City Manager
**DATE:** December 8, 2026
**SUBJECT:** Launch of the Service Line Replacement Program — Scope, Schedule, and Assignments

The service line inventory that Public Works and the Water Division filed with the New York State Department of Health in October identified 6,140 lead or unknown-material service lines in the Ashmont Falls system, against the 2,800 lines that our 2023 capital plan assumed. I want to lay out for department heads and the Council what that finding means and how the city intends to respond over the next several months.

The gap between the planning estimate and the inventory result is not a matter of poor prior work; it reflects the number of lines for which historical tap cards and prior contractor records listed "unknown" material, which the federal Lead and Copper Rule Improvements require us to treat as lead until proven otherwise through excavation or approved verification methods. Under the rule, we have until 2037 to complete replacement of all lead and unknown lines system-wide, with interim service-line replacement rate requirements that begin in the first full program year. A 6,140-line obligation compressed into an eleven-year window is a materially different undertaking than the 2,800-line program the city budgeted for, and it changes both our financing needs and our construction pace.

I am directing the following program structure, effective immediately:

Public Works will serve as the lead department for design, contractor procurement, and field execution. Faisal, your office will develop annual construction packages sized to keep us at or ahead of the federal replacement-rate schedule, with the first package advertised for bid this winter. I want a package size in the range of 600 to 650 lines for Year 1, informed by what our two-person inspection crew can realistically verify and document, and by contractor capacity in this market.

Grace, the Water Division will handle notification logistics, coordination of water quality sampling before and after each replacement, and day-to-day liaison with residents on scheduling. I am asking you to build a tracking system now — before the first line goes in the ground — so that every property has a documented material determination, replacement date, and post-replacement sample result. The health department has separately asked that we prioritize the 411 households in the system with children under six, and I want that prioritization built into the sequencing from the start rather than retrofitted later.

Nadia, Finance will need to revisit the financing plan built around the 2,800-line estimate. We have an $18 million commitment from the Drinking Water State Revolving Fund, including $6.2 million in principal forgiveness, that was structured against the smaller number. I am not asking you to have final numbers before the bid opens — that would be premature — but I do want a memo to me and the Council within two weeks of bid opening laying out what the actual low bid means for the financing plan and the rate increase currently proposed at 11 percent over two years.

Faisal, I also need a policy recommendation on the private-side portion of each replacement — the section of pipe between the curb stop and the building, which our engineer's figures put at an average of $3,100 per property. State law permits municipalities discretion in how this cost is allocated, and given that roughly 29 percent of affected addresses are rental units, I want a recommendation that accounts for landlord-tenant dynamics, not just a straight billing mechanism. I would like this to the Council's public utilities committee no later than the first meeting in March.

On timing, my expectation is that the first construction package advertises in early February, opens for bid by month's end, and begins field work in April, weather permitting. I recognize that is an aggressive schedule given how recently the inventory came in, but the earlier we establish a repeatable annual package process, the more predictable this program becomes for residents, contractors, and the rate base alike.

I will be briefing the full Council on this memo's contents at the December 15 work session. Councilman DuBois has already indicated he intends to raise questions about the private-side cost allocation at that meeting; I would ask that Public Works and Finance be prepared to speak to the range of options under consideration, even though the final recommendation is not yet before them.

Please direct questions to my office. I would rather resolve ambiguities now than after the first package is under contract.

## Memorandum 2

**TO:** Yolanda Priestley, City Manager; City Council
**FROM:** Nadia Petrosyan, Finance Director
**DATE:** February 24, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Revised Rate and Financing Plan Following Bid Opening

This memo responds to the City Manager's December 8 request that Finance revisit the rate and financing plan once the first construction package's bid results were known. The bid opened on February 11. I want to walk through what came in, why it came in where it did, and what it means for the rate increase currently before the Council.

**Bid Results.** The city received four bids on the Package 1 contract, covering 620 service line replacements. The engineer's estimate, prepared last fall, assumed a unit cost of approximately $10,800 per line, for a total estimated construction cost of roughly $6.7 million. The low bid, from Halloran Underground Utilities, came in at $14,900 per line — 38 percent above the engineer's estimate — for a total bid of $9.24 million. The next two bids were higher still, at $16,100 and $17,650 per line, respectively; the fourth bidder's submission was non-responsive and was rejected on that basis. Public Works has reviewed the Halloran bid for completeness and recommends award, and I concur that re-bidding is unlikely to produce a materially better result given current conditions.

The cost increase is not unique to Ashmont Falls. Contractors and our engineer both point to the same drivers: statewide demand for directional-boring and open-trench crews has risen sharply as utilities across New York accelerate replacement programs under the same federal deadline, and copper and PVC material costs remain elevated. I do not believe this is a one-time anomaly correctable through better procurement; I believe it is the price environment we should plan around for at least the next two to three annual packages.

**Effect on Total Program Cost.** If the Package 1 unit price holds across the remaining roughly 5,520 lines in the inventory, the public-side construction cost of the full program rises from an originally budgeted figure in the neighborhood of $66 million to a figure closer to $91 million, before private-side costs, engineering, inspection, and contingency. I want to flag that this is a projection based on one data point and should be treated as the upper end of a range until we have results from a second or third package. But it is the figure I am using for planning purposes until we have reason to revise it downward.

**Financing Plan.** The $18 million Drinking Water State Revolving Fund loan, including $6.2 million in principal forgiveness, remains committed and unaffected by the bid result; it will fund Package 1 in full, with the balance applied toward Package 2 design and early construction costs. That leaves the loan sized appropriately for roughly the first two years of the program, not the program as originally scoped. We will need to return to the state SRF program for at least two, and likely three, additional funding rounds over the life of the program, and I am directing my office to begin that application process for Round 2 immediately rather than waiting for Package 1 to close out.

**Rate Recommendation.** The 11 percent increase phased over two years that this office proposed last fall was built on the $10,800 unit-cost assumption. At the $14,900 actual unit cost, that increase does not generate sufficient revenue to meet debt service coverage requirements under the SRF loan covenants by the end of the second year. I am revising the recommendation to a 16 percent increase, phased over three years rather than two — roughly 6 percent in year one, 5 percent in year two, and 5 percent in year three — which smooths the impact on ratepayers relative to a front-loaded two-year schedule while still meeting coverage requirements on the revised debt schedule. I recognize this is a larger and longer increase than the Council was told to expect four months ago, and I am prepared to walk through the underlying debt service model at the Council's convenience.

I want to be direct about one further point: this revised rate plan does not include the private-side connection cost currently under discussion in the public utilities committee. Whatever the Council decides on that question will affect either the rate model or a separate billing mechanism, and I have held that variable out of this projection so the two decisions can be evaluated on their own terms. I will update this model once a private-side policy is adopted.

I am available to present this memo's findings at the Council's March 2 meeting, or sooner if the Manager prefers.

## Memorandum 3

**TO:** Yolanda Priestley, City Manager; Councilman Warren DuBois, Chair, Public Utilities Committee; Nadia Petrosyan, Finance Director; Grace Muriuki, Water Superintendent
**FROM:** Faisal Chaudhry, Public Works Director
**DATE:** March 9, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Property Owner Consent and Refusal Policy for Private-Side Service Line Replacement

Per the City Manager's December 8 memo, this sets out the policy Public Works recommends for obtaining property owner consent to private-side replacement and for handling owners who decline to participate. This has been the most difficult piece of the program to work through, and I want to explain both the recommendation and the alternatives we rejected.

**Why the private side matters to program success.** Federal guidance and our own engineering judgment are consistent on one point: partial replacements, where the public-side lead line is replaced but a private-side lead line remains, produce worse water quality outcomes in the near term than leaving a full lead line undisturbed, because the disturbance of a partial line can dislodge scale and elevate lead levels at the tap. We cannot in good conscience replace only the public side at a property and consider that address complete. Full replacement requires access to the private side, which requires the owner's consent, and in the case of the 29 percent of affected addresses that are rental properties, the practical reality is that we are often dealing with an owner who does not live at the property and a tenant who does.

**Consent Process.** I recommend the following sequence for each property scheduled in a construction package:

First, the Water Division sends written notice to the property owner of record at least 60 days before scheduled work, describing the project, the estimated private-side cost of $3,100 (which will vary somewhat by lot depth and surface conditions), and the financing and billing options available. Second, for rental properties, we additionally notify the unit's tenant of record, both because tenants are entitled to know work will occur and because tenants are frequently the ones home to provide site access on the day of construction. Third, the owner has 30 days to return a signed consent form, which may elect a payment option (lump sum, or the assessment-based installment billing that Finance has developed, spreading the $3,100 over the property's water bill across a period of up to 10 years). Fourth, if we have not received a response within 30 days, Water Division staff will attempt direct phone or in-person contact before treating the property as a non-response.

**Handling Refusals.** This is the section the committee has focused on, and I want to be precise about what "refusal" policy actually governs. It does not govern whether the city may enter and replace the public-side line — that portion is city-owned infrastructure regardless of what happens on the private side, and Package construction will proceed on schedule for the public side at every address in the package. What the consent process governs is the private side only.

For a property where the owner affirmatively declines private-side replacement, we will complete the public-side line to the curb stop and document the refusal in the property file, including written notice to the owner that the line remains partially lead and that this fact will be disclosed in any future real estate transaction consistent with state disclosure requirements. We will offer to revisit consent annually for the remaining life of the program.

For a property where we cannot obtain a response despite the notice sequence above — what I am calling a non-response rather than a refusal — I recommend a different default. Given the health department's concern about lead exposure and the fact that non-response is disproportionately likely at rental properties where an absentee owner is simply not engaged, I recommend the city proceed with private-side replacement under implied consent after the 90-day notice-and-contact period has run, billing the cost through the installment mechanism, unless the owner affirmatively objects before construction begins. This reverses the default from what the committee discussed in February, where non-response would have meant no private-side work. I believe the reversal is necessary because leaving a non-responsive property with a partial lead line indefinitely undermines both the health objective and the water quality rationale described above, and because in my experience non-response is far more often inattention than a considered decision to keep a lead line.

**Prioritization.** Within each construction package, we will sequence work so that the 411 households with children under six identified by the health department are scheduled first, followed by the balance of the package in the order that minimizes disruption to a given street or block. This does not delay any property beyond its already-scheduled package year; it only affects sequencing within a package.

I recognize the committee has concerns about billing owners for private-side work at all, separate from the consent mechanics addressed here. I understand the City Manager intends to address that policy question directly, and I have limited this memo to process rather than to the underlying charge, since the two questions are related but distinct.

I am happy to walk the committee through this policy at its next meeting.

## Memorandum 4

**TO:** Faisal Chaudhry, Public Works Director; Yolanda Priestley, City Manager
**FROM:** Grace Muriuki, Water Superintendent
**DATE:** June 22, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Program Status Report — First 400 Replacements

This memo reports on the first 400 service line replacements completed under Package 1, out of the 620 lines in the contract, as of June 19. It covers crew productivity, water quality sampling, and issues encountered that I think warrant attention before we scope Package 2.

**Crew Productivity.** Halloran Underground Utilities mobilized two crews in mid-April. Combined, the crews have averaged 22 replacements per week over the nine full weeks of work to date, against the contract's assumed pace of 25 per week. The shortfall is attributable mostly to two factors: private-side consent processing has lagged the public-side construction schedule at roughly 15 percent of addresses, requiring crews to skip and return to properties out of sequence, which costs mobilization time; and we have encountered unmapped utility conflicts — gas and telecom primarily — at a higher rate than the engineer's estimate assumed, averaging just under one conflict per ten replacements, each adding one to three days of coordination and locate work. Neither issue is contractor performance; both are planning-level factors I recommend we build into Package 2's schedule and unit-cost assumptions.

At the current pace, Package 1's remaining 220 lines will complete in early October, roughly five weeks behind the original contract schedule but within the float built into the SRF disbursement timeline, so I do not consider this a financing concern at present.

**Water Quality Sampling.** Per the protocol the Water Division developed in January, we are taking a first-draw sample at each property before excavation, a follow-up sample immediately after replacement, and a third sample at 30 days post-replacement. Results through the first 400 properties are as follows:

Pre-replacement first-draw lead results ranged from non-detect to 38 parts per billion, with 61 properties — about 15 percent — above the EPA action level of 15 ppb, consistent with what we would expect from a lead or unknown-material population.

Immediately post-replacement sampling showed an expected short-term elevation at a subset of properties, consistent with the disturbance effect described in Faisal's March memo: 34 properties showed a temporary spike above pre-replacement levels, with the highest single reading at 61 ppb. We flushed those lines per protocol and provided bottled water and filter kits to affected households, as authorized under the program's public communication plan.

At 30 days post-replacement, of the 287 properties for which we now have third-round results, 281 show lead levels at or below 2 ppb, and the remaining 6 show levels between 3 and 9 ppb, still well under the action level and consistent with plumbing fixtures rather than the service line itself as the likely residual source. I consider this a strong early result and consistent with the outcomes the state's guidance predicted for full replacements.

I want to flag one data point for the health department's attention directly: of the 411 priority households with children under six, 118 have been completed to date, and pre-replacement sampling at those addresses showed a higher proportion of elevated results — 22 of 118, or about 19 percent — than the overall property population. I read this as validation of the prioritization decision rather than a new problem, but I think it is worth the health department having the figure.

**Refusals and Non-Responses.** Of the 400 completed properties, 11 owners affirmatively declined private-side work under the policy in Faisal's March memo, and public-side-only replacement was completed at those addresses with the required disclosure notice on file. A further 34 properties proceeded to private-side replacement under the implied-consent provision after non-response; I have had no owner objections raised after the fact at any of those 34 properties as of this writing.

**Recommendation.** I recommend Package 2 be scoped with the same crew-pace assumption revised downward to 22–23 per week rather than 25, and that we build in additional lead time on the consent notice sequence — 75 days rather than 60 — so that private-side consent status is resolved before crews reach a given block rather than during construction. I am prepared to discuss both at the next program coordination meeting.

## Memorandum 5

**TO:** Councilman Warren DuBois, Chair, Public Utilities Committee
**FROM:** Yolanda Priestley, City Manager
**DATE:** July 28, 2027
**SUBJECT:** Response to Your Objection to Private-Side Connection Charges

Thank you for your June 30 letter setting out the committee's objection to billing property owners for private-side service line replacement, and for the additional conversation we had on July 14. I told you I would respond in writing before the August Council cycle, and I am doing so now.

Your letter raises three points, and I want to address each directly rather than in summary.

**First, that billing property owners for work the city compels amounts to an unfunded mandate on residents who did not choose to have a lead line.** I do not disagree with the premise — no property owner chose the material their service line was built with, in most cases decades before they owned the property. But the private-side portion of a service line has always been the property owner's asset and the property owner's responsibility, both under our municipal code and under longstanding practice for every other private-side utility failure — a broken sewer lateral, a collapsed private water line unrelated to lead. The city has never assumed responsibility for private-side infrastructure, and the replacement program does not change that underlying allocation of ownership; it changes the circumstances under which private-side work becomes necessary. I recognize that is a legal distinction more than it is a comfort to a resident facing a $3,100 bill, and that is why Finance built the ten-year installment option into the billing structure — to convert what would otherwise be a large one-time cost into a manageable addition to a water bill, roughly $26 to $31 a month depending on financing terms.

**Second, that the rental property share of affected addresses — 29 percent — means the charge will frequently fall on absentee owners who have no incentive to consent promptly, while the health risk falls on tenants who have no control over the decision.** This is, in my judgment, the strongest part of your objection, and it is the reason Faisal's March policy includes the implied-consent provision for non-responsive owners after the 90-day notice period. I understand the committee's discomfort with a city crew entering private property without an owner's affirmative signature, and I do not take that lightly. But the alternative — leaving a partial lead line in place indefinitely at a rental property because an absentee owner never responds to mail — asks a tenant, often a tenant with no lease leverage to compel the owner to act, to simply live with elevated lead risk for the life of the program. Between those two imperfect options, I believe the implied-consent approach after documented notice attempts is the more defensible one, and Grace's June report showed no owner objections raised after the fact at any of the 34 properties where it has been used to date.

**Third, that the committee was told in December to expect an 11 percent rate increase and is now facing a 16 percent increase plus a separate private-side charge, and that this amounts to the city changing the terms of the program after the fact without adequate Council input.** I want to take responsibility for this directly rather than attribute it to the bid environment alone. Nadia's February memo explained why the rate revision was necessary once the actual bid came in 38 percent over estimate, and I believe that revision was handled appropriately — it went to the full Council, not just my office, and it was presented with the underlying debt service model available for review. But I recognize the private-side charge was developed on a separate track through the public utilities committee, and that the cumulative effect on a ratepayer's household budget — a higher water rate and a private-side assessment — was never presented to the Council as a single combined figure. That should have happened, and I am asking Nadia to prepare a combined household impact analysis for the August Council meeting so that this body is voting on the full picture, not two separate pieces considered in isolation.

I do not believe the answer to your objection is to have the city absorb the $3,100 private-side cost into the rate base for all 14,800 accounts, which is the alternative your letter proposes. Doing so would spread a cost that only 6,140 properties will actually incur across the entire ratepayer base, including the 71 percent of affected addresses that are owner-occupied and the majority of accounts not affected by lead service lines at all. I think that raises its own equity problem, arguably a larger one than the one you have identified.

What I am prepared to bring to the committee is a modification to the installment terms — extending the financing window from 10 to 15 years to further reduce the monthly impact, and directing Public Works to prioritize outreach at rental properties specifically, rather than relying on mail notice alone, to reduce the number of properties that end up in the non-response category in the first place. I would like to discuss both at the committee's August 11 meeting, alongside Nadia's combined impact analysis, before this comes back to the full Council.

I appreciate the committee's persistence on this question. I think it has made the policy better than it would have been otherwise, even where I have not agreed with every proposed remedy.

I have asked Faisal and Grace to attend the August 11 committee meeting as well, since several of the scheduling and outreach questions your letter raises are operational as much as they are financial, and I think the committee will want both perspectives in the room rather than relayed through my office secondhand.

One further item I did not address in your letter but want to raise now, since it bears on the same underlying question of fairness across the affected property population: Grace's June status report noted that 118 of the 411 priority households with children under six have been completed, with a materially higher rate of elevated pre-replacement lead readings than the property population as a whole. I have asked the health department to prepare a short supplemental note on what, if anything, that finding should change about outreach intensity at the remaining 293 priority households, particularly the rental properties among them. I will forward that note to the committee as soon as I have it, and I think it is relevant to the implied-consent discussion, since a non-response at one of those addresses carries a different weight than a non-response at a property with no young children in the household.

On the question of the combined household impact analysis, I want to set expectations accurately. Nadia's office can produce a figure showing the rate increase and the private-side installment charge together for a representative account by the August meeting, but a fully individualized figure for every affected property — accounting for lot depth, financing term elected, and whether a given property falls in Package 1, 2, or a later package — will take longer, likely through the fall. I do not want the committee to hold the August discussion waiting for a level of precision that will not be ready in time; the representative figure should be sufficient to evaluate the policy question you have raised.

Finally, I want to note for the record, since your letter characterized the private-side policy as having been adopted "without adequate Council input," that Faisal's March 9 policy memo was provided to you and the full committee in advance of the March meeting, and the committee did not, at that time, request a vote or a hold on implementation pending further review. I raise this not to relitigate the point but because I think it is useful for the committee to consider, going forward, whether it wants a standing practice of voting to affirm or modify department policy memos of this kind before they take effect, rather than raising objections after implementation has begun. That is a governance question separate from the merits of this particular policy, and I would be glad to discuss it with you and the full Council if there is interest.

I will see you and the committee on August 11.

**Attachment A: Representative Household Impact — Combined Rate and Private-Side Assessment**
*Prepared by the Finance Department for the August 11 Public Utilities Committee Meeting*

For an owner-occupied property with a private-side line requiring replacement, financed under the 15-year installment option described above, and subject to the revised three-year rate schedule from Nadia Petrosyan's February 24 memo, the estimated combined monthly impact is as follows:

A typical residential account currently pays $58 per month for water service. Under the revised rate schedule, that account would pay approximately $61 per month in year one, $64 per month in year two, and $67 per month in year three and thereafter, reflecting the full 16 percent increase phased across three years. Added to this, the private-side installment charge at $3,100 financed over 15 years at the SRF-linked rate carries a monthly payment of approximately $23, appearing as a separate line item on the water bill beginning the month after private-side work is completed at that property.

Combined, a household that has already completed private-side replacement by year three of the rate schedule would see a total monthly water bill of approximately $90, against a pre-program baseline of $58 — an increase of roughly 55 percent at that account, driven roughly two-thirds by the rate increase and one-third by the private-side assessment. Finance notes that this figure will vary by property depending on lot depth, surface restoration requirements, and which construction package year the property falls into, and that the analysis above assumes the 15-year term; a household electing the shorter 10-year term would see a higher monthly private-side charge and a correspondingly shorter repayment period.

Finance has not applied any assumed rate design changes beyond what the Council has already reviewed, and this attachment should be read as illustrative of a single representative account rather than as a household-by-household projection, which remains under development as noted in the City Manager's memo above.
