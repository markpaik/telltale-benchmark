## 1. Final-Mile Delivery Network — Atlanta and Charlotte
**Program review completed July 18, 2025 | Review period: July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025**

### Finding and recommendation

The final-mile network beat every target set for it in FY2025, and the review office verified the results as reported. Volume, unit cost, and service all cleared plan by wide margins. The finding is nonetheless qualified: a material share of the outperformance is a consequence of route density that the program did not create and cannot carry with it into a new market, and 41 percent of network volume sits with a single shipper whose contract renews in March 2026. The program is a success. It is not yet a proven template.

The review office recommends that the Executive Committee (a) move final-mile out of program status and into the FY2026 base budget as a permanent line of business under Operations; (b) approve $2.6 million in expansion funding for one additional metro, released in two tranches, with the second tranche conditioned on a route-density gate defined below; (c) direct Finance to produce metro-level contribution margin reporting by the close of Q2 FY2026; and (d) release the $340,000 in unspent program contingency to the general capital pool rather than roll it forward.

### What the evidence shows

The network moved 2.4 million parcels against a plan of 1.9 million, an overage of 26.3 percent. Cost per stop finished at $4.18 against a $4.75 target, 12.0 percent favorable. On-time performance closed at 97.2 percent, clearing the 96.0 percent service floor written into two of the three anchor contracts and eliminating the penalty exposure that had been carried as a risk since program approval. The review office reconciled delivery scans against billing records for four sample months and found a variance of less than 0.4 percent, which is within tolerance.

The unit-cost result deserves closer reading than it has received. Cost per stop is not independent of volume; it falls as stops per route rise, because the fixed cost of the driver, the vehicle, and the route hour spreads across more deliveries. Atlanta averaged 118 stops per route-day and Charlotte 104 during the second half of the year. A launch market, absent an anchor shipper's volume from day one, would run closer to 60 to 70 stops per route-day. At that density the same cost structure produces a cost per stop in the range of $5.90 to $6.30. The $4.18 figure is therefore a description of two mature, dense markets, not a portable operating standard, and the review office cautions against presenting it to the Board as one.

The volume overage warrants similar care. Of the 500,000 parcels above plan, the review office attributes approximately 380,000 to two sources: the promotional calendar of the network's largest shipper, which ran heavier than forecast in November and December 2024, and a competitor's service failure in the Atlanta market during the same period, which moved volume to Cumberland on short notice and largely retained it through the spring. Neither source is a repeatable capability. Stripped of both, the network still finished ahead of plan, by roughly 6 percent, which is a genuine and creditable result — but it is a different result than the headline suggests.

Concentration is the material risk. The largest shipper accounts for 41 percent of network parcels and, by the review office's estimate, a larger share of contribution because its density profile is the most favorable in the book. That contract renews in March 2026. Loss or material repricing at renewal would cut route density in Atlanta below the level at which the current cost structure holds, and the effect would be non-linear: the network would lose the volume and the unit economics at the same time.

### What could not be verified

The review office was unable to state contribution margin by metro. Cumberland's finance system reports final-mile revenue at the Southeast region level and allocates injection line-haul across the region on a weight-based key that predates the program. Delivery cost is clean and auditable; revenue attribution is not. The program's fully loaded FY2025 cost, including injection line-haul, dispatch, and technology, was $8.4 million, of which $6.0 million was direct delivery cost against approximately 1.44 million stops. Against that, the review office can say with confidence that the program is cheaper to run than plan assumed. It cannot yet say, with the precision an expansion decision deserves, how profitable it is in each city. This is a reporting gap, not a performance concern, and it should be closed before the FY2027 cycle.

### Disposition of committed funds

Final-mile was funded as an operating program rather than a capital project, so there is no asset base at risk and nothing to write off. The variable budget flexed with volume as designed. The $340,000 contingency, held against a Charlotte facility expansion that higher-than-expected throughput per door made unnecessary, remains unspent and should not be rolled into FY2026; the review office recommends its release to the capital pool, where it is available to offset the recommended expansion tranche.

The $2.6 million expansion request should be structured in two releases. The first, $1.1 million, funds site selection, fleet, and the first ninety days of operation in a single metro. The second, $1.5 million, should be withheld until the launch market sustains 85 stops per route-day for six consecutive weeks. That gate is the operative test of whether the FY2025 economics travel. If the gate is not met, the correct action is to hold the market at its achieved scale rather than fund it to plan.

### Decision requested by August 14

Approve base-budget conversion, the two-tranche expansion authority with the density gate, the Q2 FY2026 reporting deliverable, and the contingency release. The review office additionally asks that Commercial brief the Executive Committee on the March 2026 renewal posture at the October meeting, not later.

## 2. Refrigerated Freight Pilot — Nashville
**Program review completed July 21, 2025 | Review period: September 2024 launch through June 30, 2025**

### Finding and recommendation

The refrigerated pilot has failed against every condition on which it was approved, and the failure is structural rather than executional. The review office recommends termination, with an orderly wind-down concluded by October 31, 2025, and asset recovery pursued on the schedule set out below. This recommendation is unconditional. The review office does not recommend an extension, a rescoped continuation, or a further capital injection, and it specifically advises the Executive Committee against approving the twelve-month rehabilitation plan the program team has indicated it will propose.

### What the evidence shows

The pilot launched in September 2024 against $6.8 million of approved capital, with a business case built on 70 percent trailer utilization as the breakeven threshold and three contracted anchor shippers to underwrite it. Through June 30, 2025, utilization averaged 41 percent. The program lost $2.3 million on the year. Two of the three anchor shippers have exited — one at contract expiry in February 2025, one on a for-cause termination in May 2025 following a temperature-excursion claim that Cumberland settled for $84,000.

The utilization gap is the whole of the case. Recovering from 41 to 70 percent requires a 71 percent increase in booked refrigerated volume. The single remaining anchor represents approximately 34 percent of current loaded miles. Closing the gap therefore requires either that the remaining anchor roughly triple its committed volume, which its own network cannot support, or that the program replace two anchors and add materially beyond them in a Nashville lane structure where Cumberland has no incumbent refrigerated position, no cold-chain certification history beyond ten months, and now one settled claim on its record. The review office interviewed the two departed shippers. Neither cited price. Both cited service reliability and the absence of backhaul coverage, which is a density problem, and density problems do not resolve at 41 percent utilization — they compound, because thin books produce empty returns, which produce the cost structure that loses the next bid.

The cost of continuation is knowable. At the FY2025 run rate, the pilot consumes approximately $192,000 per month in operating loss before any recovery spending. A twelve-month extension therefore carries an expected additional loss of $2.3 million at current volumes, against a recovery scenario the review office assesses as unlikely on the evidence above. That is not a close call, and the review office states plainly that it should not be treated as one.

The review office also notes, for the Executive Committee's attention on process rather than on this program's merits, that the pilot's monthly reporting showed utilization against a 55 percent "ramp target" rather than against the 70 percent breakeven on which capital was approved. The 41 percent result read as a 14-point miss in the operating reviews when it was in fact a 29-point miss against the approval basis. Reporting against ramp targets rather than approval thresholds should be prohibited.

### Disposition of committed funds

Of the $6.8 million committed, the review office's recovery estimate is as follows, based on secondary-market quotations obtained in June and July 2025 and on a facilities assessment by Corporate Real Estate:

- **Refrigerated trailer fleet (40 units, $4.6 million original):** recoverable at $2.8 million to $3.2 million on the secondary market. Reefer trailers of this vintage and specification hold value well, and two brokers have indicated appetite for the full lot. Recovery is time-sensitive; the market softens after the Q4 produce season.
- **Nashville cold-dock retrofit ($1.6 million original):** substantially sunk. The insulated dock area and refrigeration plant are fixed to a leased facility with seven years remaining. Corporate Real Estate assesses a sublease to a regional food distributor as plausible, which would recover $180,000 to $340,000 in net present terms, but this should not be booked as an expectation.
- **Cold-chain telematics, monitoring, and compliance systems ($600,000 original):** approximately $110,000 redeployable to the dry fleet; the balance sunk.

Total expected recovery is $3.1 million to $3.6 million against $6.8 million committed, with a residual write-down of $3.2 million to $3.7 million to be booked in FY2026. The review office recommends the Executive Committee approve the write-down in the August cycle rather than defer it, so that FY2026 opens clean. Deferral does not improve the outcome and complicates the FY2026 variance narrative.

The wind-down itself carries cost that must be funded rather than absorbed: approximately $410,000 covering the remaining anchor's contractual notice period, driver severance or redeployment for eleven refrigerated-qualified drivers, lease-hold reversion obligations, and disposal transaction costs. The review office recommends this be appropriated explicitly rather than left to the Nashville terminal's operating budget, which cannot absorb it.

Of the eleven refrigerated-qualified drivers, Talent has confirmed that nine can be placed into open dry-van seats in Memphis and Nashville within thirty days. The review office recommends they be placed rather than released; the fleet is short, and the reputational cost of a visible layoff in the Nashville driver market exceeds the payroll saving.

### Decision requested by August 14

Authorize termination with a wind-down completion date of October 31, 2025; approve the $410,000 wind-down appropriation; approve the FY2026 write-down of $3.2 to $3.7 million; direct that trailer disposal commence immediately rather than await formal closure, to preserve seasonal pricing; and adopt the reporting rule that program performance be reported against approval thresholds, not against interim ramp targets.

## 3. Driver Academy
**Program review completed July 22, 2025 | Review period: July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025**
**Program owner: Yolanda Brissett, Talent Director**

### Finding and recommendation

The Driver Academy produced its intended output and cannot presently demonstrate that it produced its intended outcome. It graduated 218 drivers at $9,400 each, a total of $2.05 million, and the metric it exists to move — twelve-month fleet retention — improved from 54 to 63 percent. But a company-wide pay increase took effect in the same quarter that the retention curve turned, and no measurement design was in place to separate the two effects. Meanwhile the Academy's own graduates in the Birmingham lane turned over at 48 percent, materially worse than the fleet average, which is difficult to reconcile with a claim that Academy training drives retention.

The review office recommends that the Executive Committee fund the Academy for FY2026 at its current scale and not above it — approximately $2.0 million for 200 seats — and attach to that funding a measurement regime capable of answering the attribution question by the Q3 FY2026 review, at which point a scale-or-stop decision should be taken on evidence. The review office further recommends that the Birmingham lane be removed from the Academy's placement pipeline and referred to Operations as a lane-economics problem, and that the Academy's service-agreement clawback provisions be enforced or abandoned, because their present condition is worse than either.

### What the evidence shows

The 218 graduates are real, verified against licensing records and payroll onboarding. Cost per graduate of $9,400 is accurate and includes instruction, the Memphis classroom lease, two training tractors, simulator amortization, and trainee wages during the program. Graduation rate from enrollment was 81 percent, which is sound for the industry and reflects credible admissions screening rather than lax completion standards.

Fleet-wide twelve-month retention rose nine points, from 54 to 63 percent. Against a company driver fleet of approximately 640 seats, nine points is roughly 58 avoided separations. At a fully loaded replacement cost of $11,200 per driver — recruiting, orientation, road testing, and unseated-truck revenue loss — the improvement is worth approximately $650,000 annually against $2.05 million spent. On that arithmetic alone the Academy does not pay for itself, and it would not be expected to in its first full year; the case for it has always rested on a multi-year retention effect and on securing driver supply in a constrained market rather than on a one-year return.

The attribution problem is the finding that matters. The company-wide pay increase took effect in January 2025 and carries an annualized cost of approximately $4.6 million. Retention began improving in the same quarter. The increase applied to every driver, Academy graduate or not. The review office therefore cannot state what portion of the nine-point improvement the Academy produced, and neither can the program. When retention improves and two interventions land together, the cheaper one gets the credit unless the measurement was designed in advance, and here it was not.

Birmingham complicates the picture further. Academy graduates placed in that lane separated at 48 percent within twelve months, unchanged from the pre-Academy baseline and worse than the fleet average by a wide margin. If Academy training were the operative retention mechanism, Birmingham graduates would show some improvement. They show none. The review office's interviews point to lane structure rather than training: the Birmingham runs are five-day out-and-back schedules with unpredictable Friday returns, and two regional competitors within forty miles pay comparably for home-daily work. No amount of pre-employment training corrects for that, and continuing to feed graduates into the lane converts a $9,400 investment into a $9,400 loss at roughly one graduate in two.

The review office also examined the eighteen-month service agreements signed by all 218 graduates. Sixty-one graduates separated within twelve months. Prorated clawback of $312,000 was billed. Collections total $47,000, a rate of 15 percent. Talent reports that collection efforts are inconsistent, that no third-party recovery process exists, and that in several cases the separation was a company-initiated release, which voids the clawback correctly but was not distinguished in the billing. An unenforced clawback is not a cost recovery, and its presence in the business case overstates the Academy's net cost position by roughly $265,000.

### Disposition of committed funds

The $2.05 million spent in FY2025 is not recoverable and should not be presented as recoverable. It purchased 218 licensed drivers, of whom 157 remained at the twelve-month mark, and that outcome is either worth the money or it is not — an unresolved question that the recommended measurement regime is designed to close.

The Academy's physical assets retain value and support continuation at current scale without new capital: two training tractors, the simulator, and the Memphis classroom build-out carry an estimated $480,000 in remaining book value. No incremental capital request accompanies the FY2026 recommendation.

The measurement regime should be specified now, because it must be in place before the FY2026 cohorts begin. The review office asks the Executive Committee to direct four things:

- Track twelve-month retention separately for Academy graduates and for externally sourced drivers hired in the same months, which has never been done and is the single comparison the program requires.
- Report retention by lane, not only fleet-wide, so that a Birmingham-type failure cannot be concealed inside an improving aggregate.
- Hold Academy intake flat across FY2026 so that cohort size does not vary with the confounders.
- Resolve the clawback: either fund an enforcement process with a defined collection target, or remove the provision and restate the business case without it.

### Decision requested by August 14

Approve FY2026 funding of $2.0 million at 200 seats with no scale increase; adopt the four measurement directives; direct Operations to return a Birmingham lane redesign or exit recommendation by the October meeting; and schedule the Academy's scale-or-stop decision for the Q3 FY2026 review.

## 4. Olive Branch Automation Retrofit
**Program review completed July 24, 2025 | Review period: July 1, 2024 – June 30, 2025**
**Program sponsor: Hasan Demirci, VP of Engineering**

### Finding and recommendation

The Olive Branch retrofit is 38 percent over its approved capital and eight months late, and the productivity it was built to deliver is real and measurable. Both statements are true, and the recommendation follows from both. The review office recommends that the Executive Committee authorize completion — because the cost of stopping now exceeds the cost of finishing, and because the verified performance in the live zone supports the underlying engineering case — but that it do so by issuing a new capital authorization on a re-baselined total rather than by approving a variance against the original $14.2 million. The original authorization should be closed as overspent. Continuation should be conditioned as set out below, and no further automation capital should be released to any facility until Olive Branch has run ninety consecutive days at full-build run rate.

The review office reports separately, and with emphasis, that the governance failure on this project is more serious than the cost overrun.

### What the evidence shows

The project was approved at $14.2 million with a March 2025 completion date. Spend through June 30, 2025 is $19.6 million. Completion has moved to November 2025. The productivity result in the one live zone — commissioned in April 2025 — is a 31 percent increase in picks per labor hour, which the review office validated independently against WMS transaction logs for the eight weeks ending June 27. The gain is genuine and is holding as the operators move past the learning curve.

The overrun decomposes into three parts. Approximately $2.1 million reflects the replacement of the controls integrator in January 2025 after eleven weeks of failed commissioning attempts, including re-engineering and schedule recovery. Approximately $1.8 million reflects scope that was added and never separately approved: a second induction line requested by Operations in October 2024, and fire-suppression work in the new mezzanine that the fire marshal required in December 2024. The remaining $1.5 million is distributed across steel escalation, extended contractor mobilization, and the cost of running the facility in hybrid mode longer than planned.

The schedule slip and the hybrid-mode cost are linked and material. Olive Branch currently runs part-automated and part-manual, a configuration that is less efficient than either the original manual layout or the finished design, and which the review office costs at approximately $95,000 per month in excess labor and handling. Every month of further delay carries that charge in addition to project spend.

The benefits case must be restated. The original approval claimed $4.1 million in annual benefit and a 3.5-year payback. Extrapolating the verified live-zone result across the full build, and net of $520,000 in new annual maintenance, licensing, and spares that the original case understated, the review office estimates a defensible run-rate benefit of $2.7 million to $3.1 million per year. The live zone was the densest and most favorable of the four; the remaining zones will not perform as well. Against a re-baselined total in the range of $21.5 million to $24.0 million — the project team estimates $1.9 million to complete, while the review office's independent estimate is $3.1 million to $4.4 million — payback lands between 7.0 and 8.5 years against a ten-year asset life and a facility lease running to 2034. That is a marginal project. It is not a failed one, and it is not one worth abandoning.

Abandonment is the alternative the Executive Committee should weigh explicitly, and it is worse. The $19.6 million is sunk. Installed conveyance, the shuttle system, and mezzanine steel would recover an estimated $2.1 million to $2.9 million net of removal cost. The facility would revert to manual operation at a cost of roughly $600,000 in reconfiguration, forfeiting the verified 31 percent gain in the live zone. Stopping therefore costs $2.1 million or more in cash and eliminates the entire benefit stream, against $3.1 to $4.4 million to complete and secure $2.7 to $3.1 million annually. The arithmetic favors completion clearly.

### The governance finding

Internal project records show that $2.9 million of the overrun was identified by the project controls team in December 2024. It was reported to the Executive Committee in May 2025. During the intervening five months the project appeared in the operating review as on budget with schedule risk. The review office does not attribute this to concealment; it attributes it to the absence of any rule requiring escalation. There was no threshold, no clock, and no route by which a controls analyst's finding reached this Committee. The review office recommends that any capital project variance exceeding 10 percent of authorization be reported to the Executive Committee within thirty days of identification, in writing, by the project controls function directly and not through the sponsor.

### Disposition of committed funds

The $19.6 million already spent should be closed against the original authorization, which is thereby recorded as overspent by $5.4 million, and capitalized as construction in progress. The review office recommends against re-baselining the original approval to absorb the overrun; doing so would erase the record of a 38 percent variance from the capital ledger, and the Board should be able to see it.

Completion should be funded through a fresh authorization of $4.4 million — the upper end of the review office's independent estimate, not the project team's $1.9 million — with the following conditions attached:

- **No further scope.** Any addition, from any function, requires Executive Committee approval before work proceeds. The second induction line and the mezzanine suppression work each entered without it.
- **Monthly gate reporting** to the Executive Committee through commissioning, prepared by project controls rather than by the sponsor, showing spend to date, cost to complete, and schedule against the November 2025 re-baseline.
- **A restated benefits case** submitted for approval before final commissioning, replacing the $4.1 million figure with the verified run-rate estimate. The FY2026 operating budget should be built on the restated number.
- **A ninety-day performance hold on all other automation capital.** Two requests totaling $23 million, for Charlotte and Dallas, are in the FY2026 pipeline. Neither should be released until Olive Branch has sustained full-build run rate for ninety consecutive days.
- **A $500,000 unallocated contingency** held by Finance rather than by the project, released only against documented cause.

### Decision requested by August 14

Close the original authorization as overspent; approve a new $4.4 million completion authorization with the five conditions above; adopt the 30-day, 10-percent variance escalation rule for all capital projects; and defer the Charlotte and Dallas automation requests pending the ninety-day performance hold.

## Appendix A — Review method and evidentiary standard

The FY2025 portfolio comprised eleven active programs. Four met the materiality threshold adopted by the Executive Committee in September 2023 — capital authorization above $5 million, annual operating cost above $1.5 million, or designation by the Committee as strategically significant — and those four are the subject of this report. The remaining seven were subject to desk review only, and are addressed in the schedule of carried items at Appendix E.

The Program Review Office applies a single evidentiary standard across all reviews: a reported result is accepted only when it can be reconstructed from a source system the program does not control. Delivery scans, WMS transaction logs, payroll and licensing records, general ledger postings, and executed contracts satisfy this test. Program-maintained trackers, dashboards, and status decks do not, and were used in this cycle only to identify questions, never to answer them.

Where the review office states a number as an estimate — route density in a hypothetical launch market, secondary-market trailer recovery, cost to complete at Olive Branch — the basis is disclosed in the workpapers and the number is presented as a range rather than a point. Where a program's own estimate differs from the review office's, both are shown. This occurred once in this cycle, on the Olive Branch cost to complete, where the project team's $1.9 million and the review office's $3.1 to $4.4 million reflect different assumptions about commissioning duration and integrator standby cost. The review office has recommended budgeting to the higher figure and considers the difference itself a useful signal about the estimating discipline on that project.

Fieldwork ran from April 14 to July 24, 2025, and included forty-one interviews, eleven of them with parties outside Cumberland: two departed refrigerated shippers, two trailer brokers, the replaced and current controls integrators at Olive Branch, and five Birmingham-lane drivers who separated during the review period. External interviews were conducted without program staff present. The review office notes that access was unrestricted in all four programs and that no sponsor sought to limit or condition it.

Draft findings were provided to each program sponsor on July 10 for factual correction. Marcus Oyelaran received written responses from Yolanda Brissett and Hasan Demirci; both corrections were accepted and are reflected above. The refrigerated program submitted no response by the closing date. Disagreements of judgment, as distinct from fact, are not resolved by this process, and the review office understands that the refrigerated program team intends to present an alternative recommendation to the Executive Committee. That is proper, and the Committee should hear it.

The Executive Committee is asked to take fourteen decisions across the four programs. They are consolidated here so that the Committee can see the aggregate financial effect in one place before the August 14 lock.

**New money requested, FY2026:** $9.41 million, comprising $2.6 million for final-mile expansion in two tranches, $410,000 for the refrigerated wind-down, $2.0 million for the Driver Academy at flat scale, and $4.4 million to complete Olive Branch.

**Money released or recovered:** $340,000 in unspent final-mile contingency returned to the capital pool, and $3.1 to $3.6 million in expected refrigerated asset recovery, of which $2.8 to $3.2 million depends on trailer disposal completing before the Q4 seasonal softening.

**Money written down:** $3.2 to $3.7 million on the refrigerated pilot, recommended for recognition in the August cycle rather than deferral.

**Capital deferred:** $23 million in Charlotte and Dallas automation requests, held pending ninety days of Olive Branch run-rate performance.

The net first-year cash effect of adopting all recommendations is an outflow of approximately $5.5 to $6.0 million against the FY2026 plan, before the deferral of $23 million in pipeline capital, which is the larger number and the one the review office would ask the Committee to hold in view. Three of the four programs in this cycle were approved on business cases that later proved unverifiable in whole or in part. A ninety-day pause on the next $23 million is cheap insurance.

## Appendix C — Cross-program observations

Three patterns appeared in more than one program and are reported here because they are properties of Cumberland's program governance rather than of any individual project.

**Measurement was designed after the result, not before the work.** The Driver Academy is the clearest case: two interventions landed in the same quarter, retention improved, and no design existed that could apportion the improvement. But the same weakness appears at final-mile, where the review office could not produce metro-level contribution margin because revenue attribution was never built, and at Olive Branch, where the benefits case rested on a $4.1 million figure that no one had traced to a measurable operating baseline until this review. Only the refrigerated pilot had an unambiguous measurement basis — 70 percent utilization — and it is the program that failed. The review office does not think that is a coincidence. Programs with a hard, single-number approval threshold are harder to defend and easier to kill, which is precisely their value.

**Reporting drifted from the approval basis to the operating basis.** The refrigerated pilot reported against a 55 percent ramp target rather than the 70 percent breakeven on which capital was granted, which converted a 29-point miss into an apparent 14-point one. Olive Branch reported schedule risk while a $2.9 million cost variance sat unreported for five months. In both cases the operating reviews were technically accurate and materially misleading. The remedy recommended in the refrigerated summary — that programs report against approval thresholds, always, with ramp targets shown only as supplementary — should be adopted portfolio-wide rather than for one program.

**There is no escalation path that bypasses the sponsor.** The Olive Branch controls team identified the overrun and had nowhere to take it. The review office recommends that the 30-day, 10-percent variance rule proposed in that summary be paired with a standing obligation on project controls and program finance staff to report directly, and with an explicit statement from the Executive Committee that doing so is expected rather than tolerated. A rule without that statement will not be used.

A fourth observation is offered more tentatively. Two of the four programs — final-mile and Olive Branch — delivered genuine operating gains, and in both cases the gain was concentrated in the most favorable conditions available: the densest metros, the densest pick zone. Cumberland's programs appear to work where the underlying economics were already good and to struggle where they were not. That is not a criticism of program design so much as a caution about extrapolation, and it is the reason the review office has attached a density gate to final-mile expansion and a run-rate hold to further automation capital.

## Appendix D — Disposition of FY2025 committed funds, consolidated

Across the four programs, $42.65 million was committed or spent during or prior to the review period: $14.2 million authorized and $19.6 million spent at Olive Branch, $6.8 million capital at the refrigerated pilot, $2.05 million operating at the Driver Academy, and $8.4 million fully loaded operating at final-mile.

Of that total, $19.6 million remains as an asset in service or in progress at Olive Branch and is recommended for capitalization as construction in progress against a closed and overspent authorization. Approximately $3.1 to $3.6 million of the refrigerated commitment is recoverable through disposal and redeployment, with $3.2 to $3.7 million recommended for write-down. The Driver Academy's $2.05 million is expensed and unrecoverable, save for $480,000 in retained training assets that carry forward and require no replacement capital in FY2026. Final-mile's operating spend is fully consumed against delivered volume, with $340,000 of contingency unspent and recommended for release.

The review office draws the Committee's attention to one recovery item at risk of slipping through the FY2026 planning process. The refrigerated trailer disposal is time-sensitive in a way that the write-down decision is not. Two brokers have indicated appetite for the full forty-unit lot at present pricing; that appetite historically softens after the Q4 produce season. If formal program closure waits on the August 14 lock and disposal waits on formal closure, Cumberland will likely realize the lower end of the $2.8 to $3.2 million range rather than the upper. The review office has therefore recommended that disposal commence on Executive Committee direction alone, in advance of closure documentation, and asks that this be recorded as a distinct decision rather than folded into the termination approval.

## Appendix E — Items carried to the FY2026 cycle

Seven programs received desk review only and are carried forward. Four of these — the Memphis yard-management system, the customs brokerage joint venture, the dedicated-fleet expansion in Louisville, and the transportation management system upgrade — will cross the materiality threshold during FY2026 on current spend trajectories and should be scoped for full review at the October planning session.

Three matters raised during this cycle fall outside the scope of program review and are referred rather than reported. The allocation methodology for injection line-haul across the Southeast region, which prevented metro-level margin reporting at final-mile, is referred to the Controller. The Birmingham lane's structural economics are referred to Operations. The service-agreement clawback process, including the distinction between voluntary separations and company-initiated releases, is referred to Talent and Legal jointly.

Finally, the review office notes that it did not examine the company-wide pay increase that took effect in January 2025, because it was not constituted as a program and therefore fell outside this office's charter. It nonetheless materially affects the interpretation of at least one program in this report, and possibly the fleet-cost baseline underlying two others. The review office recommends that the Executive Committee consider whether interventions of that scale should be brought within program governance in future, or whether a separate post-implementation review mechanism is required for them. The present arrangement — under which a $4.6 million annualized decision receives less structured after-the-fact scrutiny than a $2.0 million training program — is difficult to defend on any reading.
